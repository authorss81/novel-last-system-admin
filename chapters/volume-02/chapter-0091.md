# Chapter 0091 — The First of October Is A Wednesday

**Monday the fourth of August 2014, ten to six in the morning, the vestibule of the Old Ward, ground floor.**

It is the smallest room in this city that anybody has ever gone into on purpose, and it has a radiator under a window that does not open and forty-one folding chairs roped off in a corner, and the corridor outside it is the one everybody in the civic offices walks under at twenty to eight in the morning whether they want to or not.

Jonas Mercer had come down at ten to six because a man with a laminated card had told him in July that he was going to be asked a question about the lowland in September, and Jonas had worked out, on the way down the stairs, that the answer to that question was going to be a date, and that the date would have to be written on a wall somewhere, and that he was the only person in this city who was going to be in this corridor at ten to six in the morning in the first week of August and who would think about it.

The laminated sheet was still on the board. It had been there since the second of June and it said what it had always said, and under the return date somebody had written, in biro, in a hand that was not Nina Calder's and that nobody has ever claimed:

> *RETURNED 4/8. SEE COUNTER.*

He stood and looked at that for about a minute and a half.

**At six o'clock exactly, two things happened, and neither of them made a noise.**

The first was a heat protocol return falling due, from a housing association of four blocks and a hundred and twenty-eight rooms, whose first answer had cost a clerk of sixty-one an afternoon in May and had put a chief officer's name into a public document with a crest on it. Seventy days from the twenty-sixth of May. It was a Monday. It was about to stop being a date and become a thing that had happened, and nobody in this building was going to know that it had happened until somebody typed it.

The second was a node lapsing. **KH-4/20**, the residential floor of a private clinic on a private road in Kestrel Hill: eleven patients and a day room above it, placed at Tier 2 on the twenty-ninth of May by the Aldbrook Trust at four thousand two hundred pounds a year, term to six o'clock this morning. Nobody had re-priced it. Nobody was required to. When a term ends and nothing replaces it, the node does not stop — it narrows, to its last verified essential use, and where no last verified essential use has ever been recorded, to its maintenance.

And nobody had recorded one.

**So at six o'clock on the fourth of August 2014, the residential floor of the Vale Clinic became a stair, a door, and a lift.**

There is no field on any plate in this city that says which of those three things the eleven people on that floor are.

---

The register of narrowings was still on the left of the board, in Nina Calder's format, on the city's own paper, because the Lattice cannot publish a narrowing and the city can.

It had **sixteen** entries on it.

It had fifteen on the eighteenth of July and it has sixteen now, and the one that was added was typed at about eleven o'clock this morning by a records clerk who did not know why she was typing it, because the drawing layer had drawn a new shape on a board in Gantry Ward at six o'clock in the morning and a shape on a board has to be dealt with, and nobody in the world had asked her to.

Six-L/16, the lowland spur and outfall, 12 May, Tier 1, held. 6-44, the mortuary van gate, 11 May, Tier 2, held. 6-41, the Saint Orra west care entrance, 11 May, Tier 2, held. A node in a block of 1938 in Carrow Lane with no water since the sixth of May, the node itself still at Tier 2 at four thousand two hundred pounds a year. A node in Tideglass withdrawn on the fourth of July, term ended at six, no replacement sought. Two nodes in Gantry Ward and one on the old canal side, deferred, no party, no address. A transport operator's line, of which nine of its eleven are not services. A row of six lowland water points with a column for the number of households served and a number in the column. A node at Kestrel Hill that came off a schedule on the seventeenth of July and went to its maintenance.

And at the bottom of the form, a **blank line**, with no rule and no caption and no label, which is not a design feature, and which Nina Calder has answered the same way four times.

Jonas Mercer counted the sixteen, because he counts things, and then he took the pen out of his coat and wrote on the back of a delivery note, standing up, in the cold, in eleven feet by nine.

> *Monday 4 August 2014, 05:52. Two dates gone in eight minutes. Lapsed at 06:00, KH-4/20, residential floor, eleven patients and a day room, placed 29/05 at £4,200 by a trust, no last verified essential use recorded, so the use is the maintenance, and the maintenance is a stair and a door and a lift nobody has ridden since 2011.*
> *From today to Wednesday 1 October 2014: fifty-eight days. Ninety days from service of notice at 11:00 on Thursday 3 July. It is a Wednesday. The Aldbrook Trust has answered a machine on 4 July and a person on 7 July and the person was sixty-three and she signed the assignment of the second of May and she is going to do her job with a notice when it arrives, and the trust has not noticed that it has been notified, and there is no instrument in this city that can make a person or a company notice that it has been notified.*
> *I am not going to do anything about either of these today.*

He put the pen back in his coat and went up the stairs.

**Monday the fourth of August 2014, twenty past ten in the morning, the counter, the records office, second floor.**

The queue was three deep and the woman at the front of it was doing a tenancy matter and had brought a folder. Nina Calder typed with two fingers and did not look up for about a minute and a half, and then she did, and she said:

"You have not telephoned Marguerite Oyelaran."

"No."

"That is five times." She let the machine catch up. "I have telephoned her three times since the twenty-eighth of May, and I told her about the hundred and six before anybody queried it, which she was extremely grateful for and which she paid for with an afternoon she has not told the department about. And you have not telephoned her once, and I am not going to tell you that is a different thing, because it is the same thing with a different person doing it and one of the two of us is going to be mentioned in about four years."

"I know."

"You do not know, you have a face." She turned the machine round to face him, which she has done four times this year. "It came in Friday afternoon. It was handed in at the counter at about ten to five and it was in by post on Monday morning and it is a public document from the moment it was complete and I have read it twice, and I am going to read you the two boxes, and then you are going to leave, and then I am going to go back to this woman's folder."

She did not read the form out. She read him the two boxes, in the flat voice, standing up, with a counter between her and the room, and there were two people waiting and one of them did not.

**Box one wants a number.** The number that went in it was **NINETY-TWO**, in capitals, in biro, on a form with a crest on it, in a box with a line the width of a ruler in it and no ceiling.

**Box two wants a name.** In box two there is M. OYELARAN, CHIEF OFFICER, CARROW LANE HA, BY POST AND TELEPHONE, and then a ring number, in the records office's own hand, because the box on the form is the borough's and the ring number is hers.

"There," said Nina Calder. "**Ninety-two.** Not a hundred and six. Deliberately, and by somebody who has read about the first one, and I am not going to improve on it and I am not going to tell you what she thinks she has done, because that is hers to say and she has not said it to me, and I telephoned her at twenty to nine this morning and she said one sentence and hung up, and the sentence was *good*."

"That is one word."

"It is one word and it was the whole of the conversation and I have had three of them this year from that woman and the other two were about money." She squared the machine. "And I am going to say the other thing, because I am the records office and this is my counter and I have been designing forms since March.

"**There are now two public numbers from that body and neither of them is about a household.** A hundred and six in May, ninety-two this morning, both in a ring-number box on a form with a crest on it, both legible to the whole borough since the day they were written. **The difference between them is a number and the difference is not a household.** There is no instrument in this city that can turn a difference into a household, and that is the only good thing about it, and it is also the reason it is going to hurt somebody, and I could not tell you this morning which of those two things is going to happen first."

"Who is going to be hurt."

"I am not going to help you with that," said Nina Calder, "because you have not telephoned her and if you telephoned her now you would be doing the fifth thing you have done this year that a person finds out about from a document, and I have read the fourth one off a public counter in a flat voice for six minutes and I would not read the fifth."

She turned back to the machine. The woman with the folder said, "Is it much longer?"

"It is about a minute," said Nina Calder, "and I am sorry, and it is not me being slow, and you may sit down."

**Monday the fourth of August 2014, about two in the afternoon, the private road, Kestrel Hill.**

He had a reason for being there and the reason was a barrel bolt on a store door that he had done in June and that had dropped a half-inch in the heat, and he went out on the bus and walked the last half mile because there is no bus on a private road.

The Vale Clinic is a two-storey brick building behind a wall, with a car park for six cars, a sign that has been repainted twice, and a green bin by the gate. There is no lift in the borough's asset register for it because it is not the borough's. There is a lift.

He stood in the car park for about four minutes and did not go in, and then he went in, because the door was open and it is a clinic and there is a day room.

**The door was open because of a fire door wedge, red plastic, on the floor by the swing of it, and it has been there since the second week of May and nobody has ever mentioned it in writing.** There is no plate on that door. There is a plate four feet inside, at ankle height, on the frame, and Jonas Mercer got down on one knee in a corridor with a cleaner's trolley going past and read it, and the plate says this:

> **KH-4/20. RESIDENTIAL FLOOR. LAST VERIFIED ESSENTIAL USE: NONE RECORDED. SERVES: THE MAINTENANCE OF THIS NODE.**

And under that, in the flat register the plates have carried since before the war, and which is the only writing on it that is not a schedule:

> **THE MAINTENANCE OF THIS NODE IS THE STAIR, THE DOOR AND THE LIFT.**

The lift has a panel beside it and the panel says **OUT OF SERVICE** and a year, and the year is **2011**, and there is a service contractor's sticker on the lift door with a date on it and the date is the eleventh of March 2013 and it is a sticker about the fireman's override and it has nothing to do with the lift working.

The stair goes up. The stair is carpeted and clean and there are two chairs on the half landing for people who cannot manage the last flight, and the chairs are on the half landing because somebody put them there, and there is a handrail on both sides.

The day room is on the first floor and it has eleven chairs and a television and a hatch and a whiteboard with the day's date and nothing else on the whiteboard, because the names go on the whiteboard in a dry-wipe pen and are wiped off at four o'clock and have been for nine years.

The man who was in it was sixty-one and had a cardigan on over a work top and was doing a jigsaw of a coastline on a table that four of the eleven chairs were pulled up to, and he had been opening that door at a quarter past seven in the morning for nine years.

"Can I help you," he said.

"I'm not here for anything," said Jonas Mercer. "I did a barrel bolt in June."

"Right." The man looked at him for a second. "Are you the one who comes about the plate."

"I'm not here for anything," said Jonas Mercer, and the man said, "Right," and went back to the coastline.

And then, about a minute later, without looking up, he said:

"**You're the first person who has come up that stair in about four months who has not wanted something off the ground floor.** Nine years I've been on that door at a quarter past seven. Nobody's ever asked me what it's for. I open it. The wedge is there because the closer was done in 2011 and the arm's bent and if that door shut on its own at seven in the morning with eleven people upstairs then that's a different sort of morning, and I put the wedge there in May and nobody's said a word about it either way."

Jonas Mercer looked at the wedge, and it was a red plastic fire door wedge, and it was the right thing, and there was no form in the world that could hold it.

"What's your name," he said, and then heard himself, and did not take it back.

"Henry. Oduya." He turned a piece of coastline round. "**Mr Mercer, whatever it is you're not going to tell me, don't tell me it's nothing's changed. Because something has changed. My key doesn't turn the plate any more, that's all I know, and I've had that key since 2011, and I put the plate in myself in April because the arm was bent, and it's not my plate, it's a badge reader now, and a badge reader at seven in the morning is a man with a card, and I am sixty-one and I am still on the paper rota, so it works. And the day after somebody reads a notice and takes me off the paper rota, that door shuts at seven in the morning, and there's no wedge in the world that survives me.**

"That's all. Don't do anything about it. I've got a jigsaw."

Jonas Mercer stood in the doorway of the day room for about a minute and a half and then went back down the stair and out past the wedge and down the half mile to the bus stop.

**He did not tell the Aldbrook Trust. He had no authority over its building, he was not a party, he had not been asked, and there is no instrument in this city by which a man who repairs permissions can require a freeholder to be notified of its own node.** And there was a notice already in the post since the third of July, and a clerk of sixty-three in Kestrel Hill with a lever-arch file who would do her job with it in October, and Jonas Mercer's opinion about whether a clinic's residential floor should be on a continuity schedule was not a thing anybody had asked him for and was not a thing anybody had ever once listened to.

**Tuesday the fifth of August 2014, ten past nine in the morning, level zero, Saint Orra west.**

He went down to level zero with a key in his hand for a mortuary van gate that wanted cutting in August, and he went past the decontamination room because the decontamination room is on the way to everything on level zero, and the door was open, and he stopped.

The verification log is on the tray on the wall by the door. It is a printed grid of **fifty-two Sundays**, one per week for the year, and under each date there is a printed box for a chamber temperature and a printed box for a signature.

**There are four books of consecutive Sundays with a tick in the temperature box and a signature box empty. There is a fifth book with neither. The supplier's regime is signed by a doctor on level two, and the doctor is not on this floor, and there is a non-conformance raised against it since the seventeenth of July with a paragraph number on a supplier's system, and the supplier's own acceptance of that non-conformance is about eleven weeks off and will be granted or refused in the autumn, and the annual compliance report comes in the autumn, in front of four hundred and twenty women in this borough whose instruments come out of this room every weekday.**

Nobody has signed it. It is the fifth of August and it has been eighty-eight days.

There was a bench inside the room with a stool, and a drum of strips in a drawer, and the strips are the machine's own record — the machine prints its chamber temperature on a strip every Sunday and the strips go in the drum and the drum is the evidence, and the only thing the twenty minutes is, is a person reading the drum and writing their name under the week.

He pulled the drum out about four inches and looked at the top strip. The temperature was in range. He pushed it back in.

And then Jonas Mercer stood in a decontamination room on level zero of a hospital at ten past nine in the morning in the fifth month of the year in which he had refused to be the person everybody says yes to, and he had about ninety seconds in which he could have done the twenty minutes, and there was a pen on a string on the tray, and he did not do it, and afterwards he said out loud, to a bench, in an empty room, which is the only way a thing like that can be said properly.

"I am not signing that, and I want to say why in my own words while there is nobody in this room, because I am going to be asked this week by somebody who is allowed to ask me and I am not going to have the reason be a shrug.

"**Filling it in is a service.** That is the whole of it. Not a duty — a *service*. Once there is a name against twenty minutes a week, twenty minutes a week has got a person on it, and that person is a person somebody can ask, and a rota with one name on it is exactly what four people in this building have been refusing since the first week of March, and a doctor refused it out loud in a room in June and gave the reason in four sentences and the reason was the finding, and a porter refused a Thursday in March and asked for it to be left with no reason on it, and both of those refusals are the failure and neither of them is cowardice.

"And I have spent five months saying, out loud, in rooms, on plates, in minutes, that this practice is not a service and cannot be made into one. **I am not going to be the man who signs the first line of one.** I would rather there be a paragraph number about it in the autumn than a name on it in August, and I know which of those two a woman of thirty-five would pick and I know she is upstairs and I am not going to use her reason as mine.

"Twenty minutes a week, fifty-two times, is about seventeen hours a year. That is not a service. That is a man with a job who happens to be in a building on a Sunday. **And the twenty minutes is not the thing that is wrong. The thing that is wrong is that the only way anybody in this city can find out whether a service is happening is to find a person and ask the person, and the person cannot say no to being asked, and if she says no then the service stops, and if she says yes then the service is a rota.** And there is no third thing, and I have looked, and a registrar looked, and a records supervisor looked, and a doctor looked, and there is no third thing in four hundred and sixty pages or in this building or in this borough.

"So no. And I am not doing the twenty minutes either, and that is the harder half and I want it written down somewhere that I did not do that either, because doing them once is the first twenty minutes of the next fifty-two, and I have been the first twenty minutes of about four things this year and every one of them ended up with my name in it in a record within about four weeks, which is a sentence I heard on a half landing in July from a man of twenty-nine who has been on nights since April and who told me not to be kind to him in a stairwell."

He put the drum back in. He did not move the pen on the string. He went and had the mortuary van key cut, which took four minutes, and went up two floors, and did not go on to level two.

**Tuesday the twelfth of August 2014, about eleven in the morning, a corridor, second floor, the civic offices, outside a door with a plate on it that says FIELD SERVICES, GANTRY WARD.**

The level-three acceptance for a report filed on the twenty-ninth of May had gone out on Monday morning on a departmental van to a building in Gantry Ward, and it was going to be signed at level three, and the signature was going to be made by whoever opened the tray, because that is what a level-three acceptance is: a tray, and a person, and a date.

"It should have been back by now," said the officer on the desk, who had been in post two years and did not know and did not want to. "The rule is eleven weeks and this one has taken a fortnight of that on a van, because field services moved offices in July and about half of what should be on a shelf is in a box in a corridor. It comes back signed, or it comes back with a query on it, and it comes back signed anyway after another fortnight. **I have never once known one to come back unsigned and I have never once known anybody to ask about the name in it afterwards.**"

"Who signs it."

"The name in it is the person who signs it. There is no other box." He turned a form round, which was the wrong form, which is what happens, and which Jonas let go of immediately. "**Look — I am not telling you that. You are not a party, and I have thirty of these on the shelf, and eleven of them are Achebe's, and one of them has been there since May, and I have not read it and I am not going to.** And I am not going to tell you who is on the level-three rota this week, because it is not the person who wrote it and it is not the person who is going to do anything about it, because nobody is going to do anything about it, and that is the entire point of level three." He put the form back. "**You cannot move a signature. You can only be in the room when one is asked for, and you are not in that room, and I would not put your name in it if you asked me to, and you have not asked me to, and that is the only reason we are still talking.**"

Jonas Mercer thanked him, which cost him nothing, and walked back down the corridor with one fact in it that he had not had a week earlier, which was that the acceptance was going to be signed by a person with no connection whatever to the eleven pages and nine hundred words and the four tonnes of stone, and that the person was going to sign a piece of paper that said *not released*, and that the person was not going to know what any of the words meant, and that nobody had thought it was worth explaining.

He had a week of August in front of him and a woman on a landing in it and two dates on it, and on the Tuesday morning of the twelfth of August 2014 he wrote four lines on the back of a delivery note, standing at the bench in the shop with the door open on the street.

> *Fifty of the fifty-eight are gone. Fifty days from today to Wednesday the first of October 2014, which is a Wednesday, and ninety days from the third of July, and the Aldbrook Trust has answered a machine on the fourth of July and a person on the seventh, and the person was sixty-three, and she is going to do her job, and the trust has not noticed that it has been notified, and there is nothing in this city that can make a person or a company notice that.*
> *On Monday at six o'clock a residential floor of eleven patients in Kestrel Hill became a stair, a door and a lift, because nobody has ever recorded what a last verified essential use is, and the person who opens the door at a quarter past seven has been doing it for nine years out of a wedge he put there himself, and he has a key that no longer turns the plate, and he asked me for nothing, and I have told nobody and I am not going to.*
> *I have not filled in the log and I have not done the twenty minutes and I have said why out loud in an empty room and I am not going to use anybody's reason as mine.*
> *The tray went to Gantry Ward on Monday on a departmental van and it will be signed by whoever is on the level-three rota that week and I cannot move a signature and I have not been in the room. That is the whole of what a transfer steward is on the fourth of August: a man standing in a corridor outside a door he may not go through, with one fact in his hand that he is not allowed to do anything with, and fifty days.*
