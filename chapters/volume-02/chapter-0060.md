# Chapter 0060 — The Number in a Chair

The Old Ward public notice board stands under a glass canopy between the ferry office and the Assembly's public doors, and in front of it, on four feet of painted iron, there are nine plastic chairs.

They have been there since before Jonas Mercer had a badge. They are municipal property, they are grey, and somebody has painted a number on the back of each one in white, from one to nine, in a hand that has gone chalky. Four is worn through in the middle of the digit where forty years of people have been putting their hands on it.

He had asked the caretaker about it in his second week in the job, for no reason except that it was there.

“The numbers are so people can find the same chair,” the caretaker had said. “Folks come in here every day of their lives, Mrs Sowle’s dog, the fella who reads the papers, the lot. You number a chair, they know where they’re sat. You don’t number a chair, they’re moving about all morning like bees and they get the cold.”

*You number a chair,* Jonas Mercer had thought at the time, and had not gone any further with it, and it had sat in him for five weeks.

He was in the porch at about twenty to nine on the morning of Thursday the first of May with a sheet of A4 in a plastic sleeve, because it had started to rain the way it does in the first week of May, sideways and without conviction.

The sheet was the second one. The first sheet, printed at half past ten on Wednesday the thirtieth of April by a records clerk called Doina Serkis who had never printed one before, had been read out loud in that porch at six o'clock on the Wednesday evening by Nina Calder, and had been on the board ever since under a sheet of acrylic that had fogged.

This one was the second reading of it. Four parties had sent it in a second time, three of them because Nina Calder had asked them to, and one of them — and this was the part that had made Jonas Mercer come down at twenty to nine in the morning — because the clerk had got a line wrong and had not been able to leave it.

---

The ninth line read:

> **NODE — A SCHOOL GATE AT TARN STREET**
> **HOLDER — A KEY**
> **TIER — NONE**
> **DATE OF DEFERRAL — —**
> **WHAT IT SERVES NOW — THE SCHOOL**
> **WHAT IT SERVED BEFORE — THE SCHOOL**
> **WHAT WOULD BE NEEDED TO RESTORE — A HOLDER**
> **WHO ANSWERS FOR IT — A KEY**

He stood and read it twice with the rain going past him and then went and sat down on chair four, which was the worn one, and put the plastic sleeve on his knee, and then got out the pad with his left hand and wrote two lines.

> *Doina Serkis put a key in a holder field because there was nowhere else to put it and the field would not take blank.*
> *The first line anybody has ever had to explain in public.*

He had been thinking about it since about eleven o'clock the previous night, lying in the room above the shop, and the reason he had been thinking about it was not the key. It was the phrase at the bottom, **WHAT WOULD BE NEEDED TO RESTORE — A HOLDER**, on a line written by a person who had never heard the word *holder* used in this sense in her life and had copied the field names off a form and filled them in the only way the question made sense to her.

Because she was right. That is the thing he could not get past. A school gate in Tarn Street, held on a key, with no tier, no deferral date and no recorded use, and the answer to *what would be needed to restore* is a holder, and Jonas Mercer had been in that building on the eighteenth of April at twenty past two in the morning in a car park watching a fifty-one-year-old caretaker open a school nobody asked him to open on his own key, and it had shut again at four o'clock on a teacher's word, and Roz Idris had refused in the yard, and there is no line anywhere in this city for what happens to a man who does that.

He had wanted to write *A HOLDER* in the field and he had wanted to write *PERSONNEL AND A POLICY* in the field and he had wanted to write *SOMEBODY WITH STANDING* in the field, and every one of those three was a thing he was inventing at twenty to nine in the morning on a plastic chair to make a form say something true and uncomfortable, and Doina Serkis, who is a records clerk of about fifty-five and has never in her life claimed to be able to do anything except put things in the right box, had put *A KEY* in it, which is a true sentence, and had then signed it, which is the part that would end up in public.

---

A man came and sat down on chair two at about five past nine, and read the board standing up first, and then sat down.

He was about seventy. He had a plastic bag with a shopping list in it on his knee.

“Six lowland ones,” he said. “That’s the one I’m after.”

“All right.”

“I’m not asking for anything. I’d like to say that first, because I’ve watched you get asked questions this week.” He turned the bag over. “My sister’s on the sixth one. She’s seventy-nine. There’s not a word on here about the sixth one.”

“There isn’t,” said Jonas Mercer. “There is a line that says *a lowland water point* and that is all the line says, and there is a registrar’s ruling on it that says the node may not be described further, and it is on the board under the sheet, and if you turn the sheet up you can read the whole of it, and I am not going to read it out to you because I would be putting it in the room.”

The man looked at him for a second.

“You are the one off the register,” he said.

“Yes.”

“All right.” He did not go. He sat on chair two with the bag on his knee and looked at the nine lines for a long time, and then he said, without any particular weight: “Then what is the form for, if it does not do anything?”

And Jonas Mercer opened his mouth to answer it and found that he had nothing, and sat on chair four in the rain under a glass canopy with a plastic sleeve on his knee and did not answer for about a minute and a half.

He could not answer it because the honest answer was *nothing yet, and I do not know whether anything is going to be built out of it, and I am the man who printed it and I cannot tell you when.* And that answer is a thing a man says in a kitchen at half past one in the morning to a woman of sixty-three and it is not an answer a man in a public porch can say to a man of seventy whose sister is on the sixth one.

The caretaker came out at about ten past nine with his tea and looked at the two of them and went back in.

---

He got the second thing at about eleven, in the shop, standing up, because Bea Osunde had a plate on the bench and there was a rule about Mondays in that shop and he had learned it.

“Two things,” said Bea Osunde, without looking up. “One, your form is not evidence of anything. Not a scrap. Not one line on it. I have read it four times this morning with a legal pad, which is Nina Calder’s joke and not mine, and every one of your ten fields is a question somebody has answered because somebody asked them nicely. That is not a return. That is a survey with a crest on it.”

“It is not adopted.”

“It is not adopted, it is not minuted, it is not in a schedule, it is not enforceable, and — ” she put the plate down flat, which is what she does with everything, “— and nobody has to give it back. That is the bit, and you have not said it out loud yet and you have been carrying that sheet down to a public porch for two days.”

“Nobody has to give it back.”

“Right. The parties holding maintenance are not required to return anything. The word *required* has been in this city for about nineteen years and it is on about four forms and none of them is yours, because the one that would require it is the instrument that would make it worth requiring, and that instrument is the thing that does not exist.” She picked the plate up again. “So you have got a sheet of paper that nine parties were asked to fill in and that nobody can be made to fill in, and it is on a public board, and on Monday the nineteenth of May a committee with a gallery is going to be asked whether the city knows what has happened to its services. And what the city knows is what nine people were asked. And when the question comes — and it will come, in about two weeks, in public, by a man with a folder — somebody is going to say *the city does not know* and they are going to be right, and they are going to be right about a form that a records supervisor printed at half past ten in the morning because the man at the basement printer told her something in a corridor.”

“He did not tell her anything. He answered a question she asked him.”

“He answered it truthfully and he did not know why he was being asked and that is the only reason it worked,” said Bea Osunde. “You are going to have to go and ask nine people. Not the city. Nine people, one at a time, on the ground, and you are going to be turned down about six times, and I am not going to hold the bag this time because I held it on the Wednesday and I am not a bag.”

---

The second thing was about the flood forum, and she said it in the middle of a plate, without stopping, which is how she says the things she actually means.

“G. Raynor-Hale has been round again. He has asked Kofi Sarr a second time and Kofi has said no again, and the man has written *declined* in a book and been very gracious about it, and he asked me on Thursday morning whether the shop would consider a speaker, and I said no, and he wrote *declined*, and I want you to understand that the book is the second one. Nora Bell has now been asked three times for a seat, an annual allowance and a badge, and the third asking was on Monday, and she said no on the second asking in a corridor and on the third asking she did not say anything at all and walked off down the middle of the crossing, which people who know her will tell you is worse.”

“He is doing it on purpose.”

“He is doing it on purpose and he is very good at it and he is not a villain, and I want that written down somewhere that is not this shop, because in about six weeks he is going to have a membership roll with a woman’s name on it and a number against it, and Nora Bell will not have refused it, she will have *not answered* it, and that is a different thing and it is the thing he is actually counting.” She looked up. “And a roll is a list. And you know what a list is, and I have told you what a list is, and I am not going to say it again in a shop.”

---

He got the school gate at about four o'clock in the afternoon, which is the hour the caretaker finishes.

Ibrahim Sesay is fifty-one and he has been the caretaker and the portal at Tarn Street Primary for nine years, and he answered the shop telephone himself, which Jonas Mercer had not expected, and he said, before Jonas Mercer had got to the second sentence:

“I have had it about the form.”

“You have had it about the form.”

“A woman from the records office rang me at about half past nine this morning and asked me whether it was correct that I held a school gate.”

“What did you say.”

“I said I have got a key.” He said it the way he says everything, which is exactly once. “And she said that is not what the form wants, and I said what does the form want, and she said a name. And I said I have been asked for my name four times this year by four different people and three of them wanted it for a list and one of them wanted it because they were building something. And then she was quiet for about four seconds and she said she was sorry and she asked me to hold on.”

He held on. There was a rustle of paper.

“Then she read something out to me,” said Ibrahim Sesay, “off another sheet, from a different one, and it said, in the box, *a key*.”

“Yes.”

“So she has put my key in a box with a crest on it, and she is very sorry, and she wants to know if I am happy for it to go on a board in the Old Ward.” A pause. “I asked her one question. I asked her whether anybody was ever going to read it.”

“What did she say.”

“She said it would be read out loud in a porch by a man with a bag on his shoulder and she was not allowed to tell me who.”

Jonas Mercer stood in the middle of the shop floor with a telephone in his left hand and did not say anything for about four seconds, and then said:

“That is exactly right. I am the man with the bag on my shoulder. That was at half past nine this morning, fifty minutes after I read it off that board, and I would have said so in the porch as well.”

“You would have?”

“I would have read the line and I would have said *this is wrong, this is a key and not a holder, and here is the man it belongs to and here is why he is not on it.* And I would have been doing that because I was in a porch and I wanted the line to be right, and I would have been doing it to about eleven people, and every one of those eleven would have gone away knowing there is a man called Ibrahim Sesay who is findable at a school gate in this city, and he would have been findable at it for a year, and he would not have put himself there.”

“No,” said Ibrahim Sesay. “I would not have.”

“All right. So the line is wrong.”

“Yes. And the line is mine.”

Jonas Mercer did not understand for a second.

“It is not a holder, and it is not a name, and I am not a holder of anything except a set of keys and a boiler,” said Ibrahim Sesay. “And the woman from the records office has put *a key* in a box on a form and signed it, which means that as of Monday morning in a building in this city it says in a box, with a crest on it, that a school gate is held by a key. And that is not true, and it is not nothing, and it is not me, and I am telling you that I am not going to go round this borough saying the city has said my name is a gate. The key is the honest answer and the key is wrong and the line is mine because I am the one who has to be wrong about it for the rest of the year.”

---

He wrote it down in the yard afterwards, on the crate, with the rain coming over the parapet in the way it does, standing up, in his left hand, and it took him a while.

> *Second return, 1 May, line eight. HOLDER: A KEY.*
> *It is not a holder. A key is not a person, a role, an office, an institution or a bounded place.*
> *A holder field on a form is not a holder. It is a box.*
> *And a form that is not adopted, not minuted and not enforceable, on which no party is required to answer, is not a register. It is a piece of paper with a crest on it and a hole in the bottom, and a records supervisor printed it in a hurry because the man at the basement printer told her the truth in a corridor.*

And then, underneath, because it was the true shape of the thing and he had learned in April from a woman of sixty-three that you write the whole sentence or you have not written anything:

> *The nine lines on the first sheet are eight answers and one absence. The nine lines on the second sheet are eight answers and one mistake. Neither of them is evidence. Both of them are the only instrument this city has.*
> *A form is not a register. Do not let anybody in June call it one, including me.*

---

Oren Pell took the shop telephone on Friday the second of May at about ten to one and gave him ninety seconds, which was generous, and said four things in it.

“One. I was in the pre-sitting on Wednesday morning from about eleven until about half eleven and not one minute after that, and there is no minute of it and there is nobody in this city who can tell you what was said in it afterwards, including the man who said it, because the man who said it does not repeat himself, which is his way of being honest and my way of being spared.

“Two. What I can tell you is what is on the tabling list, because the tabling list is published and it went on the website at four o'clock on Wednesday afternoon like everything else. Schedule four, the covering submission, a determination of the seventeenth of April which I suspended, and the register of attachment, which is on there under *noted*, and which somebody in that building is going to read out on Monday as an item of business and which I did not create to be an item of business.”

“And what is not on the tabling list,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Everything that matters,” said Oren Pell. “There is no item on that list about a nurse who is on a rota with a hole in it. There is no item about a lowland. There is no item about a school gate or a mortuary gate. There is no item about the fact that eleven people were in a Methodist hall on Carrow Lane four days into a month they were not paying for and nobody in that room has said so. It is a tabling list for a schedule and a schedule has two things in it and both of them are about money.”

“That is what Rook said in the stairwell,” said Jonas Mercer. “On Wednesday night.”

“Then he said it before Wednesday,” said Oren Pell, “and I have no idea how, and I have thought about that for a day and a half and I have decided that I do not want to know, because if I find out that he is that good then this city has been arguing with the wrong man for five weeks and if I do not find out then I can go on being a registrar.”

He put the telephone down. Then he picked it up again.

“Write this down. Do not put it in the shop. *The nineteen-day median first attendance is a departmental figure and it is on a laminated card in a dispatch office and it is not evidence of anything in a hearing, and if a member of that committee puts it in their mouth as a city figure the department will be asked to produce it, and it will produce it, and it will be a laminated card, and everybody in the gallery will understand exactly what it is.*”

---

Gunnar Halvorsen telephoned the shop at about seven on the Friday evening of the second of May and Jonas Mercer took it standing up in the yard because it was still coming over the parapet.

“I have written to him again,” said Halvorsen.

“The porter.”

“The porter. I wrote to him on the thirtieth, I did not tell you I was going to, and I have had a reply, and it came back this afternoon on a piece of hospital writing paper with a date on it and a signature, and I want you to understand that the reason I am telephoning you about a piece of writing paper is that it is now the only document I have got on this, and I am a service manager with nineteen years in post and a folder with nothing in it.”

“What does it say.”

“It says — and I am going to read it to you because I have read it about nine times and I want another person to have read it — *I still have not got the people who do not come in a van.* And then it is signed, and I am not going to say the signature to you on a telephone, because if I say his name to you then you have got a name, and I have watched you be careful with names for eleven days and I am not going to be the man who hands you one on a Friday evening because I have had a bad two weeks.”

He stopped, and Jonas Mercer could hear a fluorescent tube and a telephone on a desk with a piece of tape on it.

“He has put it in writing,” said Gunnar Halvorsen, “which nobody asked him to do, and which means that as of the thirtieth of April there is a piece of paper in a hospital in this city that says the word *still*, in a signature, and *still* is a word that means he is not going to get them either. It is the same answer. It is the same answer with a date on it, and a date is the only thing this whole business understands.”

“And the gate.”

“The gate is open. It has been open every night since the twenty-sixth of April at one minute past midnight and it is open because a night orderly of twenty-nine, who is not on the rota and does not know what the field is, wrote four words in biro on the back of a laundry delivery note in about nine seconds, and I have not given you his name and you did not ask me for it and I have noticed that, and that is the entire reason six forty-four is still a gate, and if you took that piece of paper off the plate the gate would stop being a gate on Saturday night and there is no money in this city for a mortuary gate. That is the good news and it is the whole good news and I want you to have it before I tell you the rest.

“The rest is that the other three have been on nothing since the twenty-sixth of April at one minute past midnight, and that is six days, and a staff stair, and a car park barrier, and a service riser, and nobody outside my four walls knows that, and inside them nobody can tell me what a service riser is for because the man who could tell me is on a lorry in Gantry Ward and the department that owns the water in it telephoned me on Wednesday and asked me for a location of record for a thing inside my own building.”

Jonas Mercer wrote two things down on the crate with his left hand while he listened, and one of them was a date.

“I told four people on the twenty-first of April that I had until midnight on Saturday,” said Halvorsen. “I did not decide by midnight on Saturday. I have had a fortnight of somebody in a corridor being reasonable with me, and every day of that fortnight has cost this site a day of not knowing what happens on Saturday night, and I am not going to tell the site board that tomorrow, because the site board sits tomorrow and I am not going to have it minuted, and it is going to be minuted anyway if I go and say it out loud, and I have thought about that for nine days and I am telling you about it instead.”

“When.”

“Saturday the tenth. Ten in the morning, in my office, with the four of them on the table and four thousand two hundred pounds next to them, and I am going to write it down and I am going to sign it and it is going to be in a file in a drawer in this building by eleven o'clock, and it is going to be the first thing I have ever put in a file that I could not have put in a file a month ago, and I have no idea whether it will help anybody.”

“I would like to be in the room.”

“No,” said Gunnar Halvorsen.

“All right.”

“Not because of the twenty-first,” he said. “You have never once tried to get into a room. I want that on your tape or your pad or whatever you are writing on. I am saying no because on the twenty-first of April I asked you a question in this office and you said no to me in about ninety seconds, and I have not stopped thinking about it, and if you are in the room on Saturday morning I will find out what you think about a staff stair at about half past ten and I will not be able to find out what the other three people on my table think about a staff stair, and there are four of them and one of you and it is not going to be one vote, it is going to be four people who have to write something down, and one of those four has to be somebody who can be wrong in public and be wrong in writing and be findable at the thing afterwards.”

“All right,” said Jonas Mercer. “I will not be in the room.”

“Thank you. Now go and do something that is yours, and I mean that, and do not telephone me on Saturday, and do not come to the site, and if it turns out that I have decided wrong you will hear about it on the Monday from a return, because Nina Calder has a form and a woman in records with a bicycle, and that is going to be how this city finds out what I did at ten o'clock on Saturday morning before I have told anybody.”

He put the telephone down and Jonas Mercer stood in the yard in the rain for a while with the receiver warm in his hand and the pad open on the crate.

Then he wrote the last three lines of the day.

> *Sat 10 May, 10:00, his office. £4,200. Four nodes, four people, four documents, and I am not in the room and did not ask twice.*
> *The gate is open on a sentence in biro and it has been open for six days and the three that are not a gate have been on nothing for six days and neither fact is in any document in this city except a form that is not adopted.*
> *He asked a technician on the twenty-first and got no in ninety seconds, and he has spent two weeks about it, and he is going to spend a Saturday morning finding out the same thing from three people who have to sign their names.*

The claims box at the Old Ward board closes on Friday the eighteenth of July. The ninth line on the second return says, in a box, that the answer to *who answers for it* is *a key*, and a woman in records signed it because there was nowhere else to put it, and a man of fifty-one on a telephone on a Friday afternoon in a shop said the key is wrong and the line is his.
