# Chapter 0072 — Block C Has No Key

The meter room on the ground floor of Block C on Carrow Lane is six feet by nine, it has a concrete floor, a window with no glass in it, a light fitting with a bulb in it that somebody has wired to a switch outside the door, and in the corner a galvanised cupboard about four feet high with a hasp and no lock on it, and inside the cupboard a stopcock, and above the stopcock on a brass tag, in somebody's handwriting, the word **STOPCOCK**, and a date that is 1998.

Jonas Mercer got in on Wednesday the twenty-first of May at about ten past ten in the morning with a torch, a screwdriver, a flat bar and about four minutes' worth of hope, and he was out again at twenty past ten with the door propped on a bucket because there is no lock and the door has never had one, and what he learned in those ten minutes was the following, and he wrote all of it in the shop book that night in the plainest words he could find.

**1.** The stopcock is reachable. It has been reachable the whole time. It is under a galvanised cover plate with four slotted screws, it has a plastic lifting handle, and the handle turns. He turned it a quarter turn against the direction of the arrow and it moved freely and nothing happened, and he did not turn it further, because a stopcock that has been closed for a fortnight against an order on the freeholder's letter of the twenty-ninth of April is not his to open, whatever else is true about him.

**2.** There is no key. There has never been a key to that cupboard in the twenty-two years anybody in this building can remember, and the reason there is no key is that the cupboard has never been locked, and the reason it has never been locked is that when it was last closed somebody could not find the key, and that has been true long enough that a brass tag with a date on it is the most official-looking object in the room.

**3.** The plate is on the wall of the meter room at eye height, and it is the association's own plate, bought with four meters in 2004, and it has four lines on it now.

He read the four lines standing up, the way you read a thing you have read three times, and he understood where they had come from, because he had watched the thing happen three times since March: the drawing layer in the Old Ward basement will put a line onto any plate it can find a frame for, it does not need a party and it does not need a reason and nobody in that basement has been asked to keep a list, and a housing association that bought a tier in March is a service district as far as that machine is concerned, and a plate inside a service district is a plate.

> *A TIER MAY BE CHALLENGED BY A SERVICE DISTRICT OR BY A NODE IN THE SAME TIER. IT MAY NOT BE CHALLENGED BY A PERSON.*
>
> *A NODE WITH A HOLDER THAT HAS NOT BEEN RENEWED FOR ITS TERM SERVES THE TERM IT WAS LAST READ INTO. AND NOT THE TERM SOMEBODY EXPECTED.*
>
> *A RETURN MADE BY A PARTY HOLDING MAINTENANCE IS EVIDENCE OF A STATE. IT IS NOT EVIDENCE OF A PERSON, A HOUSEHOLD, OR A NUMBER.*
>
> *AND THEN, underneath the other three, in the same flat register with a full stop after each word, printed some time on Thursday or Friday, and a different hand of type from the first three:*
>
> **WHERE A SERVICE IS NARROWED FOR A REASON OF SEASON, THE NARROWING IS PERFORMED AND ENTERED, AND THE ENTRY IS MADE IN THE FORM AND IN THE LOG, AND THE TWO ARE NOT REQUIRED TO AGREE.**

He read it three times and then went out and stood in the yard at the side of the building with his back against a wall that was hot enough to hurt through a shirt, and worked out what it meant, and then worked out that he had already worked out what it meant and that he did not want to have worked it out.

The line is not about Block C. The line is a condition, and it is printed the way the other three are printed, and it is true everywhere or nowhere. It says that when a service is narrowed because of the season — because it is summer, because it is hot, because a borough has decided what a given service can be asked for in August — **the narrowing is performed and entered**, and it goes in Nina Calder's form, and it goes in the service's own log book, and the two are not required to agree.

And this building had had no water in it for fifteen days.

He got his book out and read the association's own log, which sits on a shelf above the meter, and which is a hard-backed book with a spine and about eleven years of entries in three hands, and the last entry is the seventh of May and reads, in pencil: *Stopcock found closed. No action. Unable to locate stopcock key. Housing Association informed 29/4.*

And the return, which is pinned to the same shelf, is a copy of Nina Calder's form, and the block's line on it is answered, correctly, in the way that this batch has now established is the only way anything gets answered at all: the state is given, and it is legible.

**CARROW LANE HA / BLOCK C / WATER. STATE: NO SUPPLY. SINCE: 06/05. WHO ANSWERS: M. OYELARAN, CHIEF OFFICER.**

A state. A date. A name. It is the correct answer to the question that was asked, and the question that was asked was *what is the state of this node*, and nobody asked a question about the twenty-two households in a hostel on the other side of the road or the eight buckets in the yard or the sixteen years.

And a notice that cannot be wrong about an empty building, he thought, standing in a yard at a quarter past eleven in the morning with thirty degrees coming off the brickwork, is a notice that somebody will quote about a full one in September. The line is not wrong. The line is going to be exactly true. That is the difficulty with conditions.

---

Marguerite Oyelaran did not want any of it looked at, and she said so in a corridor at half past eleven with the door of the meter room open behind her and a person she had not authorised standing in the room.

“I have known about that stopcock since the twenty-ninth of April,” she said. “I have known about it since a letter came, and I have known about the cupboard since 1998 because the tag in it says 1998 and my predecessor put a note about the tag in the file in 2001 and the file has been in a carrier bag since 2004.

“And I am not going to have it looked at, and I want you to understand that this is not guilt. It is the other thing. The day I look at it is the day it is a record, and it is already a record — it is on your plate and it is in my log and it is on Nina Calder’s form with my name on the line. And if somebody comes into my meter room with a camera, then I have got a photograph of a meter room with a council crest on the wall, and a crest on a wall is the beginning of a circular, and I have got two of those in eleven years and one of them cost a friend of mine a job and the other one cost me four hundred and twenty pounds and a year of somebody’s wages.

“So no. You can come and look at it because looking at it is what you do. You cannot write it down.”

“I have written it down,” said Jonas Mercer, “in the shop book. Not for you. For me.”

“Then do not come back and put it on my desk.”

The person standing in the meter room was the freeholder's agent, and she had come about the meters, and her name was **Nadine Prentice** and she was twenty-nine and had a clipboard and had been on the job fourteen months, and she said the thing about the meters in the first ninety seconds and then said a second thing that she did not have to say and that Jonas Mercer thought about for two days afterwards.

“The agent's position is that the meters come out,” said Nadine Prentice. “That is a standing instruction and it was issued on the fourteenth of April and it is not about you. There are four meters in that cupboard and one of them is on a boundary between a freehold and a leasehold that has been in an indeterminate state since 2009, and the agent's position is that it cannot be resolved while the supply is off, and the agent's position is that it should not be on when the supply comes back on.

“And the other thing,” she said, and she looked at the plate on the wall rather than at either of them, “is that I was told to come and look at the stopcock. I was not told why. So I have looked at it, and it is under a plate, and there is a tag, and there is no key, and I am going to write *stopcock accessible, no key, tag reads 1998* in my own file, because I am not going to write *no key* in a file that goes to a landlord about a block where a stopcock I cannot reach has been closed for fifteen days and my instruction was to look at the stopcock.

“I am not going to be thanked for that and I am not going to be blamed for it either, because I will not have it both ways and the second one is more likely.”

---

They went round the back at about half past one on the Wednesday.

The standpipe at the end of the lane is a fifty-millimetre galvanised riser with a tap at knee height and a bucket on a string, and it is fed from a main that belongs to nobody who has ever come and looked at it, and on the twenty-first of May at half past one in the afternoon it was at the end of its flow, running about a mouthful every forty seconds, and there were nine households' worth of empty buckets and two trugs lined up against the wall, and the trugs belonged to a man who was not on the rota and had not been asked and had brought them anyway.

And in the yard of Block C, in the shade of the block on the west side where the wall does not get the sun until four o'clock, there were eleven plastic containers, four of them buckets, and a stack of the blue crates that the fish market leaves on the lane, and **eight households had been carrying the water for thirty rooms in them since 1998.**

Not since the sixth of May. Since 1998.

The internal riser in Block C died in the winter of 1998 and the fix was quoted at a figure that was in a file in a carrier bag, and nobody raised it again, and the water for thirty rooms has come ninety yards from a standpipe on the end of a lane ever since, in buckets, by the households themselves, in shifts that were never written down.

Nobody had ever put that in a letter.

Jonas Mercer stood in the shade and let a woman of seventy-two called **Ivy Marsden** tell him about it, because he had asked her and she had said yes before he had finished the sentence, and she told it in about ninety seconds, flat, in order, with dates, and then stopped and would not say anything else about it because there was nothing else about it.

“Nineteen ninety-eight, February, the Tuesday, a Wednesday to me, the pipe went on the Friday,” she said. “Eleven of us then, before the young ones. Mrs Adeyinka was in number nine and she is not here now. We did it in the morning before work, and then in the evening, and then when there were people working days we did it at half five in the morning and at eight at night. There were four of us who could not carry a full bucket and the young men did it and there were three of them. It is not a rota. It is a rota we have not written down.

“And from the sixth of May this year it has been every bucket, for the whole thirty, and there are eight of us left in this block and I have done the ones on the top landing twice on Tuesday because the stair is a stair.”

“I am sorry about that.”

“Do not be sorry about it, it is water,” said Ivy Marsden. “Do not be sorry about it and then go and do something about it and forget. I want one thing. I have wanted it for sixteen years and I have asked for it twice and I have been told both times that the answer is the same, which is that it is not the association’s building.

“I want it in a letter. That is all. One letter that says that in 1998 the riser went and that we have carried it ourselves ever since and that the buckets are ours and the shifts are ours and nobody paid us and nobody has ever asked us. I do not want a tap. I have not asked for a tap in sixteen years. **I want a piece of paper that says the buckets are ours, because in about two years I am not going to be here and nobody is going to believe me and the letter will still be in a file.**”

And Marguerite Oyelaran — who had walked round the back with them and had not gone more than twenty feet into the yard because she was in shoes — said:

“Then I will write it, and I will put it in the file with the date on it, and I will not put a photograph of it anywhere, and I will not put a number on it.

“And I want to say why I am going to do it, because you have both asked me two questions in a fortnight and both of them were about whether I would look at something.

“I am not writing it because you asked. **I am writing it because I have been taking two pounds sixty a week off eight households for a service those households have not had since the sixth of May, and the reason I have not stopped it is that I cannot stop it.**”

They both looked at her.

“It is a standing order. I could stop the standing order this afternoon with a telephone call and I am not going to, and here is the whole reason and it is not a moral reason. If I refund eight households in Block C then I have admitted in writing that a service in one of my four blocks has stopped. And a service that stops is a node that goes into failure. And a node in failure is a thing this city has a document about, and I have read that document, and I read it in your shop on the ninth of May, and a node in failure is a thing that can be put on a schedule, and if one of mine can be put on a schedule by somebody else then so can the other three, and in March I would have been the only person in this city who could have said that this could not happen to me.

“So the money keeps coming in. It is two pounds sixty a week off eight households for water that is not in this building, and the reason is that I would rather be hated for it than find out in August what I have done by stopping it. You are welcome to put that in your book. I would rather somebody wrote it down than had it in my head.”

Ivy Marsden said, “That is the first straight answer I have had about that money,” and Marguerite Oyelaran said, “Yes, and you are the first person I have given one to, because you asked, and because I am not going to be thanked for it, and because it took me eleven years to be able to say it out loud in a yard.”

---

**And the thing that nobody in that yard said out loud, and that Jonas Mercer only understood on the Friday, was this.**

The eight households were carrying the water. The eight households were the only people in Block C who had a reason to want the schedule to keep working.

Not the twenty-two in the hostel. Not the hundred and twenty-two who signed in January. Not the twelve who want out. **The eight.** Because the schedule — Tier 2, four thousand two hundred pounds, four nodes, four blocks, bought on the eleventh of March — is what keeps the node funded, and if the node came off the schedule it would go into failure, and it would be deferred, and it would serve its last verified essential use, and there is none recorded, so it would serve its own maintenance, and the maintenance on a domestic water riser in a 1938 block is a meter, a stopcock, and a cupboard.

So the eight who carry the water in buckets are the only people in that building who would be damaged by the fee stopping.

And they are not on the agreement. They did not get a form. They are not a party and they cannot be one. And a tier may not be challenged by a person, and the people who would want to challenge it are the ones with buckets.

He said it to Marguerite Oyelaran on the Friday morning at half past nine, in the corridor, in about two minutes, and she listened to the whole of it and then said the thing she had been carrying since the twenty-eighth of April.

“No,” she said. “No, and do not come back to me with it.

“The answer to *what happens to the eight if the money stops* is that nothing happens to the eight, because nothing happens to them now. That is not me being brave. That is the arrangement. In 1998 the riser went and nobody fixed it and I have not fixed it and nobody has fixed it and there is no money in this city for a riser in a 1938 block where thirty households have lived for sixteen years without one, and there is not going to be any, and if I put it in a return tonight it becomes a claim, and a claim is a thing somebody has to answer for, and I cannot answer for it, and neither can you.

“You want to put eight households in a document so that the document is honest. And then the document is honest and it is on the shelf with my log and Nina Calder's form and your plate, and it is a fourth thing in a room with a crest on the wall, and in September somebody will take it out of that room and read it out in a Committee.

“**And I do not know which of the two I am more afraid of.** That is the honest answer and I have not given it to anybody since the twenty-eighth of April. I am afraid of the four thousand three hundred and sixty-one pounds and the sixty's, because in November there is no money and thirty rooms have no water and four blocks have no money, and I have said that out loud to nobody.

“And I am more afraid of the other one. I am afraid that in about two years this association is going to be a thing with a number in it — a hundred and six rooms, or ninety-eight, or however many are left — and that a number is the only thing anybody can be measured on, and that I did the measuring. I have signed a hundred and twenty-two of them, I have answered a circular in ten minutes, I have signed a man’s name onto a page because his block was on a photocopy, and I have done all of that correctly, every time, and I would do it again, and I have started to be unable to tell the difference between the day I did it because it was right and the day I did it because somebody was in the room and I could not say no.

“I have signed this thing. I do not want it in a return. I want it in a file with a date on it and no crest on the wall of the room it lives in, and I want to be the one who decides when it comes out, and I have wanted that since Wednesday, and it is the first thing I have wanted for six weeks that was not money.”
