# Chapter 0049 — Not a Service

The stand on the Weir had been dead for about thirty-one hours and the bucket was still on the chain.

Jonas Mercer found that out at about ten past eight on Monday morning, standing on the road at the top of the slipway with his bag, and the first thing he did was the thing he has done every Monday morning since the second of March, which was to look at it and wait for it to ask him something.

It did not ask him anything.

That is what a plate does when the term it is carrying has ended. In five weeks he had got eleven notices off eleven plates and he had learned to read them the way a man learns to read a face across a room. This one had nothing on it. No fault, no condition, no line of type in the Lattice's flat civic register. The green pillar with the bucket and the forty-slot rota board nailed to the fence beside it was standing in the rain doing the specific nothing that a post does when nothing is asking it to do anything, and there is no fault class in any manual in this city for that, because it is not a fault.

It is a supply.

He had said the word out loud to himself, twice, on the road, and then had a bad quarter of an hour about it, because it was not his word. It was nobody's word yet. It was a word he had made up out of the shape of a thing and he did not want to be the kind of man who says a word in a street on a Monday morning and then hears it again in June in a room he does not control.

---

Margo Doré came out of the kitchen of the net loft with a jug and put it on the step and looked at the water in it and did not say anything about it, and the water in it was the colour of weak tea, and everybody who came up the path that morning looked at it and nobody said anything, because it is the water off a standpipe whose intake is silting and that has been in everybody's mouth since the seventh of April.

“Right,” she said. “You have come to look at it.”

“I have come to see whether it can go back on a term.”

“No,” said Margo Doré.

She said it before he had finished the sentence, which she has been doing to him for five weeks and which in March used to make him feel like a man being tested and which now just made him wait.

“You have come up that path a lot in five weeks,” she said, “and every single time you have come up it there has been a term at the end of it, and a holder, and a claim, and a person who could say no to you. And there is nothing at the end of this one now. It expired at six o’clock on Sunday morning. Bo read it out to me and he read the claim out first and told me it was not true, and I took it for one window, and the window shut at six o’clock on Sunday morning, and that is the whole of the civic record of this water point for the last thirty-two years and it is a rectangle of nothing where a term used to be.”

“Margo.”

“I am not finished. You have a lamp in your hand and you have had a lamp in your hand every week since the second of March, and I want to say one thing about that and then I am going to make tea, and I am going to say it once.”

---

Perdita Sow was on the road.

That was the part of the morning Jonas Mercer had not known about, and he found out at about half past nine when she came up the embankment footpath on the slow side with a shopping bag over the crook of her right arm and her left hand in a sling, and she is forty-four and she is out of a corridor at Saint Orra east after four nights, and she had been given eleven days and a follow-up and a leaflet.

She swept the Weir road on Sundays for eleven unpaid years. Nobody had ever given her a map with that road on it. Nobody had ever asked her to be on anything.

On Monday morning, on the Monday, with a bag of her own, she came up the path.

“You have not written anything on that wall,” she said, when she got to the stand.

“There is nothing to write. The term ended.”

“That is what I came to see,” said Perdita Sow. She put the shopping bag down on the wall and worked her right arm out of the sling strap without being asked, and then put it back in, which took her about four seconds and which she did not enjoy. “On the seventeenth of March I watched you put a wall of paper in a kitchen with your own handwriting on it. And on the second of April you sat in a room in the Old Ward and would not read a line out of a plate, and I read the newspapers about that, because a road sweeper reads the newspapers, and everybody in this city read the newspapers about that. And in April a man with a lamp came and measured a slab in Cooper’s Reach to the millimetre so that eleven flats would have to go, and that was in the newspapers too, and I could not tell from the newspaper which of you was the good one.”

“I measured it because it was unsafe.”

“I know why you measured it,” said Perdita Sow. “I am not here about the slab. I am here about the wall.”

She nodded at the plate on the pillar, which had nothing on it.

“Five weeks,” she said. “Every single thing you have done has ended up on a piece of paper that somebody in a building two miles from here is going to read out loud in a room. And there is a woman on this street who has been counting the doors on it every April since 1994 and has not written one of them down, and a boy of seventeen who is on a water rota and does not know what a claim is, and me, and that is the list. Three people, and a ring of pencil round a space with nothing in it, and you have never once put any of us on anything, and I want to say that plainly, because you have spent five weeks being careful and I do not think anybody has thanked you for it and I am not going to, and I am also not going to let you stand on a wet road this morning and think that the carefulness did the work.”

“Which of us swept this road.”

“You,” said Perdita Sow, “but you were not on it, and I was, and on Sunday morning I could not tell you which of the two of us you are.”

---

They cleared the trap anyway.

Not because there was a term for it. There was no term for it and there had not been a term for it since six o’clock on Sunday morning, and clearing a silt trap is not a civic function and cannot be authorised as one, and Jonas Mercer said so out loud on the step before anybody put a bucket in the water, and Margo Doré said, “Then it is a bucket and three people, and you are one of them, and if you are going to keep standing on my step telling me things are not services then you can get in the channel and be useful for an hour.”

So he got in the channel.

It took an hour and ten minutes and it got nine inches out of a trap that had eighteen inches out of it on Friday and a foot and ten of it in, and it is the truth of the matter that the trap wants two people on a Wednesday morning at low water with a shovel and about three hours, and there were three people, and it was a Monday, and one of them had a dislocated finger and eleven weeks of being angry about it ahead of her.

Nobody was paid. Nobody was on anything. The bucket came up four times and the silt went into a builder’s sack behind the cook-house and the sack went down to the skip on the embankment and there is no record of the sack anywhere in the city of Nacre.

And the trap is not clear and it is not going to be clear this month.

---

At about a quarter to twelve Jonas Mercer sat on the wall at the top of the slipway, out of the wind, and tried to do the thing he does, and found that there was nothing to do.

He put his hand flat on the plate on the pillar. He had a reading for it in a fortnight when the trap was silting. He had a reading for it in March when the stand was serving from a silting intake. He had a reading on the eighteenth of March, three days after he wrote *the holder keeps this point true until the silt trap is clear* into a plate and read the claim out loud on a step to a boy of seventeen and did not read it into a public record.

There was nothing on the plate at all. No Margin, no thread, no fault standing in a place where a fault could stand. A term that had ended had taken the fault with it, and the water was coming out of a main, and a main has an owner, and the owner was a company with a letterhead, and the post was a post.

He sat there with his hand on cold iron for about four minutes and understood for the first time in five weeks that he had nothing to be good at, and that this was not a fault in the post, and that the post was not asking him to fix it.

“There is no term on it,” he said, to nobody, and then, to Margo Doré, who was in the doorway with a second jug, “so there is nobody to ask for anything.”

“That is right,” said Margo Doré.

“That is a different thing from there being nobody who wants anything.”

“It is,” she said. “And in about six weeks, when something comes down the road that has a word in it, that is going to be the whole of this city’s position on this street, and I would like you to have said it out loud in this street on a Monday morning in April and not for the first time in a hearing room in June, and I am not going to be thanked for it either.”

---

She went in. Jonas Mercer got up off the wall to go, and she said his name, and he turned round, and she was standing in the doorway with the jug in both hands and had evidently been about to go in and had not.

“There is one more thing and then I am making tea,” she said, “and you are going to write it down in that book of yours in full, exactly as I say it, and if you leave anything out of it I will know, and if you put anything in it that I did not say I will know that too, and I do not care which, because a half thing on a page is how a street becomes a place on a form.”

“All right.”

“In March,” said Margo Doré, “I had a letter. It came through the post and it was from the water authority and it was about my own supply, and it was three paragraphs long, and I read it and I put it in the cook-house stove, and that is all that happened to it, and I did not tell you and I am not going to tell you what was in it.”

“Margo —”

“You may write that a woman of sixty-three on the Weir burned a letter from the water authority in March about the water on this stand, and that she did not tell a man with a lamp what was in it, and that she has declined to explain.” She looked at him for about two seconds longer than was comfortable. “Write it in those words and not in the ones you would use.”

He wrote it in those words. It took him a minute and a half because the right hand would not hold the pen properly and he had to use his left, and he read it back to her at the top of the slipway in the rain, and she said it was correct, and she went in, and the door shut.

And Jonas Mercer stood on the road at the top of the slipway on Monday the twenty-first of April with a book in his left hand and a bag over his shoulder and the stand doing nothing behind him, and worked out that he now had a refusal in a book that was better than any of the other five, and that he had not earned it, and that he was going to have to earn it, and that there was no mechanism in the city of Nacre that he knew of which anybody earned a refusal.

Then he went down to his car, and the term at the west clinic ran out at six o'clock the following morning.
