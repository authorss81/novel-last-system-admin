# Chapter 0088 — A Registrar's Answer

**Tuesday the eighth of July 2014, twenty to eleven in the morning, the records office.**

"You cannot ask me that," said Nina Calder, "and I am not going to tell you that you cannot ask me, because you can ask me anything and I will answer anything I am required to answer and quite a lot that I am not.

"You have asked me the thing twice in seven minutes and the answer to both is no, and it is the same no either way. **Nobody in this building can make CivicCore repay four months of a tier.** Not the chair, not the Committee, not the department, not a registrar, and not a man with a register. It is not ours. It was never ours. The 2008 regulations put the duty on a registrar to enter and to record and to rule on the shape of a thing, and they did not put a duty on anybody to recover money, and that is not a gap, that is a division of labour, and somebody sat in a room in about 2006 and decided which side of the line a registrar stands on, and they were right.

"And the second one — no, there is no mechanism to make a person take a duty, and I have wanted that written down since the nineteenth of April and it is now the second time this month that a man has come into this office and asked me for it and both times I have said no, and the reason I have not written it down is that it is in a book."

She put her pen down.

"Go and ask Oren Pell on Wednesday, and take nothing, and I am not going to tell you why you should take nothing, because you have worked that out four times already and it is the only thing you are good at."

---

He rang Alden Rook at about twenty past twelve on the Tuesday, which took him nine minutes to decide to do, and Rook answered on the fourth ring.

"Mr Mercer. Two things, because I am about to go into a hearing about a boiler and I have got a train.

"**One: do not ask me.** I am a party to a chain that you have broken in the middle and I am instructed, and I am instructed gladly, to say that CivicCore placed the parent claim under a standing continuity authorisation on the fourth of March and that everything done since has been executed by parties with authority on days when they had authority, and if that is required in a room in September I will say it, and I will say it without being asked, and I have already had my solicitor write it down.

"**And two, which is free, and which is the only thing I am going to say to you on a telephone in July.** You are about to ask a registrar who pays when a chain is broken. Don't. Not because he will not tell you — he will, and he will be right, and it will be a door and not a wall, and I have watched that man be right in public four times this year and I have never once been able to beat him. Don't because **the answer does not change what you do on the first of October, and you are going to want a number on Wednesday, and there is not going to be one, and you will spend the afternoon afterwards being a man who was told no by a registrar, and you have been that man four times this year and you have got very good at it and it has never once helped anybody.**"

"What does help."

"Having the date," said Rook. "You have got the date. Everything else is a way of not looking at it."

---

**Tuesday the eighth of July, half past two in the afternoon, the shop.**

Tomas Renn came in at about half past two and had a rucksack and a folded piece of paper he did not take out, and stood at the end of the bench where he stands.

"Right. Two things and I am not sitting down.

"One. **The eleventh of October.** I am not going to talk to you about the eleventh of October in a shop. I have told three people that this month and two of them were right to be told, and it is not because it is secret. It is because every time the two of us put it in the same room it comes out as a plan, and it is not a plan, it is a Saturday night, and if you and I have a plan for it then two tired porters in a corridor at ten o'clock have got a thing to be disappointed about, and that is how the last one went wrong.

"It is the third Saturday in October and it is half past ten at night and I will be in the room, and I will not have a paper, and you will not have a paper, and that is the whole of what either of us can do about it in July."

"Two. **The first of October.** Because it is twelve weeks away and it is a Wednesday, and you have a form with your name on it going onto a public board on Monday afternoon, and I read it on the board at about a quarter to five, along with about nine other people, and I want to say three things about it and I am going to say them quickly.

"A registrar has told me the answer to the first of October is a clause and that nobody can do anything about it. Fine. **Here is what I want to know and I have not asked you because you would have told me the truth and I am not ready to be told it in a shop.** What happens to a chain of valid assignments when the person at the end of it is right? Not on the first of October — on the day after, and the day after that, and the week after. Because the clause says the placing party may re-assert it without the consent of anybody whose name is in the field. And if she is in the field again on the second of October, because she has to be, because nobody else can be, and a consent can be withdrawn and can be given again — then the clause has not been defeated. **The clause has been complied with. Twice.**"

Jonas Mercer did not answer that, and the reason he did not answer it was that it was correct.

"You have not got an answer," said Tomas Renn.

"No."

"Good," said Tomas Renn, and he put the folded piece of paper in his rucksack, unopened, and went out, and it is the same piece of paper he put on the crate on the tenth of June, and it is a menu for a Sunday in October with four people on it and no rule attached.

---

**Wednesday the ninth of July 2014, ten in the morning, the registrar's office, second floor of the civic offices.**

Oren Pell had the fan on the windowsill and the window open and a file of about two hundred pages on the desk with four tabs in it, and he had a sandwich in a bag that he did not take out, and he looked at Jonas Mercer for about four seconds and said:

"You brought nothing."

"No."

"Good. Sit down. I have got until eleven and I am going to spend about twenty minutes of it on you and the other forty on a determination about a stair, and I am going to tell you the four things you came for, and I am going to tell you which of them I can do, and you are not going to like two of them, and one of the two is going to be the correct answer and you are not going to be able to tell which until about the third one, and I am not going to help you.

"**Question one. Can a chain of valid assignments be broken other than by the consent of the party at the end of it?**

"No. Not other than. There is no other door and there has never been any other door and I have checked all four hundred and sixty pages twice since Friday. An assignment is undone by a person, and only a person, and the person at the end of the chain. Everything before her is unimpeachable. **A power of attorney, four assignments, six hundred pounds, a managing agent with nineteen years in post, and a torch in a cupboard. Not one of those people can be asked to undo any part of it, and I include the man who wrote the clause.** Eleven seconds. That is question one and it took eleven seconds and I have wanted to say it since the nineteenth of May."

"And question two," said Jonas Mercer. "Can the placing party be made to carry the cost of what its chain did."

"No," said Oren Pell.

"Can anybody."

"**No,**" said Pell, "and I am going to say the second half of that in the same breath and then I am going to sit down for a minute because it is the whole of the last four months and it took me a fortnight to get it down to a sentence.

"**There is no mechanism in this city to make the top of a chain pay, and there is no mechanism in this city to make a person take a duty.** Those are the same sentence. A price makes a duty into an asset, and an asset can be assigned, and a duty that is an asset can travel down a chain getting lighter each time, and at the bottom of it there is a person, and there is no mechanism — none, none at all, in the borough, the department, the Schedule, or this office — to make that person do anything, or to answer for anything, or to be asked.

"**And there is no mechanism to make the person who did it carry the cost of it. Which is not the same as saying they did nothing wrong, and I want to be extremely careful here, because you are going to want me to say something stronger and I am not going to, and if I say something stronger it will be me being impressive and not the law.**"

He opened the file and turned it round and there was a sheet on top with eleven lines on it and a paragraph number in the left margin.

"**Here is the ruling and it is short and it is the fourth one this year and it is a door and not a wall, and I am going to read it into a record this afternoon whether you are here or not, and I am reading it to you because you are the only person in this city who has an interest in it and you have behaved correctly about every single thing since the third of April and I would like somebody to be in the room.**

> **A claim discharged for the holder is discharged for the holder. It is not discharged for the party that placed it, and it is not discharged for any party that assigned it, and it is not discharged for the party that received the assignment.**
>
> **The person who caused the arrangement may be findable. Findability is not answerability. There is no power in this city by which the fact that a person can be located can be converted into a liability for what they arranged, and no registrar may convert it, and no Committee may convert it, and it is not converted by the fact that a person has said a thing out loud in a room and been right.**
>
> **Nothing in this determination exonerates any party. It records that the discharge of a claim runs one way: from the person who holds it outward to nothing at all, and not from the person who caused it back to anybody.**

"That is it," said Oren Pell. "That is the answer to the thing you have been carrying since the fourth of March, and I have watched you carry it, and I want to tell you what it is in plain words and then I am going to stop.

"**You did the right thing and the right thing cost a woman a day and a half of daylight and it will cost her again on the first of October unless twenty-nine other people and a woman on a landing do something about it in eleven weeks, and there is no mechanism by which any of them can be made to, and the party that assembled the chain is in Kestrel Hill and it is not going to pay for a pipe in Tideglass and it is not going to be asked to.**

"That is transfer stewardship. Not the transferring. The part where the person who moves a thing does not get rid of it. **I have been a registrar for nineteen years and I have watched about nine hundred claims go out of this office and I have never written that sentence down before this year and I have written it four times this year and I am tired of it and I am not going to soften it.**"

---

He closed the file and said, "Question four."

"You did not ask a fourth question."

"You said four things."

"I said four things," said Oren Pell. "**Who pays for the refusal.** That is the fourth one and you did not ask it and I am going to answer it anyway, because you have been not-asking it in a shop and on a telephone and in a records office for four days and I would rather it was asked.

"The answer is nobody. There is no mechanism. **And I want to be precise about why, because the why is the whole of what this city has got wrong for five months and I have been the only person in it saying it and I am the wrong man to be saying it and I am saying it anyway.**

"It is nobody because a person in this city may be required to hold a duty and may be required to answer for a service and may be required to answer a Return in public on a form on a date — and every one of those is a thing the law has spent six years getting right, and the 2008 regulations are, in the parts that matter, an excellent piece of drafting. **What the law has not got, in sixty years, is any way of making a person carry a cost that somebody else arranged.** And that is not a hole in the law. That is what a law is for. A law makes things happen. A law does not make amends, because amends is not a thing you can make happen to a person who is not in the room, and the moment a law can do that, then anybody with a form can do it to anybody, and this city has had five months of a preview.

"So: nobody pays. **And that is not an absolution and it is not a loophole and it is not going to be fixed in September by anybody with a lanyard.** It is simply the answer, and it has been the answer for the whole five months, and the reason nobody in this city has said it out loud until this week is that everybody has been waiting for a man with a register to be able to do something about it."

He put his hands flat on the desk.

"And that is the fourth time this year that I have had to make a ruling which is a door and not a wall. April: a hollow term is not struck, because a function is not a drafting error. May: *a party that answers may be asked what it will do and not what it will cost*, and I did not know where the chair had got it from and it is on a plate, and it is the best sentence in four hundred and sixty pages. June: a person cannot be required even to be told. And this.

"**Doors, all four. And nobody has ever walked through one of them and got what they wanted, and every one of them was mine, and I am not complaining, and I am telling you because you are the only person who will write it down without improving it.**"

---

They went out at twenty past eleven, and Pell walked to the door with him, and then said:

"One more thing and it is not a ruling and I am going to say it in a corridor, and I am going to say it once, and then I am going to go and do a stair."

"You have not heard it from me in a corridor."

"I have said once *at a table with a witness*. I have not said it standing up in a corridor to a man who is not a committee clerk," said Pell, and stopped walking. "**There is a regulation about this. It is 2008. It is section eleven.**"

And then he stood in the corridor of the second floor of the civic offices with a sandwich in a bag and a fan on a windowsill behind him and said:

"**Frances Tolley has a copy of it in her own hand. It has been read in this office twice and neither time by anybody who could act on it.**"

He looked at Jonas Mercer for about two seconds, to see whether he was going to ask the follow-up, and Jonas Mercer did not ask the follow-up, and Pell said:

"That is the whole of what I have. **I have no power to constitute anything. The chair has no power to constitute anything. You have no power to constitute anything and you are not a body and there is no instrument in this city that would let you be, and if anybody tells you there is, they are selling you something at a tier.** Go and do your shop."

And Oren Pell went back into his office and closed the door, and it is the fourth time this year that a man in this city has said a true sentence in a corridor and walked away from it, and the first three were said by other people.
