# Chapter 0078 — What a Heat Protocol Asks A Landlord For

Gunnar Halvorsen did it at ten past eight on the morning of Friday the thirtieth of May in the corridor at Saint Orra west, on foot, with a piece of A4 in his hand, in daylight, in front of about thirty people, because that was what he had said on the ninth of May in a doorway and he was a man of nineteen years' standing who did what he said he was going to do.

Dr Sanaa Iqbal was coming off a night with a coffee in each hand and she stopped when she saw him, and he said, before either of them had said good morning:

“Twenty-nine May, nine o'clock. I told you on the ninth that there would be a letter and that I would tell you in person and not on a telephone, and it went on my desk at nine o'clock on Monday the nineteenth, and I did not tell you on the nineteenth, and I have had eleven days to work out why and the reason is that on the nineteenth I was about to be in a Committee room and I did not want to be thinking about it.”

He handed her the A4. It was the suspension. The part-time on-call clinician's line, suspended from the first of June; the line that had been paid for out of nine hundred and thirteen pounds in a dead 2011 coffee-and-cakes appeal, committed on the twenty-second of April under a site-administration order because it was under four thousand pounds and did not need a board.

“I am not asking you for anything,” said Gunnar Halvorsen. “I have not asked you for anything since the ninth of May and I am not starting at ten past eight on a Friday. I have a decision on Thursday and it is mine and it is about a figure and I have told the only other person who is not going to be in the room. I am telling you because you found the four hundred and ten and you refused to be thanked for it in advance, and because I have watched you for five weeks and there are two things that are true about you and I know both of them and I am not going to pretend to only know one.

“The first is that you would have said no to me and been right. The second is that you would have said yes to me and been right, and you would not have enjoyed it, and it would have gone in a governance file with a paragraph number on it in about eleven weeks, and there is a room in this building where that file is.”

“I have not read it,” said Sanaa Iqbal.

“No. And I am not going to summarise it for you in a corridor, because you will do it in about ninety seconds in your own head and I have watched you do it four times and it is always right and I have never once been able to help with it.”

He put his hands in his pockets.

“There is one other thing and then I am going to go and answer about ninety telephones. The twenty minutes a week on the decontamination verification has not got anybody against it. It is on a board in that corridor in my handwriting and there is no name near it and there has been no name near it since the ninth of May, and the supplier will take a signature from any named member of staff on a Sunday, and the log goes to the governance file, and in November somebody is going to read that log and there is going to be a Sunday on it with a name that nobody recognises and it is going to be a small thing and it is going to be the last small thing anybody had time to stop.”

“Thank you for telling me,” said Sanaa Iqbal.

“It has been eleven days.”

“It has,” she said, “and you did it on foot, in daylight, with the paper, which you said you would do, and I have been waiting eleven days to find out which of us was wrong about the other, and I would like to say one thing about that and then I am going to drink this coffee and go to work, and I am not going to say it again in the month of June.

“You did not telephone me because you have a rule in that building about asking, and the rule is that every time somebody asks one of those four people about a date, one of them ends up on a rota because of the asking. I know that rule. I gave that rule to two other people in April. It is a good rule and it is the correct rule and **it is a rule that means nobody ever tells anybody anything, and the people it protects are the four people and not the twenty thousand.**

“I am not thanking you. I want to be exact that those are the words. I am not thanking you, and I am not saying it was right, and I am not saying it was wrong, and in four weeks’ time I am going to be in a room with nine people in it, four of them on that roster, and I am going to have to explain to them why a clause that needed two people in a corridor at ten at night went forty hours without either of them, and I am not going to use you, or a letter, or a budget line, or a man with a register, and you will not be in that room either, and I have thought about that a great deal since the ninth of May and I have not told you that I have thought about it.”

He said, “I would not have come.”

“I know,” said Sanaa Iqbal. “That is why I have been able to sleep.”

The Methodist hall on Carrow Lane has a stage, a piano that four men have promised to remove since 2009, about ninety stacking chairs in the store room, and a noticeboard with a laminated sheet on it that says the hall is for church purposes and the rent for non-church purposes is £45 for four hours and the keyholder lives at number sixty-one and is not on Sundays.

On Friday the thirtieth of May at about half past eight in the evening there were about sixty people in it and eleven more outside in the road because it was thirty degrees at half past eight and the hall does not have windows that open.

Jonas Mercer got there at about ten past six and spent forty minutes carrying chairs in and setting out rows, and he did it with the caretaker's wife and with a man called **Dele Fashanu** who has lived in Block B for nineteen years and who said, on the fourth chair:

“You are the man off the register.”

“Yes.”

“Good,” said Dele Fashanu. “I want to say something to you and I want to say it now and I do not want anybody else in the room. The last three men who came into this hall worked for somebody. One of them worked for the council and he had a list. One of them worked for a charity and he had a hat. One of them worked for a company and he had a photograph. **You have not got a list and you have not got a hat and I can see you have not got a photograph, and I do not know why you are here and I have decided that I do not mind.**”

On Saturday the thirty-first of May, at about half past ten in the morning, there were about forty people in the hall, which is fewer than the sixty-odd on Friday night because Saturday daytime is a working day and because eleven households are in a hostel four hundred yards away and come when they are told.

Block C was there. Six of the eight households who have been there since 1998 and who have been carrying the water for thirty rooms in buckets since 1998 came at twenty past nine, on foot, from four hundred yards, in thirty-one degrees, and they brought their buckets, and the buckets were put by the door because there is nowhere else to put them, and Ivy Marsden was seventy-two and had walked from the top landing of a 1938 block that has had no water since the sixth of May.

Marguerite Oyelaran was late by nine minutes because a woman in the hostel rang at a quarter past ten, and she came in with her folder under her arm and no coat on and stood at the front for about eleven seconds before she said anything, looking at about forty people, and then she said:

“I have been in this room three times in eleven years and on both of the other two times I had a form. Tonight I have got a folder and there is nothing in it that I am going to give anybody, and I want to say why in about four sentences and then I am going to start and I am not going to stop for nearly an hour and there will be a queue and I will get to all of it.

“In January I put a standing order form for two pounds sixty a room a week through a hundred and twenty-two letterboxes on this road on a Saturday morning, on my own, on foot, with a bag, because the agreement had to be in them before the twenty-eighth and there was no time. I knocked on every door as well. I have never once pretended to anybody that that was a consultation and I have read every one of the hundred and twenty-two marks twice and I will defend every one of them in a room in September.

“Tonight I am going to say the thing I have been carrying since the twenty-eighth of April, and I am saying it once, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and I am not going to be asked afterwards whether I meant it.”

Nobody said anything, and about eleven people had their arms folded, which is the correct number and she had counted it in January.

“**The fee does not stop.**” She said it the way you say the name of a road. “Two pounds sixty a room a week is not a contribution to a service. It is the whole of what keeps a door open in four buildings on this road, and it is the whole of what puts a man in Block D on a Wednesday to look at a lift that has not worked since 2011, and it is the whole of what has paid for the water in these blocks every month since March, and it does not stop. The money came from the fee, and now the money has gone — sixteen thousand eight hundred pounds in one year, paid on a schedule the association did not write, for four nodes, and the four thousand three hundred and sixty-one pounds and sixty pence it did not come back with comes out of my salary, which I have paid out of my own pocket since 2011, and will go on paying after I am gone, because there is no mechanism by which it stops.

“So the answer to the question everybody in this room is actually asking, which is *what happens on Monday* — the answer is **it is exactly what happened on Monday, and it is exactly what happened in March, and it is exactly what happened in 1998**, and I have been a chief officer for eleven years and I have watched this road be re-costed four times and I have never once seen a month where the answer was different.”

Then she put the folder on the table, and did not open it, and looked at the room.

“Now. I am going to be honest with you in a way that I have not been with this road before and I am not going to be afterwards either, because there are things in this room tonight that are worse than the fee and I have been sitting on all of them and I am not going to do that again.

“Twelve households on this road have asked to leave the agreement. Twelve. Eleven of them did it in one morning, in their own handwriting, on their own doorways, in the same hour, on the second of May, and the twelfth was handed to me face down by a woman from Block B on the Thursday, who counted on Saturday morning at breakfast and has told her daughter. That is the number. **That is a number and it is not a group and I am not going to stand here and tell you that twelve households of this road have a view, because they do not. They are twelve people and eleven of them did the same thing on the same day and I do not know why and I am not going to find out in a room.**

“I am not telling you which ones. I have been asked three times this week by people you would recognise and I have said no three times, and I will say no again, and if somebody in this borough needs a list of the households on this road that are unhappy then they can come and count the letters I have written, and the letters are in a drawer in two rooms above a betting shop, and there is a line in my own file that says *the answer to any request for a list of the people in my stock is two rooms above a betting shop on Carrow Lane*, and I wrote that line in 2016 after the last time, and I have now got eleven years and a caretaker and a form to enforce it, and I would like somebody in this room to take that as the answer.”

A man at the back, who was about sixty and lived in Block A and had been a fitter for thirty-one years, said: “You are telling us it is us versus you.”

“I am telling you it is me versus a piece of paper,” said Marguerite Oyelaran, “and the piece of paper was not written by anybody in this room and I have signed it and I have put your names on it, one hundred and twenty-two of you, on a Saturday, with a bag, and I have read the clause that says you cannot take it back until the twelfth of April next year, and I did not tell you that clause existed before I put it through your letterbox, and I have not told you it exists tonight either, and somebody will tell you, and when they do I am not going to be able to help you, because I am not your lawyer and I have never been your lawyer and the only lawyer in this city who would come to this hall for nothing is a woman of thirty-nine who says sentences in galleries and I have not rung her and I am not going to.”

---

The hour went the way hours go. A man wanted to know about the tank on the roof of Block B. Two women wanted to know about a bin store. Somebody wanted to know what happened to the people who had gone to the hostel, and Marguerite Oyelaran said that eleven households were in a hostel and two flats at her expense and had been since the seventeenth of April, and that she had visited all of them on the twenty-second of May, and that one of them had a telephone and it does not work, and that she had said on the second of May that the fee does not stop and it did not stop then either and she was not going to be asked to say it again.

And at about twenty past eleven she stopped in the middle of something, because somebody at the back had said a word she did not catch, and she said:

“No — say that again. Say the number again, because I have written it down and I have not written it down before, and I have been sitting on it for two days and I have been frightened of it, and there is a man in this room from the borough and I would rather he heard it here than from a record.

“There is a figure in a minute of a Committee of the Council. It was read out in public on the nineteenth of May, at about ten past eleven in the morning, and a woman in the second row heard it and did not sleep. It is **eleven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds a year**, and it is for six water points on a lowland, at a thousand eight hundred and eighty pounds a point, and it is the only thing anybody in this city has ever put in a public record about the price of a thing that people drink, and the person who wrote it down first was me, on the twenty-eighth of April at about ten past eleven in the morning, in this road, on the wall in my own office, and it said *nobody has asked me*, and I wrote it to see what somebody would do with it.”

Somebody at the back said, “What did they do with it?”

“They read it out,” said Marguerite Oyelaran. “That is what they did with it. They read it out in a room with forty-one chairs in it and put it in a minute and did nothing else, ever, and I was in the gallery, and I have been sitting with it for nine days.

“**And here is the part that I would like somebody in this room to understand, because I have worked it out and I do not think it is right and I do not think it is wrong.** The minute says six lowland water points. It does not say where they are. It does not have a district, a number, a location of record, a holder, a date of deferral, a name, a street, or anything at all, and there is no map in this city that has got them on it, and the Committee itself could not say. **You cannot say which six. I cannot say which six. A man with a lanyard and a September date could not say which six.**

“And that is the sentence, and it is the only sentence in this whole business that I have believed since March, and it cuts both ways, and I am going to say the other side of it out loud because I am not going to be a person who only says the half that is useful to her.

“If nobody can say which six the water points are, then nobody can say whose six they are either. They could be on a road like this one. There are people on this road who have been drinking out of a bucket at half five in the morning for sixteen years and who have not been to a meeting since 1998 and who would not come tonight. And they are not in the minute and they are not on the map and they are not in anybody’s list, and if the minute is right they might be in it, and if the minute is right they might not, and the man who read the number out in a room on the nineteenth of May did it **without asking anybody**, and I have not decided yet whether I am going to be able to live with that, and I have had nine days and I have not decided.”

The hall was very quiet, and about four of the forty people looked at Jonas Mercer, who was standing at the back by the door with his bag at his feet and who had not said one word in an hour and a quarter.

Somebody at the back said, “Are you the one who read it out?”

“No,” said Jonas Mercer. “I wrote down a number that a woman in this hall wrote first, and a chair read it out in a room, and I did not choose the six and I have never been able to find out who the six are, and that is the whole of what I have done about it.”

“Right,” said the man.

And that was the end of that, and the meeting went on for another hour and a quarter, and he carried chairs back into the store room afterwards, and a woman of about seventy stopped him at the door and said, “You did not say the word schedule once in an hour and a quarter and I have been coming to meetings about this road for twenty-six years and I can tell you that the only men who come into this hall are the ones who want something in it.”

“No,” said Jonas Mercer. “I have got nothing to add. I would have said so at the beginning if I had thought anybody would let me.”

“Nobody is going to let anybody,” said the woman. “That is the whole of what happens at these meetings.”

Marguerite Oyelaran had lost Block C and had told thirty-nine people and a caretaker's wife that the fee does not stop, and had told them that there are twelve and had not told them it is a number, and had put on the table a folder she did not open, and had a queue of nine people at the door at half past twelve on the thirty-first of May.

And four miles away, in a building with a corridor and a plate on the wall and a card table at the end of it, the second named on-call clinician in the history of that site finished her last shift at twenty-two hundred on the evening of Saturday the thirty-first of May, and walked out through a door she had opened herself, in a building with a published gap on a board from eleven o'clock to half past one every weekday and a refused Thursday with a man's name against it and a request, made on the fifteenth of May by a woman of forty-one with a daughter of eleven, that it be left where it was.

**From the first of June the line is nothing. Priya Ranganathan's two days a week are a number in a line on a budget that came out of a dead appeal from 2011. Dr Sanaa Iqbal did not telephone anybody about it, then or afterwards, and had not been thanked, and had told a service manager in a corridor at ten past eight on a Friday morning that the rule which stopped anybody from telling anybody anything had been protecting four people and not twenty thousand. And on a board in a corridor in a building called Saint Orra west, in a service manager's handwriting, there were twenty words a week against a steriliser and no name against them, and nobody had put their name near it since the ninth of May, and it was still there on the first of June, and it would be there on the seventh, and there is a Sunday in it somewhere.**
