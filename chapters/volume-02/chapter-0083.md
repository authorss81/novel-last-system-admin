# Chapter 0083 — What Thirty-One People Signed

**Tuesday the twenty-fourth of June 2014, about eleven in the morning, the records office on the second floor.**

Jonas Mercer read the two returns properly, which he had not been able to do on Thursday because he had been standing at the back of a gallery with about fifty people in front of him, and the determination of the nineteenth of May read them into a record as they were received, and that is a very efficient way of getting something into a record and a very bad way of reading one.

The borough's form is four pages. Page one asks what the node is. Page two asks what the node is for. Page three asks for the party holding continuity and for the fee and the tier. Page four is a declaration and a signature and a date and nothing else at all, which is the correct design and which has been in use since March and which four hundred people in this city have signed.

Beverley Annis signed it on the sixth of June, for a firm, with a printed job title, and the job title is *principal*, and the firm is named, and the claim reference is in a box on page three, and the answer to *who answers for this point* is **the Aldbrook Trust, as freeholder of the fee payer, one-half**.

The other one is four pages as well, and the second of the four pages is a printed one that says *answer every box or the form is not a return*, and Nina Calder's is one page and says *there is no box for this*, and this is a form a maintenance contractor filled in on the sixteenth of June with a pen, and the answer to *who answers for this point* is **the named steward of the building**, and there is a name in the box under it, and the name is Yolanda Fofana, and it is a name she did not write, on a form she has never seen, in a records office four miles from the block she has been in for nineteen years.

He read the rest of it and then he did the thing he had come to do, which was the only arithmetic in the room.

**Fourteen returns were received. Thirteen of them were answered by the party that holds the node, and the party that holds a node in this city is nearly always a body.**

The fourteenth was answered on the sixteenth of June by a firm that maintains the node, on behalf of a person it had never spoken to. The answer is correct. The person it names did not answer, was not asked, and did not know.

And here is why nobody noticed, and it took him four minutes to see it and then he sat down on the edge of a table in a records office for about two minutes.

**A person in a field is not a party until somebody has told her that she is one, and no instrument in this city tells her.** The determination of the nineteenth of May required every party holding continuity to answer. She is a party. She was required. She answered nothing, and the return that answers for her was filled in by a maintenance firm that put her name in a box on page two of four, and the box has her whole name in it, and page two of four is not a line anybody reads out loud in a room.

**And the minute has two entries about that meter room and both of them are correct and neither of them is her, and if you read the minute of the nineteenth of June you would say there were two companies interested in a meter room in Tideglass, and the two companies are a trust that has not looked at a cupboard since March and a firm that has, and the woman is on the second of the two in a box, and nobody wrote *person* anywhere.**

"You have found the thing I have been trying to say to a man in Kestrel Hill since Monday," said Nina Calder, from the table, without looking up.

"I have found the thing you have been saying since the nineteenth of May," said Jonas Mercer, "which is that a form with two entries in a minute and a hole in it can both be complete and both be wrong, and I have not told anybody in Kestrel Hill anything, and I am not going to, and I am going to ask her one question on Friday afternoon in daylight and it is not going to be this."

"You are going to ask her whether she knows," said Nina Calder.

"I am going to ask her what she will do about it," said Jonas Mercer, "and I am not going to ask her what it will cost, and if I ask her what it will cost somebody will write in a minute in September that the question was put to a person, and a person is not a thing you put questions to in this city, and I am not going to be the man who did it."

"Friday," said Nina Calder. "It is Friday tomorrow."

"Friday," he said. "Twenty-seven minutes past two in the afternoon, outside, because it will be twenty-six degrees and she will be inside and she will come out, and I will not go in."

He wrote the two dates on his hand and did not write anything else down, and at the door Nina Calder said the sentence she had said in June, in June's words, which she says roughly once a fortnight and which is not a complaint:

"**A form is how a plate gets falsified in four days by nobody doing anything wrong.** You are about to go and ask a person a question on behalf of a document. Go carefully."

---

**Thursday the twenty-sixth of June 2014, half past seven in the evening, the furniture workshop under Wren Court, Salter's Yard.**

It is the last Thursday of the month and there are twenty-six of the thirty-one, and there have been twenty-six or thereabouts at the monthly meeting since 2011, and the reason is not apathy. It is that eleven households work nights and two are seventy-eight and eighty-one and come in the day. The meeting is at half past seven because that is the only hour at which a person who finishes at seven can be in a room with a person who starts at eight, and it has been at half past seven for nineteen years and nobody has ever moved it, and the month of June is the last month it will be the last Thursday, because on Thursday the third of July she has called a three-night sitting of the whole block and has told nobody why yet.

The workshop has a planer bench, a stack of window frames from a job in 2011, a tea urn that works, eleven folding chairs that came from a pub, and a table made of two trestles and a door.

**Yolanda Fofana** is fifty-four. She has been in the block nineteen years and named steward for fourteen of them, and she was a ward sister at a hospice in Tideglass for nine years before that, and she does the whole of this in about ninety minutes a week, of which about forty minutes is this room, and she has never been paid for any of it, and she has never asked to be.

She has known for three days.

She has a reference number written on the back of her left hand in biro — **T3/11400/0304** — and she has not washed it off, and she has looked at it about nine times, and it has not changed.

On Thursday she had two things to say and she had known since Monday which of the two was going to cost her more, and the one that cost her more was not the Tier 3.

---

The first thing happened at about ten past eight, when Esio Kotto, who is twenty-nine and works in a call centre in a converted mill and who has been in the block for three years, put a folded sheet on the table face up.

"That came through the door on Tuesday," she said. "Not a bill. A letter about the water."

Cynthia Akintola, who is sixty-one and has paid eleven pounds a week into that tin since 1995 and has never once been late, said: "I know. It came in March. I signed it."

"You signed the first one," said Esio Kotto. "This is a different one. This one says the weekly amount goes up by two pound sixty on the first of June, and it is on the letterhead of the building's water people, and it has got a reference on it that I have looked up, and it is not the water people who have set it."

"Who has," said Malcolm Reid, who is forty-four and works nights at a distribution centre and who is one of the twelve who did not sign.

Nobody answered for a bit. Two urns' worth of conversation came out of the room.

Then Yolanda Fofana said, and this is what she said, and she said it in the flat voice she uses for readings:

"Right. I will say it, and then I will say it again in a fortnight, and I have been waiting nineteen years to be the person in this room who is given the questions, and this is what happened.

"**The water in this building went onto a paid schedule on the eleventh of March.** The block's supply was not maintainable on a service basis and the freeholder's engineer wrote to us in February and said the choice was a schedule at a fixed sum or a supply at a maintenance basis, and the maintenance basis for a pipe that is fed through a riser nobody has looked at since 2011 is the pipe's own maintenance, which is a stopcock and a bucket. And the schedule costs money. And the money comes out of the tin. And that is why the weekly figure is thirteen pounds sixty and it was eleven pounds."

"That is it?" said Malcolm Reid. "That is the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of it," said Yolanda Fofana.

And that was the discovery, and it was discovered wrongly, and she was the one who made it wrong.

**She had three days to say the rest of it and she said this instead**, and she was not lying, and every word of it was true, and it was the first time in nineteen years that she had managed a room by leaving out a thing she had been asked not to leave out.

Because the rest of it was: that the schedule was not bought for this building; that it came into this building through three assignments from a clinic in Kestrel Hill; that the name in the field at the bottom of the chain was hers; that nobody had asked her; and that if she said that in this room then thirty-one people would be in a room with a tier and a reference and a public minute, and eleven of them had been on a lapsed term on the seventeenth of April, and a person in this city who is on a lapsed term in a building that has a chain in it is a person who can be found in about four days by anybody with a counter and a form.

Cynthia Akintola said, into the noise: "So the council's put the water on a list."

"No," said Yolanda Fofana. "It is not the council. It is not anybody's council. I have looked and I am not going to bore you with the looking, and I will tell you one thing about it that I have not told anybody, and then I am going to move on, because we have got the bins."

"Tell us the one thing," said Esio Kotto.

"That is not a decision anybody took about this building," said Yolanda Fofana. "That is the thing I want in your heads before I move on. **Nobody in this city decided anything about us.** It came the way weather comes."

---

The second thing was clause nine, and it happened at about twenty to nine because Malcolm Reid had been carrying it in his coat for three weeks and got it out.

Cynthia Akintola read it out. She reads well and she had read it twice.

> **9. If any weekly payment is not received within sixty days of the due date, the provider may disconnect the supply. One visit will be made. A photograph of the meter will be taken. Failure to provide access on the day of the visit will be treated as refusal.**

There is a silence in a workshop in Salter's Yard that four people heard and nine did not.

"Nineteen years," said Cynthia Akintola, to nobody, "and that has been in it the whole time."

"It has been in it the whole time," said Yolanda Fofana.

"And how many people have read that before tonight."

"Eleven," said Yolanda Fofana. "Three of you. And it has been in the folder in that drawer since the twelfth of March and it has been in the folder the whole time and I have not taken it out and I have not read it out, and I am going to tell you why I did not read it out, and then you can decide about me."

She put her hands flat on the door-table, which is a thing she has done at this table about forty times.

"Because if I had read it out in April, on the eighth of April, then a woman who lives in flat twenty-two would have known on the eighth of April that the term on her own flat had lapsed on the first of April and that she was living here on nothing, and there are eleven of you who were in this building on the seventeenth of April when the water was at half, and not one of the eleven would have stayed."

The room did the thing rooms do.

Then Yolanda Fofana said: "That was the wrong thing to do, and I am telling you that it was the wrong thing to do, and I would do it again, and both of those are true, and if anybody wants to take that apart before the third of July, the meeting is on Thursday and I will chair it, and I have called one for the Wednesday, and I am going to tell you why on Thursday and not before."

---

Then she did the thing that the chapter is for, and she did it flatly and without any of the four sentences she had prepared, and it turned out to be the shortest speech anybody in that room had heard her make.

"The twelve of you who did not sign," she said. "Malcolm, Esio, two in the back, and eight I am not going to count out loud. I have been going to say something about that for a fortnight and I have got it wrong twice and this is the third time and I am going to say it in one sentence.

"**You did not refuse the price. You refused a document, and a document is a different thing, and I have been standing here for a month treating you as though you were difficult.**

"Malcolm, I know what you have done and I know what you have not done, and I am going to say both in this room and you are not going to enjoy the first one. You have paid eleven pounds a week into that tin for eight years without missing a quarter, and I have your name on my own list and I do not hand my list to anybody, and the day you did not sign in March is the day you decided you would rather be a household in my building than a signatory in my folder, and you did not tell me that either, and I found out from the count.

"And you read a document that says a photograph of the meter, and then you did not sign it, and you have been living since March under a clause nine that says a photograph of you can be taken, and you have not said the word out loud in here because saying it out loud in here would have been saying it about somebody else, and I have watched you not say it since the second of March and I have said nothing either and that is the second half of the wrong thing."

"And I want to say the other half, because I have been wrong twice and I am not going to be right by accident.

"There are four of you who missed a month. I am not going to say who. You know who you are and I know who you are and that is the whole of what I have and I am not going to turn it into a list, and anybody in this room who wants a list of the four can go and stand outside and I will help them, and the answer to any request for a list of people in this building is the address of this building and nothing else, and I wrote that line down about six times in a diary in 2004 and I am not going to soften it now."

Malcolm Reid said, "Yolanda."

"I am not finished."

"You said you were not charging anybody for the water," he said. "You said in April the fee does not stop. I have told two people on this road that you said that."

"I said that," said Yolanda Fofana. "On the eighth of April I said it in this room, and I have said it on the last Thursday of every month since, and there is a line in my own agreement that my own term lapsed on the first of April and that the fee does not stop anyway, and I have carried that since the first of April and I have not told any of you, and if I had told you tonight then the eleven who were in this building on the seventeenth of April would have known on the same evening, and that is the second reason I did not read clause nine out in April, and I have just said it out loud, and there it is."

She looked at the door-table and not at the room.

"The fee does not stop. That has not stopped. And it has not stopped for a reason, and the reason is not moral, and I am going to tell you the reason in three weeks' time and not before, because I have to be able to say all of it in one go and I cannot do it in pieces, and there is one part of it that is about me."

---

Nobody asked her about that, which is worth recording, and it was not politeness. It was that the twenty-six people in that room have been listening to a woman read things out for fourteen years and they know the difference between a thing she is going to say and a thing she is not.

Esio Kotto said, at about ten past nine, into the noise of everybody deciding whether to believe her:

"Yolanda. Both of those numbers are going to be in a public record by the end of the month."

"What two numbers."

"Nineteen and twelve," said Esio Kotto. "Nineteen of us signed and twelve of us did not, and that is going to be in a public record, and it is going to be in one before the end of the month, because I have been to the records office on the second floor of the civic offices twice this month — for my own reasons, for a tenancy thing, it is nothing — and there is a form you can get at that counter with a crest on it, and it is a public document, and anybody can get it, and it has got a section on it where you put a number in, and a number in there is a *number of households in a building where a party answers*, and it is not a name and it is not an address, and it is the only box on the whole form that anybody will ever fill in and you cannot get rid of it.

"And I have worked out, sitting here, why nobody ever wrote *nobody* on it, and it is because you cannot write *nobody* in a number box, and somebody in that building on the nineteenth of May said the word *nobody* out loud from the public seats and got it minuted. So it is coming. It is coming either way. **And the block meeting on the third of July is going to have thirty-one people in it and by then both of those numbers are going to be legible to anybody who walks into a counter in this city.**"

Yolanda Fofana sat down on a window frame for the first time in about nine years.

"Say that again," she said.

And Esio Kotto said it again, and did not soften it, and Cynthia Akintola wrote it on the back of her hand, and Yolanda Fofana took the reference number off her own hand and looked at it and then looked at Esio Kotto and said:

"Then that is the meeting on the third of July, and I am going to say everything in it, and I am going to say it in this room, in my own words, and I am going to say it in front of the whole block and not in a corridor, and there is not going to be a vote, and I am not going to ask one single person in this room to sign anything.

"And I want to say the last thing about it now, while there are twenty-six of you here, and then I am going to stop.

"**A number in a box is not a household and it is not a person and it is not an address, and this building has not got one.** And every single thing that has happened to us since the first of March has come in through that gap, and the gap has never once been a fault. It has been the only door. And I have stood in it and I have charged a weekly amount through it for nineteen years and I have never once asked the people on the other side of it what they thought the number was for, and if I had asked, they would have told me the truth, because I have never once had to lie to anybody in this room, and I have known that for nineteen years and I have never once used it."

She got up off the window frame.

"The third. Wednesday, Thursday and Friday, half past seven, in this room. And the thirty-one, not the twenty-six, because by then I will have been to the doors of the five of you who are not here, on my own, one at a time, and I am not ringing anybody."

---

She shut the workshop at about ten past ten and walked up the outside stair to her own flat on the second floor, and there was a reference on the back of her left hand in biro, and she looked at it in the kitchen with the light on.

**T3/11400/0304.**

On Monday a clerk of the records counter had told her, in about four minutes, correctly, that a woman's name was in a field on a form, that the form was a public document, that the claim was at Tier 3, that it had been placed on the fourth of March and assigned four times, that no party in the chain had confirmed that the party at the end was aware, and that she could withdraw the claim in her own name at the counter without notice to any other party and without any formality.

She had asked for the reference so that she could write it down. She had asked whether anybody was going to come and tell her what it was. She had been told that the counter could not say and that the records office does not send anybody anywhere.

She had not told the managing agent. She had not told the four on the second floor. She had not told the nineteen, and she had not told the twelve.

There were two things in that kitchen.

The first was that on Monday she had been, for about nine seconds, frightened, and she had not been frightened of the trust and had never heard of the trust; she had been frightened of the word *holder*, because she had lived above a furniture workshop for nineteen years and had never in her life been a holder of anything, and had spent fourteen of those years signing things in this building on behalf of other people, and had a drawer two feet from where she was standing with eleven of their terms in it in a folder with a rubber band, and four of those eleven had lapsed on the first of April and she had told this room on the eighth of April that there was a mains matter.

The second thing was that a woman of twenty-nine had said *both of those numbers are going to be in a public record by the end of the month*, and had been right, and had been right in about nine seconds, and had said it without any idea at all that she was the reason a housing association's chief officer had a photocopy of her own four blocks inside eight days of a form being printed for a counter.

And the two numbers were nineteen and twelve, and they added to thirty-one, and thirty-one people lived in the building, and there was no instrument in this city in which a block was thirty-one people. It was nineteen, or it was twelve, and both of those numbers were about who had signed a document, and neither of them was about whether the water was on.

She washed the cup. She did not wash the biro off, and it took eleven days to come off, and on the day it came off she did not notice, and nobody in the block ever mentioned it.
