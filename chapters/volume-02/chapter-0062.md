# Chapter 0062 — The Nineteenth of May Is Not a Hearing Yet

The thing Jonas Mercer found out on Saturday the third of May, at about ten past three in the afternoon, standing outside the ferry office in the Old Ward with a bag over his shoulder because he had come up the road from Carrow Lane and had ten minutes to kill, was that there are two pieces of paper describing Monday the nineteenth, and they were written by two different people, and neither of them had read the other.

The first was the tabling list. It had been published on the department's website at four o'clock on Wednesday afternoon along with everything else, and it is what anybody who is not in the room will read, and Jonas Mercer had read it four times.

> **STANDING COMMITTEE ON CIVIC CONTINUITY — MONDAY 19 MAY 2014, 10:00, COMMITTEE ROOM TWO.**
> **1. Determination of 17 April 2014 (Saint Orra west care entrance) — noted and suspended. Registrar's record of 18 April 2014.**
> **2. Cover note by the clerk.**
> **3. Schedule 4 to the Saint Orra service agreement of 2019 — continuity pricing. Covering submission, CivicCore Consortium, 24 April 2014.**
> **4. Register of attachment — noted.**
> **5. Schedule 4 (continuity pricing) — referred on.**

Item five. It is the item everybody is going to read, it is the item Alden Rook has spent a fortnight being careful about, and it says **referred on**, which is the word a committee uses for a thing it is not deciding, and Jonas Mercer had gone into Friday believing that the committee was being asked to decide whether Schedule 4 stood or fell.

The second piece of paper was paragraph two of the covering submission, and it had a heading, and the heading was **PURPOSE OF THE SUBMISSION**, and under it it said:

> **The Committee is respectfully invited to APPROVE Schedule 4 (continuity pricing) as a continuity arrangement for service districts within its remit, and to adopt the schedule as a schedule of the Committee for the remainder of the financial year.**

That is the whole of it. *To approve. And to adopt.* He stood on a step outside the ferry office on a Saturday afternoon and read them about nine times and then went and sat down on the fourth chair, which is the worn one, under the glass canopy, in the rain, with a plastic sleeve on his knee that no longer had anything in it.

The tabling list said *referred on*. The submission said *approve and adopt*. Those are not the same instrument, they do not have the same result, and a member of that committee who read only the list would go into that room on Monday morning with a completely different picture from a member who read the covering submission — and four hundred and sixty pages of schedule, and a document dated the twenty-fourth of April, and a finding in it written in a shop steward's handwriting, would be sitting in the folders of all six of them, and every one of them had read the list, because the list is what gets sent out, and nobody in this city reads a covering submission unless they are somebody's lawyer or somebody's continuity counsel.

He wrote it in the pad.

> *List, item 5: Schedule 4 — referred on.*
> *Submission, para 2: the Committee is invited to APPROVE Schedule 4 and to ADOPT it as a schedule of the Committee for the remainder of the financial year.*
> *Nobody has moved that it be approved. The submission asks for it. That is not the same instrument and it is not the same result, and if it happens nobody will have tabled anything, because a submission is a document and a tabling list is a document and the thing that decides between them is a chair.*

---

He tried three doors over the Sunday and got three different answers, and all three of them were correct.

Oren Pell took ninety seconds on the telephone on the Sunday morning and gave him one sentence about it and then refused to give him any more.

“If the committee approves a schedule, that schedule is a schedule of the committee, and the challenge clause on page nine goes from being a contractor’s clause to being the city’s rule of standing, and no person may challenge it under either. So no, I cannot tell you whether it will be approved, and I have not asked, and I am not going to ask, because the registrar does not get to know what a committee is going to do before it happens, and if he does he is the man who was in the room and not the man in the corridor. Write to the chair. I am not taking it.”

“I am not a member and I am not a party and I am not an officer of the department.”

“Then you will not be in the room,” said Oren Pell, “and I want to say the next part because you will work it out on Sunday and I would rather you worked it out from a registrar. **You can be in the gallery. You can be in the gallery because a gallery is for people who want to listen and anybody can want to listen, and nobody in a gallery is a party, and if somebody puts a microphone on you I will stand up and say so.** That is the entire extent of what being a man on a register buys you in that room and I have known since the nineteenth of April and I have not told you because you have been busy.”

---

Fenella Marsh would not let him see the papers.

He found her at about twenty past eleven on the Sunday morning outside the committee clerk’s window on the first floor, which is where she was between a Sunday and a Monday when there was anything to be done, and she was carrying a box file, and she stopped, and she was not unkind about it.

“Mr Mercer,” she said. “I am not going to refuse you on the grounds that you are not a party, because I do not think that is a good ground and I have four members who would tell you it is a good ground, and I have spent two years being frustrated by that. I am refusing you because in about four minutes a man with a lanyard is going to ring this office and ask me whether the papers went to the man off the register, and I have not got an answer for him that is better than the truth, and the truth is that they did not, and the reason they did not is that you are not a member, a party, or a departmental officer, and I am not going to invent a fourth thing to get round it.”

“That is fair.”

“It is fair and it is also the entire answer and I have been giving it in that form since the nineteenth of April, and I would like you to notice that I have never once said *not yet* to you. I have said *no*, and the no does not have a review in it.” She shifted the box file. “Now. What is it you actually want, because you have stood in that corridor for four minutes and you have not said what you want and I have got eleven days of chairing in front of me and a contractor with a folder.”

He told her about paragraph two.

Fenella Marsh did not interrupt him. She listened with her head slightly on one side, and when he had finished she said, “Say the two words again.”

“To approve. And to adopt.”

“Right.” She put the box file down on the window ledge, which she should not have done, and which Jonas Mercer noticed and did not use. “Then that is a discrepancy between a tabling list and a covering submission and it is the single commonest discrepancy in the history of this committee, and it happens about four times a year, and it has happened in this room since 1974. And I will tell you what happens in about four cases out of five, which is that nothing happens. Nobody moves it. It sits there. Somebody says *I take it that the schedule is before us for information* and everybody in the room is relieved because a schedule for information is a schedule nobody has to vote on, and the week goes on.

“And in about one case in five, somebody moves it, and gets it, and by the middle of June the city has adopted a contractor’s price list as a schedule of its own standing committee and the sentence *it may not be challenged by a person* is the sentence that decides every argument in this borough for two years. And I do not know which of those two weeks this is going to be, and neither do you, and neither does the man with the folder, and I have been chairing this committee for six years and I have never been able to tell in advance, and I have stopped being interested in being able to tell in advance.”

“What would tell you.”

“Wednesday,” said Fenella Marsh. “If by Wednesday lunchtime there is a motion in the papers, I will know. If there is not, there is about a one in four chance somebody does it from the floor, and I have a way of finding out about that at about ten past ten on Monday morning, which is that I count the faces in the room and I count the ones who are not in the room.”

“Who is not in the room.”

“That is not a question I can answer to a member on a Sunday morning, and I will tell you why not: because if I can answer it then I am doing a headcount on my own committee, and I did that once in 2011 and it cost me a member and I have not done it since, and I would rather be surprised on Monday than be a registrar on a Sunday.”

She picked the box file up.

“Two things and then I am going. One: I am not asking you to tell anybody what you have just told me, and I would be grateful if you did not, and I have not got a way of saying that that does not sound like an instruction from a chair to a technician, so I will just say that if it gets into a corridor before Monday then I lose the ability to be surprised, and that is the only reason I have given it to you.”

“And you have given me one more thing and I am going to refuse to be grateful for it in a corridor, which is that you have made me check it twice, and I had not checked it once in six years.”

“You noticed already. You noticed in about ninety seconds on a step. I have twenty-one minutes left of my Sunday and a box file and no motion in it, and I am going to go and find out whether I can make one, and if I can I am going to make one, and I am not going to tell you what it is.”

---

Nell Ardent tried to table it on the Sunday afternoon and got told, in about four minutes on the telephone by a very pleasant woman in a member’s office, that she could not.

The member was Sore, and the office belonged to Sore, and the woman’s name was not given and does not matter, and what she said was this, in a voice that had clearly said a version of it before.

“Only a member of the committee may table an amendment. That is standing order eleven, and it is not a rule this office invented and it is not something anybody can stretch. The clerk will accept a motion from a member in writing or by hand, and the chair must put it to the committee, and a member may move it from the floor without notice at any point in the sitting.

“So the answer is yes, and the answer is that it has to come from a member, and the members are six people, and four of them do not think that *holder* is a question this committee should be answering in public, and the two who do have not told us which way they are going to do it and I am not going to find out from you and then tell them.”

“I am not asking her to move it,” said Nell Ardent. “I am asking whether it is already in.”

“It is not already in. I have the papers in front of me in a folder on my desk because I am the person who collates them, and there is no motion, and there is no member’s note, and there is nothing in the correspondence file about the definition of *holder* other than the contractor’s covering submission, which is item three, and which says *refer to the Committee*.” A pause. “Can I say one thing that is not advice, because if it is advice you will not be able to use it and if it is an observation you can do what you like with it?”

“Yes.”

“If four members had wanted it moved, it would have been in the papers on Friday morning,” she said. “It is not in the papers. That is not a conspiracy. Nobody is hiding it. It is four people who have each independently assessed that moving a definition of *holder* into a public sitting on a Monday in May is a worse idea than leaving it alone and being criticized afterwards for leaving it alone. I have watched three of those four do that to a colleague and I have watched it be right twice.”

“So the risk is that it happens anyway.”

“The risk is that it happens *from the floor*,” said the woman, “because a floor motion needs no notice, and a floor motion cannot be withdrawn once it is seconded, and on Monday morning somebody with a good sentence and a contractor's finding in their mouth is going to get a definition of *holder* into a public minute in about eleven minutes, and every one of the six of them will let it happen because stopping it is not the chair's job, and the chair has said so herself, in a room, to somebody, and I am not going to say who.”

Nell Ardent put the telephone down and said, to nobody, in an office on the first floor of a building on the Old Ward, “Eleven minutes. He said eleven minutes on Wednesday. He said it in a stairwell and he was wrong by nothing.”

---

He found the last thing at about half past three on the Sunday afternoon, in the records office, with Nina Calder, who was on her own time and told him so.

“Two things,” she said. “One of them is an answer to the question you did not ask and I can see the shape of it on you, and it is no, you are not getting the papers, and I have never had the papers, because the papers belong to the committee and the committee’s clerk is Frances Tolley and Frances Tolley has been the clerk of that committee for twenty-two years and she does not let a records supervisor read the papers of a sitting, and if she did I would not let her.”

“Then what did you find.”

“The tabling list,” said Nina Calder. “Which I put into three systems on Wednesday afternoon, as I do, and which is a public document, and which I put a background line on at about ten past five that same evening because a background line is where the context goes and there was nothing else in the field.”

She turned a terminal round so he could read it. It is a line on a form, in her typing, under a heading, and it says:

> **Background. The Committee is asked to note that the contractor's covering submission is accompanied by a statement, made by the contractor to a third party on 30 April 2014, that a definition of the term *holder* would be drafted by whichever party places a schedule before the Committee. The Committee is not asked to define *holder*.*

Jonas Mercer read it four times.

“Nina.”

“Yes.”

“That sentence is a prediction, attributed, in the public papers of a committee, with a date on it, and a name in it.”

“It is,” said Nina Calder. “It is Rook’s. You told me the wording on the Wednesday night and I wrote it up on the Wednesday night and I put it in the field because it was the most useful piece of background anybody handed me in eleven days and I have not got a way of saying a thing is true without saying whose it is, because if I do that then in about four months the first person in this city who is asked *how did the committee know* will get an answer out of Nina Calder and not out of a document, and I would like there to be a document.”

“It is a good line. It is exactly the right line.”

“It is a good line and I have been standing here since half past three on the Sunday afternoon looking at it and doing arithmetic,” said Nina Calder, “and here is the arithmetic. The papers for Monday went to Frances Tolley on Friday morning at nine o’clock and the first print went out at two o’clock on Friday and the second print went out at eleven o’clock on Friday night, and it is Saturday, and it does not get reprinted unless there is a material change, and I cannot get a line out of that field now without a fresh print and a fresh set of papers and Frances Tolley’s initials in the corner, and Frances Tolley is not here today and on Monday morning she will not take a late item for a background line because she has never taken a late item for anything in twenty-two years.”

“So it stays in.”

“It stays in,” said Nina Calder, “and it is going to be read out on Monday, because everything in the papers gets read out, that is what papers are, and there is going to be a member of the Standing Committee on Civic Continuity standing in a room in the Old Ward at about a quarter to eleven in the morning quoting a document that says the contractor told a technician in a stairwell that somebody would try to define *holder* on the nineteenth of May, and it is going to be the truest thing anybody says all day, and it is going to be in my field, on Friday, at ten past five in the evening, because I could not think of a better place for it.”

He put his hand flat on the desk for a second, and then took it off, because the desk was not his and he had been told off for that in April by a man on a bench.

“Can it be characterised in the room.”

“It can be characterised by whoever stands up, and it will be, and they will be right, and I will not be able to say a word. I have been a records supervisor for nineteen years and I have never put a line in a field that I could not defend and I can defend this one for about ninety seconds, and after ninety seconds it is a prediction in a public paper and the prediction has come true, and the only question anybody will ask me is why a records supervisor printed a prediction.”

“That is the right question and you asked it in the wrong order,” said Nina Calder. “You asked me why I put it in. The answer to that is that it is the truest thing anybody handed me in eleven days and I have got a field where true things go.

“What you should have asked me is whether it was any use to anybody before I printed it, and it was not, and it was the only thing in that folder that was not.

“And the answer to that, which you can have now and in writing and you may put it in whatever you are holding, is the man at the basement printer. The Lattice cannot publish a narrowing. It draws a shape and a term and a date and a claim and it has never in the history of that machine drawn what a thing stopped doing. So the city has to do it on paper, and the reason the paper is any good at all is that two men who were not asked said two true things to each other in a basement and a stairwell in the last three weeks of April, and the entire instrument that exists in this city on the first of May is standing on those two conversations, and neither of them is an office and neither of them is anybody’s plan.”

She turned the terminal back.

“Now go away, because it is nearly five on a Sunday and I am not on duty and I have not been on duty since Friday and I have answered a question I did not have to answer and I would like a cup of tea before the week starts.”

---

The cover note came out at about five o'clock on the Sunday evening of the fourth of May, and it is a thing that is on the papers of every sitting of that committee, and it is one paragraph, and it has been the same paragraph since 1998, and the fourth sentence of it is the one that matters.

> **The papers for this sitting comprise the itemised list at the front; the tabled papers for each item, in the order tabled; and this note. The notes of the previous sitting are circulated. Members are reminded that no paper before the Committee has the status of evidence, and that a submission is a submission. The Return of a Deferred Node, circulated with item three, is a form issued by a records supervisor on 30 April 2014 and is not before the Committee, is not adopted, is not minuted, and is not enforceable; it establishes no service, no holder, no district, and no number of nodes in this city, and it is printed on the papers solely because the covering submission at item three refers to the number of deferred nodes and no other instrument in this city is capable of supplying that number.**

Four sentences. One paragraph. Printed on the papers of the Standing Committee on Civic Continuity, in the public papers, which go to the library at the Ferry office, which are on the website at four o'clock on Friday, and which will be in the hands of forty-one people in a gallery on Monday morning at ten o'clock.

Nina Calder read it standing up in the Old Ward porch on the Sunday evening of the fourth of May with a cup of tea in her hand and gave it to him, and then took it back, and then gave it to him again.

“The clerk wrote that on Friday afternoon,” she said, “after the second print, and she rang me at about four o’clock and read it to me over the telephone because she wanted to know whether I minded. And I said no, and she said *good, because it is true, and it is the only true sentence in a document that otherwise asks a committee to adopt a price list* — and then she said, *Miss Calder, I have been the clerk of that committee for twenty-two years and I have written that note four hundred and ten times and I have never once had anybody phone me about it, and I would like you to understand that I wrote it this time on purpose, and that the reason I wrote it is that I have read your form, and I have been a records clerk and then a committee clerk for twenty-two years, and I know what a form with no adoption behind it is worth in a room full of men who have brought folders.*”

“Will she read it out.”

“She reads the cover note first. It is the first thing that happens.” Nina Calder drank her tea. “She will read those four sentences, and she will read the fourth one slowly, because she reads everything slowly, and there will be a member of that committee who will be grateful to her and a man from CivicCore who will not, and in about four minutes somebody will ask whether the return is going to be made a proper instrument, and somebody else will say that the answer to that is that the form is a mirror and a mirror is not a register, and by then it is the fourth sentence of the cover note and it is in the paper and it is in the minute and it cannot be got out.”

She put the cup on the ledge of the glass canopy.

“That is the whole of what the city puts into the record on Monday,” she said. “A registrar’s record from the eighteenth of April, a form with a hole in it, and a sentence in a paragraph that a woman of about sixty wrote on Friday afternoon because she did not trust anybody else to write it. And a sentence is not a thing that wins.”

“No,” said Jonas Mercer. “It is not.”

“It is a thing that stops, for about a year, somebody in that room from being able to say the city does not know,” said Nina Calder. “I have watched men in rooms for nineteen years. Knowing and not knowing are the only two things in this city that anybody can actually be frightened of, and I have spent a month building a thing that says the city knows, and I have known since about ten past ten this morning that a woman with a stapler is about to take half of it away from me in four sentences, in public, for free, without being asked, and I have decided that I am not going to be the person who is angry about that.”
