# Chapter 0066 — Nine Hundred Pounds

The message on Thursday the eighth of May was on a piece of A5 in the middle of the handover book with the word **JONAS** on the top of it in capitals, and it said: *corridor. ten past four. Not the clinic. Come on foot, I have got twenty minutes and one of them is yours.*

He read it standing up and then did something he had not done since the second of April, which was nothing at all with the rest of the day.

He had no reason to be there. That was the new thing and it took him about an hour to work out what it was, standing in the yard at the shop with a spanner in his hand that he did not need, and then sitting on the crate. For five weeks he had gone into every room in this city with a thing he wanted, and in nine days he had been refused twice and had been told to stay out of a third, and the two he had got right had both cost somebody else an afternoon.

On Thursday the eighth of May he had nothing. He had a card in his coat with eleven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds on it in a woman's handwriting, and he had written on it on Saturday *do not spend it before the nineteenth*, and it was the eighth, and there was nothing else on any list he was carrying, and so he spent the day in a yard doing a filing job that does not need doing and reading a service agreement he has read four times, and at four o'clock he walked to Saint Orra on foot because it is two miles and because a woman had written *come on foot* and he understood that it meant she did not want to see a car outside a hospital at ten past four in the afternoon for reasons she had not written down.

---

The corridor at Saint Orra west at ten past four on the eighth of May had about sixty people in it and she was standing at the window end with two coffees, and she was in her own clothes again.

“Say it,” said Dr. Sanaa Iqbal. “You have been walking for two miles with a question and you have had two miles to arrange your face and it has not worked, so say the question you did not arrange, and then I will tell you what I did not write on the paper.”

“All right. What did you want.”

“I wanted you to be a person I could say a piece of money to,” said Sanaa Iqbal. “That is the whole message. That is what twenty minutes is for and it is not about the clinic and it is not about Priya and I am not going to talk about Priya for nine minutes and then get round to her, because in nine minutes you will be listening for her.

“Tell me what you would do with four thousand two hundred pounds.”

Jonas Mercer did not have an answer, and it took him about four seconds to establish that, and the four seconds were visible.

“Say that again.”

“You asked me what I would do with four thousand two hundred pounds and I have not got one,” he said, “and I have not got one because on Saturday the tenth of May, at ten in the morning, in Gunnar Halvorsen’s office, he is going to put four thousand two hundred pounds on one of four nodes, and I have been asked twice not to be in the room, and if I say one now I will have an opinion about Saturday and he will be able to feel it from here and I do not want him to be able to feel it from here.”

“Fine,” said Sanaa Iqbal. “Then I will tell you what I have been told and you can be angry at the right person, which is me, because I have decided not to be.”

She put one of the coffees on the sill.

“A bench steriliser in the decontamination room on level zero. Ours is four years old and it has been working since the second of April and it is working now. Its supplier’s service contract expired on the thirtieth of April, at midnight, on the same night that six lowland points went thin, which I am aware is a ridiculous sentence to say out loud in a corridor.

“The contract is **eleven hundred pounds** for the year. The trust will not pay it out of capital because it is not capital, it is an activity, and it is due in the activity budget by five o’clock tomorrow afternoon, and if it is not in the budget by then the decontamination room goes on the compliance report in June, in public, with a paragraph number on it, and there are four hundred and twenty women in this borough whose instruments come out of that room every weekday and not one of them is going to know anything about it and every one of them is entitled to know.

“Eleven hundred pounds. **And the activity budget had nine hundred and thirteen pounds in it in a dead line item from a coffee-and-cakes appeal that shut in March 2011, and on the twenty-second of April a service manager wrote an order through site administration committing all nine hundred and thirteen of it to a part-time on-call clinician line, because it was under four thousand pounds and did not need a board.**”

He put his hand flat on the sill, on the other side of the coffee, and did not say anything for a while.

“So the money that bought my relief is the money that would pay for the thing that sterilises the instruments,” he said.

“Yes. And I have known that since Wednesday afternoon and I have sat with it since Wednesday afternoon and I have not told you because there was nothing to tell you, because I am not allowed to make you carry a thing I could carry, and because until about an hour ago I did not know which way it went.” She drank her coffee. “It went like this, and then it went like that, and I am going to tell you both and I am not going to be dramatic about either.

“It went like this: the four thousand two hundred is site budget and it is a **continuity fee** and a continuity fee buys a **node**, and a supplier’s annual service contract is not a node and cannot be put on a continuity schedule, and so the four thousand two hundred pounds cannot help. It is a pot of money that is only allowed to be spent on a schedule, and a schedule will not buy a service contract. That sentence has been sitting on me since Wednesday and I have told it to two people and both of them said *yes* and neither of them said anything else, and that is how I knew it was true, because it is the kind of thing people agree with immediately and do not repeat.

“It went like that: I have been asked — by the trust, in writing, on Wednesday — to sign a clinical statement that the decontamination room may continue in use pending a service contract. Which I can sign. I have signed eleven of them this year for other rooms. It is a completely ordinary thing for me to sign and there is nothing dishonest in it. **If I sign it, the room stays open, the four hundred and twenty women are served, and in June there is a report with a paragraph in it, and in November somebody reads the report and finds I signed it, and finds out that the money was there on the eighth of May in a dead line item and went to a second on-call clinician instead.**

“And that is the whole of the trap and it took four seconds, and the reason it is a trap is that it is not a trap. Both of those things are correct. Signing is correct. Not signing means a decontamination room with no service contract and no compliance position, on my signature either way, and I am not going to stand in a corridor and tell you that a service contract is more important than four hundred and twenty women having sterile instruments, because I do not believe it and because I did not believe it on the eighteenth of April when I signed a different version of the same kind of thing in a car park for a registrar.”

“So what did you do,” said Jonas Mercer.

“I did not sign it, and I did not refuse it, and I did not go and find a third thing, because there is no third thing and I am not a person who finds third things,” said Dr. Sanaa Iqbal. “I did the second boring thing. I telephoned the supplier and I asked for the price of not being a full annual contract, and I asked for it as a physician and not as a buyer, which means I asked what they would do for a quarterly visit and a documented verification routine in between, and it took me eleven minutes and it cost me nothing and it was the whole of my contribution to this.

“**Four hundred and ten pounds.** A quarterly service, and a logged temperature verification on the chamber every week, twenty minutes, on a machine that logs, with the log going to the governance file — and on the same afternoon she found out what else the supplier wanted, which is that the trust signs a self-certification regime instead, and that regime is signed by her, and she says it is a worse piece of paper than the one she has refused, and she signed it, and she did not tell me about it until it was signed. And I want you to hear the last part of that sentence properly, because I have said it out loud twice today and both times it sounded better than it is. *Twenty minutes a week.* On a corridor. In a building where the handover is two people at ten at night and there is a gap on the board from eleven to half past one every weekday, and where one man has refused a Thursday and asked for the refusal to be left where it is.

“So the four hundred and ten buys back a year of sterile instruments for the price of twenty minutes a week of somebody’s time, and I do not know who that somebody is, and I have not asked, because I am not allowed to ask, because every time somebody in this building asks a person about a date that person ends up on a rota because of the asking. Halvorsen told me that on Saturday and I did not believe him until Wednesday.”

She put the empty cup on the sill and squared it.

“And I am not going to be thanked for the four hundred and ten. I want to say that now, in advance, in a corridor, in daylight, because I have watched this building for five weeks and I have worked out that the thing you do with a number is take it off somebody, and if you thank me for this in June I will not be able to say no, and I need to be able to say no.”

“Noted,” said Jonas Mercer, and then stopped, because she was looking at him.

“No. Do not do that. Do not do the *noted*.” Dr. Sanaa Iqbal turned round and faced the corridor. “You are not allowed to write that down and put it in a coat. In four weeks there is going to be a review of this clinic’s own witness structure, chaired by me, because I signed the renewal clause that needs two people in a corridor at ten at night, and I am going to have to sit in that room as the doctor who was in it, and I have told you exactly what I am not going to do in that room, and one of the things I am not going to do is arrive with a piece of paper that a man off a register has been carrying since April explaining how good I am about money.”

“I was not going to.”

“You were absolutely going to,” said Sanaa Iqbal, “and I have watched you not do it four times in five weeks and I would rather tell you now than find out in June.” She picked her coat up. “That is my twenty minutes and about four of them were yours and I have not even asked you a question, which is the first time in five weeks, and I have noticed, and I have decided not to make anything of it.”

She got to about four steps away and stopped.

“**The ninth of June.** Ten in the morning. It is not the committee, it is not CivicCore, it is not a hearing, and it is a review of the clinic’s own witness structure, and I am chairing it because I signed the clause that broke for forty hours, and I am the only person in this city who can chair it and I have not asked anybody whether I can. It is in a room with nine people in it and four of them are on this roster.

“You are not on it. There is nothing on it for you, and I am telling you the date now rather than in four weeks because otherwise you will be standing in a corridor on the eighth of June asking me when it is, and I have watched you ask people things in corridors and it is one of about four things I actually like about you and I am not going to have it wasted on a date.”

She went off down the corridor towards the east site with an empty cup, and Jonas Mercer stood at the window end of a corridor with sixty people going past and did not follow her, and did not write anything down.

---

He did not go back to the shop on the Friday morning, and he did not go to the site, and he walked in at about twenty to one on the Friday afternoon of the ninth of May and stood in the doorway of the service manager's office on level two at Saint Orra west, and Gunnar Halvorsen had a telephone wedged against his shoulder and a letter on the desk with the top of it torn off, and he had three hours and ten minutes.

“Do not come in,” said Halvorsen. “Stand in the door. I have four hours and I want to spend ninety seconds of them on you and the rest of them on a piece of paper, and if I let you in you will sit down and I will lose the rest of the day.”

“All right.”

“I am going to say it in one go because I have been in a car park outside this building for ten minutes deciding whether to say it at all. I have two pots. Pot one is four thousand two hundred pounds of site budget and it is a **continuity fee** and a continuity fee buys a **node** and there are four nodes in this building in failure and I can pay for one of them. Pot two is the west site’s activity budget, which is fed by what people pay into it, and which had nine hundred and thirteen pounds in a dead 2011 coffee line until the twenty-second of April when I committed all of it to a part-time on-call clinician line.

“Eleven hundred pounds for a service contract. It is not capital, it is an activity, and it is due in pot two by five o’clock tomorrow afternoon or the decontamination room goes on a report in June. Pot two has nine hundred and thirteen pounds in it and nine hundred and thirteen of them are already spoken for, and the rest of that line is coming out of the same budget, and so **eleven hundred pounds out of pot two is the decontamination contract or the second clinician line, and it is both of them or it is neither.**

“And here is the sentence that has been sitting on me since Wednesday, and I want you to have it, and then I want you to go away. **The four thousand two hundred pounds cannot help me. A continuity fee buys a node. A supplier’s maintenance contract is not a node. I have four thousand two hundred pounds in this building that I am only allowed to spend on a price list, and the price list will not buy the thing that keeps four hundred and twenty women’s instruments clean, and I have been in post nineteen years and I did not know that until the twenty-eighth of April.**”

He put a pen down.

“So on Saturday morning at ten o’clock I am going to put four thousand two hundred pounds on one of four nodes, and none of the four of them is a decontamination room, and the decontamination room is going to be on a clinical declaration signed by a woman who is the best clinician I have ever worked with, and I am going to have to go and ask her for it, and she has already worked out that I am going to ask her, and she has told me what she is going to say, which is that she will sign it and that I should never do this again, and both of those are correct, and I have been sitting in this room since half past eight this morning trying to find the version where one of them is not, and I have three hours and ten minutes, and the letter with the top torn off is a letter to the education office and I have not sent it.”

“Will you take the riser,” said Jonas Mercer.

“That is the question, and I asked it of four people in March and you were the fifth, and I have got an answer from three of the four and it is the same answer, and I am going to tell you what it is and then I am going to ask you anyway, because I have discovered this month that being told the answer and having it asked of you are two different things.

“If I take the riser, the decontamination room has funded water for a year, and I do not have to ask a doctor to sign anything, and nobody in this building has to write anything. And a wet riser is a pipe in a building and a pipe in a building is not a service, and I have got a man in estates who has already written *this is a pipe in a building* on a sheet of A4 the size of a door, and he is right, and the day somebody asks in a room in June the riser comes off the list and the decontamination room goes back to a declaration in July with a report already on file.

“If I take the gate, then the gate is funded, and the gate is a real service with a real person’s handwriting on it, and in four years’ time there is a binder in an office saying what that gate is for, and that binder is the best argument anybody in this country has got, and I would put it in a museum. And the decontamination room goes on a declaration and on a report in June, and I have to ask a doctor to sign, and I have to live in a building where she is right about me.”

“And the steriliser.”

“The steriliser is the fourth hundred and ten pounds,” said Gunnar Halvorsen. “Which she found. Which I could not have found in eleven years, because I would have rung a supplier and been told the price of an annual contract and I would have believed it, and she asked a different question because she is a physician and physicians ask *what would you do for less* and I ask *what does it cost* and we have been in the same building for nineteen years.”

He picked the pen up and then put it down again.

“I am going to do the following and I am not asking your opinion. I am going to take the gate. And I am going to sign the decontamination contract at four hundred and ten pounds out of pot two, and I am going to write to the education office on Monday morning and tell them that the part-time on-call line is suspended from the first of June, and I am going to tell Dr Iqbal myself, in person, in this office, and I am not going to telephone her, and I am not going to send her anything in writing before I have said it to her face, because I have watched two people in this building be told things in writing this month and one of them was told about her own money in a corridor and it was me and she was extremely gracious about it and she should not have had to be.

“And I am going to write twenty minutes a week into a rota in a corridor in a building where a man has refused a Thursday and asked for the refusal to be left where it is, and I do not know whose twenty minutes it is, and I have decided that I am not going to find out by asking, and I would like to say that in writing to somebody, and the only person in this city who would understand the sentence is standing in my doorway, and he is not allowed in.”

“All right,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Then go home,” said Gunnar Halvorsen, “and I have asked you a money question and you have not answered it, and I want to say that the fourth time I have asked a man on this site a money question he has declined to answer it, and that in nineteen years I have never had that from a technician, and that on the twenty-first of April I asked you which node was worth four thousand two hundred pounds and you said no in about ninety seconds, and I have thought about that every day since including today.”

He put the telephone down.

“The four hundred and ten is signed,” he said, “and the letter to the education office is not, and it will be on my desk at nine on Monday morning, and it will say that a part-time on-call line is suspended from the first of June, and I am going to tell her in this room on Monday at nine o’clock and I am not going to telephone her tonight, and I have thought about that for about twenty minutes and I have not got a better idea.

“And the twenty minutes a week on the verification log goes into a rota in that corridor this afternoon and nobody has taken it, and I have not asked and I am not going to, and if you ever see a line on a board in that building that says *verification, decontamination, twenty minutes* with nothing against it, then that is me, and you will not tell her that either.”

He looked at the doorframe where Jonas Mercer was standing in it.

“Go home. It is the ninth of June that she has told you about, and I have not asked her what is on it and I would not, and I would like it noted that I have worked out in four seconds that a man off a register is going to walk into a room in June with a date in his pocket and a page in his coat. I have not stopped her. I have not told her. And if I had told her she would have said no to both of us and been right about both.”
