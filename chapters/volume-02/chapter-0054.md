# Chapter 0054 — The Meeting in the Community Room

The block at Wren Court in Salter's Yard is thirty-one rented rooms over a furniture workshop in Tideglass, it has no civic address, it has a named steward, and it has a meter cupboard at the bottom of the stairs with a plate on it, and Jonas Mercer had never been inside the building in his life and got into it on the Thursday evening of the twenty-fourth of April because Yolanda Fofana had told him at the yard on the sixteenth that her residents were to be told that a card existed before anything else, and had then told them, and had then sent him a card.

The card said: *There is a man coming on Thursday at eight. He is not the answer to anything. He wants to see the participation file. You may be in the room. You may leave the room. He will not be given a list of anybody and if he asks for one the answer is the address of Wren Court.*

“Thirty-one people and one workshop,” said Yolanda Fofana, at the door, at five to eight on Thursday evening. “I have had a stairwell conversation with every one of them and I have not told one of them what you are coming for, because I do not know, and I am not going to dress it up. Four of them are in the room because they want to be. Twenty-odd are upstairs and are not, and that is their business and I am not going to go and fetch them, and I want that written down in whatever you are holding.”

“Agreed.”

“Second thing, before we go in. You said in that yard on the sixteenth that I am not the answer to the Weir.”

“I did.”

“I am going to say it first this time so that it is in the room in front of people who were not in that yard.” She looked at him. “I am not the answer to the Weir. I am a private thing on a list in a building with no address, and if I ever stop being that then somebody has given me an address, and the day somebody gives me an address is the day thirty-one people in this block become thirty-one people the city can find. So I will say it in every room I go into for the next ten years and you can all get used to it.”

“That is fair.”

“It is not fair. It is a preference, and preferences are what you have got when you have not got a right, and I have not got a right.” She turned round. “Third thing. The fee does not stop. Not tonight, not in June, not because a man in a good coat stood up in a library on Wednesday. Whoever is in the room in ten years, the fee does not stop, and if it stops somebody does not get water, and the somebody is not going to be you.”

---

The file was a ring binder with a cardboard spine and it had been to every meeting in the borough for nineteen years and it was in her own hand and it was the only document in that building that anybody had ever let a stranger read.

“Nineteen of thirty-one,” said Yolanda Fofana. “I have not got thirty-one. I have never had thirty-one. I have got nineteen, and the nineteen signed on the twelfth of March in the community room opposite, at half past seven in the evening, in daylight, in front of eight other people, and there was a pen on a string, and everybody was told what the increase was, and I read the numbers out loud four times because I have watched people be handed a page in a hurry and nod.

“The increase is two pounds sixty. Eleven became thirteen sixty. That is two pounds sixty a room a week for the water and the lift and the Tuesday and Thursday sessions and the bin store, and it comes off the rent roll on the first, and four people have missed a month since April.”

“Four.”

“Four. And here is the thing I have not told them.” She turned the page round and pushed it across the table. It was a single column of thirty-one rows with a tick or a blank against each and no names, and a date at the top, and a second date, and a line between them.

**SIGNED 12 MARCH. LAPSE 12 APRIL. RENEW OR LAPSE ON 12 APRIL 2015.**

“That is the whole instrument,” said Yolanda Fofana. “Four lines. And I have signed nineteen of them and I have signed my own, and the association signed, and there is a witness line, and the witness is a person, and it is a good piece of paper, and Nell Ardent says it is badly drafted by somebody who is not a lawyer and Nell Ardent is right and I am not going to get it redrafted because I have been getting my water for nineteen years without redrafting anything.

“My own lapsed on the first of April.”

She said it flatly, the way she said everything.

“I missed a February payment on the association account — that is a different account, that is my salary, that is a mistake I made and I have not told anybody and I am telling you — and so the association took the block off the schedule on the first of April, and the block went to the last verified essential use, and there is no recorded one, because I have never written one, and the water went off on the Thursday.”

She stopped and let that sit.

“Not off. It did not go off. That is the part nobody outside this building is going to believe.” She turned the page over. “It went **thin**. For eleven days in April there was water in this building at about half what there is now, and on the fourteenth of April it came back to full, and I have not yet worked out what happened on the fourteenth, and my money says nothing happened on the fourteenth, because the money had not changed on the fourteenth.

“And I did not tell the thirty-one. I told the block meeting on the eighth that there was a mains matter. I have never used the phrase *last verified essential use* out loud in this building and I am not going to start, and I would like you to notice that I have just used it three times to a stranger in a stairwell, which is what people do when they are ashamed.”

---

“You have got a hollow term in a hospital and you have got one in a block,” said Jonas Mercer, “and they are the same shape and I have known the hospital one for about twenty hours.”

“Then tell me the hospital one.”

He told her. He told her all of it, standing in a kitchen at the bottom of thirty-one rooms, in about four minutes, and he did not soften it, and when he got to the fourth line of his pad — *I knew on Tuesday and I did not go* — he read that out too, because she had told him on the fourteenth of April to say when he got it wrong and he had not done it then and he was doing it now.

Yolanda Fofana listened to the whole of it with her arms folded and did not interrupt, and at the end she said:

“Right. Two things and then I am going to put the kettle on, because it is half past eight and I am fifty-four and I have had a day.

“One. The hospital one is a good man doing a kind thing in nine seconds and it will keep a gate open. Mine is a good woman doing an honest thing in four minutes in a room in daylight in front of eight people, and it has taken the water off thirty-one people for eleven days and me for one, and I chose to do it, and I would choose it again, and if you write down that the hospital one and mine are the same shape then you are going to have to write down that they are not the same size.

“Two.” She put her hand flat on the file. “You are going to ask me for the nineteen names, and I am going to say no, and then I am going to tell you why I would say no, and I want you to hear the reason properly, because you have not heard it properly from anybody. It is not *my residents*. If it were my residents I would give you the list, I have given landlords worse.

“It is that there is no version of this in which thirty-one people at this address are findable, and if they are findable then this is a block of thirty-one people who live in a building with no address, and there is a housing association in this borough that has been asking for exactly that list for four years for exactly its own reasons, and the day that list exists anywhere is the day the block is a data set. Not a block. A data set. And you can move out of a data set much more easily than you can move out of a staircase.”

“All right.”

“I know it is all right. You have written that sentence down six times this year, I can see you doing it.” She went and put the kettle on. “Do you want the file, then, or the shape of the file, because you are not getting the file out of this building and if you take it out of this building I will know, and the reason I would know is that Mrs Adeyinka on the second floor reads a noticeboard and she reads every noticeboard.”

“The shape.”

“Good.” She came back with two mugs. “Then the shape is this, and I have never said it out loud to anybody including the eleven people who were in here on the eighteenth of April, and I have been not saying it for four days, and you are a technician and I am going to say it to you because you cannot do anything with it.

“Nineteen people signed. Four have missed a month. That leaves fifteen who are inside the term and paying.” She looked at the column. “Eleven of the nineteen who were in this building on the night of the seventeenth signed in March, and every one of the eleven was already lapsed. So on the night of the seventeenth, when the water came over the road and the dip filled and the low block’s back doors went at ten past five in the morning, **eleven people were in this building and eleven of them were on a lapsed term and the water was at half.**

“Not off. At half. Which is the same as off, in this building, in April, and I know that and you know that and the four of them know that and I have not told them, and if I tell them tonight then they will go and look at their own date on that sheet and they will work out what it means, and one of them is seventy-four and moved in in 1995 and has a bad chest.”

She put her mug down without drinking from it.

“That is the shape of it,” said Yolanda Fofana. “I have run this block for nineteen years and I have never once had a month where the water got thin because of a date, and I have had four in six weeks, and it is not because the money ran out. It is because the money is on a calendar.”

---

At about ten past nine she took him down to the meter cupboard, which is a cupboard under the stairs with a fuse board in it and a plate on the door, and the plate had the sort of line on it that eleven plates in this city have.

> **THE HOLDER OF THIS POINT PAYS FOR IT. THE POINT IS NOT THE PAYER'S.**

“They cannot take it off you,” said Yolanda Fofana. “I have been trying. Since the first of April. I went to the association and I went to the water people and I went to a man at the council with a badge about participation and I have been told four times this month that the holder of a point is the person who pays for the point and that the point is not the payer’s, and every one of those four people was polite to me, and I want to say that they were not wrong and that I have started to think they are the most useful people I have spoken to this year, because they are all telling me the true thing, in the same words, four times.

“The money does not buy the water. The water does not care whose it is. And I pay for it. And the day I cannot pay for it, it does not stop, it goes to whatever the last thing anybody wrote down was, and nobody has written down a last thing, and so it goes to the pipes, and I get half a stop of water in a block of thirty-one people for eleven days, and I put *mains matter* on a board in a room with twenty people in it, and I have been doing that since 1995 and I have never once had to mean it.”

She shut the cupboard and turned round in about six inches of space, and said her own name once, out loud, to nobody, and then carried on, because she has been called two things by two different men in nineteen years and has never got used to the second one.

“You are not the first person in this building to get that wrong,” she said, “and I have never corrected either of them, and I have just corrected you, and you may make of that what you like and I would prefer you made nothing of it.”

“Now go home,” she said, “and do not come back here on Sunday, because if you come back here on Sunday I will have to work out what you want, and I have got enough on my plate at eleven at night on a Thursday to be thinking about a technician who wants to be let in twice in four days.”

“I will not come on Sunday.”

“You will come on Sunday,” said Yolanda Fofana. “And you may come, and you may come often, and it will not help and it will not be a rescue, and I have known four men who came often. Come if you must. Do not pretend you are doing it for the file.”
