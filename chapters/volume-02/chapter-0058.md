# Chapter 0058 — Nineteen Days

The lowland spur went at twenty to twelve on Tuesday night the twenty-ninth of April and Emeka Rusk found it from a gauge.

He telephoned the shop at eleven minutes to midnight and Jonas Mercer answered it on the second ring because he had not been in bed since ten, and Emeka Rusk said four words.

“Lowland spur is down.”

“How do you know it is the spur.”

“Because it is my gauge and because the outfall has stopped moving,” said Emeka Rusk, “and because I have been watching that gauge for twenty-six years and it does not stop for anything that is not a burst main, and there is nothing on the lowland that is a burst main, because I would have heard it.”

“Where on the lowland.”

“I do not know,” said Emeka Rusk. “That is the whole of it and I have been standing at that gauge for ten minutes working it out and I cannot tell you and I have a van and a bar and a torch and a lamp and I have got a bolt cutter and I have got no idea. Twenty-six years and I have never once been able to tell you where on that spur it is, because there is no stopcock on the lowland between Salt Row and the dip. Four hundred metres of main with no interruption. That is not a fault. That is an economy.”

Jonas Mercer was in the car at nine minutes to midnight.

---

They found it at twenty past twelve, in about forty minutes, in the dark, in a chamber off the road four hundred metres short of the dip, and it was not what either of them had expected and Emeka Rusk said so twice, out loud, to nobody, while they were in it.

A commissioning clamp. Two hundred and thirty millimetres of galvanised strap and a bolted collar, put across a joint on the eleventh of April, three weeks before the surge, to hold a repaired coupling in place while it cured.

It is a temporary thing. It goes on and it comes off. It has a works number stamped into it and a return date and the return date was the eighteenth of April.

“The works order,” said Emeka Rusk, in the chamber, with his torch in his teeth. “Order number. There.”

Jonas Mercer read it off the clamp with a torch and then off a phone photograph, because the right hand would not hold anything.

> **LOWLAND SPUR JOINT 3, PROVISIONAL COLLAR. CIVICCORE EMERGENCY WORKS, CONTRACTOR REF H. SOWERBY, ORDER 4471, 11 APRIL 2014. REMOVE ON RETURN, 18 APRIL 2014.**

“Eleventh of April,” said Jonas Mercer. “Eighteen of April. It has been on there eleven days.”

“They were there on the eighteenth,” said Emeka Rusk. “They were on the lowland on the eighteenth with a lorry and a generator and forty men because the water was coming, and somebody clamped a joint on the eleventh and somebody on the eighteenth said *we will come back for that on the way out*, and they came back for it, and they wrote the order closed on the twentieth, and the collar went round the pipe and stayed round the pipe.”

“And it has been on for eleven days and the pipe has been trying to expand eleven times a day into a steel band.”

“Twenty-two,” said Emeka Rusk. “Once up, once down. Twenty-two times a day. Into two hundred and thirty millimetres of strap. I have been doing this for twenty-six years and I have never seen one of these do it and I would not have believed it if you had told me, and I have seen a lot of pipes.”

They took it off in about fifty minutes with a bar and a lump hammer and a lot of swearing and Emeka Rusk's shoulder, and the noise when the collar came off was the noise a bell makes, and it went up the lowland for about a mile and the two of them heard it come back.

“Right,” said Emeka Rusk, at about ten past one. “Main is sound. Now the other thing.”

Jonas Mercer knew what the other thing was before Emeka Rusk said it, and he had known it in about two seconds, standing in a chamber in the dark at half past twelve, and the knowing came up out of the Wednesday morning in a mortuary yard.

A burst main is a pipe. A pipe gets repaired. Repairing a pipe is a thing a man with a bar and a torch and a bolt cutter can do, and they had done it, and the collar was in a skip at the roadside and the main was sound.

The six lowland water points are not a pipe.

“The spur is sound,” said Emeka Rusk. “And the six points are on the spur, and the spur is fine, and I could put water back into the spur at the Salt Row boundary in about four minutes, and I am not going to, and here is why, and I have been a duty engineer for eleven weeks and this is the first time I have had to say this sentence out loud to anybody at one o'clock in the morning.

“The spur is a node. Six points off it are nodes. There is a restoration route for the spur and the route has got a term on it and the term is a price, and there is nobody on the tier, and I have looked, and the tier column in this borough is empty from the eighth of April, and I do not have the authority to put a name in it at one o'clock in the morning on a Tuesday, and neither do you.”

“Who does.”

“The duty engineer for the district, and she is nineteen minutes away at this hour, and I have telephoned her, and she is answering, and she is going to say no, and I want her to say no on the telephone in front of you so that you have heard it from a person who can do it and not from me.” He held the phone out. “Mira, tell him.”

Jonas Mercer listened to a woman of about forty say no for about forty seconds, in the plainest English he has heard a professional use in five weeks, and then hang up.

“She has said she will send a written declaration at eight in the morning,” said Emeka Rusk. “And she has said that the six points get a restricted supply at eight, and that a restricted supply on a point with no recorded last verified essential use means the point serves its own maintenance, and that the six points therefore serve a pump.”

“Whose pump.”

“The one that is in the silt trap,” said Emeka Rusk.

---

They were standing in a chamber four hundred metres short of the dip at about a quarter to one in the morning, and Jonas Mercer took out the pad with his left hand and wrote two lines and could not write the third one for about a minute because of the cold and because of the hand.

> *Six points, no term in force, no last verified essential use recorded → the use is the node's maintenance → the use is the pump in the trap.*
> *So the trap cannot be cleared. By anybody. Ever. The water that would clear it is only authorised for the pump that would clear it.*

“No,” he said.

“No,” said Emeka Rusk. “And I have known that since about a quarter to one this morning and I have not told anybody because there was nobody to tell. Say it to a woman of sixty-three on a street with no address at two o'clock in the morning, Mr Mercer. Go and say it. I have got a van and I will follow you down, and then I have got to go back to the outfall and sit on a gantry until seven because there is a restriction coming off a spur at eight in the morning on the Saturday and somebody has to be standing next to it when it does, and that is four hours of my own money and I have already done the first two.”

---

There was a car in a turning place at the bottom of the embankment at about twenty to one, with its lights off and its hazards on, and a man of about thirty-eight got out of it.

“Wendell Pryce,” he said. “Continuity field, CivicCore. I have been on the embankment since half past twelve because your pump operator telephoned our duty line when the gauge went and that is the arrangement, we watch the lowland, you have got the wrench. I have not come up the road. I have been sitting in this car for fifty minutes doing nothing, which is a thing I do sometimes, and I would like to say that I was not waiting for you and I was waiting for a pressure reading, and you have happened to walk out of the dark at the same time the pressure came back.”

He had a folder. He had it open in the car with a reading light on it, and Jonas Mercer could see from ten feet away that it was four pages stapled at the corner.

“I am going to say three things and then I am going to get in the car,” said Wendell Pryce. “I have said to two other people this month that when I say these things I am doing my job, and I am doing my job.

“One. There is a power of attorney sitting in the folder. The party holding the standing continuity authorisation may place nodes on a schedule at any time. If I ring the duty engineer at one o'clock in the morning and the lowland spur and the six points go on Tier 2 at four thousand two hundred pounds a node, then in eleven minutes those six points have a restoration route and a term and a price and a holder, and the restricted supply lifts, and the pump comes off a restricted node, and by half past two o'clock in the morning there is water in that dip for the first time in three weeks and there is a person who can take a bucket down to a silt trap in the morning.

“Eleven minutes. It is eleven minutes. I have done it twice and the longest was thirteen.

“Two. It is not a favour. I want to be extremely clear about this because a man in a good coat who turns up at one o'clock in the morning and says *let me help* is doing something you can say no to, and if I let you say no to it then I have come here for nothing and I have wasted an hour of your pump operator's time and about four hundred pounds of my own.

“It is a purchase. Four thousand two hundred pounds a node, six nodes, twenty-five thousand two hundred pounds, on a schedule for a year, paid by a company, and in exchange that company holds a claim that outranks every unfunded node in this city. That is what you would be buying. You would be buying a rank. It is a real rank, it is enforceable, it works, and it is exactly what we sold the Carrow Lane blocks for in March, and I know you have been in that room, because Marguerite Oyelaran is extremely good at her job and she has been extremely rude about me to four separate people this month and none of them was you.

“Three.” He shut the folder. “I am not able to turn that into a favour. I have tried to work out how I would do it in the twenty minutes I have been sitting in this car and I cannot, and I would like to tell you why, because it is the most useful thing I am going to say tonight and I have thought about it and I have had a manager tell me not to say it.

“If I do it for nothing, then what I have done is spend about four hundred pounds of a company's money to improve the position of a district that cannot pay, on a night when that district is dark, with a man from the register standing on an embankment. And then in three weeks somebody in an office is going to ask me why I did it, and I am going to have to say: because a technician was standing there, and he had a register with his name on it, and I did not want the register to be the reason. And in six months somebody is going to work out that the fastest way to get a district bought is to be in the room at one o'clock in the morning when it is dark. And in two years there is going to be a rota of nights.”

He put his hand on the roof of the car.

“I have been doing this eleven years. I have held a lowland out for a hospital twice. I did one of those in 2011 and it is the reason I have this job. I would do it again tonight and I would do it for a man I liked more than you and I would sleep perfectly well, and I would be wrong, and it would take about two years for the wrongness of it to show up, and by then it would be a system and there would be a schedule for it and it would have my name in the first line of a policy that says *emergency discretionary continuity, section four*.”

He looked at his watch.

“It is a quarter past one. I have been in this car fifty-five minutes and I have said three things and none of them was a trick and I am going to get back in it. I am not going to wait. If you ring me at eight o'clock in the morning and the duty engineer has put the lowland on a schedule out of her own money, which she cannot, then I will put it on for nothing and I will not say a word about it and neither will you.”

He got in the car and drove off, slowly, on the embankment, with the hazards on.

Jonas Mercer stood on a lowland road at about a quarter past one in the morning on the twenty-ninth of April.

---

They drove up to the Weir because it was on the way and because Emeka Rusk said, “You have got nine minutes before you want to be at the top of that slipway and you know that you have.”

Margo Doré was awake. There is no other version of that. There is a net-loft kitchen on a street with no address and it is half past one in the morning and a man of sixty-three is awake and boiling the kettle, and she had put a lamp on the table because the bulb over the range is out and has been out since the surge, and she looked at the two of them coming up the path at half past one in the morning with a bar and a torch and no vehicle lights and she said:

“Say it.”

“Say what.”

“You have that thing you do,” said Margo Doré. “You have gone back to the car to get something. You are sixty per cent technician and forty per cent man who cannot arrive at a door with his mouth shut, and you have been standing on my path for a minute and a half not saying it, and I have known you for five weeks and you have never once arrived at a door with your mouth shut in your life. So say it, and say the whole of it, and I have got about eleven minutes before that pump man has to be back on a gantry.”

So he said it.

He said it in the order he had worked it out in a chamber at half past twelve, which is to say he said the six points, and then the restricted supply, and then the last verified essential use, and then the use being the node's own maintenance, and then the maintenance being the pump that is in the trap, and then the sentence, which was the one she had put on a step in the rain six days before and told him to remember:

**There is no term on it, so there is nobody to ask for anything.**

And Margo Doré stood at the table with the kettle in her hand and did not say anything for about eleven seconds, and then she put the kettle down.

“On Monday morning,” she said, “you said that to me on this step and I told you to say it again in a fortnight in a room where it matters.”

“Yes.”

“It is Tuesday morning,” said Margo Doré, “and I have had a week in which nothing has happened, which in this street is a good week, and I have been standing in this kitchen every night since Sunday watching a man with a lamp not come, and tonight you have come at half past one in the morning and it was not a lamp.”

She sat down.

“I want to be very careful how I say the next thing,” she said, “because I have been alive for sixty-three years and thirty-two of them on this street and I know exactly what I would like you to do, and what I would like you to do is go and find a pump and pay for it.”

“Yes.”

“I do not want you to find a pump and pay for it. I want that more than I have wanted anything since the seventeenth of March.” She put her hands flat on the table. “Because a pump is a thing and a thing can be bought, and the day this street has a pump somebody is going to ask what it is attached to, and it will be attached to the stand, and the stand will have a claim on it, and the claim will be a piece of paper with a name or a body on it, and then it is not a bucket and three people on a Monday. It is an installation. And I have watched two streets in this borough get a pump and become a data set and I have watched one of them get a pump and be *delighted*, and I have watched the delight last eleven months, and then the second pump came and the first one went on a rota, and I said no to the rota and there was nowhere for the water to go for four days in February of this year.”

She looked up.

“So I want the trap left packed and I want the bank left gone and I want this street left with nothing on it, and I want you to go away. And I want that said out loud in a kitchen with a lamp on it at half past one in the morning by a woman of sixty-three, because in about four months there is going to be a man in a good coat with a folder and he is going to be extremely good at it and he is going to offer me a pump, and he is going to be right that a pump is what a street with no water needs, and I am going to have to be the one who says no to a pump, and I would like there to be one other person in this city who has heard me say it first.”

“All right,” said Jonas Mercer. “The trap stays packed.”

“Thank you,” said Margo Doré. “Now go and do your actual night, because that man has been sitting in a car for an hour and he did not have to, and I have read the papers and I know what a power of attorney costs a company, and he has not told you that and I have. Go on. And do not come up here at two o'clock in the morning again without something that cannot wait, because you are the man with the lamp and I have said the thing about the lamp and I will say it again.”

---

They did not buy anything.

The written declaration came in at ten past eight in the morning on the Wednesday: the six points on a restricted supply, the pump authorised for the trap because the pump is a maintenance function of a node whose maintenance is a pump. The borough main came back at ten to six. The six points had a trickle from about ten past six, which is not water, and the Weir stand was the same as every other point, and the trap stayed packed.

For nine hours on the night of Tuesday the twenty-ninth into Wednesday the thirtieth of April there was no mains water on the lowland at all, and there are fourteen households at the bottom of that street with kitchens at the back, and there is a block of eleven Brine Wharf households in Cooper's Reach who were already four days into a month they were not paying for, and there is a net loft in Tideglass that had thirty-one people in it on the eighteenth and now had a hundred and nine.

Marigold Adjei is seventy-seven and has a chest and cannot manage the stairs twice a day. She was at the top of them. Aurrie Nkemdirim carried six bottles up in a crate at about ten to five in the morning, one at a time, on her own, because there is one way up and it is stairs, and she was sixty-one, and nobody asked her to, and she has not been asked about it since and will not be.

Nobody wrote it down. There is no line on any board in this city about six bottles carried up a hundred and nine steps at ten to five in the morning by a woman who is not on any rota, and that is not an oversight, and if anybody had written it down she would have asked them to take it out. Jonas Mercer knows that because of a Monday morning on a slipway, where a woman of sixty-three had made him write a refusal down in full and had it read back to her in the rain, and had told him that the day she was asked to be on a piece of paper was the day the loft was a data set.

At about ten past three in the morning on the Wednesday Emeka Rusk came off the gantry at the Carrow Lane outfall, alone, at fifty-three, and put a radio message through to a man at the outfall house, and the fault went in at ten past four with the operator box blank, because the operator box on that form is blank and he has decided that is the correct outcome and has not been argued with since the twentieth of April.

At about a quarter to eight in the morning on the Wednesday Jonas Mercer stood at a standpipe at the top of the embankment with a torch off and looked at a plate, and it printed one line, in the flat civic register, with a full stop after each word, and he read it three times.

> **THE ORDER OF TIERS IS SET BY WHAT IS PAID. IT IS NOT SET BY WHAT IS NEEDED.**

He had it in a pad before seven on the Wednesday morning, and then he sat in a car on the embankment with the pad on his knee and worked out the only thing about the last nine hours that was actually his, which was not the main and not the collar and not the nine hours, and it was this:

The restoration route for a burst pipe is a price. The price is a term. The term is on a plate, and the holder field on the plate is the name of a company, and a company is not a person and a person cannot be argued with and does not get tired and is not standing in a chamber at half past twelve in the morning.

He could mend a seam. He could mend a field, at one hinge, at one local rule, at a time, and only where somebody with local standing instructed it and a named holder took it. He had done it three times in five weeks and every one of them had been a person in a room saying no to somebody or yes to him, and both of those had been *human*, and this was a column of figures with a rank in it and there was nobody to say no to it and nobody to say yes.

He had spent five weeks learning that he could not make anybody consent.

He had never until half past seven on the morning of the thirtieth of April had to think about the possibility that the thing standing between a street and its water might be something nobody had ever said no to, because nobody had ever needed to, because it had never been in a room.
