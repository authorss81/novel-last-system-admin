# Chapter 0080 — The June Reckoning

On Sunday the eighth of June Jonas Mercer did four things and none of them was preparation.

He got up at half past six. He walked to the shop because it is forty minutes and it was already twenty-six degrees at seven. He rebolted a bracket on the shop's own riser cabinet that had worked loose in May, and he did it well, and it took eleven minutes. And then he sat on the crate in the yard with the pad in his pocket and did not take it out, for about fifty minutes, while Bea Osunde cut a length of two-inch galv pipe at the bench and did not speak to him, and then he got up and went and held one end of it while she rolled it round, which is a thing you can do and which does not require a conversation.

The temperature had been below twenty-seven for four days after the twenty-ninth of May and above it every day since Thursday the fifth. Monday the ninth was the fourth consecutive day, which put it eleven days into the run and about nine days past the return date that a laminated card in a civic office said was the fourth of August, which was the fourteenth of August, which was a date on a card in a drawer in a records office and had never been read out loud.

At about eleven he said, “Do you want to say anything about Monday.”

“I have four things,” said Bea Osunde, “and I have had them since Wednesday, and I am going to give you one and keep the rest, because you have a face on this week like a man going to a dentist.

“One. She has told you not to bring anything and you are not going to bring anything, and the reason she told you is not manners, and if you turn up on Monday morning with a sheet of paper in your hand that you have been carrying since April, then whatever she says in that room will be about the paper, and it will not be about the paper’s fault, and you will have spent the only thing you have ever been good at in this city in order to be the second man in the room holding one.

“Two is for Wednesday. Three and four are mine. Go on.”

He did not bring anything.

---

The room was on level two at Saint Orra west, off the corridor, with nine chairs round one side of a table and a whiteboard that had not been written on since 2012, and at ten o'clock on the morning of Monday the ninth of June **Dr Sanaa Iqbal** took the chair at the head of it because she had signed the renewal clause in the first week of April and she was the only person in this city who could chair a review of it and she had not asked anybody whether she could.

On the table: the five lines of the care entrance plate as they had read on the second of April, the card table log for April and May in a plastic wallet, the rota board in its acrylic with a gap in it, a red diary, and a printed agenda of one page with four items on it, one of which was **standing item: is the structure a rota, and if it is not, what is it** and had been on the page since the eighth of May.

**Ruth Nwosu.** She reads them out loud, and she has a red diary in her bag and a nineteen-year memory of a handover, and she has said no four times in five weeks to being asked anything about a date, and she is on this because she is on the roster.

**Ezra Mbeki**, twenty-six, on since the second of April, doing the two-to-ten as well as the handover, and he has read the five lines back twice at every handover since the third of April because he does not trust the first time, and he is on the roster.

**Bram Osei**, who has refused a Thursday since the first week of March, whose refusal is on the board in the corridor in his own words at his own insistence, and who came, and who is on the roster.

**Priya Ranganathan**, forty-one, part-time, on the west roster as a named on-call clinician since the second of May, two days a week until Friday the thirty-first of May, and since Friday a name on a list with no money against it, and a daughter of eleven, and who came on her own time on a Sunday after a late shift.

**Gunnar Halvorsen**, nineteen years in post, who has a budget, four nodes, a fee, a hole in a rota, twenty minutes a week with nobody against it, a decision on a Thursday, and no assistant.

**Mina Okafor**, nine years at the card table, who reads things back and writes down who put a piece of paper up and at what time.

**Yvonne Amoah**, forty-one, eleven years as the Saint Orra ambulance duty officer, and the second bearer of the west lane, and the only person in the room who has ever demanded the wording *at the time*.

**Fiona Grieve**, fifty-two, trust governance and quality, who has come because the minutes of this have to go into a file somewhere and the file has an owner, and who said at the start that she is not on the roster and has no standing to say anything and will only speak if the chair asks her, and the chair asked her once, and she said *nothing*.

And at the back, on a chair against the wall that had come from the corridor, Jonas Mercer, with nothing in his hands.

---

She did not open with a summary and she did not open with a finding. She opened by putting the acrylic rota board on the table face up and saying:

“This is the third of June. That is the board as it stood on the third of June and it has not been written on since the ninth of May, in this building, by me, in my own hand, and that is the first item and it is the only item I am going to speak to for about twenty minutes, and I want to say before I start that I am not going to defend it.

“The finding of this review, which I have already written and which is one sentence, is this. **The renewal clause has not failed because it was badly written. It has failed because a rota does not exist.**

“That is the whole of it and it took me five weeks to get to it and I am aware that I signed the clause, and I want to say the next four sentences in the order I have thought about them and I have thought about them since the fourth of April and I have got them in the right order now.

“The clause needs two people in a corridor at ten at night. It does not say which two. It cannot say which two, because it cannot name a person, because naming a person in a clause is making a person a holder, and a holder may be asked what it will do and not what it will cost, and if the clause names a person then that person is in a document forever and cannot be got out of it until somebody with a power that I do not have takes them out of it.

“So the clause depends entirely on a rota. And a rota is not a clause. A rota is a thing that four tired people make for themselves on a Friday, out of nothing, with no money, in about nine minutes, and it is the only instrument in this city that has ever actually opened a door. And in this building there has not been a rota since the twelfth of February, and everybody in this room knows that, and the reason everybody in this room knows that is that the rota is the thing that broke, and nothing else broke.

“Here is what I have learned about it since April and I have learned it the hard way and I am going to say it in a room with nine people in it. **A clause that depends on a rota is a clause that will break on the first surge. And the second time it breaks, the gap is longer. And the third time it breaks, somebody will write a schedule, and the schedule will be a good schedule.** Because it will be: it will be clear, it will be priced, it will have a term and a renewal and a party who answers, and it will cost somebody £4,200 and it will keep the door open. And the reason it will be adopted is not that anybody is wicked. It is that by the third time, everyone in this room will be so tired that a thing with a party who answers will sound like a mercy, and it will be one, and I want that written in the minute of this review in those words, in my hand, before I am asked to chair anything else.”

Nobody contradicted her.

“And then the other half, and I have saved this one because it is the one that matters and it is the one that nobody in this city will say out loud for about a year.

“**The same structure that failed is the structure that kept the door open for forty hours.**

“Not despite. Because of. I want to be exact, because I know what is coming and I would like to have said this first. Nobody could have opened that door at twenty to five on the third of April with a piece of paper. Not the department, not CivicCore, not a register of attachment, not a man off a register, not me. It opened because a night orderly of twenty-nine who was not on anybody's rota wrote four words in nine seconds in biro on the back of a laundry delivery note so that a mortuary gate would not narrow down to its own maintenance, and a registrar of sixty-one declined to strike them in ninety seconds on a telephone, for the correct reason, which was that striking them would have emptied the field and the maintenance on a mortuary gate is a man with a key.

“That is the whole of the design. Two strangers doing the correct enormous thing for no reason, and neither of them knowing the other existed, and a piece of paper between them with a laundry number on it.

“**And a witness arrangement is a promise made by people who are tired at ten at night.** That is the only kind of promise anybody in this city has ever been able to make. I have been on the other side of it — I have signed a clinical declaration in a car park for a registrar, and I have put my name in a fallback clause in a plate knowing that one day it will ring me in a resuscitation room, and I have done both of those things and I would do them again.

“So if anybody in this room, or in this city, or in a committee in six months, is going to propose replacing this with something that is not made by tired people at ten at night, then they need to read April first, and they need to read it properly, and not the finding, and not the recommendation. The forty hours. That is what it was for. It was not for anybody in this room. It was for a woman of eighty-four and a man of eighty-one and about forty other people who arrived in a building that had been told in writing by a price list that it was no longer connected to anything.

“And I will not be doing the second half of my job if I stop there, so here is the second half, and it is the part that is going to be uncomfortable, and it is about me.”

---

She put her hands flat on the table, and the right hand was the one with the seam she had never asked him about and never mentioned.

“I have found £410 in this building twice. I found it in the first week of May and it paid for a steriliser and it came out of a dead appeal from 2011. And I found nothing else in six weeks. And the reason I found nothing is in the last line of item two on the agenda and it has been on that agenda since the eighth of May and nobody has brought it to the table until this morning because everybody in this building has been extremely careful not to ask anybody about a date.

“There is a steriliser on level zero. Its service contract is now a quarterly visit and a logged weekly verification, twenty minutes a week, and the log goes to the governance file, and the supplier's regime is signed by me, and the twenty minutes a week is on a board in that corridor in Gunnar's handwriting, and there has been no name against it since the ninth of May, and there is no name against it this morning.

“Gunnar has not asked anybody, and he told me on the ninth of May that he was not going to, and his reason is in the minute of the ninth of May if anybody wants it, and the reason is right. **I have not signed it either, and I am not going to, and I want that in the minute too, and I am going to give the reason and the reason is the finding.**

“If I put my name against twenty minutes a week, then twenty minutes a week has got a person on it, and that person is a doctor, and the next time the twenty minutes comes round in a corridor at ten at night on a Sunday I will be the one who is already in the building, and then the twenty minutes will be a rota with one name on it, and a rota with one name on it is what everybody in this room has been refusing for four months, and Bram's Thursday is a rota with one name on it and he asked for it to be left in exactly those words.

“And I could do it. I want to be clear that I could do it. I have been in that decontamination room at eleven at night and at seven in the morning and I am the only person in this city who is allowed to sign that regime, and the honest answer to the question *is the verification happening* is no, it is not, it has not happened once, and if it does not happen before the quarterly visit in July then there is a file with no signature in it and a compliance paragraph in the autumn.

“**And I am not going to do it, and I would like the room to understand that the reason I am not going to do it is the same reason this structure has no rota, and neither of us is being cowardly, and both of those refusals are the failure.**”

Yvonne Amoah said, from the table, "Chair, that is the first time in eleven years I have heard a doctor say *I could do it* and then not do it, and I want it minuted in those words, and I am not being funny."

“Minuted,” said Dr Sanaa Iqbal, and Mina Okafor wrote it down.

---

They got to item four at about twenty to eleven and Jonas Mercer had been sitting against the wall for an hour and twenty minutes with his hands on his knees.

“Mr Mercer,” said Dr Sanaa Iqbal, without looking up, “you have been sitting there for eighty minutes. You have the right to one question and I am telling you now that I am giving you it, and I am giving it to you now, before the last item, because I would like the last item to be about this structure and not about you, and because if I leave it until the end you will use it up on something you have been carrying since April.

“Ask it.”

He asked the one he had thought about on the walk in.

“Sunday the thirty-first of May, on the decontamination log. Was there a name on it.”

“No,” said Dr Sanaa Iqbal.

“Was there a name on any of them.”

“No. There has not been one signature on that log since the ninth of May, and there is no facility on it for a name, and I have looked at it, and the machine logs its own chamber temperature on a strip that goes in a drawer, and the weekly verification is a **person** looking at the strip and writing their name under it, and nobody has written a name.”

“Then what happens at the quarterly visit in July.”

“The supplier comes and asks for the log,” said Fiona Grieve, from the table, in the only three sentences she said all morning. “And if there is no log, there is a non-conformance, and a non-conformance has a paragraph number, and a paragraph number is on a public compliance report in the autumn, and four hundred and twenty women in this borough have their instruments out of that room every weekday. So the answer is that in July there will be a conversation, and somebody in it will say *the log exists*, and it will not exist, and the person who says it will not be me because I have never been in that room in my life and I am not going to learn the answer in a review.”

“Thank you,” said Jonas Mercer, and that was his one question, and he did not say anything else for the rest of the morning.

---

Item four was standing item, is the structure a rota, and if it is not, what is it, and it had been on the page since the eighth of May, and she had put it there herself.

“It is not a rota,” said Dr Sanaa Iqbal. “A rota is a list of names against days that somebody has made and somebody else has agreed to, and it has a maker and it has a date and it can be broken and somebody can be asked whether they are coming. We do not have that. We have a **practice**, and it is a real thing, and it is not nothing, and it is the only thing that has worked in this building since February, and I will not have it called a rota in a minute because the day it is called a rota somebody will be asked to sign it.

“What we have is: five lines on a plate, read aloud in a corridor at ten at night by two people who can both say when the term ends, one of whom has been doing it since the second of April. Four of those five lines have been read aloud at least four hundred times since April. I know that because the card table has a wallet on it and Mina puts everything in the wallet, and I have never once asked Mina for the number, and Mina has told me twice, unasked, because that is what she does.

“And the finding of this review is four lines and I will read them out and then somebody else will type them and then I am going to go and do a clinic.

> **THE RENEWAL CLAUSE REQUIRES TWO WITNESSES IN A CORRIDOR AT 22:00 AND CANNOT REQUIRE ANY PERSON TO BE EITHER. THE CLAUSE HAS NOT FAILED THROUGH DRAFTING. IT HAS FAILED THROUGH THE ABSENCE OF A ROTA. A PRACTICE OF UNPAID, UNSCHEDULED, VOLUNTARY WITNESSING HAS HELD A CARE ENTRANCE OPEN AND IS NOT A SERVICE AND CANNOT BE MADE INTO ONE BY ANY INSTRUMENT HELD BY THE BOROUGH, THE CONTRACTOR, OR ANY PERSON. THE THIRD TIME A SURGE BREAKS THIS, A SCHEDULE WILL BE WRITTEN, IT WILL BE A GOOD SCHEDULE, AND IT WILL BE ADOPTED.**
> **The eleven o'clock to half past one gap is published and unfilled. The refused Thursday is on the board in the words of the person who refused it. Neither is to be altered by this review.**

“Then what do you want to happen,” said Gunnar Halvorsen, “because I have got a decision on Thursday and it is about a figure and I have got a committee on the nineteenth of June and I have got about ninety thousand pounds in a room somewhere with eleven places on it and one of them has been empty since March, and I am asking a chair a question and not asking you, because the answer is yours.”

“I want nothing to happen,” said Dr Sanaa Iqbal, “and I have thought about that sentence for a week and I am going to say it anyway, and then I am going to answer it, because otherwise it is a pose and I do not do poses.

“I want this review to produce a **record of what the practice is**, in a document, with a date, that says what the five lines are and when they are read and by whom and what a person may refuse and what happens when a person refuses, and I want it to say — in its own fourth line — **that this is not a service, that it is unpaid, and that any proposal to make it a service must come back to this table.**

“I am not asking for a rota. I am asking for a piece of paper that stops the third time, because the third time is when somebody writes a schedule, and I know that is coming, and I have thought about it since the fourth of April and I have decided that I am not going to be the person who lets it arrive without saying this, and I cannot stop it and neither can anybody in this room, and **a schedule is a good schedule.** I have read all four hundred and sixty pages. Page nine is a good sentence. It is written by people who thought about it, and it is correct, and it is the sentence that has made a person never in the way, and I have built a career out of a service and I am telling nine people on a Monday morning that I would rather have the practice than the service, because the practice can be refused and the service cannot.”

Bram Osei, who had not spoken for eighty minutes, said: “Chair, I asked for my Thursday to be left exactly as it is, and it is exactly as it is, and I want to say one more thing and it is the only thing I am going to say, and it is for the minute.

“I have refused that Thursday since the first week of March because I could not do it and I could not say why, and it has been on that board for twelve weeks and about forty people have walked past it. And in December somebody is going to come and look at that board and find a refusal with no reason on it, and they are going to be right that it is a bad refusal, and it is a bad refusal, and I would like the record to say that it is a bad refusal and that it has stayed up anyway, and that the second refusal anybody puts on that board will be asked about it, and I have thought about that for twelve weeks and I have decided I would rather be the bad one on the wall than be part of a good one on a form.”

Priya Ranganathan said, "Chair, my name came off the funded line on Friday. I would like it to stay on the record of who has held it, because in about two years somebody is going to ask whether the second clinician ever existed, and she did, and she was on it for eight weeks, and she was paid out of a coffee-and-cakes appeal from 2011, and I would like that in a document while there is still one person left who remembers the appeal."

“It goes in,” said Dr Sanaa Iqbal. “Both of you. Unedited.”

---

They got up at four minutes to twelve.

Fiona Grieve asked, at the door, whether there was anything she needed to do with it, and the chair said three things: it goes on the governance file today, it is not a compliance document and must not be produced as one, and the fourth line is to be copied word for word into anything that describes this clause in any other document in this city, without amendment and without a summary.

Jonas Mercer was last out of the room, because he was at the back, and he did not say anything to anybody on the way, and he went out through a fire door into a service corridor where there was a bin and a fire blanket and a notice about a bicycle.

He stood there for about two minutes. Then he went back into the building, down the corridor to the plate, and read the five lines in daylight with about ninety people going past, and the line about the term it was last read into, and the corridor board with a gap in it from eleven o'clock to half past one and a refused Thursday with a man's reasons on it, and a plate that has carried a Tier 2 line since the tenth of May with a Sunday in October on it and no field anywhere on it for the money.

Then he went downstairs and sat on the front steps in thirty degrees with the pad in his pocket and took it out for the first time in two days, and turned to the page that has the twelfth of October on it, and did not write on it, because there is nothing to add to a page that has got the right date on it, and because the thing that is coming is not a fact about a plate.

He wrote four lines instead, at the bottom of a different page, and they are the last four lines he wrote in the first week of June, and nobody in this city read them until the summer.

> *The practice held the door for forty hours. The practice is four tired people making a rota out of nothing on a Friday, and it is not a service, and it cannot be made into one, and it is not going to last.*
> *Everything anybody in this city has agreed to do about water, about heat, about a form, about a tier, about a name, and about a door has been correct, and the district has still run out of water, and it is going to run out again, and in six weeks somebody is going to price it.*
> *So the question, in the only words I have got: can a city keep a shared service floor when urgent access is legally priced — and when a resident refuses the price, who pays for the refusal?*
> *The man in a kitchen at the top of a slipway knows the answer and has said she would rather be the bad refusal on a board than the good one on a form. There is nobody else in this city who has said anything about it, and I have not asked her, and I am not going to.*
