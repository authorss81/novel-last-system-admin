# Chapter 0071 — One Station, Four Miles Away

The notice was on a piece of A4 with a council crest on it in a typeface of about 2011, and it had gone up on the frame of the Old Ward public notice board on the morning of Monday the nineteenth of May while forty-one people were sitting in committee room two with the door shut, and it was still there on the Tuesday morning at twenty past nine with a drawing pin through the top corner and one corner curling off the board because of the sun coming off the bridge.

Jonas Mercer read it standing up, with his bag at his feet, in about ninety seconds, because he had read it on the Monday and had spent Monday afternoon and Monday evening and both halves of Sunday not reading it.

> **BOROUGH HEAT THRESHOLD PROTOCOL — ACTIVATION ON THREE CONSECUTIVE DAYS AT 27°C OR ABOVE. FROM THE DATE OF ACTIVATION, RESIDENTIAL CARE PREMISES ARE REQUIRED TO CONFIRM OCCUPANCY AND A NAMED RESPONSIBLE PERSON FOR EVERY ADDRESS IN THEIR CUSTODY. ENQUIRIES: [a number].**

Below it, in a smaller line, in a different typeface, which he had not read on the Monday:

> *The threshold is measured at the borough's designated station. The designated station is the Saltmarsh Station, Fourteen Dock Road.*

It was thirty-one degrees on the Old Ward bridge at nine o'clock that morning, which was the warmest it had been since the second of April and which nobody in the city had recorded anywhere, because there is no instrument in Nacre that will tell you what the temperature is on a street, and the recording station for the borough is four miles out on the saltmarsh where there is nothing but a fence and a hut and a man from 1974 who is dead.

He telephoned the number on the notice at ten past nine and it rang four times and a woman answered it and said the words *borough heat threshold* twice before she asked him who he was, and he gave his name, and she said, in a tone that had been used for this exact call before, that she was not able to discuss an individual premises but that she could take a general enquiry, and he said he had a general enquiry.

---

The definition of the word *residential care premise* is on the second page of the protocol, in a schedule, in a typeface of about 2011, and it took Gareth Raynor-Hale about four minutes to find because he had the folder in a plastic sleeve in his bag and had had it since Friday, and he had taken it out twice over the weekend and had not liked either time.

He gave it to Jonas Mercer across a table in the ground-floor canteen of the civic offices at half past ten on Tuesday the twentieth of May, standing up, because there were about forty people in the canteen and the heat had got into it in a way that nobody was going to acknowledge.

“Premises providing residential accommodation to persons who may require assistance with, or in respect of, their daily living,” he said. “That is it word for word. It is the same wording as the heatwave guidance from 2009 and the 2009 wording is the wording that was written after the Ravenscourt inquest, and Ravenscourt is a nursing home in Gantry Ward with thirty-one beds and the inquest sat for nine days and the recommendation was about the temperature in the building at night and not one word of it was about water and not one word of it was about who you can telephone.

“Your housing association is inside that definition and it is not inside it by accident and I want to be exact about how it got there, because the answer is that the housing services team asked public health for a list of premises in the borough likely to be in front of a vulnerable person in a heat, and public health went through the licensing register, and the licensing register carries every residential address in this borough that anybody has ever registered for anything, and a housing association with a hundred and twenty-eight rooms in four blocks is on that register because of a gas inspection in 2011.

“She is not on it because of the care. She is on it because of a boiler,” said Gareth Raynor-Hale. “I have got the email. I have not shown it to anybody because there is nobody to show it to, and because I have read it four times and every line of it is correct.”

Jonas Mercer asked the one question he had come in with.

“Where is the station.”

“The saltmarsh. Four miles and a bit. It is the only station in this borough with a continuous daily record going back to 1974, so it is the designated station, and that is in the protocol, and I have asked twice whether a second station could be named and I was told that a threshold that moves is not a threshold.” Raynor-Hale put the folder back in the sleeve. “The reading at the saltmarsh at four o'clock yesterday was twenty-eight point one. The reading at the Old Ward bridge at the same time, which I took off the top of the public box with a thermometer my own mother gave me, was thirty. **The borough is on its second day. Carrow Lane has been over twenty-seven for two days already and it will be over twenty-seven tomorrow and the day after that, and if the saltmarsh holds it across the weekend then the protocol activates on Monday morning, and the thing it activates is a form.**”

“You have taken a reading with your mother's thermometer.”

“I have taken about forty of them, since Saturday, in a car park, at four o'clock, on the bridge and in front of a betting shop on Carrow Lane, and there is nowhere to put them, and that is not a complaint, that is the finding.” He closed the bag. “There is no form for it. There is a protocol that says the heat has started and there is a thermometer in a car park that says it started three days earlier, and in September somebody is going to produce the protocol and somebody else is going to produce a number off the internet, and both of them will be right, and the people who are in the middle of the difference will not be in either document.

“And I will tell you the other half of it, because you have come in with a question and I have got a month of this. The saltmarsh has been under on eleven days in May and over on eleven and the middle days are the ones that decide this city, and the thirty degrees on that bridge on Friday was a number in a car park, and if Monday is the day the protocol fires then it will be because four miles of estuary did what four miles of estuary has done every May since 1974, and not one person in this borough will have been in a position to notice either fact.”

---

The basement printer was called Ellis Fenn, he was thirty-one, he had a print cardigan on in a basement at twenty-seven degrees in May because the extractor had been broken since the February, and he had been asked for the drawing layer three times in six weeks and on two of those occasions by a records supervisor and on the third by a man with a bag.

Jonas Mercer asked him the question he had come down with, which was whether a plate or a return or a notice of any kind in this city could say what the temperature was in a particular street, and Ellis Fenn thought about it for a long time, longer than the question deserved, and then said no, and then said the rest of it.

“No. Nothing down here will do it and nothing upstairs will do it either and it is not because they have not thought of it. **The layer will draw you a shape and a term and a date and it will tell you the claim under it, and it has never once in the history of that machine drawn what a thing stopped doing.** And that is the whole of what it does. It is a machine for drawing promises. It has no idea what is true on a Tuesday afternoon on a road with no name on it, and it is not going to acquire that idea, and the woman who prints on it has known that since April and has used it to make a form with a hole in the bottom.”

“Then what does it do with a heat.”

“It draws a shape with a date on it, if there is a term in force, and where there is no term in force it does not draw a shape at all, which is what it did with the Weir in April and has done ever since, and which nobody in this building has been able to correct and which I have been told four times not to write about in a file.” Ellis Fenn put the lid down on the toner drawer. “There is nothing in this basement that will tell you it is thirty-one degrees on the lowland. There is nothing in this city. You have to stand on it.”

---

The two rooms above the betting shop on Carrow Lane had one fan, a fan, a window that opens about four inches because the frame is out, and a telephone that runs until three, and Marguerite Oyelaran had a piece of A4 with a council crest on it lying face up on the desk in front of her where it had been since Monday morning, and she had not rung anybody.

Jonas Mercer stood in the doorway, because there was one chair and the desk and the floor, and he had been in this room twice before.

“You have had that since Monday,” he said.

“Since about ten past nine on Monday, and it was on my desk when I came in at half past seven, and it came in a plastic wallet with twenty-six other sheets and it is the only one of the twenty-six I have read twice.” She turned the sheet round and pushed it two inches towards him, which was an offer and not an invitation. “I did not ring anybody. I have had it for twenty-seven hours and I have not rung the number and I have not rung that trade desk that rang me on the second of May and told me my morning was gone, and I want you to hear the reason from me before you decide what it is.”

“All right.”

“I have been waiting eighteen days to find out whether anybody in this city would ask me a question before they asked me a question about me,” said Marguerite Oyelaran.

She said it flatly, and then she picked up a pencil and put it down again without marking anything, and looked at the board on the wall behind the door, which was the one she had written the six water points on on the twenty-eighth of April, and which had a £11,280 on it in her own hand that was now in a minute in a building four miles away with a chair's name attached to it.

“Eighteen days is from the second of May, and the second of May is the morning a man rang the borough association of housing officers and asked me to confirm the number of my stock by the afternoon of the same day, and I spent the morning on the telephone telling him that my morning was gone. And on the fifth of May a man I have never spoken to rang that same desk and asked them whether a housing association was a residential care premise under the heat protocol, and the desk did not ring me, because he did not ask them to ring me, and they did not think of it, because it was a Wednesday and they had twenty other things.

“And on the nineteenth of May a chair in the Old Ward read out a number that I wrote on a wall in this room, in this room, in front of you, on the twenty-eighth of April at about ten past eleven in the morning, and you gave it to her at ten to eight on the Monday morning, and I was in the gallery and I heard her say it, and I have not been able to work out since Monday whether I would rather you had asked me or whether I would rather you had not.

“If you had asked me, I would have said no, and I would have been right, and the number would not be in a minute and neither would the fact that I said no. If you did not ask me, the number is in a minute and there is a sentence in a document I have seen saying *I do not ask any party to place the six nodes on a schedule*, and that sentence is the only thing in this city that has ever stood between me and a piece of paper, and I did not write it and I have not decided yet whether to be glad of it.”

She picked the pencil up again.

“Now. What does it want, this thing, and who does it want it from.”

“It activates on Friday,” said Jonas Mercer. “It wants every residential care premise to confirm occupancy and a named responsible person for every address in their custody. Your four blocks are inside the definition. It is the same wording as 2009 and the 2009 wording is about a nursing home with thirty-one beds and it is on your register because of a gas inspection in 2011 and I have got the email and it is every line correct and Gareth Raynor-Hale is not going to show it to anybody because there is nobody to show it to.”

“Then I have a hundred and twenty-two people who signed a thing in January and twenty-two households in a hostel on my own road and eight in a block with no water and one hundred and twenty-eight addresses, and a form wants a number and a person.”

“It wants a number and a person.”

“Right,” said Marguerite Oyelaran. “Well, that is a form with a crest on it, and I have had a month of those, and I am not going to ring them today, and you are not going to tell me not to, and here is why I am not ringing them, and it is the last thing I am going to say about this sheet of paper today.

“In January I put a hundred and twenty-two consent marks through a hundred and twenty-two letterboxes myself, on Saturdays, because the agreement had to be in the letterboxes before the twenty-eighth and because there was no time, and every one of those marks is good and I will defend every one of them in a room. And in May I answered a circular in ten minutes and it cost me an afternoon, and on the eighth of May I signed a man's name onto a page because his block is on a photocopy, and I would do that again, and if anybody asks me in September I will tell them I did it and I will tell them why.

“**And I have not been asked one single question about anything since the second of May, and everybody in this city has been extremely careful not to ask me, and I have noticed, and it is the first nice thing anybody has done for me since March.**”

She turned the sheet back round.

“You can go and stand in that corridor of yours now, and I have got four letters and a rent book and the two o'clock is a woman about a meter who has been three times.”

He went down the stairs and out onto Carrow Lane at eleven minutes to one and the road was like the inside of a car in the sun and there were two hoses running into the gutter at the far end where somebody had been watering a yard, and he stood on the pavement for a moment with the bag at his feet and did not go and look at the meter room, and did not go up the lowland, and did not telephone anybody, and the fan was on in the room behind him and he could hear it from the stairs.
