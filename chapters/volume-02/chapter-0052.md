# Chapter 0052 — The Appeal That Everybody Expected to Fail

The Cooper's Reach panel sat in a room above a library on Carrow Lane on Wednesday the twenty-third of April from half past nine until twenty to twelve, and about sixty people were in it, and Nell Ardent had said on the telephone the night before that she was going to lose it, and Jonas Mercer had not offered to come and she had not asked him to, and he had gone anyway and sat at the back with his bag between his feet because a sentence in his own notebook from the fourteenth of April said *I have never lost anything in my life* and he had been carrying the other half of that sentence since a corridor on the nineteenth.

She won nothing.

The order made at seventeen forty on the thirtieth of March and served at seventeen fifty-two and effective at eighteen hundred on the thirtieth of March was **upheld**, on a subsubstituted ground that was narrower and worse than the ground it had been argued on, and the £2,000 continuation bond was payable within fourteen days, and the £340 deposit came back, and nobody in that room had expected any of it to go any other way, including the woman who had filed it.

Prue Calloway was fifty-eight and had chaired borough safety panels for eleven years and had the manner of somebody who has read a document twice and is going to read it a third time in front of you.

“I am going to be very brief,” she said, “because I have read this file and I have read the order and I have read your skeleton and I have read the fifty-four millimetres, which is more than the department's, and I want to say first that the department was not trying to deceive anybody and that the figure has not been challenged and that the building is unsafe. That is not in dispute. Nobody in this room is here to say the building is safe.”

Yasmin Rohan was fifty-three and had been the residents' steward at Brine Wharf for eleven years and was sitting in the third row with her hands in her lap. Joseph Anum was eighty-one and lived in flat nine and had lived in flat nine since 1991, and he had come, and he had come on a bus.

“The appellant has been asked,” said Prue Calloway, “to state in her own words what the safety position would be if the building stayed open. Ms Ardent, I am obliged to put it to you that nobody has been able to put it to me.”

“Yes,” said Nell Ardent. “Because I have not got one.”

“You have a fifty-four millimetre measurement and a monitoring pin that was removed on the ninth of February.”

“Yes. And the pin that was removed on the ninth of February is on the department's side and not mine, and if the pin had stayed in, then the position in March would have been arguable, and it is not arguable now, and I am not going to pretend to you that a measurement taken in 2013 is a plan for a building with a slab dropping into the corner the escape route discharges into.” She had a folder and she did not open it. “I have been doing this for eleven years. It is very rare that the other side's own expert does my job for me. It happened today.”

“All right,” said Prue Calloway. “Then I am going to read you the subsubstitution, and I am going to read it slowly, and I am going to explain why I have taken it, and then you are going to tell me whether you want to say anything about it or not.”

She read it.

> **The order is upheld. The panel's reasons are: (1) the structural finding is not disputed; (2) the appellant has not established a safety case for the building remaining open; (3) the panel is required to weigh the civic claim attaching to the premises, and by the determination of 22 April 2014 the premises' water and refuse services are recorded as a supply and not a service, and no civic claim attaches to the supply of a supply, and the panel therefore has before it a building with no service in it and an order that is the only civic instrument in reach.**

Nobody spoke for about four seconds.

Then somebody at the back said, quite loudly, “That is not a reason.”

“It is a reason,” said Prue Calloway. “It is a bad one and it is the only one available to me and I have written it down in those words, and I would like it minuted that I said I would like it minuted that I had written it down in those words. Section nine of the panel rules lets me record that a reason is unsatisfactory and give effect to it anyway. Nobody has ever used it. I have wanted somebody to use it for eleven years.”

And she did look up, briefly, at Jonas Mercer, at the back, and he understood that she had no idea who he was, and that if she had known she would not have done it, and that it had not been for him.

---

Afterwards, in the corridor, with a coffee in his hand that he did not drink, Jonas Mercer said the only thing he had come to say.

“The store.”

Ferdy Oduya is thirty-four and holds the second key to Lowland Refuge Store 1 and works nights at the grain terminal, and he was standing in that corridor in a work jacket and he had not been on the panel's list either.

“It is in a lock-up on Marsh Lane,” said Ferdy Oduya. “It has been in a lock-up since the second of April. There are two thousand four hundred sandbags in it and sixty foam rolls and a pump and a generator and a hundred and forty blankets and it is in a lock-up, and the key is with a contracted man on a seven-to-seven clock, and I have telephoned him four times since the seventeenth and he has been very helpful and he is not a witness and I cannot make him one.”

“You cannot get the key at two in the morning.”

“I cannot get the key at any time,” said Ferdy Oduya. “That is the point of a seven-to-seven clock. It is not that I cannot get it at two in the morning. It is that I cannot get it at eleven in the morning either, and the reason I cannot get it at eleven in the morning is that he is not there, and the reason he is not there is that he works for somebody.”

“It is a Wednesday,” said Jonas Mercer.

Ferdy Oduya looked at him.

“The cliff-side lorry is a Thursday vehicle on a Thursday rota,” said Jonas Mercer, “and today is not a Thursday, and tomorrow is, and it will be at that lock-up at about ten past eight tomorrow morning with four men, and it could have the store open and the sandbags on the embankment inside an hour and a half, and I am telling you that now instead of asking you for it, because if I ask you for it tonight you will go and get it and I will have spent it.”

“Why,” said Ferdy Oduya.

“Because it is half past eleven on Wednesday the twenty-third of April and I have been in a room for two and a half hours and I have not asked a single person for a single thing, and there is nothing in a store in a lock-up on a Wednesday that wants a lorry on a Wednesday.” He put the coffee down on the sill. “The water did that on Thursday and the lorry would not have helped on Thursday. And at about ten past eleven tonight I am going to know whether that order stands, and if it stands then tomorrow morning is the day I ask you for that lorry, and it will be the first thing I do, and I am telling you that now so that you do not have to be waiting for me to think of it.”

Ferdy Oduya looked at him for a second and then said, “Right,” and went to find a telephone, and Jonas Mercer stood in a corridor in Cooper's Reach and understood that he had just described a plan for tomorrow to a man he had met once, and that the plan had no forecast in it at all, and that it was going to be either right or not by half past six that evening.

---

The third refusal happened at twenty past eleven, in a side room with the door open, and Jonas Mercer was not in that room and heard all of it.

The CivicCore man was Wendell Pryce. He was thirty-eight, he had a lanyard and a badge that said CONTINUITY FIELD, and he had a folder, and he had come to a borough safety panel about a five-storey building in Cooper's Reach, and he had said in the room that he was not there to make an offer and was not going to make an offer, and he had then taken out a sheet of paper and put it on the table in the side room with four people in it.

It was not an offer to buy anybody. It was an offer to put **four blocks** of the Carrow Lane Housing Association on **Tier 2** continuity, at **£4,200 a year each**, and to hold the balance of the fee for **eleven weeks**, and to run the Carrow Lane Thursday clinic out of a CivicCore room for eleven weeks.

He read it out in about forty seconds and gave the date on it, which was the seventeenth of April, and which was the night of the surge, and which meant it had been written by somebody who did not know that eleven people were in a Methodist hall at six in the morning.

Yasmin Rohan said no.

It was the same sentence. It has been the same sentence in three rooms since the thirty-first of March: *the lowland declines, thank you, because in the fourth year of this we would be a client.*

And then she did something that none of the people in that room had seen her do before, and Jonas Mercer heard it through a door that was open because nobody had thought to close it, and it went into him for about a week.

“No,” said Yasmin Rohan. “And I am going to say why, because I have said it twice and both times I gave the same reason and I have learned something in the last three weeks and I would rather this were the third no with a reason in it than the fourth no without one.

“It is not a client. You are right about that. In the fourth year of this we would be a client. But that is not why. The reason is that every one of the people in this side room has spent three weeks watching a price come into a street, and I can see it, and you can see it, and I am not going to hold up my hand and pretend to you that I cannot, because I cannot. On the eighteenth of April I said no to a piece of paper in a kitchen and eleven people in that room had not heard me say it the first time and they found it strange, and I have thought about that every day since and I have decided that the strange part was mine and not theirs.

“If I take eleven weeks of clinic and a fee paid by somebody else, then in week twelve there is no clinic and there is a room with a CivicCore sign on it, and in week twelve the people who did not sign anything and did not agree to anything and did not want anything find out that the thing they were using was bought. And by then they have a habit.”

She stopped, and she was not performing, and Jonas Mercer could tell from about nine feet away through a door.

“I have run a register for eleven years,” she said. “A register is not a service. I have been the steward of a building with no civic address and I have known since the nineteenth year of my life what it is to be a private thing on a list, and I would rather be poor and know exactly what I am than be funded and be a client in the fourth year of this. That is the whole of it and I have been saying it badly for three weeks.”

Wendell Pryce stood up and put the paper back in the folder.

“That is a completely coherent position,” he said. “I am not going to argue with it and I would like to note for the file that you did not refuse the clinic. You refused the price. Those are two different things and the offer stands on the table for the clinic alone until the last of June.”

“You do not have a clinic without a fee.”

“No,” said Wendell Pryce. “I do not.”

And he left, and four people stood in a side room in a library building in Cooper's Reach and nobody said anything for a while.

---

Nell Ardent found Jonas Mercer in the corridor at twenty past twelve and had a folder open before she had said hello.

“Look at this,” she said. “Not now. Walk.”

They walked.

“Two April,” said Nell Ardent. “I have had this since the second of April. It came into the clinic on the second because somebody brought it in to be witnessed and I witness things on Tuesdays, and it is a participation agreement and it is between a housing association and a person, and it is one page, and it is very badly drafted by a person who is not a lawyer, and I read it on the second of April and I thought: that is a fee, and I know that fee, and it is not the mechanism here.

“Read me sub-clause four, lettered.”

Jonas Mercer read it.

> **4(c) A signature on this agreement is a consent mark for the purposes of the Civic Lattice and may not be withdrawn except on the same date next year, on which date the signature may be renewed or allowed to lapse.**

“That is a date,” said Jonas Mercer.

“That is a date,” said Nell Ardent, “and it is dated the second of April, and it is the *twelfth of April* in the version in this folder, and the twelfth of April was the Saturday, and on the Saturday I was asleep in a Methodist hall on Carrow Lane for the second of five nights, and that is the only reason I have got this and not their version.

“Now listen to me properly, because I have thought about it for three weeks and I have gone round it about nine times and I keep arriving back at the same place and I do not like it.

“Those people were not deceived. I want that on whatever you write down. They were not tricked and they were not coerced and there was no man in a good coat in that room. Nineteen adults read a page of numbers, in a room, in daylight, and were told what it cost, and they signed, and most of them signed because the alternative was that they did not have the lift and the water and the clinic in June.

“Four of the thirty-one have since missed a month. Their signatures have not been withdrawn. They cannot withdraw them. Not because the association will not let them — the association will let anybody out of a participation agreement on paper, in writing, in a fortnight — but because the Lattice does not know about the housing association’s paper, and what the Lattice knows is a consent mark with a date on it, and the date on it is next year, and so for about eleven months the person who cannot pay is still inside the term they signed in March.”

“Nell — a person can stop paying a bill.”

“A person can stop paying a bill,” said Nell Ardent. “A person cannot un-sign a door. And I have been doing this for eleven years, Jonas, and I have spent eleven years getting people to sign things, and I have never once been able to make one of them un-sign anything, and this morning I read a piece of paper with a date on it that says the city has finally built the un-signing button, and it is the shape of a calendar, and there is no appeal against a date.”

She shut the folder.

“I told them on the first of April that the appeal would probably fail,” she said. “I told them it would fail today. I have slept two of five nights in a hall with eleven people and I have told them nothing else about the law, because there is nothing else about the law, and now there is this, and I am going to go and find out whether the people who signed it have been told.”

“Have they?”

“No,” said Nell Ardent. “They have been told the fee went up. They have not been told what the up is.”

---

Prue Calloway found him at the top of the stairs at ten past one, on her way out, on her own, and she had a coat on and a folder under her arm and she was clearly not starting anything.

She stopped anyway.

“You were at the back,” she said. “You are not on my list and you were not on the appellant’s list and I did not ask you anything so I am not going to be surprised if you say nothing. But I am going to say one sentence and then I am going to a committee meeting, and it is not about the order and it is not about the building.

“The only route that would have won this appeal was a location of record. If that building had a location of record then it would have had a service, and a service has a claim, and a claim has to be weighed against a measurement, and I would have had to weigh them. There is nothing to weigh this morning. The supply of a supply is not a claim and I have written that in my reasons and I would like somebody in this city to look at it in a year and tell me whether I was right to write it.”

She put her hand on the rail.

“Which building has no location of record?”

“They all have one,” said Prue Calloway. “That is the entire difficulty and it is thirty years old. A building has an address. It is the *people* who have no location of record, and a panel cannot weigh a claim from a person it cannot locate, and I have not been able to work out in eleven years how to write a hearing for people who do not appear in the index of the people who can be heard.”

She went down the stairs.

Jonas Mercer stood at the top of a stairwell in a library in Cooper's Reach at ten past one on Wednesday the twenty-third of April, and thought about a green pillar on a street with no address, and a woman of sixty-three who had burned a letter in a cook-house stove in March, and a registrar's line in a car park — *the nearest person who can be reached other than a resident of an unnamed district* — and understood that the phrase he had thought was a protection had a second clause to it that nobody had ever read out loud in a room with forty-one people in it.

Then he went down to his car, because it was Wednesday afternoon and the field on the plate of a mortuary gate in Saint Orra was still empty, and because a hospital does not stop, and because somebody was going to be in that yard at four o'clock in the morning on Saturday whether anybody in this city had asked them to or not.
