# Chapter 0051 — The Field Nobody Fills In

The list of people who might know what a mortuary gate is for was four names long and it took Jonas Mercer the whole of Tuesday to get through all of them, and he got through three of them and the fourth was not a name, it was a job.

He started at seven in the morning, which was the earliest any of them was in, and the first was Halvorsen himself, who had already written to the other three at about ten past six and who said so, and who gave him the letters in a bundle, because Gunnar Halvorsen is a service manager who has been in post nineteen years and does not hide a thing he has done in a corridor from a technician he has decided to tell things in front of.

“They are all very good letters,” he said. “I wrote three of them before you got here. Do not tell anybody that.”

“What did you say to them.”

“I said that the gate is going to stop being a gate on Saturday night and that I have four thousand two hundred pounds and four things, and that I have asked each of them to tell me in writing which of the four is the one, and that I have given them until Friday morning, and that if I do not have a piece of paper by six o'clock on Friday I am going to have to write to the board and the board sits on the fourteenth.” He put the bundle down. “That last part is true and it is the part I am going to be judged on, and I would like you to understand that I have already lost that one either way and I wrote the letters anyway.”

He lost two of the three on the Tuesday afternoon and it was worth the day.

**The facilities manager** was on a lorry in Gantry Ward until Thursday and his deputy, on the telephone, at about eleven o'clock, in a stairwell with the door propped, said that the deputy could not answer it because the deputy had been the facilities manager for six weeks and had not been in the mortuary yard at night and did not know what the gate was for and would not put a sentence down that he could not answer for in June.

**The trust chief executive's office** said he would be asked on the Friday and that it would be minuted and that the minute would be a resolution of the trust board, and that a trust board does not resolve anything before nine o'clock on a Friday.

**The mortuary porter** was the one that mattered and he said no in about four seconds, and Jonas Mercer had a fair idea it was coming because the man said *I know what you want* before Jonas had finished the second sentence.

“No,” said the porter. He was sixty-one and he had been in the building thirty-four years and he had a mop and a trolley and a radio that was not on.

“I have not said what I want.”

“You want me to write down what that gate is for,” said the mortuary porter. “And I am telling you that I have not got it, and it is not because I cannot think of it, and I will tell you what I have got. I have got a gate, and I have got eleven years of nobody in that yard between ten at night and five in the morning but the two of us, and in eleven years I have been the man who opened it and it has been a van and somebody who is upset, and I could write that down, and it would be true, and in four years’ time somebody is going to read it on a Friday afternoon in a room and say: then what happened to the people who did not come in a van.”

He leaned on the trolley.

“I have not got those people,” he said. “I have not got a single thing about those people and nobody in this city has, and if I write *a van and somebody who is upset* on that plate then in four years the gate opens for a van and somebody who is upset and it shuts for everybody else and there will be a piece of paper in a binder saying that is what it is for. I have not got the other half. So no.”

“All right,” said Jonas Mercer.

“That is genuinely no, not wait-until-later no.”

“All right,” said Jonas Mercer. “Thank you. That was the right answer and it took you four seconds and it has cost the hospital a fortnight and I would like to say that out loud to you now rather than in a corridor in June.”

The porter looked at him for a second. “You are the one off the register,” he said.

“I am the one off the register.”

“Then you will not write it either.”

“No,” said Jonas Mercer, and it came out of him a great deal faster than the rest of it did, and he heard it come out fast, and that was the whole of Wednesday.

---

He had about nine hundred words left to try by the middle of the afternoon and he spent them on Bea Osunde at the bench, which he knew before he started, and which she let him finish before she answered.

“The field wants a person,” said Jonas Mercer. “Not a department, not a site. A person who stood there and knows.”

“Yes.”

“I have been on that gate from the pavement twice and I could write four words in that field that are true and nobody would be able to prove they are not.”

“No,” said Bea Osunde, “you could, and that is the problem, and I have watched you all week.”

She put the plate she was working on down flat on the bench, which is what she does with everything.

“If you write in that field, then that field has a technician in it. And in eight months somebody in a room goes down the column and finds a name in a field on a mortuary gate, and the name is a man who reads cards, and then every one of those four millimetre marks on the corner of a map is a man who reads cards, and the whole city gets very interested in the men who read cards, and it is not a conspiracy. It is an office. That is how you get an office. Not by anybody deciding to build one.”

“I have spent five weeks refusing to be the nearest pair of hands.”

“You have spent five weeks refusing to be the nearest pair of hands when somebody was standing next to you who could say no,” said Bea Osunde. “There is nobody standing next to you in that field. There is a box with a cursor in it, and if you put your hand near it you are the only person in the city who can write there, and that is not restraint, Mr Mercer, that is a vacuum with your face in it. You do not get to be modest on behalf of a mortuary gate. Somebody is going to be in that yard on Saturday night whatever is in that field, and it is going to be whoever is nearest, and it is not going to be you, and I am going to say one more thing and then I am going back to the plate.”

“All right.”

“You did not have a plan for Friday,” said Bea Osunde. “You had four names and a bag. And you have spent the whole day not having a plan, and that is the correct thing to do, and I want it written down that I said it was the correct thing to do, because I have spent five weeks telling you what you did wrong and I would like one sentence in the other direction before the end of the month.”

---

At about twenty past seven on the Wednesday morning the twenty-third of April, Jonas Mercer was in the mortuary yard with a torch in his teeth for the second time in two days, and the plate on the bollard had changed.

He got the cover off. Five of the six fields were the same as they had been on Tuesday, and the sixth had a line under it in the flat civic register that had not been there before, and it was not a fault notice and it was not a fault, and it read:

> **A DEFERRED NODE SERVES ITS LAST VERIFIED ESSENTIAL USE. IF NONE IS RECORDED, THE USE IS THE NODE'S MAINTENANCE.**

He read it standing up in the rain with a roofing slate under the cover and his forearm across it and understood the sentence in about three words, because he had been carrying the other half of it around since ten to seven on Wednesday morning.

The maintenance on a mortuary gate is a man with a key.

He had known that on Tuesday. He had written it in a pad on Tuesday. What he had not known on Tuesday was that a plate would say it, in that order, with that full stop after *maintenance*, and that the plate would say it on the Thursday morning, which meant it had been read and it had been registered, and that somewhere in a building in this city a person had put it on the board for a reason.

He put the cover back on and drove to the clinic and got there at about twenty to eight, and Mina Okafor was at the card table and Ruth Nwosu was on at eight and the corridor had eleven people in it, and there was a new line on the board in Ruth's hand from the Wednesday night renewal, and the retainer line was still struck through in Selah Mora's own hand from the eighteenth, and the box underneath still said nineteen minutes and forty minutes and *care entrance open on a live term, two holders, one witness, five lines read aloud — 18 April*, and none of it had been tidied, and none of it was going to be.

“Where have you been,” said Mina Okafor, without looking up, “and I am asking that as a person and not as a clerk, and if you say *a site* I will put your name in the book as having been somewhere.”

“Mortuary yard,” said Jonas Mercer.

Twice a day, four times, he had told himself on the drive that he would not tell her, and he had told her in about a second and a half, and he had told her because she had said *as a person and not as a clerk* and that had been a door left open on purpose by a woman who is extremely good at her job.

She put her pen down.

“The van gate,” she said. “The new line on the board at six o'clock. It is on the board and it is not a door and it is a service and it is the only thing on that board this month that is not a door, and it says a node is not disconnected, it is deferred, and the whole of that line is in the same handwriting as the rest of the board, which is a maintenance company’s, and it went up at eight o’clock this morning in a frame by the facilities department.” She picked her pen up again. “I have not written it on the public box because it is not an event. I do not think that is right. I think that is a decision I have made at a quarter to eight in the morning and I would like somebody to tell me it is wrong.”

“It is a decision,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Yes,” said Mina Okafor. “Thank you. I will have it in the box by eleven and it will say who put it up and at what time, and if it is quoted at me in June I will have said so first, which is the only protection I have ever found in this corridor and it is not a good one.”

---

There is one other thing about the Wednesday and it is the shortest paragraph Jonas Mercer wrote that week.

Halvorsen telephoned the shop at about ten past four and said three words and then put the receiver down without explaining them, and when Jonas Mercer asked what they were Halvorsen said, “You will find out at half past nine tomorrow,” and would not say another word about it. He is going because the Thursday clinic is the thing at risk and the west site has been asked to say what the hospital can absorb, and he has a folder, and he does not want to go, and he is going.

Jonas Mercer went at ten past nine.
