# Chapter 0069 — Six Points and One Number

He did it on the back of a departmental form, in the room above the shop, on the evening of Sunday the eighteenth of May, with the right hand held under his left elbow, and it took him eleven minutes and he wrote it out twice before the second one was any good.

It is one side of A4. It has a heading and four lines under the heading and then his four lines and then two more lines and then a line with his name on it and a line with the time on it. It is not a claim. It does not go in the box at the Old Ward board, and the box is four hundred yards from the room he wrote it in and closes on Friday the eighteenth of July, and a claim put in it would have established nothing, and would have established nothing *and* put eleven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds and a district nobody can name into a public document ninety days before a hearing in which a contractor is asking the city to adopt a price list.

He had been told twice, in five weeks, in different words, by two different women, that the count is the day somebody comes with a clipboard.

Nell Ardent had said it on the first of April in a corridor and had said it as a warning and he had written it down. Bea Osunde had said it in a shop on the seventh of May while holding a plate, and had said it as a description, and had said *a card with a name and a day and a time is fine, and a card with a subject on it is a list, and a hearing is the one room in this city where a list is not a list.*

So he was not going to say it.

He was going to put a number in the record before he said it out loud, and to make sure that when he said it, in a room with forty-one people in a gallery, he would be **reading a document that had existed since the twenty-eighth of April**, written in another person's hand, in another person's office, on another person's wall, and that the only thing he would be contributing was the reading.

---

> **FOR THE STANDING COMMITTEE ON CIVIC CONTINUITY, MONDAY 19 MAY 2014.**
> **Item: Schedule 4 (continuity pricing) — number of nodes in this city not on a continuity schedule.**
>
> **Source. Carrow Lane Housing Association, corridor board, Carrow Lane. Written in the hand of M. Oyelaran, chief officer, on Monday 28 April 2014 at about ten past eleven in the morning, in the presence of the undersigned.**
>
> **The figure on that board, in that hand, is as follows and is quoted exactly:**
> **SIX LOWLAND WATER POINTS. TIER 1. £1,880 PER NODE PER YEAR. £11,280 A YEAR. NOBODY HAS ASKED ME.**
>
> **Four statements by the undersigned, and I make them so that the figure cannot be read as more than it is.**
> **1. I do not ask any party to place the six nodes on a continuity schedule. I ask that the figure be in the record of this committee.**
> **2. The six nodes have no number, no location of record, no district, no holder and no recorded date of deferral. Nothing in this paper supplies any of those, and the registrar ruled on 30 April that the node may not be described further, and I accept that ruling and have not sought to work around it.**
> **3. No person, household, occupancy figure, or list is asserted here, and none may be derived from this paper. The claims box at the Old Ward board states that a claim so stated does not establish a service, does not require a name, and may not be used as evidence of occupancy, population, households or persons. The same is true of this.**
> **4. I am not a party to this committee, a member of it, or an officer of the department, and I have not asked to be. I am in the gallery. I have read the union's finding, filed 18 April, and the covering submission of 24 April, and I am not making a submission and I am not speaking to the Committee.**
>
> **MERCER, J. — civic standing, register of attachment. Sunday 18 May 2014, 9.40 p.m.**

He read it four times. He crossed out the word *lowland* on the fourth reading and wrote it back, because the chief officer had written it and he was not going to improve her.

Then he drove down to the embankment at about half past ten at night and did not go up the path.

That is the whole of it and it took nineteen minutes. He parked on the embankment where a man in a good coat had sat with his hazards on on the twenty-ninth of April, and he sat in the car with the engine off, and he looked up at a slipway in the dark with a green pillar at the top of it that he could not see and did not need to see, and he did not get out.

He had two promises from one kitchen, and both of them were made at half past one in the morning on the thirtieth of April with a lamp on the table and the bulb over the range out. The first was that he would say a sentence she had asked him for on the twenty-first of April in a room where it mattered, and he had said it in that kitchen nine days early, and she had counted the nine days out loud before she would let him sit down. The second was not to come up the path at two in the morning without something that cannot wait. It was not two in the morning and he had got out of the car on the embankment, so he had kept that one. He had also done the other thing, which was the thing she had actually been talking about, and he knew it while he was sitting there, and he did not go up and knock, and going up and knocking would have been worse.

Because the number was going into a public record in a building in the Old Ward in about ten hours, and one of the six lowland water points is the stand at the top of that slipway, and the woman who keeps that street has said in her own words that she wants the trap left packed and the bank left gone and the street left with nothing on it, because a pump is a thing and a thing can be bought, and she has said she would rather have the bucket back on the chain than a Tier 1 price on it, and the reason is not about money and he has never been told what it is about.

He was not going to put a Tier 1 price on a point that is her stand, in a public building, without asking her, and he had four hours to ask her, and he had not asked her one thing in six weeks and she had told him on the twenty-eighth of April that she wanted to be able to look at him afterwards and find out whether he was going to do anything with a number, and there was nobody left in the city to look at. He had been in this city five weeks and had learned four things from four people and the four things were: the asking is not the problem, the being is; do not argue a refusal; a person who says yes in four seconds to a thing they know is wrong is worse than a person who says no; and nobody on a street with no address can be described in any document that leaves it.

And he had spent nine of the four hours of his Sunday night writing a number into a document, in a room above a shop, two miles and eleven hundred steps away from a woman who had asked him for one thing, which was to come back in a fortnight and say a sentence in a room where it matters, and who had then told him not to come up the path at two in the morning without something that cannot wait.

She had been right about the lamp. She had been right about the trap. And on Monday morning at ten o'clock he was going to stand up in a gallery in the Old Ward and say a number that put a price on her water, and he was not going to have asked her, because the number had a date on it and the date was the twenty-eighth of April and the number was worth nothing in nine days and was worth something on the nineteenth of May, and he had told her that on the twenty-eighth of April in a room with a board on the wall, and she had said *write it down and read it back to me, and if it is in your coat it is in your coat.*

So the sentence he was going to need on the nineteenth of May was one she had asked for on the twenty-first of April, and he had said it in her kitchen at half past one on the thirtieth, and he was going to say it again on Monday in a room with forty-one people in it, and she would not be there, and he had not told her, and he sat on an embankment for nineteen minutes and then drove home.

He wrote one line on the pad on the way back and he did not cross it out.

> *Did not go up. Did not telephone. Said it again on Monday in a room and she is not in it, and she is the only person in this city who asked me for a sentence and I have never once told her I was going to use it in one. That is the third time I have kept a promise in the exact way that breaks it.*

---

Committee room two opens at half past nine on the morning of Monday the nineteenth of May, and the doors to the gallery open at ten, and Jonas Mercer was outside the room at ten past seven with a sheet of A4 in his left hand, and he had been there since a quarter past seven because he had wanted to get it into the room before the clerk arrived and then had decided that was cowardice, and had waited for the clerk.

Frances Tolley, the committee clerk, is a woman of about sixty and has been the clerk of that committee for twenty-two years, and she came in at half past seven, and she looked at Jonas Mercer standing in a corridor with a sheet of paper and said, “You are the one off the register.”

“Yes.”

“You are not on the agenda.”

“No.”

“You are not a party.”

“No.”

“You are not a departmental officer.”

“No,” said Jonas Mercer, “and I have been told all four of those in four rooms in three weeks by four different people, and I have got one sheet of paper and I have not got an envelope and I am not going to put it under the door, and I would like to give it to the chair and I would like to say why in one sentence and then I would like to go and sit in the gallery.”

Frances Tolley looked at the sheet for about three seconds without touching it.

“Give it to me and I will give it to her, and I will tell you now that she will not read it to the Committee.”

“She is welcome not to. I have not come to ask her to read it to the Committee. I have come to ask her to have it before I say anything, so that when I say it I am reading a document that was not made this morning.” He stopped. “That is the whole of it. I am sorry it has taken you two minutes.”

“It has taken me about ten seconds,” said Frances Tolley. “You are the fourth person this month who has come to a corridor with a piece of paper and asked me for the truth of a procedure, and three of them wanted something. Go and sit in the gallery. It will be full at about eleven and the front two rows go to the press list and the press list is not checked at the door, so if you want a seat, be in before ten, and take the left-hand side, because the right-hand side has a radiator in it.”

---

Fenella Marsh came in at ten to eight and Jonas Mercer said one sentence to her in the corridor outside committee room two, standing up, at a volume that two people could hear.

“Chair. I have a sheet of paper. It is a figure, and the figure has a source, and the source existed on the twenty-eighth of April, and I am not asking you to treat it as evidence, because it is not evidence and the committee will say so and it will be right. I am asking you to have it in your hand before ten o'clock so that if anybody stands up in a gallery today and says a number, that number is already in the record and it is not a man producing a list.”

Fenella Marsh took the sheet.

She read it standing in a corridor at ten to eight in the morning, and it took her about fifty seconds, and she is not a fast reader and she did not hurry. She read the four lines under the heading, and then the four statements, and then the name and the time, and then she turned it over and there was nothing on the back.

“Where did the figure come from.”

“A corridor board, in a housing association’s office above a betting shop on Carrow Lane, in the hand of the chief officer, on the twenty-eighth of April at about ten past eleven in the morning. I was in the room when she wrote it. I watched her write it and she told me she was writing it so that she could look at me afterwards and find out whether I would do anything with it, and I did nothing with it for twenty-one days, and that is on the sheet if you want it and it is not on the sheet.”

“No,” said Fenella Marsh. “It should not be on the sheet. I am not going to have you writing a confession into a document that goes into a minute. It is a bad habit and you have it from a union submission.” She folded it once. “I am going to read the four sentences of the clerk’s cover note at the top of this sitting, and the fourth one says that the return establishes no number of nodes in this city. And then I am going to have a piece of paper in my hand that says a number of nodes in this city, with a source and a date and a hand on it, and the two of those are going to be in the same minute four minutes apart, and **I am not going to let that be an accident that anybody in the room gets to enjoy.**”

“What will you do with it.”

“I will read it out,” said Fenella Marsh, “in full, at the point in the sitting where the return is taken, and I will not comment on it, and it will be in the minute as a document received, and that will be the whole of it, and Mr Mercer, I want you to hear the shape of that sentence, because I have been a chair for six years and I have worked out what I actually do with a piece of paper like that.

“I do not rule on it. I do not accept it. I do not put it in front of the committee as an option, and I do not let anybody make an amendment out of it, and if a member stands up at two o’clock and says *the chair has read a figure into this room* I will say *the chair has read a document into this room, which is what the chair is for, and the document is dated the twenty-eighth of April and it names its source and it asserts no person, and the member is welcome to make a motion.* And then we will have a motion, and the motion will be about a number, and the number will be in the record whether anybody likes it or not, and the minute will not say that anybody adopted it.

“That is the whole of what a chair has got. I cannot make anybody do anything about eleven thousand pounds. I can make sure that the sentence exists in a place where it can be read out.”

She went into the room. Jonas Mercer went down the corridor and got a coffee he did not want and sat on the left-hand side of the gallery at about half past nine and watched forty-one people find forty-one seats, and at about ten to eleven the doors were shut and there were about thirty people standing along the rail, and the caretaker came round with two chairs out of a stack by the door and gave them to two of them and did not give one to the third, and nobody argued with him about it.

At ten o'clock the chair said, “Item two,” and the clerk read out the four sentences of the cover note, slowly, in a room with a gallery, and on the fourth sentence a man from CivicCore leaned forward about two inches.

And at about eleven minutes past eleven, with the gallery listening, Fenella Marsh said:

“There is one further document received by the chair, dated the twenty-eighth of April, which I am going to read in full and on which I am going to comment on nothing, and which will go into the minute as a document received and not as evidence of anything.”

She read it in full. Six lowland water points. Tier 1. One thousand eight hundred and eighty pounds per node per year. Eleven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds a year. Nobody has asked me.

Then she said, “Thank you,” and went on to the next thing, and the clerk typed it, and that was the whole of it.
