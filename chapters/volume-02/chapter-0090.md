# Chapter 0090 — The Floor

**Monday the fourteenth of July 2014, eleven o'clock in the morning, the corridor at Saint Orra west, level two.**

The board is in the corridor opposite the card table and it is the only thing in this building that nobody has improved, and the state of it on the fourteenth of July, in the fourteenth day of the hottest month anybody in this borough can remember, is this, and it is the state of it.

A weekly grid, five days, four rows, made out of a magnetic strip and about forty magnets, and the Thursday row has **a gap in it from 11:00 to 13:30 with no name against it, and it has had no name against it since the second of April**, and under the gap somebody has written *gap — published* in a marker that has not run.

The Thursday row, above the gap: a printed card in Bram Osei's own words, refused since the first week of March, asked for by him twice since then that it stay exactly where it is, and which reads *cannot do Thursdays, no reason I can give, sorry* and has been walked past by about six thousand people since March. Under his card, at a different angle, a second card in Priya Ranganathan's handwriting, added on the second of June at her own insistence: *Priya Ranganathan held this line 2 May – 1 June, paid out of the 2011 coffee-and-cakes appeal. I asked for my name to stay here. It goes in the minutes unedited.* Nobody has taken it down and nobody is going to.

And on the plate by the door, the five lines, and a tier line since the tenth of May, and the terms on all of them **six days**, and the last handover read on the Sunday night at ten o'clock as it is read every night, by two people who can both say when it ends.

Jonas Mercer stood in front of the board for about four minutes and did not ask anybody anything, and then he went and looked at the decontamination verification log on level zero, through the door of the room, on the tray on the wall.

It is a printed grid of fifty-two Sundays, one per week, for the year. There is a printed box under each date for a chamber temperature and a printed box for a signature.

**There is a name against none of the fifty-two, and there has not been one since the ninth of May, and the twenty minutes a week has not happened once.** There are four books of consecutive Sundays with a tick in the temperature box and no signature, and a fifth book with neither, and the supervisor's regime is signed by a doctor on level two, and the supplier's quarterly visit is on Thursday.

**Tuesday the fifteenth of July 2014, four o'clock in the afternoon, the Old Ward board room.**

The public board is on the ground floor and the room it is in is **eleven feet by nine**, with a radiator under a window that does not open, a table, forty-one folding chairs stacked against the wall in a roped-off corner, and nothing else. It is the smallest room in the city that anybody has ever gone into on purpose, and the council's own estates office calls it *the vestibule* in a schedule, and it has a name on a door plate that says nothing.

The **register of narrowings** is pinned to the board on the left, printed on the city's own paper, in Nina Calder's format, and it is a public document and not a Lattice record, because the Lattice cannot publish a narrowing and the city can.

It has eleven entries on it, and the eleven are the eleven that anybody can check, and a register of narrowings is a public document about states and not about anybody. Six-L/16, the lowland spur and outfall, 12 May, Tier 1, held. 6-44, the mortuary van gate, 11 May, Tier 2, held. 6-41, the Saint Orra west care entrance, 11 May, Tier 2, held. **A node in a block of 1938 in Carrow Lane, no water since 6 May, the node itself still at Tier 2 at four thousand two hundred pounds a year.** A node in Tideglass, **withdrawn 4 July 2014, term ended 06:00, no replacement sought.** Two nodes in Gantry Ward and one on the old canal side, deferred, no party, no address. A transport operator's line, of which nine of its eleven are not services. And a row of six lowland water points with a column for the number of households served, and a number in the column, and nobody who has read the board this year has worked out that the number in that column is a count of who.

And at the bottom of the form, a **blank line**, with no rule and no caption and no label, which is not a design feature and has been asked about four times since May, and which Nina Calder has answered four times with the same nine words.

And on the other side of the board, a **ninety-day claims box** with a slot in the lid and a printed card on a string round its neck:

> **A CLAIM MAY BE STATED HERE FOR NINETY DAYS. A CLAIM SO STATED DOES NOT ESTABLISH A SERVICE, DOES NOT REQUIRE A NAME, AND MAY NOT BE USED AS EVIDENCE OF OCCUPANCY, POPULATION, HOUSEHOLDS OR PERSONS. THIS BOX CLOSES FRIDAY 18 JULY 2014.**

It has one card in it. It has had one card in it since the nineteenth of April and nobody has ever taken it out, and the card is about a stair, and Jonas Mercer has read it four times and is not going to print it here.

And above the box, pinned to the same board, where anybody coming up the Old Ward's ground-floor corridor has to walk under it, the **Borough Heat Threshold Protocol**: a laminated sheet with a crest on it, activated 26 May, three consecutive days at twenty-seven degrees, residential care premises to confirm occupancy and a named responsible person for every address in their custody, return by **4 August 2014**, and a room number in the civic offices.

Nobody has read it out loud to anybody. It has been in this city since the second of June. It is on a wall and it is going to be read on the fourth of August by a man with a clipboard, and there is no instrument anywhere in the borough that will record the temperature on a street, and the threshold is measured at one station on a saltmarsh four miles out.

And on the same board, since about the second of July, in the flat register, with a full stop after each word, and it is the same condition as the one on the panel in the records office in June, and it is the one condition in four hundred and sixty pages that this city has never once argued with:

> **A PARTY THAT HAS BEEN ASKED TO CONFIRM OCCUPANCY HAS BEEN ASKED FOR A STATE AND NOT FOR A LIST.**

**Wednesday the sixteenth of July 2014, ten past ten at night, the same corridor at Saint Orra west, level two.**

He had been in the building twice that day for money reasons — a bracket on the shop's riser cabinet that has needed doing since the eighth of June, and a key cut — and he was going up to level one for the key, and Ivo Bamba was coming the other way up the stairs in a work top with his sleeves already pushed, twenty-nine years old, on a late, on his way to do a thing at eleven o'clock at night that he has never once been asked to do by anybody and has never once been asked to explain.

They met on the half landing.

"Ivo."

"Mr Mercer."

"I am not going to thank you," said Jonas Mercer, "and I want to get in before you tell me to, because if I thank you it is on the record that I did, in about four weeks, and I have found out what happens to that."

Ivo Bamba stopped on the step.

"Right," he said. "Say the other thing."

"You held a mortuary gate open for nothing on the twenty-third of April with four words on the back of a laundry delivery note in nine seconds, and a registrar of sixty-one did not strike them in ninety seconds, and on the ninth of June a doctor said in a room of nine people that the same clause cost forty hours and a woman of eighty-four and was the reason the gate opened at twenty to twelve on a Wednesday night, and both of those are true and she said *not despite, because of*, and I have been carrying that sentence since the ninth of June and I have used it in a room once and I am not going to use it again in a room.

"So. Thank you. And I am saying it in a stairwell with a key in my hand and nobody listening, and I know what that is worth, and I know what it is not worth, and I am doing it anyway, because there is a version of this where nobody ever says it to you and you carry it on your own for the rest of your life, and I have met four people in this city who are carrying exactly that, and I can name two of them, and one of the two has stopped speaking to me, and I am not going to say the other one's name on a step in a stairwell."

"Do not do that," said Ivo Bamba.

"Do what."

"**Be kind to me in a stairwell.**" He said it flatly, without heat, the way a man of twenty-nine says a thing he has thought about. "That is what that is. I know exactly what that is, because I have been on nights since April and I have had about forty of them and there have been two men from the council in this building and both of them were kind to me in a corridor and I could not work out afterwards what either of them wanted, and then about a month later somebody asked me whether I had been the one who wrote the four words.

"And I want to say the thing about it and then I am going to go and do my shift, and I have thought about it for three months and I am going to say it in about nine seconds so you can decide about me now.

"**If you thank me, I am a person in a record.** That is all thanking does. Not the thank you. The *record of the thank you*, in a corridor, in somebody's memory, which becomes a thing in about four weeks. And a person in a record is a person somebody can ask. And on the third time a surge breaks what I did on the twenty-third of April — and there is going to be a third time, there is always a third time, this is the fifth month of this year and it has been a long year — somebody is going to write a **schedule**, and it is going to be a good schedule, and it is going to be clear and priced and it is going to have a term and a renewal and a party who answers, and it is going to cost somebody four thousand two hundred pounds, and it is going to keep the gate open.

"And there will be a name on it. **Not mine. I will not put my name on it. I am twenty-nine and I have got four more years of nights in me and I am not spending one of them on a rota that somebody else reads.** And the name will be a name, and it will be in a field, and the field will be a holder, and the holder will be a person, and the person will not be me, and it will be the next night orderly, or the porters, or somebody in a depot in Tideglass who has never been in this building.

"So no. Do not thank me. **I did nine seconds of work that I was not asked to do and I would do it again at a quarter past twelve on a Sunday and I do not want it to have a name on it, because the day it has a name on it somebody is going to be able to use it.** And you can tell them that from me, and I do not mind, and I will say the same thing to whoever asks."

He went up the last flight and Jonas Mercer stood on the half landing for about a minute, and then went back down.

Mina Okafor was at the card table at about eleven o'clock, and she has been at that card table for nine years, and she reads things back and writes down who put a piece of paper up and at what time, and everything anybody hands her goes into a plastic wallet, and there are about forty wallets in a drawer.

"Can I ask you a question," she said, "and it is a silly one, and it is not silly, and I have been waiting since the ninth of June and I am not going to put it in the wallet."

"Ask it."

"**Can the practice be written down.** The five lines, and the two people at ten at night. I am not asking whether it can go on a plate. I am asking whether it can go on *paper*, in a drawer, anywhere, so that if a person asks in two years what happens at this door at ten at night, there is a piece of paper that says what happens."

Jonas Mercer did not answer, and he said so.

"I am not answering it because I am not one of the nine people who was in that room," he said, "and I do not have it and I am not going to be told, and if I invent an answer I will be doing the exact thing this whole business has been about since the fourth of March."

"That is fair," said Mina Okafor, "and I did know that, and I asked you because you would give me that answer and nobody else would have." She put her pen down. "Yvonne is coming past at eleven. Ask her. She was in it."

Yvonne Amoah, forty-one, eleven years as the Saint Orra ambulance duty officer, came past at about four minutes past eleven with a card under her arm and stopped, and listened to about forty words of it, and said:

"No. You cannot write it down. And I am going to tell you why in about four sentences and then I am going to go and I am not going to be thanked for it, and if anybody thanks me I will put it in the wallet with the time on it and I will read it out at the next handover.

"**Writing it down is the beginning of it being made into a service.** That is not my opinion and it is not a preference, and it is not a way of keeping control of a room. It is this: a service has a name against it, and a name can be asked, and a rota with one name on it is what this building has been refusing since the first week of March — and Bram's Thursday is a rota with one name on it, and he asked for it to be left with no reason on it, and that is the same decision yours would be making and yours is the worse version because yours would have a reason on it.

"The record already exists. Fiona took it off the table on the ninth of June and it went on a **governance file**, and it is four lines and one of them is the fourth line and the fourth line says: **any proposal to make it a service must come back to that table.** That is a governance document. It is not a compliance document. It is not going in a folder that anybody audits, and if somebody wants to make it a service then they have to come back to that table on the ninth of June with a proposal, and there are nine of us and that is the whole of the mechanism.

"So the answer is no, and the answer has been sitting on a governance file since the ninth of June, and you have not read it because you were not in the room, and that is the correct reason and not an accident."

"Can I have a copy," said Mina Okafor.

"No," said Yvonne Amoah, "and that is the second thing you have been refused this month, and I would like you to notice that the refusal came from a rota."

Mina Okafor wrote that down and put it in a plastic wallet and wrote the time on it.

Then she said, "Yvonne. Who knows?"

"Knows what."

"That there is a third time coming." Yvonne Amoah looked at the card table and then at the board in the corridor. "**The nine of us know. That is it. That is the entire population of this building that has read the finding, and it is a governance document and it is not a compliance document, and it is not going to be produced as one, and it has not been produced as one.**

"And we have not told the people who do the practice."

"No," said Yvonne Amoah.

"**You have read a document that says a schedule is coming, and nobody in this building who is doing the practice has been told, and you have decided that not telling them is the correct thing.**"

"It is the correct thing," said Yvonne Amoah, "and it is the worst correct thing anybody in this city has done this year, and I am not going to soften it for you at eleven o'clock on a Wednesday, and you are not going to soften it for me. **Because a person who is told they are about to be scheduled either argues, and the argument is a rota, or they agree, and the agreement is a service, and there is no third thing and we have looked.** And so we sit on it, and it sits on a governance file, and on the third time somebody writes a good schedule with a party who answers and a term and a renewal, and it will cost somebody four thousand two hundred pounds, and it will be adopted at about five o'clock in the afternoon on a day when everybody is too tired to write a good objection, and that is the sentence, and the sentence is in a document, and the document is not a compliance document, and **I am one of the nine and I have just said all of that out loud in a corridor at eleven at night and I would like it noted that I said it, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and if I am thanked I will put it in the wallet.**"

She went. Mina Okafor wrote the time down.

**Friday the eighteenth of July 2014, ten to five in the afternoon, the vestibule of the Old Ward, ground floor.**

He had been in eleven feet by nine for about ten minutes, reading the register of narrowings, and he had not written anything, and at ten to five the records office closed the claims box.

The officer who closed it was a young man in the second-floor records office and he is not in this account, and he had a key and a sealed envelope, and he did the thing in about forty seconds, and he did it the way a man does a thing he has done once before. **Friday the eighteenth of July 2014, seven in the evening, the service floor at Gantry Ward.**

He went because it is there and it is the thing the volume has been arguing about since April, and Rook's chalk board is the only floor in this city that anybody has ever been able to photograph.

It is a room with a counter and a hook board and a chalk board and about six people on it at seven in the evening. **Two hundred and forty customers. Forty-one hinges on the board at a time, and nine of them not Rook's staff.** The board is chalk, and the entries are in four hands, and there is a card on the wall with the shop's telephone number on it and nothing else, and a pot of tea, and a woman called **Perdita Sow** behind the counter with a splint on her right hand and a ring number box in front of her that is empty and is not going to be filled.

"You have not been here since the twenty-eighth of April," she said.

"No."

"You did not telephone."

"No."

"That is the third time you have not telephoned me and I have got a piece of paper with the dates on it," said Perdita Sow, "which I made in a fortnight when I was angry, and I have not thrown it away, and I am not going to read it to you at seven o'clock on a Friday, and you are not going to apologise for it, because an apology is a document and I have had a good week."

Somebody at the back of the room said, "Is that the register man?"

"It is," said Perdita Sow. "It is the man who does not get things done, and the pot of tea is by the kettle and there is a cup on the hook for him if he wants it."

He took the cup.

And then he stood in front of the chalk board, which is what he came for, and it is a good board, and Rook is right about that and has been right about it since April: **a price is more honest than a queue nobody is accountable for**, and the board is the proof, and there are forty-one hinges on it with a name and an address and a time against each one, and nine of the forty-one are not CivicCore's staff and would not answer the telephone at CivicCore and are on that board because somebody who works here put them there.

And in the fourth column, at about the level of his shoulder, there was a ghost.

It was not an entry. It was the wet smear where a line had been and the board had been wiped, and the chalk had gone into the board and had not gone out of it, and it read, in a column that had held four names all evening, **TIDEGLASS 3**, and then nothing, and the erasure was recent enough to be a matter of days and old enough to have gone grey.

He stood in front of it for about a minute.

Nobody rubbed it out. That is the thing. There is no cleaner on that board and no man in that room would have wiped a line off it, and the line is not there, and the ghost of it is, and **the drawing layer has no delete operation and neither does a chalk board, and that is the only piece of good news anybody has had in this business for four months and it is nine millimetres tall.**

Rook came in at about ten past seven from a van, and looked at him, and did not come over, and said, from the door:

"You have come to look at my floor."

"Yes."

"Then look at it," said Alden Rook. "**And I want to say one thing to you about it and then I am going to do the nine o'clock handover and you are not needed for anything.**

"The board is the best thing in this borough and it is not a service, and it is not a schedule, and nobody can put a tier on it, and nobody can challenge a person on it, and **there is no instrument in this city that would let a person on that board be put in a room and told to stop, and the reason that board has never been copied in four months is not that it is expensive. It is that it could not be copied without finding somebody to answer for it, and the two people who could answer for it do not want to and the four hundred who could be asked are not allowed to be.**"

"I know," said Jonas Mercer.

"Then you know the whole of it," said Rook, "and you have known it since the nineteenth of May, and you went to a registrar on Wednesday and got a sentence about it that is the same sentence I am giving you for nothing, and I would like to know which of us said it better."

Then he did the nine o'clock handover.

At about half past nine Jonas Mercer walked out of the building on Gantry Ward and stood on the pavement, and the day had been thirty-one degrees since about eleven o'clock and it was the eighteenth of July, which is six weeks to the fourth of August and twelve weeks to a Wednesday in October, and he has not been asked for either of them.

He took the pad out. Not a delivery note this time. The pad, on a pavement, at half past nine, outside a service floor, with a cup of tea in him and a piece of paper still folded in his pocket that had not gone in a box and was never going to.

He wrote four lines on the pavement in front of a service floor at half past nine on the eighteenth of July, and only two of them are facts.

> *On a chalk board in Gantry Ward there is a ghost of a line that said TIDEGLASS 3 and it has been wiped and nobody wiped it and it is nine millimetres tall and it will be there in November. That is the whole of what anybody has got to hold on to and it is a smear on a board and I am not going to be able to build anything out of it.*
> *The two dates. The fourth of August is on a laminated sheet on a wall in the Old Ward and it is a return somebody has to make and the number that goes in that box is going to be chosen this time and it is going to be legible, and nobody has read it out loud. The eleventh of October is a Saturday night, at ten o'clock, on a stair at a hospital, and the renewal begins at seven the following morning, an hour after the money stops, and four people could read that term and all four have said no for correct reasons, and there is no rule for two of them.*
> *Neither of those dates is mine. I have not been asked for either of them. I have been asked for a number in September and I have refused it, and that is the first thing I have done in this city in five months for no reason at all.*
> *The question, and it is not going to be answered by me and it is not going to be answered by a committee: **can a city keep a shared service floor when urgent access is legally priced, and when a resident refuses the price, who pays for the refusal?** And the answer that a woman on a landing has already given, in nine seconds, in June, to a man in a stairwell, is a person, unpaid, findable, and unchallengeable, and it lasts about two years. And in September there is a quarterly report with a number in it that one woman keeps in a book in pencil, and it is not a number of people, and it is nine hundred and forty times that somebody could not get across, and it is the only figure in this business that anybody in this borough would recognise as hardship, and it is not a count of anything.*
