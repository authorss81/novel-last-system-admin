# Chapter 0073 — Four People and a Sunday

On Thursday the twenty-second of May, Jonas Mercer was at the River Stacks shop, and he was there because on the previous Saturday a shop steward of forty-four had said *do not come into the west site on the twenty-second or the twenty-ninth or any day between, and if I need you I will ask in a way that can be refused*, and because on the Tuesday of that week Tomas Renn had said the same thing in eleven words in a yard, and had not softened it the second time.

There is a particular kind of discipline in not going somewhere. It is not the same as the discipline of going. It has no moment in it. He got up at half past six because he always did, and he walked to River Stacks because it is a forty-minute walk, and he spent the day on a cabinet in the corner of the shop that had a fault in it that he had been putting off since February on the grounds that it was not urgent, and on Thursday it was thirty degrees in the yard by ten o'clock and the cabinet was in the shade and it was not going to get better.

Bea Osunde was at the long bench with a plate in a vice and a length of two-inch galv pipe in a vice next to it, and she did not look up when he came in, and at about eleven she said, without any preamble at all:

“He will have done it in a corridor and it will have taken six minutes and it will have changed nothing, and you will not find out until tomorrow, and I have nothing to do with it and I want that said out loud in this yard before the day starts.”

“Understood.”

“And you are not going to telephone him this evening.”

“No.”

“Good,” said Bea Osunde, and went back to the pipe.

The cabinet took until about two. He found the fault at twenty past twelve — a dry joint and a gland nut that had been done up by somebody in a hurry in about 2011 — and he did it properly, and the parts came out of the shop's own drawer because they were the shop's own drawer, and he wrote it in the book with the date, which he does, and read the Sunday's tide table in a drawer while he waited for the paint to dry, and the spring tides were on the twenty-eighth and the twenty-ninth of May and the first of June, and he noted it and thought about nothing else in particular for about four hours, which was the single most useful thing he did that month.

At half past four the telephone rang and it was Mina Okafor from the card table at Saint Orra west, and she did not say good afternoon, because she does not telephone people to say good afternoon.

“I was not in the corridor,” she said. “I want that first because you will ask me and I would rather you had it. I was at the card table from seven and I am at the card table now and there is a queue at my end and I have about ninety seconds so I am going to say the shape and then you are going to ask me one question and I am going to answer it and then I am going to go back to my queue.

“The shape is: four people in that corridor at about eleven o’clock this morning for about six minutes, near the plate, and one of them was not one of us, and the other three were, and I counted them because counting heads and minutes is what I do and I have been doing it since 2016.

“The one thing I know is this. At about ten past twelve Ruth Nwosu came to the card table and put a piece of A5 down on it with the word *unscheduled* on it in capitals and nothing else on it, and she said, and I am giving you her words and not mine: *if you see a man with a date on the back of his hand, put it in the box, and do not put a name on it.* And I said what date. And she said that was hers to give and not mine to ask after.”

“What did you do.”

“I put it in the box at ten past twelve with the date on it and said who put it up and at what time and I said I did not know where it came from, and I wrote *unscheduled* in capitals because that is the word Ruth used and I do not improve people. It is in the box now. It says *unscheduled*, and it is the only piece of paper in that box in five weeks that does not have a name on it, and it is the first one I have ever put up that I would not be able to answer for if somebody came down here with a lanyard and a date and asked me whose hand it was.

“That is my ninety seconds and my one fact. Ask your question and then I am going to go back to my queue, and if you ask me what they said in that corridor I am going to tell you that I was at a card table and that six minutes of four people saying nothing I could hear from the end of a corridor is not evidence of anything and I have never put anything in that box I could not hear.”

He asked her one question, which was whether Bram Osei was all right, and she said, “Bram Osei is Bram Osei, he is in that corridor every Thursday and he has been since March and I have never once seen him come off a shift and I do not know why everybody in this city is frightened of that man and I have never had the time to work it out,” and then she hung up.

---

Tomas Renn came into the shop at about twenty past eight on Friday the twenty-third and did not say good morning, and stood at the end of Bea Osunde's bench with his hands in his pockets until she said *say it then, you are standing on my offcuts*, and then he said:

“Eleven.”

“Eleven what.”

“It was eleven last night and twelve tonight, and I want the eleven, and I counted them myself, out loud, in that corridor, in front of all four of them, on purpose, and I counted them out loud on purpose because I have been thinking about it since Wednesday and it is the only thing I have got.”

He did not sit down.

“There is no rule. I asked the question with the date on it — the eleventh of October, ten o’clock at night, the handover, and if two of the four of you say no — and I asked it in those words, and I asked it properly, and all four of you gave me a correct answer, and not one of you answered the question.

“Ruth said she does not know and has never known and would not put it in writing. Ezra said that nothing has ever happened, which is the whole of the problem and he is right. Priya said nobody has ever written it down and that if it is not written down it cannot be asked, which is also right and is the sentence that has been running this city since April. And Bram said — ” and he stopped for a second, and then said it flatly, “Bram said *there is no rule, and I am not going to be one*, and then he said *and stop asking us, because every time you ask one of us about a date one of us ends up on a rota because of the asking*, and he is right about that as well, and that is not a complaint about me, that is a fact about the building.

“So: eleven Thursdays. From the sixth of March to the fifteenth of this month. That is eleven handovers at ten o’clock at night on a day when the man who used to do it has refused, and every one of them was done by a day porter, and a day porter is not on that roster, and the handover needs two named people, and the second named person on a Thursday in March and April and May has been whoever was on days.

“Eleven times. In four months. That is the number, and I have it because I counted it, and I counted it out loud in a corridor on purpose in front of four people who did not ask me to, because in April the registrar stood in a room and said there are about nine hundred of these and that nobody counts them, and he was right, and he was talking about a different thing entirely, and the reason nobody counts them is that counting one is a man standing in a corridor going through a book.

“Nine hundred terms and nobody counts them. **I counted one. One term, one Thursday, one chain, four months, eleven, and I did it in nine minutes off a shop diary and a porter’s wage book, and I have written it down, and I am going to give it to nobody, and it is the only thing I have got.**”

“You wrote it down.”

“Yes.”

“Ezra asked you not to write it down.”

“He did,” said Tomas Renn. “He asked me in the corridor, in front of the other three, and he did not ask me twice, and I have been thinking about it all night and here is what I think, and I am not going to be careful with you because you are the only person in this borough who has never once asked me for a page.

“Ezra is twenty-six. Ezra has done the two-to-ten since the second of April as well as the handover, which is a shift that ends at ten o'clock at night and a handover that starts at ten o'clock at night, and he has done that eleven times unpaid because a man who has refused is not a problem to be solved by asking somebody else, and a refusal is not a hole in a rota, it is a refusal.

“And I counted his name out loud, in front of the four people whose business it is, and in about half a second I turned that corridor into a place where a twenty-six-year-old man knows that a shop steward has him in a diary. And he asked me not to write it down, and I wrote it down anyway, in a shop book, in a yard, at twenty past eight on a Friday morning, and I have not sent it to anybody and I am not going to.

“**So there is the number, and there is the one thing the number costs, and I have got both of them in the same hand, and that is the first honest thing anybody has handed me since the eighteenth of April, and it is mine, and I would like it noted that I noticed.**”

“That is the third time you have said that sentence.”

“It is the fourth,” said Tomas Renn, “and I am going to keep saying it until somebody stops me, and you are not going to stop me, and I have got to be at the shop at ten and I am not going to be late, and I am going to tell you the last thing and then I am going to go and open up.

“Priya asked me on Friday night, on the telephone, from a kitchen, with a child in the background, whether it was possible to have a Sunday in October put in front of the board in the same box as Bram’s refused Thursday. In the same words. With her name on it and a date on it. The eleventh of October, the handover, in the same words as his Thursday.

“And I said I would ask the board and put it in front of them and that I would not put her name to it without her saying it twice.

“And Bram said she may not do that.” Tomas Renn was already at the door. “Not may I. *May not.* He said *you may not put your name next to mine, because the second one on that board is not a second refusal, it is a reason for the first one to be looked at, and in about four months somebody is going to use her to get me, and she has got a daughter of eleven.*”

“He is protecting her.”

“He is protecting the refusal,” said Tomas Renn. “Those are not the same thing and in about four years they are going to be the same thing, and that is the whole of what he has ever been on this board for, and I have never once been able to say so to him, and I have had four months.”

He went out into a yard at thirty degrees and opened the shop.
