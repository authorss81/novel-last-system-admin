# Chapter 0068 — The First Transfer That Works

The chamber off the lowland road, four hundred metres short of the dip, has a plate on the wall at about chest height, and Jonas Mercer got the cover off it at about ten past seven on the morning of Saturday the seventeenth of May with a roofing slate under the edge and his forearm across it, and Emeka Rusk held the torch.

> **6-L/16 — LOWLAND SPUR AND OUTFALL. SALT ROW BOUNDARY TO THE DIP. 480 m, NO INTERRUPTION.**

Under that, five fields, and all five of them had been filled in at some point in the last six years, and two of them had been re-inked in 2013 by somebody in a hurry.

**HOLDER.** It had a name in it once. Emeka Rusk put his head in and read it out.

“Duty engineer, district. Named. The name under it is in biro and it has gone and I am not going to guess at it and neither are you.”

**TIER.** **1.**

**DATE OF DEFERRAL.** Blank. It was not deferred. That is the first thing and it took Jonas Mercer about a minute to understand it standing in a chamber at ten past seven in the morning with a torch in his mouth.

“Tier one,” he said.

“Tier one,” said Emeka Rusk. “Somebody paid eighteen hundred and eighty pounds for this spur and put it on a schedule. Not the borough, because the borough has not got eighteen hundred and eighty pounds for a pipe on a lowland and I have asked. Not a district, because a district does not schedule its own. **Somebody with a standing continuity authorisation for this chain, and there is one of those on the lowland and he sat in a car on an embankment at one o'clock in the morning on the thirtieth of April and drove away without waiting to be told.**”

“When.”

“Tuesday. I found the tag on it on Wednesday morning when I went down to look at the collar site and I did not tell anybody, and I have been sitting on it since Wednesday, and I have been sitting on it for three reasons and the first one is that I did not believe it until I read the fee stamp.”

He put his own torch on the tier line.

“Eighteen hundred and eighty. That is a Tier one price out of Schedule four, and I know that price because you read it out of a car window at one in the morning, and it is dated the twelfth of May, which is the Monday at the end of your committee week, and it is signed with a power of attorney and not with a name, and it does not say who in the world put it there.”

“Then what does it say.”

“It says: *the party holding the standing continuity authorisation may place nodes on a schedule at any time.* And the party holding the standing continuity authorisation for this spur is the company that maintains the gauge, because the gauge is what told me at eleven minutes past midnight on the twenty-ninth of April that the lowland was going, and I telephoned their duty line because we are on that gauge because they hold the maintenance of it.” Emeka Rusk took the torch out of his mouth. “So a contractor has paid eighteen hundred and eighty pounds to keep a pipe they maintain on a schedule, and I have got no argument with it, and I would not have paid it either, and it is the best piece of administration anybody has done on this lowland in my lifetime.”

Jonas Mercer got the notice first and read it before the fields, which he had not done in five weeks and had no idea why he did it.

> **A NODE THAT IS ON A SCHEDULE AT A TIER IS NOT HELD BY THE PARTY THAT PAYS. IT IS HELD BY THE PARTY THAT ANSWERS. A PARTY THAT ANSWERS MAY BE ASKED WHAT IT WILL DO AND NOT WHAT IT WILL COST.**

He read it three times in a chamber with his forearm across a cover and then said it out loud, because Emeka Rusk was there and it is the rule about witness that Bea Osunde taught him in March.

“The money is the company’s. The holder is not the company. The holder is whoever answers.”

“Which is me,” said Emeka Rusk. “No. Which is not me. Which is the **duty engineer for the district**, and she is nineteen minutes away at one in the morning, and I telephoned her at one in the morning on the thirtieth of April and she said no in forty seconds and she has not been in this chamber since the twenty-second.”

“What is she answering for.”

“She is answering for the spur. She is not answering for me and she is not answering for the company.” He stood up straighter, which he could not do very far. “And Mr Mercer, I have been sitting on that tag since Wednesday and I have been working out the thing that is actually wrong with it, and it is not that a contractor paid for a pipe. Here it is.

“**The outfall.** That is the duty. When the supply to that spur is restricted, or when the gauge drops, somebody has to go down to the outfall at the bottom and look at the screens and the chamber, and put a fault in, and stand there while the restriction is on. That is not on a plate. That is a duty on a district, and it has existed since about 1998, and it used to be held on this node, and I know it used to be held on this node because on the thirtieth of April I was on a gantry from ten past three in the morning until about ten past four and the only reason I went is that the duty came off the plate when the term broke.”

He put his hand flat on the wall next to the plate.

“Six hours and ten minutes. The term was not in force for six hours and ten minutes. The node went into failure at twenty to twelve on the Tuesday night. And at twenty to twelve on the Tuesday night the outfall duty stopped having a holder, and it has not had one since, and **there is no such thing as a lapsed duty, Mr Mercer, there is a duty that is now being done by a man who is not on it and is not paid for it and has been doing it since the thirtieth of April and put the last fault in with the operator box blank because there is nobody to fill the operator box in.**”

“So the duty is unheld.”

“The duty is unheld. And the company has paid for the node. And the company does not answer for the duty and is not asked to. And the district answers for the duty and is not on the schedule and has not been asked. And I have been doing it, and I have got a gantry and a van and a torch, and I am fifty-one, and if I stop, then in the next restriction on that spur, nobody looks at the outfall at all, and that is not a catastrophe, that is a silted chamber, and a silted chamber is what the Weir has.”

---

They came up out of the chamber at about twenty to eight and Jonas Mercer stood on the lowland road in the sun for a minute and said, out loud, to a man of fifty-one:

“Can it be moved.”

Emeka Rusk looked at him.

“Moved where.”

“Given to somebody. A held claim, on a duty, to a person who consents to holding it. There is a thing in this city that can do that and I have never done it and I have been told four times in five weeks that I cannot do a version of it that does not need a person in the field, and this is the first one in five weeks where the field does not want a person.”

“Why does the field not want a person,” said Emeka Rusk. “Go on. Say it slowly, because you have got a face on you.”

“The party that holds a duty does not have to be on the site the duty is on,” said Jonas Mercer. “There is no address in it. There is no district. There is no household and no door and no room and no name of anybody who lives anywhere. It is an act — go and look at the screens, put a fault in, stand there while the restriction is on — and an act can be held by a man who is at a desk in Gantry Ward with a finding in his name, and the finding does not put the lowland in a book, and there is nothing on that plate that anybody can read and go *there is a street there.*”

Emeka Rusk took about six seconds.

“Pell ruled on the ninth of May that describing the water point would need a location of record, and a location of record would be a survey, and a survey is a count, and a count is the price.” He said it slowly. “And you have just worked out that an **outfall** is not a water point.”

“A duty is not a place.”

“No,” said Emeka Rusk. “It is not. And I have been on that outfall since 1998 and I have never once thought of it as anything except the thing at the end of my job, and you have just described it as a thing a man in a yard could be handed, and I am not going to pretend I did not hear it, and I am going to tell you the two things that are wrong with it.

“The first is that whoever holds it does the work or pays for somebody to do the work, and the last time somebody in this city held a duty on a lowland structure without paying for it, that was a road sweeper, and she is alive, and I am not going to use her.”
“The second is that you have to ask a man, and he has to be allowed to say no, and if he says no then you have wasted the morning and you have told a man in a register that you have been thinking about giving him a thing, and he will spend a fortnight deciding whether you are being kind or clever.”

“Yes,” said Jonas Mercer.

“So ask him,” said Emeka Rusk, “and I am not going to be in the room, and I am going to go and look at the screens at the outfall this morning because I have got a gantry and nothing has been on that spur since Tuesday and I would like to see what Tuesday did to it.”

---

He telephoned Royden Achebe at about twenty past eight on the Saturday morning and it was answered on the second ring, and the first thing Royden Achebe said was: “I have got about half an hour and I am walking to a bus stop, so say it.”

“The outfall inspection duty on six dash L slash sixteen.”

Royden Achebe did not say anything for four seconds, which from him is a paragraph.

“How do you know there is a duty and not a pipe.”

“Emeka Rusk told me at twenty to eight this morning and there is a plate in a chamber four hundred metres short of the dip and the plate has got Tier one on it, which somebody paid eighteen hundred and eighty pounds for on Monday, and the money is the company’s and the holder is not the company.”

“Of course it is not the company,” said Royden Achebe. “Say the rest of it.”

“It has not got a holder since the twenty-ninth of April at twenty to twelve at night, and there is a man doing it unpaid, and he has been doing it for seventeen days, and the last time somebody in this city held a duty on a lowland structure without being paid for it, she is in a sling with a dislocated finger and eleven weeks to go on it, and I am not saying that to put you in a room, I am saying it because it is the reason I am asking you and not somebody else.”

“Where is it going to be held.”

“In your name. In your own name, on the interim lowland post, which you took on the first of April in about four seconds because a department cannot hold a plate and a man can, and because you told me on the eighteenth of April that you would rather have had it before the post.”

Royden Achebe laughed, once, without any humour in it at all.

“Go on then,” he said. “And use the word *move* and not the word *give*, because I have been in this city for four months now and I have worked out that the word people use for a thing they want is *move*, and *give* is the word they use for a thing they are about to explain to you why you should not mind.”

“It moves,” said Jonas Mercer. “Six dash L slash sixteen, the outfall inspection duty, from a lapsed held claim to the interim lowland maintenance post, in your name. I do the moving. You hold it. The duty is: when the supply to that spur is restricted or the gauge drops, somebody goes to the outfall at the bottom, looks at the screens and the chamber, enters a fault, and stands there while the restriction is on. It is not a water point and it does not have an address in it and there is nothing in it that anybody can read and go and find a street.”

“And the maintenance on that outfall,” said Jonas Mercer, “which is not in the record I have just read you, and which is not on any document in this city. **It has not been on one since the term broke on the twenty-ninth of April, and a man has been doing it unpaid, because a gantry and a van and a torch come to less than a ticket.**”

Achebe did not say anything.

“Nobody knows,” said Jonas Mercer. “Not the company, not the district, and not me until this morning. And I have not asked him, because asking a man to inspect a chamber is asking a man to do a job, and I have been told four times in five weeks that the asking is not the problem and the being is.”

Royden Achebe was quiet for a second.

“I have a garden in Tideglass with a bench in it and a shed and a water butt and a lemon tree in a pot that I have kept alive for six years through what I would describe as neglect,” he said. “I will read it in my garden at eight o’clock tonight. Bring the papers and do not bring anybody you have to bring.”

---

The room on the second floor of the civic offices on Saturday the seventeenth of May, at about two in the afternoon, had a table, four chairs, a printer, and Nina Calder, who was on her own time again and said so before anybody asked her to.

“There is a form,” she said. “There is not a form. I want to say that first, because you have both come in here with the shape of a form in your faces and there is not one, and I have been a records supervisor for nineteen years and I have never in nineteen years recorded a thing that moved from one person to another, because in nineteen years in this city nothing has ever moved.”

“There is a registrar’s rule,” said Jonas Mercer. “Pell’s. A claim may be recorded against a person with standing where a named person with standing consents to hold it, and the record names the person who made the claim, and the claim, and the date, and the term. Six items. He used it on the eighteenth of April for six dash L slash fourteen and it is still on the file and he did not make an instrument out of it, he made an **entry**.”

“An entry,” said Nina Calder, “which I can do, and which is the whole of what I have ever been able to do. And I am not calling it a register. A register is a list, and I have spent a week in May learning exactly what a list does to a hundred and twenty-eight people in four blocks, and I am not putting one on a table in this city in the first week of June. This is an entry on a file that already exists. There will be forty of them on it in nine years and by then it will be a list, and by then I will not be here, and that is a problem for the person who comes after me and not one I am solving this afternoon.”

She typed it, and she read it back, and before she typed it she made him say the words and she wrote them down and made him initial them, which is a thing she learned from a shop steward in April and has never told anybody about.

> **CLAIM TRANSFER — series 6, lowland chain. Entry of 17 May 2014.**
> **17 May 2014. Claim: LOWLAND OUTFALL INSPECTION DUTY, 6-L/16. Basis: held claim lapsed 29 April 2014, 23:40, on the term of the parent node. Duty described as an act and not a place; no location of record created; no address, household, or person recorded.**
>
> *Nina Calder typed the words “outfall and screens, Carrow Lane” into the claim line on Friday afternoon and then took them out on Saturday morning before the reading, and she wants it in the record that she put them in first, and why: the plate in the chamber has said SALT ROW BOUNDARY TO THE DIP on it since 2011 and the agent’s letter has a reference on it, so the structure has an address of sorts; but a duty does not need one, and the first person who writes one down in a claim line is making a location of record by hand, and she is not going to be the registrar who does that in a file of her own typing.*
> **Moved by: MERCER, J. — civic standing, register of attachment, released by no person.**
> **Held by: ACHEBE, R. — interim lowland maintenance post, in his own name.**
> **Term: from the reading, to 06:00 on the last day of the fee year of the parent node, without carry-forward. Renewal by the holder only.**
> **Withdrawn by: —**
> **Claim: the holder inspects the outfall and screens after any restriction on the supply, and stands the restriction down, and enters the fault. THE CLAIM IS UNPAID AND THE PARTY THAT PAYS FOR THE SPUR DOES NOT ANSWER FOR THIS.**
> **Witness: E. RUSK, pump operator, docks, twenty-six years. Second witness: NIL.**

“That is the first record in nineteen years with *moved by* on it,” said Nina Calder, “and the last two lines are going to be read in a room by somebody who is angry, and the second one is the true one and the first one is the reason anybody will be angry, and I have put them in that order on purpose and I would like somebody to notice that I have put them in that order on purpose.”

Royden Achebe read the whole thing twice, and then asked what a held claim was, and was told that the registrar's word for a duty that once had somebody's name in a field and does not have one now, and that nobody outside the registrar's office and this room uses the phrase, and that he should get used to it because it is going to be on every piece of paper about this for the next year.

“*Moved by, Mercer, J.*” He put the page down. “You have just written my name on a duty and your name under it, and in about four months somebody is going to ask me why that outfall was not inspected on some Tuesday in August, and I am going to say *ask the man who moved it*, and you are going to get a telephone call.”

“Yes,” said Jonas Mercer.

“I want that in the terms. Not as a threat. As a **term**, in your own words, in the record, so that in August I can point at a piece of paper and not at a memory.”

“Done.”

“And the second witness is NIL,” said Royden Achebe, “and I know why, and the reason is that there is nobody, and that is not an oversight and I would like it recorded that I have read the word NIL four times.”

“It is not an oversight,” said Nina Calder. “I typed it because it is the truth and because a form with a hole in it is the only honest thing available to me, and I have had a week of finding out what happens when the hole gets printed.”

---

They read it in Royden Achebe's garden in Tideglass at about eight o'clock on the evening of Sunday the eighteenth of May, because the registrar's rule is that a transfer of a held claim is read aloud to the person receiving it, in daylight, in the place where that person keeps what they are holding, and it was still light at eight and it was the only evening in the week that anybody had.

Emeka Rusk came. He brought a torch and a length of blue string and did not bring a van, and he stood at the bottom of the garden with his hands in his pockets and read the claim out loud when it got to it, in his own words, which is a thing he is allowed to do because the duty is his by sixteen years of doing it and not by a record.

Then Royden Achebe read the six items back to Jonas Mercer, slowly, the way the plate reads a term aloud at ten at night in a corridor two miles away, and he got one of them slightly wrong, and Emeka Rusk corrected him, and he took the correction.

And then Royden Achebe said, “I am the holder. I am not going to pretend to anybody in this garden that I have not read the claim, and the claim is unpaid, and the company that pays for the spur does not answer for it, and if a restriction happens on a Tuesday in November I am going to have to find nineteen hundred pounds to get somebody to that chamber, and I have not got nineteen hundred pounds, and I do not know anybody who has, and I am telling you that now at eight o'clock on a Sunday evening in front of two witnesses, and then I am signing the box, because a duty that is not held is a duty that is not held.”

He signed the box.

They read the rule back from the record, the one Nina Calder had typed on Saturday afternoon out of a registrar’s telephone call of ninety seconds.

> **A HOLDER MAY OFFER A HELD CLAIM BACK ONCE, IN WRITING, WITHIN SEVEN DAYS OF THE READING. THE OFFER IS RECORDED. THE OFFER DOES NOT TRANSFER THE CLAIM. THE CLAIM IS RELEASED ONLY WHEN A SUCCESSOR CONSENTS TO HOLD IT. A PERSON WHO HAS NOT BEEN ASKED MAY NOT BE NAMED.**

Royden Achebe read that four times and then said, “Say that again, the last sentence.”

Jonas Mercer said it again.

“Then I have offered it back,” said Royden Achebe, “and I have not moved it, and I have until the twenty-fifth and I am not going to use a minute of it, and I want to tell you both exactly why, and then I am going to pour the water on the lemon tree, which needs it, and which I have not done since the nineteenth of April.

“The last sentence of that rule is the whole of this. The claim is released when a successor consents to hold it, and a successor has to be asked, and a person who has not been asked may not be named. **So the only door out of this is to make somebody findable, and I am not going to do that to a man I have met once at a bus stop**, and I am not going to do it to Emeka Rusk, who has been doing the job for seventeen days unpaid and who is fifty-one, and I am not going to do it to a duty engineer who is twenty minutes away at one in the morning and who said no in forty seconds in the dark on the thirtieth of April and who was right.

“I have been in this city for four months and I have been handed two things. One came off a chamber in April because a registrar had to put it somewhere and he could not put it on a street, and I took it in nine words on the telephone from a yard in Gantry Ward and I said I would rather have had it before the post. This one came off a plate on Saturday morning in a chamber and it took a man in a register and a records supervisor and a pump operator and a garden, and it took all of us about eleven hours, and I am the same man in both cases and I have exactly the same number of exits.

“So: I am the holder. I have seven days to offer it back and I have offered it back inside the first hour and I am not going to use the rest, and in four months somebody is going to ring you about a Tuesday in August and I am going to tell them to ring you, and you are going to answer, because that is the difference between a burden that arrives at you and a duty you accepted, and it is the only difference there is.”

“Which is?”

Royden Achebe shut the gate on the latch and turned round in a garden in Tideglass with the last of the light on the lemon tree in its pot.

“I would rather have had it on the twenty-ninth of April,” he said, “and that is a different sentence from the one I said to you in April, and I want you to hear the difference and then I am going to stop talking about April.

“In April I was refusing a thing. Refusing is what a man does. **This week I am asking to give one back, and asking is what a man does, and asking puts a date and a name and a shape on me, and there is nobody in this city who is on more shapes than I am, and every one of the shapes so far has had somebody else's name in it, and this is the first one where the shape is me and the name is also me.**”

He picked up the watering can.
