# Chapter 0093 — The Nineteenth Of June Minute, Again

**Tuesday the nineteenth of August 2014, ten in the morning, the records office, second floor.**

The letter had come on the eighth. It was from the chair's office, on Committee paper, and it was the fourth request in ten weeks for a public and minuted record of the nodes in this city with no party answering for them, and it had the words *in preparation* in the last paragraph, and Nina Calder had put it in the tray with three other things and had not answered it.

Jonas Mercer had come in about a quarter past ten for a copy of a determination that anybody could have, and Frances Tolley was at the counter with a lever-arch file, which she has never once put on a client's side of the counter in twenty-two years.

"You've been in twice this month," said Tolley.

"I've been in eleven times since March," said Jonas Mercer. "You told me the gallery fills at about eleven."

"That was for a hearing." She put the file down. "This is for a piece of paper. **Section eleven of the 2008 continuity regulations. Four pages. I've had it in my own hand since the thirtieth of May and I've read it twice and I copied it out myself because I did not trust myself not to improve it.**"

"You've read it twice and you haven't shown it to anybody."

"I've read it twice and I showed it to a registrar in a corridor in July, in nine words, and he read it, and I have not shown it to a records supervisor in nineteen years who is sitting nine feet away with a letter in a tray, and I have thought about that for about eleven weeks and I have got a reason and the reason is that I did not trust myself not to improve it either, and I would rather you worked it out for yourself in about a fortnight and asked me for it."

"That is a very good answer," said Nina Calder, without turning round.

"It is a terrible answer and it is the only one I have," said Frances Tolley. "**I would like a one-page note, Nina. Eight seats are going to be filled in about a fortnight and somebody is going to hand a piece of paper to each of them and I am not going to be the one who invents it, because I have spent five weeks watching this city invent pieces of paper and every single one of them turned into a list.**"

Nina Calder turned round on her chair.

"No."

"You have not heard the request."

"I have heard it and I have heard it three times since June, in a corridor, on a telephone and in a minute that I typed for a clerk, and the fourth time you ask me a question with a number in it in front of a man at a counter then the answer is going to be the same and it is going to be in front of the man at the counter, and I do not care, because it is true and it is flat." She put her hands flat on the desk. "**No, and I am going to say the reason in the right order this time, because you have copied out a page of a regulation and handed it to a man and I have read it twice and I am not going to have anybody say that I refused for a page number when the reason is not a page number.**

"The reason is this. I have designed forms since March. I have designed two of them that anybody can get at a counter. I have refused three times to make a fourth, and one of the three was in a bin, and one of the three is the thing I am most proud of in five months and it is on the wall in the vestibule with a person's name in a row, and if I draft one page of guidance for a body of nine seats **then I will have designed the instrument that makes nine people a thing that exists, and a thing that exists is a thing that can be added to, and a thing that can be added to is a list, and I have said that sentence in this office since the thirtieth of April and I am not going to un-say it in September to be helpful to a clerk.**

"And I will say the other half, since you have not read it and I have. **A body that cannot be required to do anything cannot be asked what it will do, and a body that may not be challenged cannot be told to stop.**"

The office had gone quiet. The woman with the folder at the next counter put her chin on her hand and stayed like that, and Frances Tolley had the file against her chest with both arms.

"You have written that down," said Nina Calder.

"I write everything down," said Frances Tolley. "I have written down two registrar's refusals and a chair's sentence and a determination with two entries in it that cannot be corrected. I do not know what to do with this one."

"Neither do I," said Nina Calder. "**Put the date on it.**"

Frances Tolley put the date on it. She wrote it on the back of her own hand, which is what she does, and Jonas Mercer watched a committee clerk of twenty-two years write four lines on the back of her own hand in a records office because a records supervisor of about fifty said *put the date on it*, and neither of them said one word about it to each other afterwards.

And then Frances Tolley unbuckled the file.

"I have been told by a clerk," she said, "that a records supervisor is not to be shown a document without her consent, and I am going to be about eleven seconds inside that, and here is the whole of section eleven and you have never read it and you have refused four times without reading it, and I would like you to read it now, in front of him, and then say no again."

---

Nina Calder read four pages of a 2008 regulation in about six minutes with a cup of tea going cold beside her, and she did not say anything while she was doing it, and she turned page three over twice.

Then she said: "**Page four.**"

"I know," said Frances Tolley.

"You know it and I have known it for six minutes and I am not going to say what it says, because it is a public document and you can have a copy of it on Thursday and Mr Mercer can have a copy of it on Thursday, and I am not going to be the person who reads a dissolution clause out of a book in a records office on a Tuesday morning to a room with a woman in it with a folder.

"And I am not going to tell the ninth seat," said Nina Calder.

Frances Tolley said, "Nina—"

"**No. And before either of you says a word, let me give you the reason, because I have thought about it for six minutes and I have had six minutes of a Tuesday in a kitchen in my life and that is exactly what it is worth.**

"There is a ninth seat in that section and it is a person who answers for a service and is not a body, and on the fourth page of that regulation there is a clause that says a board of that kind is dissolved when six of the nine seats fall vacant. **And the ninth seat cannot be removed by the other eight, which is on page two, and which I have now read, and which is the sentence you copied out by hand and did not improve and did not read the rest of.** So the one seat in that body that cannot be emptied by anybody else is the only seat that can empty it, and if the woman who takes it knows that, then she is findable in a way she is not now, and in about four years somebody will be very fond of that fact.

"**A person may not be required even to be told.** That is not a sentence I have invented. It is a sentence a chair said three times in a room in June and it is in a minute and a registrar said it in eleven seconds in June and it is the load-bearing wall of everything anybody has done since the fourth of March. And she is not going to be told, and I am not going to tell her, and I am not going to let the department tell her, and **I am not going to be the person who walks up a landing in Salter's Yard and does a kindness that turns a woman into a mechanism.**

"And that is not me keeping a secret from a body of nine. That is me not putting a fact about a person into a room. Write that down too if you like. I have found in nineteen years that the difference between those two things is the whole job."

**Tuesday the nineteenth of August 2014, about eleven in the morning, the vestibule of the Old Ward, ground floor.**

He went down the stairs on the way out because he goes down those stairs.

The register of narrowings is on the left of the board and it has sixteen entries on it and a blank line at the bottom of the form. Beside it, where the nine-millimetre ghost is on somebody else's chalk board four miles away, there was now a printed card, and it had been there since Friday, and Nina Calder had put it there herself and had not told him, and it read in the flat register, without a crest and without a form number:

> **A BODY THAT CANNOT BE REQUIRED TO DO ANYTHING CANNOT BE ASKED WHAT IT WILL DO. A BODY THAT MAY NOT BE CHALLENGED CANNOT BE TOLD TO STOP. THE TWO SENTENCES TOGETHER ARE THE REASON A REGISTER IS NOT A ROOM.**

Underneath, in the same flat register, in the same typewriter face, a date and two initials: **printed 14/8. N.C.**

It is not a plate and it is not on the Lattice and it is not a form. It is a piece of paper on a board in a corridor, printed on the city's own paper by a records supervisor of about fifty, who initialled it because **a condition that nobody has put their name to is a condition that can be put in an edition, and a condition in an edition is a rule, and the whole of this borough's trouble this year is that rules have been arriving in editions.**

He read it twice standing up. He did not say it out loud. He had been in this room eleven times since March and he had read everything on this board every time, and he had a piece of paper in his coat with a date on it and a sign on it, and there was no field anywhere on that card for either.

**Thursday the twenty-first of August 2014, ten in the morning, committee room two.**

It was a determinations meeting and it had four items on it and one of them was on the papers twice.

The chair was Fenella Marsh. Oren Pell was in attendance. Frances Tolley was at the clerk's table. Petrie Okonjo, Douglas Imre, Hargreaves and Denman were in, and Councillor Sore had sent a note rather than come, which was itself minuted. There were nineteen in the gallery and a press list that nobody checked at the door, and there was a man of thirty-two in the second chair from the left at the back with nothing in his hands, and a member's aide said into a microphone that he was not on the agenda, not a party, not a member and not a departmental officer, and the minute records it.

**Item three was the minute of the nineteenth of June 2014, which contains two entries for METER ROOM 3, SALTER'S YARD, TIDEGLASS, one naming a trust and one naming a person, which are the same claim and cannot both be the answer.**

"There is no procedure for amending a minute in a sitting," said the chair, before anybody had spoken. "The one procedure that exists requires this Committee to be satisfied that it has been misled. **It was read to. That is the finding and it is the correct finding and I am not going to have anybody try to improve on it in a room.** Both entries stand. Neither can be withdrawn. I have been told that in four months somebody will say that this Committee was deceived and that is not what happened and I would like the minute to say so in those words, in my words, and I would like it said now while I am in the room."

The clerk took it down and Petrie Okonjo said, quietly, "And I would like the minute to record that the person who read them in is not in the room."

"Noted," said the chair. "It is not the same as being misled and both of them go in."

Then Jonas Mercer asked his one question, in nine words, and the chair let him ask it because the register of attachment is a procedure and a procedure is a thing you may do to a room.

"Does this Committee require anybody to be told anything."

"No," said Fenella Marsh. "**And I am going to give the Committee something it did not ask for, because it is the fourth time in this city that anybody has asked me that question and the answer has been the same four times and nobody has ever written down what the answer actually is.**

"The records office is required to answer an enquiry. A counter is required to answer a telephone. **A counter is not required to make one.** There is no duty on any officer of this city to begin an enquiry, to write a letter, to hand anything to anybody, or to tell a person that a thing has happened to them, and I am not going to pretend to this Committee that one exists, and if one of you thinks one does then you have not read Schedule 4 and neither have I.

"And I want the minute to record a fact which is not about this Committee and is about a woman in Salter's Yard, and I am going to say it and I am not going to be asked about it by anybody in this room. **The person named in the holder field of Meter Room 3 was told what she was holding on the twenty-third of June, by a party, at a records counter, because she rang it herself.** The counter is required to answer a telephone. She rang. **That is the whole of the mechanism by which that woman knows, and it happened by accident, and it took seven weeks, and it is the only reason this Committee has a person in a field who is not a mystery.**

"So: no. This Committee requires nobody to be told anything, ever, in any way, and it has now said so four times, and the fourth time is in a minute with a date on it, and if in a year's time somebody asks how the woman in that field came to be told, the answer is that she telephoned a counter, and a man with a register put a number in a box on a public form in a corridor and did not have her name, and a records supervisor wrote *merely* in a tray in a minute about the weight of it, and that is the entire chain of causation and none of it was designed."

Nobody spoke for about eleven seconds.

"There is one more thing and it is a small one and I would like it read into the record by the clerk rather than said by me." She read from the paper. "**Where a party relies on either of the two entries at Meter Room 3, the other entry is to be read with it.** That is a reading rule and it is not an amendment and it does not cure anything and it does not change a word. I am told that the Committee has power to make rules of evidence about its own papers. I am using it. **It is the first thing this Committee has done in four months that is not a form.**"

And then the September record was tabled, and it was one page, on the city's own paper, and Nina Calder had produced it in nineteen years' worth of evenings, and there was no crest on it and no form number and no index:

> **A PUBLIC AND MINUTED RECORD OF THE NODES IN THE CITY OF NACRE WITH NO PARTY ANSWERING FOR THEM. Prepared 21 August 2014. To be published September 2014.**
>
> **The layer draws nine service districts. Eight are named. The ninth is not drawn.**
> **Nodes on a continuity schedule on the day this record was made: a number, held in a register with chairs, and a node in a schedule is a node with a party, whatever else is true of it.**
> **Nodes in a state of deferral on the city's own public register: sixteen, listed by reference, all of which are checkable by any person at a counter.**
> **Nodes on the lowland: six water points, of which one is a stand, and a chamber, and a spur, and an outfall. NONE OF THEM IS ON THE LAYER. NONE OF THEM HAS A NUMBER, A LOCATION OF RECORD, OR A DISTRICT, AND NONE OF THEM HAS A HOLDER. The spur and the outfall are one entry on a public register in the ground-floor vestibule of the building this Committee is sitting in, and a register is not a district, an entry is not a holder, and the date on a register is the date it was written down and not a date of deferral. Their presence in this record is a figure of six and a figure of six is not a list.**
> **THIS IS A RECORD OF SHAPES. IT MAY NOT BE MATCHED AGAINST ANY LIST OF ADDRESSES, AND ANY DOCUMENT THAT ATTEMPTS IT IS RECORDED AS ATTEMPTING IT.**
> **It names no person. It is a document and it is not a service and it establishes nothing.**
> **There is no field on this page for the person who put it together, and none has been designed, and that is a decision and not an oversight.**

"There is no name on it," said Douglas Imre.

"There is no name on it because a person may not be required even to be told," said Nina Calder, from the public seats, "that they are the person who made a record, and I have been designing forms since March and I know exactly what happens on the second Tuesday of a month when there is a box for a name, and I have watched it happen three times in this borough in 2014, and the third time it happened a housing association's night porter was on a chalk board in Gantry Ward for six weeks before anybody asked him a single question.

"**You want me to put a name on it because you think a record with no name on it cannot be argued with. It can. It is being argued with right now, by you, in a room, in public, and I am glad of it.** It is easier to argue with than a form. That is the whole point of it."

**Thursday the twenty-first of August 2014, twenty past twelve in the afternoon, a corridor, second floor, the civic offices.**

There were six of them in a corridor and two of them were going to be in a hall on Carrow Lane on Thursday the fourth of September to be told what they had become, which was on Friday's agenda and had been on it since Tuesday, and one of them was a records supervisor of about fifty who had read a fourth page of a regulation six minutes on Tuesday morning and had not told anybody and had said why.

"Can a body of nine seats remove the ninth seat," said Frances Tolley, "in front of the other eight."

"**You have copied one page out of section eleven and section eleven is four pages,**" said Oren Pell, "and the answer to your question is not on the page you have got, it is on the fourth, and the fourth page is a dissolution clause, and I am not going to read you the fourth page in a corridor, and neither is Nina Calder, who read it on Tuesday morning, and neither is the man with the register, who has not read it and will not be told.

"And before anybody says anything: **I know what the fourth page does and I have read it twice this year and I have not acted on it, and that is the fifth time in this city somebody has read a thing and not acted on it and I am including the finding on level two, which has a fourth line on it, in a file, in a building I drive past every morning.**"

"Which is a dissolution clause at six vacancies," said Frances Tolley.

"Yes."

"And the ninth cannot be removed by the other eight."

"**Yes, and both of those are on the same regulation and they are two hundred yards apart on the same afternoon, and I have been a registrar for nineteen years and I have never once been asked what happens when a body of that kind is dissolved, and I have got an answer and I am not going to give you it in a corridor with six people in it, and I would like to go and do a stair.**

"Go and read the fourth page, Frances. In your own hand. **And then do not telephone me about it, because the person it is about is a person, and a person may not be required even to be told, and I have been the registrar in this city for nineteen years and I am not going to be the man who tells a woman in Salter's Yard that the only thing she has to do to end a board is walk out of it, when there is not one person in this corridor who is going to be able to act on it and one of us would then have put it in her head for nothing.**"

Nobody said so out loud, and Frances Tolley wrote the question down and underlined it and did not write anything under the line.

Nina Calder said: "I am going to my office. Frances, put the fourth page in the file and not in a tray, and Frances, and I mean this — do not put it in a tray with three other things in it. **A tray is where a thing goes when nobody is going to answer it and that is how I got a letter from a chair's office on the eighth of August that I have not answered.**"

And they went, and Jonas Mercer walked to the shop, and it was the twenty-first of August, and there were forty-one days to Wednesday the first of October and fifty-one to Saturday the eleventh, and the fourth of September was in two weeks, and the ninth of the four was going to be a woman in an apron in a hall on Carrow Lane, and nobody in that corridor had told her one single thing.
