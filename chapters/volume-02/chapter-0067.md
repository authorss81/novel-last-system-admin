# Chapter 0067 — What Two Clinicians Cost

The plate on the door had been carrying a sixth line since the tenth of May and Jonas Mercer had been coming to look at it roughly every second day for five days, and on Thursday the fifteenth of May at about ten o'clock at night he was standing in the middle of the Saint Orra west corridor with his bag at his feet and a roofing slate in it, doing nothing, because he had been told at about eleven o'clock on the Saturday morning that he was not allowed to do anything, and he had spent the day finding out whether that was bearable.

It was bearable for about two hours.

There have been eight handovers in that corridor since the second of April and Jonas Mercer has read the five lines at seven of them, standing out of the way, with a slate, and once with his forearm across a cover. On the fifteenth of May there were four people in the corridor at ten o'clock, and he knew three of them, and the fourth was the reason he had come.

**Ruth Nwosu** read them out loud, because she reads them out loud. **Ezra Mbeki** read them back, and then read them back a second time, because he is twenty-six and has been doing it since the second of April and does not trust the first time. **Mina Okafor** wrote the times down, because it is her job and she has said so twice to two different people.

And the fourth was **Priya Ranganathan**, forty-one, part-time, on the west roster as a named on-call clinician since the second of May, who had never witnessed a handover in this building, and who had asked Mina Okafor on Monday afternoon whether there was one coming up that she could sit in on, and who had been told that the handover was at ten and that she should come if she was going to come.

That is the whole of why she was there. She was not on the rota for a Thursday night. She had come to a handover because a handover needs two named people and there was nobody else, and she had spent three quarters of an hour in a corridor being taught a thing she is legally party to by a porter of twenty-six and a night watch of sixty-three.

Then she read the five lines back, which nobody asked her to do, and got one of them slightly wrong, and Ezra Mbeki corrected her, and she took the correction and did not argue with it and did not pretend she had not got it wrong, and Ruth Nwosu said, “That is the fifth one this month and the first one from a clinician.”

---

At about ten past ten there was a car at the ambulance bay and a woman of eighty-one on a trolley with a bed booked on level two and a receiving shift named, and Ruth Nwosu went down with it, and Ezra Mbeki was at the lift, and there was ninety seconds in which the plate in the middle of the corridor said HOLDER NOT NAMED and the fallback line said that an emergency opening must be declared in writing by the named on-call clinician.

Priya Ranganathan declared it. She did it in about seventy seconds, in writing, on the back of a departmental card, with the time and her own name and the bed and the receiving shift on it, and she read it back to herself before she handed it to Mina Okafor, and Mina Okafor wrote it in the box with the date and did not say anything about the fact that it was the ninth one this month.

The door was open at twenty past ten and closed again at twenty-three past ten and Ruth Nwosu came back up the corridor at half past ten with a woman of eighty-one settled on level two and a strip of paper in her hand, and she gave it to Mina Okafor, and then she looked at the man standing by the wall with the bag at his feet.

“You have got about four minutes and then I have to do the eleven o’clock round,” she said, “and I know you have not come at ten o’clock at night for four minutes.”

“I have not come at ten o’clock at night for four minutes.”

“You have come at ten o’clock at night for the fourth time in three weeks and you have not once been asked and I have stopped saying so, and I have not stopped because of the notice, and I want to say that plainly, because you have been careful with me for five weeks and I have earned the right to be rude about it.”

“All right,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Right,” said Ruth Nwosu, and went to do the eleven o’clock round, and took the red diary out of her bag on the way past and did not open it.

---

Priya Ranganathan did not go home.

She stood in the corridor with a paper cup and read the plate, and she read it the way a person reads something they have been party to for thirteen days and have not yet read. Jonas Mercer watched her go over it four times, slowly, left to right, and then a fifth time with her finger on the sixth line, which is the one that came out of a plate on the tenth of May and is not one of the five and is not a fault and is not a notice.

“There is a date on this,” she said.

“Yes.”

“There are two dates. The term is six days and it ends at six in the morning on the sixth day. And there is a tier on this plate and a schedule and an expiry, and the expiry is the twelfth of October at six in the morning.” She turned round. “What happens on the twelfth of October at six in the morning.”

“The schedule ends.”

“And the term?”

“The term ends whenever it ends,” said Jonas Mercer, “and it has been ending every six days since the second of April, and the reason it is six days and not longer is written in a handover book in Ruth Nwosu’s handwriting and it says *no six-day term until the rota is built again.*”

“And the rota has not been built again.”

“No.”

Priya Ranganathan put the cup on the ledge of the window at the end of the corridor and came back and stood in front of the plate.

“Then the two dates are not the same date, and they are not even near each other, and nobody has asked what happens in between.” She said it slowly. “The term is six days. The window is seven in the morning to six in the morning six days later. So count backwards, and then do not tell me I have got it wrong.

“The handover on the fourth of October, at ten o'clock on a Saturday night, read a term that ran from seven in the morning on Sunday the fifth of October until six in the morning on Sunday the twelfth. **That is the last term in this building that exists entirely inside a funded schedule.** Six days. It was read on the fourth by two porters who did not know what they were reading and neither did I, and neither did anybody who has written it down anywhere.

“And the schedule ends at six in the morning on Sunday the twelfth of October. Which is the same six o'clock. So the term read on the fourth ends at the exact moment the money stops, to the minute, and nobody put them there on purpose, because nobody has ever thought about either of them at the same time.

“Which leaves the handover on **Saturday the eleventh of October at ten o'clock at night.** A term read then runs from seven in the morning on Sunday the twelfth until six in the morning on Sunday the nineteenth. Seven in the morning on the twelfth is one hour after the schedule has gone. **So the handover on the eleventh is the crossing.** Whoever reads that term, in that corridor, at ten o'clock on a Saturday night, performs the act that takes this door off a funded schedule, and there is no field on this plate that says so, and there is no line in it that says *this is the last one that counts*, and the two people who read it will be tired.

“And it is not a Sunday anybody has to come into. The term that is read on the eleventh runs to the nineteenth, so nobody has to do a Sunday at all. That is the good news and it took me eleven minutes to find it and I have not told anybody and I am telling you because you are not on the rota.

“The bad news is the other half of it. If that renewal does not happen on the eleventh, then the term read on the fourth dies at six in the morning on the twelfth along with the schedule, and this door is not then on a reduced term or a deferred term or anything at all. **It is on nothing.** No term. And the fallback asks a named on-call clinician, and there are two of them, and one of them is me, twice a week, and I have told you what I have told you, and the other one is not in this building on a Sunday morning at six.”

“Because it is not a scale. It is a cliff, and there is a cliff inside it, and nobody writes a scale between two cliffs.” She was not asking him. She was thinking out loud in a corridor at half past ten at night and had been doing that for about a minute and a half. “And the plate says what it always says, which is that a node serves the term it was last read into and not the term somebody expected. So on the twelfth of October at six in the morning this door serves a term that was read on the eleventh, which began an hour after the money stopped, and there is nothing in this building that knows which of those two facts is the important one.”

She turned and looked at him.

“I want you to tell me the word for that, because I have been a physician for seventeen years and I can say *decompensation* and I can say *triage* and I can say *step-down* and I can say *time-limited authorisation* and none of them are the word, and I have been standing in this corridor for about ten minutes trying to find out whether I already know it and I do not.”

Jonas Mercer stood by the wall with his bag at his feet and felt the entire weight of five weeks arrive in one place.

*Declining reduction.*

He could have said it. It was in him. He had had it since the Saturday morning when a man in a good coat had offered six water points in eleven minutes, and it was the shape of everything: a door that is not closed but is less, and then less again, and then a thing that nobody can name, and then nothing. A deferred node narrows. A care entrance on a schedule that expires on the twelfth of October does not shut on the twelfth of October; it gets thinner, and then thinner, and the last thinning is not a door at all, it is a rota, and there is no rota.

But he was not on the rota. He was not a clinician, not a holder, not a witness, not a steward. He had no standing in this building. He had a bag and a slate and a register entry that gave him no power and no right to refuse an attachment, and on the twenty-first of April a man in this building had asked him a money question and he had said no in ninety seconds and had spent a fortnight being the most careful person in the borough about that.

He could not mend it. He could not instruct it, because a stage-2 mending requires a person with local standing to instruct it and a named holder to take it, and there were four people in this corridor and not one of them was asking him for anything.

“The word you want,” he said, “is *declining reduction*, and I am not going to say any more about it than that.”

Priya Ranganathan looked at him for about two seconds.

“No,” she said. “I did not think you were going to.”

She turned back to the plate.

“Then I am going to say the rest of it, because I have worked it out and I am not going to sit on it, and you are the only person in this corridor at half past ten at night and you are not on the rota, which makes you the only person I can say it to who cannot put it on a board.

“On the eleventh of October somebody reads a term at ten o’clock at night, and on the twelfth of October at six in the morning this node is not on a continuity schedule, and a node not on a continuity schedule is not disconnected, it is deferred, and it serves its last verified essential use, and if none is recorded, the use is the node’s maintenance. **And the maintenance on a care entrance is a rota.** And there is no rota. And so on the twelfth of October at six in the morning this door serves a rota that does not exist, which means it serves itself, which means there is nothing on the plate at all except the engraving and a date in 2014.

“And what is left after that is the fallback, and the fallback names a route and not a man, and a route is a door, and a door is not a person, and on the twelfth of October at ten o’clock at night the fallback is going to ask a question and the only people in this city who can answer it are a woman of sixty-three who has been on four consecutive shifts this month and has a red diary, a porter of twenty-six, a man of forty-nine who has refused a Thursday since the first week of March and asked for the refusal to be left where it is, and me, twice a week, on a part-time contract.

“**And the eleventh of October is a Saturday.**”

Nobody said anything for about four seconds. A lift went somewhere. A porter came past with a covered bucket and did not stop.

“So there is no scale,” said Priya Ranganathan, “because there is no scale, and there is also no Sunday, and I want both of those in the same breath because people keep offering me the Sunday as the fix.”

“I am not going to be able to mend it,” said Jonas Mercer.

“I know you cannot. I worked that out nine minutes ago, standing at this plate, before you came out of the corridor, and I want to tell you the reasons in order, because I have read enough of your file to know that you will not take one.”

“Go on.”

“One: a mending needs somebody with local standing to instruct it, and that is a clinician or the site manager or the registrar, and I cannot ask any of you to instruct it, because every time somebody in this building asks a person about a date, that person ends up on a rota because of the asking, and I have been here thirteen days and I have already seen that happen twice and I have been one of the two.

“Two: a mending needs a named holder to take it, and there is no rota, so there is nobody to take it, and a plate will not take a bare role, and I am not putting my own name in a field on a care entrance for a term that runs to an unknown date in October, and I would be doing it for the same reason you did not put your name in six lowland holder fields, and I have read the reason.”

He had not told her that story. He checked her face and she had not been told it; she had worked it out.

“Three: I have a daughter of eleven and I do this two days a week and I have told the trust I can do two days a week, and if I put my name on this plate in a corridor at half past eleven at night because a term is going to run out in October, then in June I am a woman who is on a plate in a hospital corridor, and that is findable, and I have spent seventeen years telling patients what findable means.”

“All three correct,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Thank you. And four,” said Priya Ranganathan, “and four is that I have not asked to be thanked and I am not going to be, and I want that said out loud in this corridor at half past eleven at night by somebody who is not on the rota, and then I want to go home, because I have come in on a Thursday for a handover I was not rostered for and I have watched a woman of eighty-one come up on a trolley and I have signed a card for a door, and I am not going to be thanked for any of it, because being thanked for turning up to the witness requirement of your own job is a kind of thanks that stops a person coming back.”

“I am not going to thank you,” said Jonas Mercer, “and I want you to know that I was not going to and that this is the first time in five weeks I have managed not to.”

“Good,” said Priya Ranganathan. “Then I am going to say the thing that is actually going to happen, and I am saying it to you because you are the only person in this building who does not have a reason to do anything about it, and I am going to say it and then I am going to go home, and you are not to do anything with it.

“**Nobody is going to offer.** That is the answer. I am not going to offer, because I do two days a week and I have said so. Ruth will not offer, because she has been on four consecutive shifts this month and she has a book in her bag and I have not read the book and I am not going to ask. Ezra will not offer, because he is twenty-six and he has been doing the two o’clock to ten o’clock shift since the second of April as well as the ten o’clock handover and nobody has asked him to do one more thing and he has never once said so out loud, which is not the same as not being tired. And Bram — ” she stopped, and looked at the board at the end of the corridor, “— Bram is going to refuse, and he is going to refuse for the reason he gave in March, which is that a refusal of a whole shift ends the shift and not the job, and he has asked for it to be left in the same box with the same words, and I am not going to be the person who asks him for a second refusal in October so that there are two of them on a board.”

She picked up her cup.

“Which leaves the standing question, and it is the oldest question in this building and I have only just read it. If two of the four of us say no about the eleventh of October, there is no rule — and it is worse than that, and I want it said: if two of the four say no, then on Sunday the twelfth at six in the morning this door is not on a reduced term, it is on **nothing**, and the answer to *what is on this node* is an empty field, and an empty field means the node's maintenance, and the maintenance is a rota, and there is no rota, and the only thing left in this building at seven in the morning on a Sunday is a door and two names.

“There has never been a rule for that either. Somebody asked it in April and it has been asked five times and it has never been answered, and the reason it has never been answered is that everybody who could answer it is on this rota and cannot answer it without being on it.”

She went to the lift.

“Write it down and do not do anything with it,” she said. “That is a sentence I have said to one other person in five weeks and I am saying it to you because you are the only one who has obeyed it.”

---

He telephoned Tomas Renn at eleven o'clock on the night of the fifteenth of May from the bottom of the stairwell, and it took ninety seconds, and it was the fourth time in four months that he had telephoned that man at eleven o'clock at night about a rota.

“Read it back to me,” said Jonas Mercer, “the one you asked in March.”

“*What happens when two people on the same rota both refuse.*”

“That has got a date on it now,” said Jonas Mercer. “The eleventh of October, at ten o’clock at night, in a corridor at a hospital door, and it is not a shift. It is the handover where a six-day term begins one hour after the money has stopped, and there are four people who could read that term and all four of them have a reason to say no, and two of them saying no is a completely ordinary outcome and not a failure, and if it happens then on Sunday the twelfth at seven in the morning there is a door in that building with nothing behind it at all.”

There was a long pause on the line, which is not a thing Tomas Renn does.

“Mr Mercer,” he said. “I have asked that five times and I have never been given a date.”

“I know.”

“I have asked it in a room with eleven people in it in March and I have been told it needs a payroll line and a training afternoon and a naming authority and a written rule, and I have three of the four, and the third is a hospital doctor’s good intentions, and I have said on a Sunday morning in a yard that I am fifty per cent of the problem instead of sixty, and I have not said that in a fortnight because I have been sitting in a shop with a man who will not telephone me.”

“Tomas —”

“No, let me finish, because I have got about ninety seconds and this is the fourth time in my working life that anybody has given me a date for my own question and I am not going to be rushed on a stairwell.” He took a breath. “Right. Here is what I am going to do with it, and I am telling you so that you do not do it for me, because if you do it for me it is your finding and I have spent April watching a contractor do that to somebody else.

“I am going to ask it in the shop on Monday morning, in a room with about nine people in it, and I am going to put the eleventh of October on the board, and I am going to ask four people in that room, three of whom are stewards, what happens when two people on the same rota both refuse on a Saturday night when the term they are refusing to read begins an hour after the schedule has gone. And I am not going to bring you in and I am not going to bring the hospital in, and Ada will vote against me and Cuthbert will not vote at all.

“And then I am going to be told that it is a hospital matter. And it is. And then I am going to go and stand in a corridor in Saint Orra west and say the date out loud to the four of them, because I have spent four months asking this in rooms where the answer could not be given and I am not going to ask it a sixth time in a room where it can be evaded.

“And I am telling you this at eleven o’clock at night so that on Monday you do not ring me, because I have read the papers, and I know what is in them, and I know the paragraph four of a circular, and I know that a man off a register is going to walk into a building on Monday and try to help. Do not come in on the twenty-ninth. Do not come in on the twenty-second. If I need you I will ask you and I will ask you in a way that you can refuse.”

“All right,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Say it properly.”

“I will not come into the west site on the twenty-second or the twenty-ninth or any day in between, and I will not write anything for you to read out, and I will not put a name in a field.”

“Good,” said Tomas Renn. “Now go and do whatever it is you are doing at eleven o’clock at night in a hospital stairwell, and Mr Mercer — the fact that you have a fourth question with a date on it and no plan attached to it is the first honest thing anybody has handed me since the eighteenth of April, and I am not going to thank you for it, and I would like it noted that I noticed.”

He put the telephone down.

Jonas Mercer sat on the bottom step of a stairwell at Saint Orra west at eleven o'clock on the night of Thursday the fifteenth of May with a pad open on his knee and the eleventh of October written at the top of a clean page and nothing under it, and the twelfth written underneath it in brackets, because the eleventh is the handover and the twelfth is the hour.

Four people. A Saturday night in October. No rule. And the only man in this city who had ever asked the question was going to stand in a corridor on Thursday the twenty-second of May and ask it, and Jonas Mercer was not allowed to be there, and had not been offered, and had been told by a woman of forty-one to write it down and do nothing with it.
