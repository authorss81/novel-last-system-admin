# Chapter 0065 — The Return Is Public

The third reading of the returns on the Old Ward board was on Wednesday the seventh of May at about ten past nine in the morning, and Jonas Mercer was in the porch for it because Nina Calder had told him on the Monday that the third reading would happen on the Wednesday and would be the last one before the papers went out on Friday, and that she was going to do something on the Wednesday and had decided on the Sunday and had not told him what it was.

She read nine lines in about four minutes, out loud, under the glass canopy, with about fifteen people standing there, and the nine lines were the same nine lines with two corrections, and then she said the thing she had come to say.

“I am making these public documents,” she said.

Nobody spoke. It is the Old Ward porch and it is a Tuesday-market morning and there was a man with a trolley.

“Yesterday afternoon I had a telephone call from a man at a trade association asking for a copy of line three. Not a member of the public. A trade association. He wanted it for a circular and he wanted it by Friday, and I said no, because the form is a form and a form is not a document, and he said — and I am quoting him, because I have written it down, because I always write it down — *Mrs Calder, I have it off a photocopy of the board, the whole page, including your crest.*”

She put her hand flat on the sheet on the ledge of the canopy.

“He is right. Somebody in this porch on Wednesday evening of the thirtieth of April, or Friday morning, or Saturday, photographed a page with nine lines and a blank rule at the bottom and the departmental crest in the corner, and that photograph is now the most widely circulated document in the housing sector in this borough and there is a woman in Carrow Lane who is on it, four blocks, a hundred and twenty-eight rooms, a tier, and a fee.

“And I have spent seven days telling this porch that the reason a form is safe is that it is not a record, and I said that sentence on the thirtieth of April in front of nine people and I meant it, and I want to say in this porch this morning that **I was half right and the half I was wrong about is the half that has cost somebody their afternoon.**

“A form with a blank line at the bottom is honest. That is true and I have not stopped believing it. But a form that no party is required to answer is also a form that four parties can decline to answer for as long as they like, and one that a trade body can hold a copy of, and one that gets a photocopy passed round a market at six in the morning, and in the eight days since I printed it the number of copies has gone from one to a number I do not know and cannot ask about.

“So as of this morning the Return of a Deferred Node is **a public document.** Form number on the corner, crest, and available to anybody who asks for it at the records office, and it is not adopted and it is not minuted and it is not enforceable and it still establishes nothing — and every party holding maintenance is on notice from today that a copy of what they wrote is going to be a copy of a public document, and that is the only reason any of the nine of them will ever answer it properly.”

She squared the sheet on the ledge.

“I have been a records supervisor for nineteen years. In nineteen years I have made four things public in this building. Two of them were a register of drainage defects and one of them was a list of asbestos locations and one of them was a mistake I made in 2011 that I have never been told about and that I still think about. **I have never in nineteen years made a thing public that a private person had written in their own hand and given to me for a purpose.** And I am doing it at ten past nine on a Wednesday morning, and I did not sleep about it, and I would like everybody in this porch to understand that I know exactly what the trade association will do with it and I have decided that a fact somebody has already got is worse than a fact somebody can ask for.”

---

The plate on the door of the meter room at the Carrow Lane Housing Association printed on Thursday the eighth of May at about half past ten in the morning, and Jonas Mercer was in the corridor with his pad and it came out under the line about tiers, in the same place, in the flat register, with a full stop after each word.

> **A RETURN MADE BY A PARTY HOLDING MAINTENANCE IS EVIDENCE OF A STATE. IT IS NOT EVIDENCE OF A PERSON, A HOUSEHOLD, OR A NUMBER.**

He read it three times, standing up, outside a locked cupboard off a corridor, with Marguerite Oyelaran's telephone going eleven feet away on the other side of a wall.

“That is two words shorter than the last one,” said Marguerite Oyelaran, through the wall, without coming out, and without stopping, because she had been on the telephone since about nine and it was half past ten.

“I am not arguing with it,” said Jonas Mercer, to the corridor.

“Nobody is arguing with you. That is a plate and a plate does not argue, and I have got a woman from the borough association of housing officers on the other end of this telephone who is reading me paragraph four of a circular that has got my four blocks on it and a fee on it, and I have been on this telephone since nine o’clock and it is now half past ten and I have not spoken to a tenant of mine today and I have a void on Block B that I was supposed to deal with at half past nine.”

“Right.”

“Mr Mercer, I want to say one thing to you quickly and then I am going to go back to it, because you are the only person in this borough who has been in my office twice and heard the other half of it. **Your plate has just told the truth and it has cost me my morning, and I am not going to be the person who blames a plate.** The second reading I made on the twenty-eighth of April of a page of a hundred and twenty-two rows was true and it cost me nothing. The third reading of it, on the seventh of May, in a building, out loud, was the same true page and it has cost me a day, and the difference is a pair of doors.”

She put the telephone down on the desk and came out into the corridor for about forty seconds.

“What happened to my day, and I am going to tell you all of it because you will find out some of it and I would rather you had the whole of it.”

“Tell me.”

“Three things. One: the borough association of housing officers has issued a four-page circular, and paragraph four of it says — and I have got it in front of me on the desk and I will not read it to you in a corridor, I will read you the sentence — the circular says that *where a housing association has placed a node on a continuity schedule, the association's chief officer is the answering party and the fee is recoverable only from rents, and officers in that position should take independent advice before the nineteenth of May.*

“Which is advice to me, and which is nearly friendly, and there is a letter with it. One page. Signed by two people I have worked with for eleven years, who have both taken my telephone calls on a Sunday in March when I was buying a schedule of four blocks and neither of whom has asked me for anything. The letter says *we are not writing to tell you that you were wrong*, and then it tells me that I was wrong, in about four hundred words, correctly, in the passive voice, and it ends *we would be grateful to discuss the position of chief officers in this position on a day of your choosing*, and I have not telephoned them back, and I am not going to, and if you want the sentence that is actually in the letter rather than the circular it is: *we do not consider that a fee recoverable from rents can be defended in a public document without an answer to a question we have not been able to ask.*”

“That is advice to you. That is nearly friendly.”

“That is a warning to every chief officer in this borough not to do what I did in March, and it is signed by two people I have worked with for eleven years, and I understand every word of it, and it has been sitting on my desk since nine o’clock this morning as a document with a paragraph number in it.

“Two: a councillor’s office rang at a quarter to ten and asked for the association’s attendance list for Monday, and I have sent it, and it is not on the committee, and I said so, and the woman on the telephone said, and I am quoting, *we are asking whether it should be.*”

“Whether the association should be in the room.”

“Whether the association should be in the room. And I have had four words of my own office back to me in four months about whether I am the right person to be doing this, and three of them were from people who have never paid anything, and the fourth was on the twenty-eighth of April from you, and you were right then as well.”

She looked at the wall clock.

“Three, and this is the one that is going to cost me. At two o’clock this afternoon I have to go into the Methodist hall on this road, which is on my books as a booking from the eleventh of April for a hall that belongs to a church, and there are eleven households in this borough living in it since the seventeenth of April at my expense, and I have to go because I have been putting money into that hall since April and I have not been inside it once, and I am not going in there this afternoon, because I am going to be on this telephone until about three, and then I am going to be in a room with two officers of an association until about five, and one of the eleven in that hall is a woman who telephoned me on Monday to ask about the continuing charge and I have not called her back.

“I am not going to be able to call her back until Monday. She has been waiting since Monday. **And in three weeks I will say the fee does not stop, and it will not be true on Monday, because I will not have been in that hall.**”

“Can somebody else go.”

“There is nobody else. There has been nobody else since the twenty-second of April, and the reason there is nobody else is on that plate of yours and I am not blaming it. I am telling you what it costs so that when it comes up on Monday you know that the plate is right and the day is still mine.”

She went back in and shut the door, and Jonas Mercer stood in a corridor above a betting shop and wrote four lines and crossed one of them out.

> *Wed 7 May. The Return is a public document. Nina Calder decided it on Sunday and told me on Monday and told me the thing on Wednesday. It is right on the principle and it is wrong on the facts, and both of those are true, and the facts are the four blocks, the tier, the fee, and a hundred and twenty-eight people whose arithmetic is now legible in a market at six in the morning.*
> *She did not sleep about it. Nobody sleeps about it. That is what I have got instead of an argument.*
> *Carrow Lane: the circular (para 4, officers told to take independent advice), the attendance list and a councillor asking whether the association should be in the room, and a void on Block B at half past nine this morning.*
> *The plate says a return is evidence of a state and is not evidence of a person or a household or a number. All three of those are on a photocopy in a trade body’s circular and two of them are about one woman with no assistant.*

---

Nell Ardent came at about four o'clock on the Wednesday afternoon and found him on the bench at the shop and did not sit down.

“I am not going to say it to you as a warning,” she said, “because I said it to you as a warning on the first of April and I was right and it did not do any good, and a warning is a thing you hand somebody so that they can put it in a drawer. So I am going to say it as a description of what has happened, and then I am going to go and do some work.

“On the first of April I told you: *the count is the day somebody comes with a clipboard.* And I was right, and I have spent a month watching it, and I want you to understand that the count did not arrive the way I thought it would. I thought it would arrive with a clipboard. It has arrived with a **form**, and a form is worse, because a clipboard asks one person one question in one room and a form asks nine parties nine questions and one of the nine is a woman in Carrow Lane and the answer she gave was four blocks and a hundred and twenty-eight rooms and a fee, and that answer is a *household*, and a number, and a person, and Nina Calder’s plate says on the wall of her own corridor that a return is not evidence of a person or a household or a number.

“Her plate is right. Her form is how the plate got falsified inside four days by nobody doing anything wrong.”

“Then what do you want me to do with it.”

“I want you to do nothing with it, which is the most irritating sentence I have said to you in five weeks,” said Nell Ardent, “because there is a woman in a trade body with a photocopy, and a councillor asking whether a housing association should be in a room, and a four-page circular telling every chief officer in this borough not to do what one of them did in March, and it will be in a report by September, and none of it is a lie. **Every word of it is true and it is being used to stop a thing that was correct.** I have spent eleven years watching that and I have never once been able to write a clause against it and I am not going to start this week.”

She put her bag down on the bench.

“But I am going to do one piece of work and it is a small one and it is free and I want you to know about it. On Monday I am going to stand in a gallery in that room and I am going to say one sentence. It is going to be this sentence: *the committee is asked to note that the only instrument in this city capable of producing the number it has been asked about was printed on the thirtieth of April and has no adoption behind it.* And then I am going to sit down and not say anything else, and I am not going to give you the sentence in advance so that you can do anything with it, and you are not going to like that, and I do not care, because you have been in a chamber once this year and it was as a man off a register, and this is the only thing I have got and I am not spending it on preparing somebody.”

She picked her bag up.

“But I am also not going to be thanked for it in a corridor on Monday, because if I am thanked in a corridor on Monday then I have been part of something and I am not going to be part of something. I am going to be a lawyer who said a sentence. Do you understand the difference? It is the difference between a witness and a person who is on the card.”

“Yes,” said Jonas Mercer.

“You always say that and I have never once known whether you mean it,” said Nell Ardent, and went out into the yard and did not say anything else, and the rain came over the parapet and did not reach her.

The borough council's calendar and committee panel sat on the Wednesday evening of the seventh of May at half past six with seven members in front of about forty people, and Jonas Mercer was in the back because the papers for the panel are public seven days beforehand and the sixth item on the agenda was *committee calendar — 19 May* and he wanted to see who turned up.

Nobody turned up for item six. It took four minutes. Two members said the hearing was properly before the standing committee and not the council's business. A third said the contractor's covering submission asked the committee to *adopt* a schedule, and asked the clerk, rhetorically, whether the council had authorised that, and the clerk said that no such resolution appeared in the calendar papers.

And then a member from Salt Row — about fifty, four years in, with a bad head cold and the only prepared notes in the room — said the thing that went into the minute, and said it in the flat way of a person who has read something that morning and is annoyed by it:

“The covering submission names a housing association. The return printed on the thirtieth of April names a housing association, with a tier and a fee and a number of rooms, on a public board, and the form is not adopted and there is nothing behind it. So the association is the only body in this city that is on the public record as having bought access to a node, and it bought it in March, and it passed the cost into a hundred and twenty-eight rents, and it has a chief officer and no assistant.

“My question is narrow and it is for the record. **Should the Carrow Lane Housing Association be a party to the hearing on Monday at all?** Because if it is, it can answer for itself, and if it is not, then the only line in that room that says what a tier costs a person who lives in the block will be read out by a chair from a piece of paper that a man off a register brought in at ten to eight in the morning, and that man is not a party either, and I do not know what that is, and I would like somebody to tell me.”

Seven members, and not one of them answered it, and it was minuted, and Jonas Mercer wrote the number of the item on the back of his hand with a biro because he was not going to write it in a pad in a room in case somebody looked over, and then he put his hand in his pocket and did not think about it for two days.

---

Thursday the eighth of May, twenty past nine in the morning, in the corridor of the Carrow Lane Housing Association, with a queue.

There had not been a queue in that office since the January weekends when she went round the four blocks with a form. There were eleven people in the corridor at a quarter past nine and about nine of them were there by ten past ten, and two of them were from Block D and one of them was from A and six of them were from B and C, and they were not all there about the two pounds sixty, and the association's office has two rooms and a landing and there is nowhere to stand.

Jonas Mercer was there because he had said he would be, and Marguerite Oyelaran had said, “You may be, and you will stand at the bottom of the stairs and you will not say anything to anybody and if anybody asks you a question about money you will say *she is the chief officer and she is through there, she is on the telephone*, and that is the only sentence you have.”

He stood at the bottom of the stairs for an hour and forty minutes and said that sentence about eleven times.

He saw the trade association's circular twice in people's hands. He saw a woman of about sixty read the third line aloud to a man who was about thirty and then hand him the circular, and he heard the man say *tier two* in a voice that made two other people look up, and he heard the woman say *four thousand two hundred pounds a node*, and he watched that move through eleven people in a corridor the way a cold does.

And at about ten to eleven, Marguerite Oyelaran came out of the back room with her coat on, because she was going to the hall at eleven, and she came out into the middle of a corridor with a queue in it, and a queue that had not existed an hour before, and she stopped in the doorway.

Then she went and got a chair from the room at the end and put it in the corridor and sat down on it, and said, “Right. One at a time, and I am going to be here until about half past twelve, and anybody who has come about the two pounds sixty, the answer is on the sheet on the wall and it is four lines and I have not written any more than that and I am not going to, and anybody who has come about anything else, I will listen to you for four minutes and I will write it down and I will do what I can on Monday, and I am telling you now that I have a telephone call at eleven and a meeting at two and a woman I have to see at three, and none of that is more important than you, and all of it is more urgent than you, and I have been the only officer in this office for eleven years and I am not going to pretend to you that those two things are not both true.”

The queue went on for an hour and a quarter and she did what she said she would do.

---

The twelfth letter came at about ten past eleven, hand-delivered, by the woman from room eleven of Block B who had come up the stairs on the Saturday before with a shift at two.

She had written it on the Saturday morning at the kitchen table, about an hour after she counted, and posted it on the Monday, and had come back on the Tuesday and again on the Wednesday, and on the Wednesday evening she had decided that she was not going to leave it in a box, and she put it on the desk in Marguerite Oyelaran's own hand, face down, because she had thought about that too.

“Saturday the third, about eight in the morning,” she said. “Posted Monday. And I have brought it back because I want to be standing in the room when it is on the desk, and I am not having the only thing I do in May be put in a box. I know there are eleven others. I counted. I counted on Saturday morning at breakfast and I have told my daughter I counted, which I have not done about anything since 1998.”

Marguerite Oyelaran turned it over and read the first line and put it face down again.

“That is the twelfth,” she said. “I am going to date it the eighth of May because that is the day it is on this desk, and your notice runs from the third of June, and I am going to write *twelfth* on the top of the file in pencil and I am not going to put it in the rent roll, because a rent roll is a thing that goes to a court.”

“Write twelve,” said the woman. “I want it written.”

“It will be written.”

---

And then a man of about forty came up at about ten past twelve and stood at the bottom of the stairs, and he did not go in, and he waited until the corridor was clear, and then he said:

“You are the man off the register.”

“Yes.”

“I am Block B, room six. I am one of the three who did not sign in January because I did not understand it, and you read it to me twice in a doorway and the second time I did not understand it either, and then you came in March and read it to me a third time and I said no, and I have been the one you have not rung.”

“I have not rung you a fourth time because she told me not to.”

“She told you not to.” He put his hand on the banister. “I want to sign.”

Jonas Mercer did not move and did not speak.

“I did not understand it in January because it is a page of numbers with a date on it and nobody in that building had ever shown me what a tier was. I understand it now. **I understand it because my block is on a piece of paper in a public building with a fee on it and there is a circular going round with the fee on it and a number of rooms on it, and I know that the four blocks are Tier two, and I know that two pounds sixty is what that costs each of us, and I know that if I am not inside the agreement then I am outside the thing, and outside is a worse place to be in than inside was.**”

“That is not a reason to sign anything.”

“It is the only reason I have got, and I have had three months of not having a reason, and I have come up those stairs because I read the return on Wednesday night and I did not know until Wednesday night that a housing association could be a line on a piece of paper, and now I know, and I would like to be inside it.” He looked at Jonas Mercer. “I am not asking you for anything. I am asking her. She is through there. I would like to say it to her.”

Marguerite Oyelaran was in the room at the end with her coat on and a telephone in her hand and eleven minutes to a hall on this road with eleven households in it that she has not visited since the seventeenth of April.

Jonas Mercer stood at the bottom of the stairs and did not say a word, because he had promised to say one sentence and he had said it eleven times and there was nothing else in the world he was entitled to add, and because he had been refused twice in five weeks by two people who were right, and because a third person was about to sign a thing for a reason that had nothing to do with understanding it, and the only instrument in this city that could stop that was a calendar, and a calendar is not a thing anybody can stand in front of.
