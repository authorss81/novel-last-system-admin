# Chapter 0057 — Nobody Has Asked Her To

The Carrow Lane Housing Association has two rooms above a betting shop on Carrow Lane and it has been in the same two rooms since 1979, and the sign on the door says CARROW LANE HOUSING ASSOCIATION and underneath it, in a different paint, a line that has not been repainted in about nine years: *ASK FOR MARGUERITE.*

Marguerite Oyelaran is forty-seven and she is the chief officer and she has been the chief officer for eleven years and she does not have an assistant, and she came out of the front room into the back one at ten past ten on Monday the twenty-eighth of April with two cups already made, and said, “You are the man off the register.”

“I am.”

“Good, that saves us both a quarter of an hour,” said Marguerite Oyelaran. “I have got eleven minutes before a void on Block C and I am going to spend four of them on you and I am going to be rude, because you are going to be here for an hour and because I have read your file and there are two things on it that I have an opinion about and one of them is that you have never once been in a room like this one.”

“Which two.”

“The register of attachment, which is a bucket under a tap, and which you knew that on the day and which I have thought about for nine days and which I am going to come back to.” She put a cup on the table. “And the fact that you have never been in a room like this one, which is a room where a hundred and twenty-eight people live and nobody in it has ever asked me what a fee is.”

She put a folder on the table. It was about the thickness of a telephone directory and it had been used.

“March,” she said. “The eleventh of March. I bought four blocks of continuity for this association, Tier 2, four nodes, sixteen thousand eight hundred pounds for the year. Not the money. Sixteen thousand eight hundred pounds is nothing, and I want to be really clear about that in this room, because I have read what people write about this city in the papers and I have never once seen anybody get the scale of it right. It is the price of a wheelie bin for two years.

“The four blocks are A, B, C and D. A and B are 1961 and 1974 and they are fine. C is 1938 and its water is on a shared stopcock in a cupboard that I do not have a key to, because the stopcock belongs to a company. D has a lift in it that is nineteen years old and that I am paying for.”

“Why,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Because in 2011 the lift in D went out for nine days and I had four people in that block who could not take their stairs and one of them was a man of eighty-three who lives on four and who went into a care home for eleven weeks and came out of it having lost the use of one side, and the last time the money for that came out of my own office, because I paid for the stair lift hire out of the void allowance and there was no void allowance in October.” She said it flatly, with the folder open. “And because in 2004 the same lift went out for six days and I did it again out of the same allowance, and there was no allowance, and I went to a board and a board said no to me in a room with nine people in it in about ninety seconds, and the reason the board said no is that a lift is not a service, it is a piece of plant in a building, and there is no claim on anybody for it.

“Then in February a contractor sent me a document. I want to be careful here, because I am about to say something that in June will look like an excuse. Nobody came to see me. I was on a distribution list. I had bought a schedule for the first time in my life and I did not know that I was doing anything anybody could object to, and when I did know, in about the fourth paragraph of page two, I read on, because I wanted to know what it cost. That is the truth about how this happened in this room and it took eleven minutes.

“Thirteen pages. Four thousand two hundred pounds a node, a tier, a fee, and a page that says what happens to a node that is not on a schedule. And what happens is *it is not disconnected, it is deferred, and it serves its last verified essential use, and if none is recorded, the use is the node's maintenance.*”

She turned the folder round so it faced him.

“That is page two,” said Marguerite Oyelaran. “And I read that and I had a very ordinary reaction, which was: well, the last verified essential use of a lift is somebody who cannot take the stairs. Which is true. Which is *so* true that I sat here for about a minute feeling very pleased with myself, because for eleven years I have been telling a board in a room that a lift is a piece of plant and a lift is not a service, and there it was in a schedule, in print, and it agreed with me, and it took eleven seconds for me to notice that the schedule does not care why a thing is a service. It cares that a payment is coming in.”

She closed the folder.

“So I did it. I put the four blocks on the tier on the eleventh of March and the sixteen thousand eight hundred came out of the association and I passed it on, because I could not carry it, because there is no version of this in which an association with a hundred and twenty-eight people in four blocks carries sixteen thousand eight hundred itself out of a void allowance. And I passed it on at two pounds sixty a room a week, which is the sum, and it comes off the rent roll on the first, and I did that by putting a standing order form through every one of those hundred and twenty-eight doors myself, on a Saturday, over three weeks, in January and February, and I put it through a letterbox and I knocked on every door, and I did not do it to one person who was not in.”

“Two pounds sixty a week for two blocks you did not need.”

“No,” said Marguerite Oyelaran. “A and B are fine and I took them to the tier anyway, because a tier is a price you pay for the whole plate and nobody has ever come and told me you can buy a good night in A and a bad night in C.”

---

“There is a clause on page nine,” said Jonas Mercer.

“There are four clauses on page nine and you have read one of them, and it is the one that is going to be read out in a hearing in June by somebody who has not read the other three.” She turned to it. “Challenge. A tier may be challenged by a district or by a node in the same tier. And that is the whole of the challenge mechanism in this city, and it is four hundred and sixty pages of schedule in total and it is the only part of it that anybody in this borough has ever complained about, and it is one sentence, and the sentence is on the door of the meter room and it is the only thing in this building anybody has ever said out loud to a member of the public.”

She got up and took him through to the meter room, which is a cupboard off the corridor with a fuse board and a stopcock and a plate on the door, and the plate said:

> **A TIER MAY BE CHALLENGED BY A DISTRICT OR BY A NODE IN THE SAME TIER. IT MAY NOT BE CHALLENGED BY A PERSON.**

“I have had that read at me by four people this month and I would like it minuted that every one of the four was being polite to me and every one of them was telling me the true thing.” She shut the door of the cupboard. “I cannot appeal my own bill. I have tried. I went to the water people in March and I went to the council in March and in April I telephoned a man at a trade body and he explained it to me in about four minutes and he was extremely kind about it.

“Four in March. I have a page here that is a hundred and twenty-two rows and there are a hundred and twenty-two signatures on it and I have not shown it to anybody, and I am going to show it to you, and I want you to understand that I am doing it because I want to be argued with and not because I think it will help.

“A hundred and twenty-two of a hundred and twenty-eight signed. Eight did not, and I have spoken to all eight and five of them said the same thing, which is *I will not sign a thing that makes my name a door*, which is the sentence of a woman in a block in this city in April and it is the best sentence anybody has said to me this year, and I have written it on a card in my own handwriting and I have not done anything with it.” She pushed the page across. “The other three did not sign because they did not understand it, and that is my fault, and I have gone back to all three and I have read it to them and two of them have signed and one has not, and his name is not on the page, and I am not going to ring him a fourth time.”

Jonas Mercer read the hundred and twenty-two rows. There were no names on it. There were block and room numbers, a tick, a date, and a second date, and there was nothing else on the page, and the answer to any request for a list of the people in this stock is two rooms above a betting shop on Carrow Lane, and he was standing in one of them.

“What happened to the nine who missed a month,” he said.

“They missed a month.”

“Not what happened *to* the block.”

“They missed a month,” said Marguerite Oyelaran, “and on the first of May they will have missed two, and the participation agreement lapses twelve months after the signature, and not before, and it cannot be withdrawn, and on the twelfth of April next year they can either renew or let it go, and on the twelfth of April next year they will still be living in these blocks with the same water.

“Do you know what the Lattice does with a signature,” she said. “It grades it by date. I do not know that from a document. I know it because eleven of the hundred and twenty-two in Block C were on a lapsed term on the eighteenth of April and the water in that block was at half, and I have said that number out loud to two people this month and not to the hundred and twenty-eight.” She put her finger on the page. “So that is a date on a page, agreed to in good faith by people who are not being lied to. And it is better than nothing for three hundred and seventy-five days, and after that it is worse than nothing, and the person cannot tell the difference on the day. That is the whole of it. That is the design. There is no villain in page nine at all. Page nine is just a calendar.”

“And I want to say to you, as the woman who has put a hundred and twenty-two of those signatures through a hundred and twenty-two letterboxes herself, that this is not a machine, and I know that is the sentence the papers want from me, and it is the wrong word and it is the wrong reason. A machine takes a signature or it does not take it. Nobody in those four blocks was processed. I knocked on every one of those doors on a Saturday morning and I read the increase out loud and I said the date, and every one of the hundred and twenty-two knew exactly what they had signed, and I could not tell you one of them from the last, and neither could they.”

She shut the folder on her thumb.

“Which is why I am not allowed to be angry. If I could get to be angry at somebody I would be out of this room in a minute and so would you, and we would both feel better, and Block C would have the same water in it in June as it has got in it now.”

---

“You have got a hold-up outside on your telephone,” said Jonas Mercer, “and it has rung five times, and I am going to leave unless you tell me not to, and I am not going to pretend I have not been waiting to ask you the one thing I came for.”

Marguerite Oyelaran looked at the telephone and then at the wall clock and then said, “Ask it, and I will tell you no, and then I will take the call, and it is a void on Block C and it is not going to wait.”

“Paragraph eleven says a node in failure may be placed on a schedule by the district in which it stands. A mending of a term needs somebody with local standing to instruct it and a holder to take it, and I cannot do it, and I have known that since March. I have been trying to work out all week who it would be, and it cannot be the association as a category, because a plate will not take a bare role, and I have watched that rule put a woman on a wall in a kitchen for five weeks.” He put his hands flat on the table. “So the answer is you. If you put your name in the holder field of a lowland water point, then the point has a holder, and the price comes off it, and there is a person to ask.”

“All right,” said Marguerite Oyelaran. “I will do it on Monday. It will take four minutes and I have done it before.”

“No,” said Jonas Mercer. “I would like you to hear the whole sentence before you say that, and then I would like you to say it again after, because I have watched two people this week do the thing where they say yes in four seconds to a thing that costs them a street, and I am not going to be the man who stops asking because the answer was quick. I will not be offended by it and it will not be a trick, and you may say no, and I will write it down in full and come back in June.”

So he said it.

“Six lowland water points,” said Jonas Mercer. “Your name in six holder fields. That means that in nine months, when there is a question about one of them, a registrar can put it to a person, and that person is findable, and every document that names the point names you, and the point is on a street that has no address and where fifty-nine doors have been counted once a year by a man of seventy for thirty-two years. An officer of a housing association who can be found at six water points is not a person on that street. It is a form with a name in a field on it, and the street will be on the form, and then there is a street.”

Marguerite Oyelaran did not answer for as long as it takes to be certain somebody has finished a sentence.

“That is exactly right,” she said, “and I knew it was exactly right about two seconds before you finished, and I said yes in four seconds anyway, which tells you exactly what I am, and I would like to write down that I did that and why, because I think a person who says yes quickly to a thing they know is wrong is worse than a person who says no, and I have done it once in my life and I have never done it in front of anybody and I have just done it in front of you for no reason except that you asked me to wait.”

“The draw ends,” said Jonas Mercer. “The job does not. I can ask you again in June.”

“I know you can ask me again in June,” said Marguerite Oyelaran, “and in June I may say yes in four seconds again, and you will write that down then as well, and I will not blame you for it, and I am not going to pretend that I have been rescued from anything by a technician with a bag.”

She picked the telephone up on the fifth ring and said, “Block C, yes, hold on,” and then, with her hand over the mouthpiece, to Jonas Mercer:

“Two pounds sixty. That is the sum. I am going to lose that block.”

---

She put the telephone down.

“Now here is the thing I am actually going to tell you, and I have not told it to Nell Ardent and I have not told it to my board,” said Marguerite Oyelaran, “and I am telling it to you because you are going to be in a room about this in June and because I am tired.

“The lowland. There are six water points on that lowland — I know because the association has serviced two of them since 2011 under a shared-maintenance arrangement that expired, which is a sentence with an interesting double meaning in it, and I have not made that joke to anybody else. Six points. Tier 1 is one thousand eight hundred and eighty pounds a year each. Six points is eleven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds a year, which is a wheelie bin, and I have known that figure since the twelfth of March, because I worked it out on the back of the void schedule in about ninety seconds in March when I was feeling pleased with myself.

“Nobody has asked me.”

She said it without any weight at all, which is how she says everything.

“In nine days I have had a registrar ring me once, a contractor’s continuity man come to this room twice, a journalist ring me and not get an answer, and you, and not one single person in the city of Nacre has come to me and said: there are six water points on a lowland and they are not on a schedule and nobody has ever put them on a schedule and it is eleven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds, and here is the money, and please put them on it.

“Not the association. The city. Not a councillor. Nobody. In four hundred and sixty pages of schedule and nine days of panic there is one number that nobody in this city has said out loud, and I have said it out loud to a technician on a Monday morning because I was going to be able to look at you afterwards and find out whether you were going to do anything with it.”

Jonas Mercer stood in the meter room of the Carrow Lane Housing Association on Carrow Lane on Monday the twenty-eighth of April at eleven minutes to eleven in the morning with two hundred and thirty-one pounds of nothing in his pocket.

He did not say, *tell me about them.*

He did not say, *I will ask.* He did not say, *put it in a claim on the Old Ward board* — and that was the one he wanted, because a claim may be stated in that box for ninety days and it does not require a name and it may not be used as evidence of occupancy, population, households or persons, and it would have been the perfect instrument and it would have taken him about four minutes.

He had spent five weeks going round a city asking people for things, and he had been told in a yard and a stairwell and a corridor by five separate people in nine days that the asking is not the problem and the being is.

“All right,” he said. “Thank you. I am not going to do anything with it today and I will tell you exactly what I am going to do with it, because I have learned that in five weeks from a woman of sixty-three who burnt a letter.

“I am going to write it on a card, in a coat, in your words, with your name on it, and I am not going to put your name on anything, and I am not going to go up there and ask anybody for a pump or a schedule or a place on a form, and when somebody in a room in June says the words *lowland* and *water* together I am going to be the one who says the number, and not before that, because in nine days that number is worth nothing, and it is worth something on the nineteenth of May, and I am not going to spend it this morning because I am in a hurry.”

“Write it down now,” said Marguerite Oyelaran, “and read it back to me, because if it is in your coat it is in your coat, and if it is on my board it is on my board, and I would like it to be on my board and I would like you to be frightened of that.”

“Write it where.”

“My board. The one in the corridor. Today. In my hand, not yours, because I have watched a technician’s handwriting go into a document in this borough and I have not been in the room where it happened and I have read about it since.” She got a pen out of the drawer of the cabinet behind her. “Eleven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds a year. Six points. Nobody has asked me.”

She wrote it on the board in the corridor of the Carrow Lane Housing Association at ten past eleven on Monday the twenty-eighth of April in a hand that was not Jonas Mercer’s and not anybody else’s, and then she put the pen back in the drawer and shut the drawer, and Jonas Mercer looked at six rows and a hundred and twenty-two on a wall and understood that he was the second person in this city to have seen that number, and that the first had been eleven years in post and had no assistant.
