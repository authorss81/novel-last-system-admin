# Chapter 0055 — The Second Named Clinician

She had left a message at the desk on the Saturday morning of the twenty-fifth of April at about nine, in the middle of the handover book, on a piece of A5 with the word **JONAS** on the top of it in capitals, and it said: *corridor. ten. I will be twenty minutes. Do not bring the file.*

He brought the file.

He was in the corridor at five to ten with the plate cover off the care-entrance plate and a roofing slate and a wedge, because there is a field on that plate he has wanted to look at since Wednesday and it is not the field anyone else would look at, and he heard her before he saw her.

“I said do not bring the file.”

“I did not bring the file. This is a plate cover and a roofing slate and a bar and I am doing the field under the holder line because it has got a cursor in it, and if you say anything about the file I will put it down on the floor and you can pick it up.”

Dr. Sanaa Iqbal came down the corridor in her own clothes with a paper cup and no bag and stopped four feet away and looked at the plate, and at his forearm across the cover, and at the seam on the inside of his right arm where the sleeve of a shirt does not reach.

“Say good morning to me first,” she said, “and then ask me what I am going to say, because you have been standing there for about forty seconds composing a question and it is not the one you want to ask.”

“Good morning.”

“Ask.”

“Have you got a second named clinician.”

“There,” said Dr. Sanaa Iqbal. “You did it in one sentence and you did not pretend, and that is the whole of the last eight minutes of my walk down this corridor.” She drank the coffee. “Yes. I have got a second named clinician. Her name is Priya Ranganathan, she is forty-one, she is a physician, she has been doing on-call cover at the east site on a locum basis since November and she has been doing it unpaid for six weeks of it, and from the second of May she is on the west roster as a named on-call clinician, on a part-time contract, on the same terms as me.”

“That is — Sanaa, that is the thing you have been trying to get since the twenty-fourth of March. That is the fifth refusal. You told me about the fifth refusal.”

“Yes. I told you about the fifth refusal. Do you remember the other four?”

“Four refusals and one no from the education office.”

“Four refusals and one no from the education office,” said Sanaa Iqbal, “and you have just recited them back to me like a list, which is the first time in five weeks you have been the man who is on my side of a conversation, and I want to say something about that and then I am going to tell you how she got on the roster, and I am telling you how because you will find out in a fortnight from a trust bulletin and I would rather you heard it from me in a corridor.”

“How did she get on the roster.”

“Gunnar Halvorsen found a participation line.”

---

She said it the way a person says a word they have been carrying for four days and have not yet found a shape for.

“Not a CivicCore line. Not a contractor. A participation line — the hospital’s own, the one that comes out of the west site’s own activity budget, the one that has been dead since 2011 and has about nine hundred pounds in it from a coffee-and-cakes appeal that shut in March. He did not create it. He did not ask the board. He found nine hundred pounds in a dead line item in the second week of April and he wrote an order through the site administration on the twenty-second of April, which does not need a board, because it is under four thousand pounds, and it is a part-time on-call line for one clinician, and it went through on Wednesday, and it is real, and it is signed, and I have got the order.”

“A participation line,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Yes. A line in a budget that is fed by what people pay into it. And that is the sentence and I have been sitting with it since Wednesday afternoon, so I am going to say the rest of it, and you are going to let me get to the end of it before you say anything, because I know exactly what your face is doing and it is the same face you had in a yard in March when I told you about a rota line and you started to solve me.”

He put the bar down on the floor.

“The thing that got me a second clinician,” said Sanaa Iqbal, “is a fee. The same shape of thing as the participation agreement a man in a good coat put on a table in a library on Wednesday. Two pounds sixty a room a week out of thirty-one rooms and a nineteen-year-old steward who has read a page of numbers out loud four times in a community room, except that this one is nine hundred pounds out of a dead line item in a hospital, and it is not a fee, it is a grant, and I know which of those two words is worse and I have been sitting with that for four days and I have got to the bottom of it and here it is: from where I am standing they are the same word.

“Ruth Nwosu is on this roster because the hospital pays her. Not well. Not enough. But it pays her, and the paying is a line, and if the line goes then she goes, and that is the whole of the design and it is a good design and I have been defending it since the fifteenth of March, and it works, and I have got eleven hours of relief on a weekday afternoon for the first time since the second of April and I got it out of a dead coffee line that nobody has spent since 2011, and somebody put money in it in 2011 because they believed a hospital would still be doing this in five years, and I got it by accident, and I have got it for as long as the nine hundred pounds lasts, which is until somebody in that building spends it on a machine.

“So that is my news,” she said. “And I am telling you it is not the same as your news, and I want that on the record from me before anybody in June puts the two in the same paragraph, because the hospital line is nine hundred pounds from 2011 and the block is two pounds sixty a week off thirty-one people, and if those are the same then the whole of what I have said to you in five weeks about care is a thing about money, and it is not, and I would like us to be careful.”

“It is not the same,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Right,” said Sanaa Iqbal. “Now the other thing, which is the reason I asked you to come, and it is not the second clinician.”

She put the paper cup on the windowsill.

“Since the second of May I am not going to be the only one who can declare an emergency opening on this door, and you worked out about eleven seconds ago what that means, and I am going to say it so that you do not have to.

“You will be able to mend it.”

“No.”

“You will be able to **try** to mend it,” said Sanaa Iqbal, “and I know the difference, and you do not, because in five weeks you have not once been refused a thing that you were right about. On the seventh of April you asked me in this corridor in front of two people and I said no in four sentences and you wrote it down in full and you did not argue, and that is the last evidence I have got that you are capable of this. So watch yourself. The moment there are two clinicians on this roster, the argument you are going to have in your own head — the one that says *there is a whole civic term here and I am the one who knows how to mend one* — that argument is going to sound like duty. It is not duty. It is the third of April with a rota and a hole in it, and it is me, standing at a bay window on the fifteenth, watching you not call me.”

“I called you at four minutes past four in the morning on the eighteenth,” said Jonas Mercer.

“You called Dr. Sethi at four minutes past four in the morning on the eighteenth,” said Sanaa Iqbal. “Do not take it off him and do not give it to me in a corridor in April. It was a good night and none of it survives Wednesday.”

---

“Then why am I here,” said Jonas Mercer, “and what is the thing you are not going to let me thank you for.”

“There is no thing I am not going to let you thank me for,” said Sanaa Iqbal. “I am not going to let you thank me for anything at all, and that is not a favour, it is housekeeping, because the second that one of us starts paying the other one back we are two people in a corridor being polite, and I have spent five weeks refusing to be polite in this corridor and I am not starting on a Saturday.

“The thing is the four refusals. I am going to give you the four refusals, and I am giving them to you for one reason, and I am going to say the reason out loud, and I would like it in whatever you write.

“I am not giving them to you because we are all going to work together. I have not said we are going to do anything. I have not said the ninth line. I have said no on the seventh in front of two people and the no stands and it will still stand in June.”

“Then why.”

“Because there are four people in this city who said no to me between the twenty-fourth of March and the twenty-second of April and I know all four of their reasons and not one of those four reasons is in a document anywhere,” said Sanaa Iqbal. “That is what I am handing you. I am handing it to you because I have watched you for five weeks and I have watched what you do with a thing nobody told you, and it is that you go and stand in front of the person who did it, which you did with the Weir and which is why there is a wall in a kitchen with a ring of pencil on it. I have got four of these. I have not got anybody to hand them to. Put them somewhere and do something with them and do not use them on me.”

He did not write them down. He asked her to say them again while he wrote, which she did, and they are these.

**The first, a consultant, on the twenty-sixth of March:** *a second on-call is a rota line and a rota line is money and I do not have a second on-call line, I have a second on-call person, and I have watched a person be the second on-call person in this city for eleven years and the difference between the two is a payslip.* She said that one had been in his mouth for a decade and he said it as though it were a principle and it was a scar.

**The second, a consultant, on the second of April:** *no, because you are asking me to take a responsibility for a rota that a service manager is going to hold, and if the rota fails I will be asked why I named a clinician onto a rota I did not staff, and I cannot be on a rota I did not staff and I cannot be off one I did not create, and I would rather be unkind than be that.* She said: *she was right, and I did not have an answer, and I still do not.*

**The third, the education office, on the ninth of April, which was the no:** *the post is not vacant.* She said: *that is the whole of the letter. It is four words and it is a statement about a fact in a building and not about me, and it is the only refusal in the four that was not about a person, and it is the one that should worry you the most, because it is the only one that could not have been argued with.*

**The fourth, a consultant, on the seventeenth of April, four days before he found the line:** *not from you.* She said: *I have a good relationship with him. I asked him and he said no and he said it as a friend, and what he meant was that he would rather not be a fact in a story that somebody in the west site administration is telling about how they solved a rota. I understood it. I still think he was wrong. I am not going to telephone him and argue with him, and I want you to have the refusal and not the argument.*

“There,” said Sanaa Iqbal, picking her cup up. “That is the whole of it. Four people, four reasons, and one of them is a post that is not vacant, and the post is not vacant because the second on-call line was cut in 2011 and nobody has ever reinstated it, and there is no document in this city that says a post that is not vacant may be made, and I have looked.”

Jonas Mercer read the four lines back to her from the pad, in his left hand, in a corridor, and got one word wrong on the first one and she corrected him and he changed it.

“Now you may say thank you,” said Sanaa Iqbal, “because you have earned it, and then I am going to say the last thing and it is the only thing I actually wanted to say on Saturday morning, and then I am going to go and do a discharge letter that is not mine.”

She did not wait.

“In June there is going to be a room, and I am going to be in it, and I am not going to be there to defend the clinic. I am going to be there because the thing that cost this building forty hours of continuity on the fifteenth of April is a renewal clause that needs two people in a corridor at ten at night, and I signed it, and I have been on the other end of the eight of them that have been read since the second of March, and I am going to have to sit in a room and explain why the clinic’s own witness structure is the thing that was vulnerable. And I am not going to be able to do that as the doctor who was right. I am going to have to do it as the doctor who was in it.

“So do not prepare anything for me. Do not write anything for me. Do not go into that room in June with something I have said in a corridor in April in your pocket. That is the whole of the favour and it is not a favour and I am not going to call it one.”

“All right,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Was that all right or was that the thing you say when it is not all right, because you have said all right to me nine times in five weeks and four of them were not all right.”

“It was the fourth kind,” said Jonas Mercer. “I have got a note from Wednesday with a field on a plate in it about a mortuary gate and the man who wrote it is twenty-nine and it is the same shape as a page of numbers in a block of thirty-one rooms, and I have been sitting on it since Friday morning because I did not think I was allowed to bring it into this corridor and I have just brought it in.”

He put the pad on the sill, open, and she read it standing up in about ninety seconds, and she did not touch it.

“Yes,” she said. “That is the same shape. That is exactly the same shape, and I want to say one thing about it and it is not a comfort.

“The gate keeps opening. That is what your man on the plate has done and he has done it for the best reason available to him and he has done it in nine seconds and it is a good record. The block’s water went thin and it is going to go thin again in May, and the gate will not go thin, and the difference between the two is not honesty and it is not consent. It is whether a person was standing in the yard or whether a page was passed round a table.

“Nobody can write a rule that says a person must be standing. That is the whole of what I have been trying to say to you since March in about nine different ways and I have never once said it in a sentence.”

“No,” said Jonas Mercer. “But you have said it in four refusals and a post that is not vacant, and I have got it in a pad, and it is going in the file. And I am not going to use any of it on you.”

“Good,” said Sanaa Iqbal. “Then go and take your slate off my plate. You are blocking a corridor and there are two hundred and forty people a day through that door and I have spent five weeks telling you that a corridor is a service and not a room, and you are standing in it holding a bar.”

He took the slate off. He put the cover back on. He did not write anything else down, and he did not say thank you twice, and she went off down the corridor towards the east site with a paper cup and no bag, and at the door she stopped and said, without turning round:

“It is nine forty-one. Go and look at your box. The clerk put something in it on Thursday and she has been telling everybody about it since and she is right to.”
