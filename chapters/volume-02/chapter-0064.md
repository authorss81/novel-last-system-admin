# Chapter 0064 — A Council Flood Forum

The Saint Orra transit platform has at least four numbered bays and platform four is the one that matters, and Jonas Mercer was standing at the rail end of platform two at about ten past ten on the morning of Friday the second of May because a man with a laminated card had been on that platform four times in ten days and Jonas wanted to see him do it.

He did not have to wait long.

Gareth Raynor-Hale is about fifty and he is the resilience officer for the **Saint Orra and District Flood Forum**, which is a council committee constituted in March of this year after a review, and which has eleven places on its membership process and about ninety thousand pounds, and which is the body that everybody in a corridor in this borough calls the council flood forum and nobody in an office will say that on paper. He has a lanyard. The lanyard is the first thing anybody sees and it has a plastic sleeve on it and the sleeve has been replaced twice.

“Mr Sarr,” he said, pleasantly. “Can I put the second one to you.”

Kofi Sarr is twenty-one years on that platform. He put a signal pen in his pocket.

“No.”

“It is a different question.”

“It is a different question and you have asked it three times and twice you have asked it at a person and once you have asked it at a poster,” said Kofi Sarr, “and I am going to say no to the question, and then I am going to do the thing I have never done, which is answer it, and you are going to write it down, and I want to say that out loud in front of a witness first.”

Jonas Mercer, forty feet away, at the rail end of platform two, did not move and did not say anything, and Kofi Sarr knew he was there, and that was the entire arrangement, and neither of them made it.

“What is it.”

“The second question is *what happened on the night of the seventeenth on the lowland embankment*,” said Raynor-Hale, “and I have put it to four people this month and three of them have said that they were not there. And I am not asking you were there. I am asking what the platform did.”

Kofi Sarr looked at the bay numbers for a second.

“We closed bay four at eleven minutes past two with a bar across it, on a stated reason, and the reason was in the book and it was that the ramp was taking water and there was a bus due,” he said. “And that was right. And about sixty people got off the last bus that came down that embankment and they walked four and a half miles, because there was nothing else, and about forty of them were over seventy. And I have got the number sixty because the fare machine counted sixty-one and one of them was a man who got on at the wrong stop, and the fare machine counted him too. **Sixty-one. Four and a half miles. Nobody was under seventy that I saw and about forty were over.** That is the answer. Put it down.”

Raynor-Hale put it down. He had a card out before Kofi Sarr started and he wrote it in four seconds in a small neat hand and then read it back, which Jonas Mercer had not expected him to do.

“That is very useful, Mr Sarr, and I am going to say where it comes from, because I do not want you to hear it in a report in September. It comes from an operations review of the lowland that was done on the twenty-eighth of April, and in that review somebody has written *lowland evacuation capacity: unresolved* and left it there, and I have been sent out to fill in the word *unresolved*, and you have just given me about half of it, and the other half is a man at the outfall who has a gauge and did not answer his telephone on the twenty-ninth of April, and I am going to him this afternoon.”

“That is fair,” said Kofi Sarr. “And now answer a question for me.”

“All right.”

“Why did you ask me and not the driver.”

“Because the driver was on a rota for six days and I would have got a rota answer,” said Raynor-Hale. “You were not.”

He put the card away and said thank you and went up the steps, and Jonas Mercer watched him go and understood that he had just watched a competent, honest, well-prepared man do in ninety seconds the exact thing that four months of April had proved nobody could do, and that it had worked, and that the reason it had worked was that a platform dispatcher with twenty-one years in it had decided in a corridor that a fare machine counted sixty-one.

---

Kofi Sarr came off shift at about half past two and Jonas Mercer was on the concourse.

“I answered him,” he said.

“You did.”

“I have said no to that man three times and I have got a laminated card’s worth of no in me about a seat and a badge and an allowance and I have never once had to spend any of it, because he has never asked me anything and I have never given him anything.” He had his coat over his arm. “And this morning he asked me a real question and I had the number. That is the whole thing, Mr Mercer. You do not refuse a man for four months and then find out that the reason you refused him was not principle. It is that you did not have the number yet.”

“That is a hard thing to have found out on a Friday.”

“It is a hard thing to have found out on a Friday and I am going to be at that platform at ten past six on Monday and if he comes back I am going to have to decide whether the answer I gave him on Friday is a fact or a source, and I have not worked out the difference and I have had four hours.”

“There is a difference.”

“There is a difference and I know what it is and it is that a source is a person and a fact is a number, and I have given him a number, and I have not given him a person, and I have had four hours to think about the fact that in about nine months he is going to want the person, and when he does I will have said no three times already, and one of the three will sound like a man who had a number and did not want to give it.” He put his coat on. “That is not a thing I can argue with. That is just the shape of it.”

---

The Saint Orra and District Flood Forum met on Tuesday the sixth of May in a room on the second floor of the civic offices with the windows open, and there were forty-one chairs in it arranged in a horseshoe, and ten of them had a card on the back with an organisation's name on it, and the eleventh was empty and had been empty since the second week of March, and about two hundred people were in the public seats, and the meeting was public, and Jonas Mercer was in the public seats because anybody may sit in them.

He counted the eleven cards on his way in, standing up, at the back, and he counted them twice, and then he read them.

A housing trust. A school. A market. A company — the company being CivicCore, and CivicCore's card said *continuity contractor (non-voting)* in a small hand, which somebody had added with a pen. And six of the ordinary service districts, which are shapes the drawing layer makes out of terms in force and which have names on them and no people in them at all.

That is ten, and the eleventh is the one nobody has been able to fill since the second week of March, and Jonas Mercer worked that out from a sheet of names in about forty seconds and then spent the next two hours looking at it.

**There was no street on it.**

Not one. Eleven places, filled by an existing civic body or an existing organisation, and every one of the eleven required a name, a constitution, a register of members or a minute book, because a council body cannot seat a person. It cannot seat *people*. It can seat bodies, and the nine service districts are bodies because a service district is a shape the drawing layer makes out of the terms in force inside it, and a shape is not a person and cannot complain, and the one that contains the lowland is not drawn at all because there is no term in force on the lowland and a district with no term is not drawn.

He wrote that down in the pad in the public seats with his left hand, and then he put a line through it, because he had written it in about six words and the six words were the volume's whole question and he was not going to have it in a pad in a room with two hundred people in it, even folded, even in his own coat.

---

Gareth Raynor-Hale put the second question to Nora Bell at about twenty past three on the Tuesday afternoon, in the horseshoe, in front of about two hundred people, and Jonas Mercer was in the public seats and heard every word of it.

He did it the way he had done it to Kofi Sarr. He said where it came from. He said *operations review, the twenty-eighth of April, unresolved*. And then he asked about the barrier end and the flood gate at the east end of the Saint Orra bridge, and he said, *I am not asking what happened on the night. I am asking what the crossing does in ordinary weather*, and he read it back from the card, and Jonas Mercer had to put both hands in his lap.

Nora Bell is nineteen years a crossing guard at that end. She is fifty-one. She said:

“I have got a number.”

“Of course you have.”

“I have had it for nineteen years. It is a pencil mark in a book and it is nine hundred and forty.” She did not look at anybody. “Nine hundred and forty times in nineteen years, somebody has stood at that barrier end and been unable to cross, because the barrier was down and the alternative was the bridge and the bridge was shut, or because the crossing was held for an ambulance or a fire, or because somebody was in the road, or because it was dark. It is not nine hundred and forty people. It is a few hundred people, more than once each. The number of *times* is nine hundred and forty and the number of people is not on the page and I have never tried to work it out, because if I work it out then it is a number of people, and I have watched what happens to numbers of people.”

Raynor-Hale wrote it down. He wrote it fast, and he did not ask her a second question, and Jonas Mercer found that he was grateful to him for about two seconds and then stopped being grateful, because Raynor-Hale said, clearly and to the room:

“I am going to say where that came from, and I want it in the minutes in these words, because it is the most useful thing anybody has said to this body in three sittings and I am not going to have it quoted without its source. **That number is nineteen years of one woman's own private record. It is not in any inspection, it is not in the crossing log, it is not in the operations review, and it exists because nobody ever asked her for it and she kept it anyway.** If this body does one thing in its whole life it should make somebody ask a person like that for it on a Tuesday and then not put it in a spreadsheet.”

Two hundred people made a noise. Jonas Mercer did not, and afterwards he was glad he had not, because the noise is what a room does when a thing has been given to it and it wants to be generous, and the noise is not the same as the number surviving a quarter.

---

Councillor Martyn Craddock is forty-four and he chairs the forum because the constitution says the chair is the longest-serving member and he has been on it since March, and he said at about ten past four on the Tuesday afternoon:

“Ms Bell. I have a membership process and it is on the wall behind you and it has a form on it and the form has a box for the name of the body, and Ms Bell, a crossing is not a body, and so you cannot take one of the eleven places, and I have known that since the first draft in March and I have not said it out loud because saying it out loud on the first day would have been discourteous.”

“Then I have had a wasted afternoon,” said Nora Bell.

“No, you have not. Sit down. I have filled in ten of these since March. A trust, a school, a market, a contractor, and six arrangements for water that are in fact not arrangements for water but arrangements for a pipe, and two of those six are the reason the *unresolved* in the twenty-eighth of April review has a seat at this table twice over. **The barrier end at the east end of that bridge has no seat and has never had one and I have known that since March and I have said nothing in a minute about it until this afternoon.**” He took his glasses off. “And the eleventh place has been empty since the second week of March, and I would like that in the minute as well, because a body with eleven places and ten on it is a body with a hole in it, and I have been the chair of a body with a hole in it for ten weeks and I did not put it there and I did not fill it. So. The eleventh place. I am going to leave it open for about a minute and then I am going to offer it to Ms Bell, and I want to say why I am doing that in the open rather than ringing her, because I have her card and I have rung her three times and she has said no twice and walked off down the middle of a crossing the third time, and I have written *declined* on all three, and I would like it said in this room that I wrote *declined* the third time when what she actually did was refuse to decline me.”

“Because you want the number,” said Nora Bell, from the horseshoe.

“Because I want the number and because you have got it and because I am not going to pretend that the two reasons are different,” said Councillor Craddock. “A body that only seats bodies is a body that cannot hear a person, and the first time I worked that out was in March and I have been ashamed of it for ten weeks.”

Nora Bell took the card off the chair.

She had it in her hand for about four minutes. She did not use it, and she did not read it, and she handed it back at about a quarter past four, and Jonas Mercer — who had been watching from the public seats and who had come to that room with the intention of asking one question and no more — understood, at the moment she handed it back, that the badge had been refused for a fourth time, and that a seat had been taken and given back, and that the reason for both of those was the number she had given away twenty minutes earlier, and that by the time she handed the card back the number had already gone into a small neat hand and out through the front of a building.

“Councillor,” she said. “Take my name off the roll.”

“I am not going to.”

“I have just given it back to you.”

“I heard you, and I am not going to, and I want to be exact about what I am and am not doing, because you have just asked me to overrule you and I am not doing that either. You have refused a badge four times and I am not touching the badge. What you have asked me for is a different thing, and you are the only person in this room who thinks it is the same thing, and I am going to tell you why it is not. A refusal of a duty is a refusal, and I would take it and let it stand in front of two hundred people. **A name on a roll is not a duty. It is a record, and nobody can be tried for a record.** and I am going to tell you exactly what I am doing and exactly why, and then you can do as you like to me about it, and I have been a councillor for eleven years and I have never had anybody in a room tell me to my face that I was about to do the unforgivable thing, so I am going to do it properly and slowly.

“Your name is on the roll. It is going to stay on the roll. I am not going to fill the eleventh place, and I am not going to remove the eleventh place, and I am not going to write *vacant* on it, because the minute says eleven and the minute is public and I am not going to be the chair who changes a number in a public document on a Tuesday afternoon in order to make one woman comfortable.

“And here is the reason, and I would like it minuted. **The roll is a list.** That is all it is. It is a list of who came, and I am the chair of a body with ninety thousand pounds and eleven places and a quarterly report, and the only tool I have got is a list, and if I start taking names off it the first time a person is uncomfortable, then this becomes a room where people only put their names down when they are certain, and I want it to be the other way round. I would rather have a roll of people who came and were uncomfortable and left than a roll of people who were certain. Certainty is what got us nine hundred and forty pencil marks in a book nobody asked for.”

He put his glasses back on.

“I am not going to pretend that is not a cost, and the cost is that your name is in a document saying that you came to a flood forum, and I have put nothing next to it and I will not put *withdrawn* next to it, and if you want a correction you may have one in your own words and it will be in the minutes in your own words and not in mine.”

“Write it in my words,” said Nora Bell. “Write: *I took a place at about ten past four and I gave it back at a quarter past four, and I am not sorry about either, and I would like it recorded that a crossing is not a body and that I was not asked whether I wanted to be a body.*”

“That is not wrong,” said Councillor Craddock. “It is going in.”

---

Jonas Mercer had one question and he used it in the corridor at about ten to five, and he used it on Gareth Raynor-Hale, and he got an answer that was honest and immediate and which he did not want.

“Mr Raynor-Hale. Is her name going in the quarterly.”

“Yes,” said Raynor-Hale. “It will be in the attendance column of the minutes of this sitting and it will be in the second quarterly report in September under *community members*. I am not going to soften that and I am not going to explain it. A roll of people who came and left is more use to this city than a roll of people who only come when they are sure, and the chair said it out loud and he was right, and I have spent nine years building things that do not have that property.” He put the card away. “You have got a question and it is a good one, and I would like to say that I do not know that I am on the right side of it, and I have known that since March.”

They came out into the stairwell.

“And the thing I actually wanted to ask you,” said Raynor-Hale, “and I have wanted to ask it since the Saturday, is this. Is the west lane on the body.”

“No.”

“It is not on the body. The west lane is a Saint Orra bridge thing and this is a lowland and estuary body, and it will not be on the body, and I have not put it on the body and I would not have put it on the body.” Jonas Mercer stood on the step. “What is on the body is the barrier end and the flood gate at the east end of that bridge, and they are on it under *crossings and outfalls*, and they are on it because of the twenty-eighth of April review, which is a document you can read, and the second handover in the crossing log from the eighteenth of April is in the public papers of the nineteenth of April, and the issuer of a live term at that hinge is a named person in a dispatch book, and all of that is in the public record and any of it can be quoted.”

“That is a very careful answer.”

“It is a true one,” said Jonas Mercer.

“There is a file on that crossing,” said Raynor-Hale. “I have been in it. I am not supposed to have been in it and I have been, because I have a card that gets me into about nine buildings and somebody gave me the ninth one. There is a clause in it from a year I do not know, called an empty crossing, and it is in there under a heading that says *to be reviewed*, and it says something about keeping a lane clear during ambulance service, and the lane it means is not the lane it means any more, because the whole arrangement changed when the bridge was done.”

“Yes.”

“It is live?”

“It is in the file. It has not been read into anything. It has been in that file since the sixth of April and I have been told by the man whose it is that it is his, that it is not a flood, that nobody is going to pay for it, and that he is going to fight it for eighteen months on his own.” Jonas Mercer stopped, because he had heard himself say the last four words, and there was nothing he could do about it now that they were out in a stairwell, and a man with a laminated card was writing nothing down but looking at him the way a man looks at a thing he intends to come back to.

“He is a named person in a dispatch book,” Jonas Mercer said. “He has a live term at that hinge. The clause is in the file and it is nine days old as a fight and it has no paid hours on it, and that is all I am going to say about it, and I have said more than I said I would, and I am going to go and do my round.”

He went down the stairs and out into a street in the middle of Saint Orra at about ten past five on the sixth of May, and he stood on the pavement for about a minute, and he understood two things.

One was that the clause was already public. It was in a file that a resilience officer with a card had been in, and it would be quoted in a report in September whether Jonas Mercer said a word about it or not, and the only private thing in it was a shop steward's dignity and a bridge dispatcher's pride, and neither of those was in the file, and neither of them was going to be in the report.

And the other was that he had said *he is going to fight it for eighteen months on his own*, out loud, in a stairwell, to a stranger, and that he had done it because Raynor-Hale had asked a direct question and had said where it came from, and that Jonas Mercer was not going to telephone Dev Singh, because Dev Singh had asked him not to be told, and had said that if he was ever told he would deny it and would not be lying.

So the sentence was in a stairwell in Saint Orra and Jonas Mercer walked away from it, and that is what it costs to be the man who asks the right question in a room that has not noticed it is a room.
