# Chapter 0100 — The Third Time

**Friday the twenty-sixth of September 2014, about eleven in the morning, the vestibule of the Old Ward, ground floor.**

It is eleven feet by nine and the council's own estates office calls it *the vestibule* in a schedule and it has a name on a door plate that says nothing, and it is the smallest room in the city that anybody has ever gone into on purpose.

The register of narrowings was on the left of the board and it had **sixteen** entries on it, and it has had sixteen since the morning of the fourth of August, and Raynor-Hale had asked about that in the corridor outside a committee room on the eighth of September and had been told, correctly, by a records supervisor of about fifty: *"Then it is a record of a period. Write that. It is a record of the period between the fourth of July and the fourth of August and it is not a register and it is not going to be a register, and if it starts again in October then somebody has broken something."*

At the bottom of the form there was a blank line. No rule, no caption, no label. Not a design feature. Asked about four times since May and answered four times in the same nine words, and on Thursday the twenty-first of August the answer had been given a fifth time, in a Committee room, by a records supervisor, from the public seats, in front of a chair who had written *no name* on a page and refused to have a box for it.

Beside the register, the ninety-day claims box with its slot in the lid and a printed card on a string round its neck, and the card said it closes, and it closed on the eighteenth of July, and the box is empty, and it stays empty until there is another one, and nothing was ever written into it.

**The one card that was in it is sealed into an envelope, dated and numbered in biro, in a drawer in a records store on a lower floor.** He went down to that floor on the Thursday of the twenty-first, on his way back from a determination, with his hands in his pockets, and stood in front of the drawer for about six minutes, and did not open it, and did not put his hand on it, and went back up.

**The card is about a stair. A stair is a thing a person climbs. A stair cannot be held in a field and it cannot be put in a holder, and there is no form in this city that would hold it, and if there were one then the drawer it was in would be a register and the box would have been the fourth list.** That is the only reason in the world and it is not a good enough reason and it is the reason he has got.

The laminated heat protocol was still on the board with somebody's biro under the return date that said **RETURNED 4/8. SEE COUNTER.**, and above the box, where anybody coming up the ground-floor corridor has to walk under it, the two conditions that have gone onto that board since August: **one in the flat register on the city's own paper, initialled, dated and explained, and one on a strip with nothing on it at all on its face and two initials and one word on the back of it in pencil.** He reads both of them about twice a month and could not tell you today which of the two is hers.

And above both of them, higher up on the same enamel strip, the line that has been on that strip since the first of March, unsigned, and not touched by anybody since March, and not going to be reissued, and not going to be read out loud by anybody, and standing there in a corridor that about four hundred people have walked under in the last six months without anybody stopping.

He looked at it for about a second and a half, the way a man looks at a thing he has decided to keep not doing, and then he read the register of narrowings again instead.

---

The aide found him at half past eleven on the Friday morning with a printed sheet and a lanyard and the same embarrassment she had had a fortnight before, and this time she got to the third line before he said anything.

"They want to know whether you'll chair it."

"No."

"I had to ask."

"**You had to ask and I am going to say the whole of it so that you can write it down properly, and then I would like you to take it to whoever you have to take it to, because I am not going to be in that room on Wednesday and I would like it to be on the record that I was asked and that I said no with a reason, and not that I was ill or that I was on a bench.**

"The board's second sitting is at half past five on Wednesday in the hall on Carrow Lane and there are nine people in it and it is a half hour after the office shuts, and somebody in this city has decided that nine people with one power should be given a chair. **I am not going to chair it and here is the reason and it is not modesty and it is not a rule.

"**A chair is a person who decides which of two things gets heard first.** That is the whole of the job. There is one power in this board and it is that a party holding continuity may be required to answer, and if there is a chair then the chair decides what is asked and in what order, and on the fourth of September a registrar told nine people in a hall at ten past nine at night that they have one power and no way to lose it and that the ninth of them is a person, and **that is already a register with chairs and it does not need a man with a register to make it one.**

"Section eleven does not require a chair. The ninth seat is not a chair. **So the nine of them will take it in turns, and whoever has the floor has the floor, and a person may decline the floor, and that is going to look ridiculous in about a month and it is the only version of this that I am willing to be in the same city as.**

"And the other half, and I am sorry, because this is the part that is about me and not about them. **In about two years somebody is going to use the word *administrator* about me in a room, and Alden Rook said so in a Committee room in June and he was right, and I have got a civic standing that was entered on a register in April that cannot be released by any person, and I have a right now to go into nine people's meeting and say *I will take the chair*, and I would be the wrong man for it, and I would be the wrong man for it in a way that four other people in this borough are currently the wrong man for it.**

"There. You may put that on the sheet in your own words. **No.**"

**Friday the twenty-sixth of September 2014, ten past twelve in the afternoon, a corridor, second floor, the civic offices.**

"Can a body of nine seats remove the ninth seat," said Frances Tolley, "in front of the other eight."

She had asked it once before, in June, in a corridor, and Pell had said nine words and gone and done a stair. She had asked it a second time on the twenty-first of August and got a different nine words. She had read the fourth page since. She was not asking for the fourth page.

Pell had no fan because it was the twenty-sixth of September and there was a window open.

"**No.** The other eight cannot, because the board's one power is to require a party to answer, and a person is not a party, and there is no mechanism in this city by which a body of that kind can require a person to do anything including leaving. **I can require the board to tell her, in writing, what she is answerable for, and I have done that and the statement you read out on Thursday night is the doing of it. I cannot require her to be removed, and I cannot require her to attend, and I cannot require her to answer, and there is no registrar's power in this city that can do any of those three things, and I have looked, and I looked again in August.**

"And I will say the fourth page, because Frances has read it and I have read it twice and there is no reason for a regulation to stay private in a building it is being administered from. **Page four: a board of that kind is dissolved when six of the nine seats fall vacant. Six.** The ninth seat cannot be removed by anybody. **The other eight can be gone in a fortnight — a person who is named without being asked may decline afterwards, and there is no mechanism to prevent that and there is not supposed to be.** So the one seat in that body that cannot be emptied by anybody else is the only seat that can empty it, and the clause was written in about 2006 by somebody who was right, and I have never met them and I have never wanted to meet them less."

He put his hands flat on the desk.

"That is the whole of it and I am not going to say the next sentence, and I want it minuted that there is a next sentence and that I did not say it, and I want it minuted by a clerk and not by a registrar. **The next sentence is about a woman of fifty-four in an apron and I am not going to say it in a corridor to six people and I am not going to have it in a determination and I am not going to have it in a file.**

"**Because if it is in a record then it is a fact about her, and she is findable, and the only thing she has ever been able to say about herself in nineteen years is that there is one of her and she has a name on a lease. And a fact about a woman is a lever. And a lever in the hand of a body of eight that cannot use it is still a lever, and in four years it will be in the hand of a body of eight that can.** I have been a registrar for nineteen years and I have watched about nine hundred claims go out of this office and I have never once been asked to protect a person from a true sentence, and I am not going to start now in a corridor at twelve o'clock on a Friday."

Nobody said the next sentence in that corridor.

Nina Calder, who was standing at the end of it with a folder under her arm and who had read page four on the Tuesday of the nineteenth of August and had given a reason in a records office and had not been argued out of it, said:

"Frances. Put *the next sentence was not said* in the minute, in those words, and put mine under it, because if you do not put mine under it then in about four years it will look like a registrar kept a thing back, and he did not. **A records supervisor read it on the nineteenth and told nobody because a person may not be required even to be told, and I have said that three times this year and I am saying it a fourth time in a corridor and it is still the only mechanism this city has.**"

**Saturday the twenty-seventh and Sunday the twenty-eighth of September 2014.**

It did not rain. On the Saturday morning he planed the surface of the bench and oiled it, and the ink in the grain came up under the plane in a long pale ghost the shape of a date that was four months old and had been on that bench since a Tuesday in May, and it came up because the plane had taken the surface off it, and there was no way of putting it back and no reason to, and he wiped the whole top with his thumb and the thumb came away grey and the wood was clean and the ghost was not.

**It is in the wood and not on it, and that is the only mark this account has produced that nobody in this city can be required to look at, and it is not a record of anything, and in four years a man with a plane will find it and think it is a fault in the timber.**

On the Sunday he did not go up the path. **He had walked past the bottom of it on the Tuesday for forty-one minutes and had walked back forty-one minutes and had not gone up, and there is a fourth line on a kitchen wall on that street with a ring of pencil drawn round a blank space, and a woman of sixty-three refused a pump in advance on the first of June in her own words to a man who came for nothing and not to a pump, and nobody argued, and she is not angry with him, and she has not forgiven him, and he has not been up that path since the first of June and he was not going to go for the price of one paragraph in a report.**

He did not ring the number in the pad. He had rung it once, on the nineteenth, and a woman of about seventy-one had given him nine seconds and had told him not to send her a copy and had not given him her name, and that is in a shop diary and nowhere else, and the number is in the pad and is going to stay in the pad.

He did not go to Carrow Lane. **A letter went into a post box on Monday morning with a year on it and his own name at the bottom and no household in it at all, and there is no instrument in this borough that can tell him whether a managing agent's clerk read it, and there is not going to be one, and Ivy Marsden is seventy-two and he has still never spoken to her, and he has written that down twice now and both times it was true.**

**Monday the twenty-ninth of September 2014, twenty past four in the afternoon, the counter, the records office, second floor.**

She had come up to see the record of the board's constitution, which is one page, and which has a crest on it and no form number and no name box on it, and which Nina Calder had typed herself and left on the counter in a plastic wallet, and there were four other people waiting at the counter on a Monday at half past four and she was the second in the queue.

Jonas Mercer came down the stairs at four minutes past five and found her reading it, and did not stop, and did not ask her anything, and went to the far end of the counter to wait, and Frances Tolley came out of the back with an armful of folders and said, in a corridor voice, not to him, to the room:

"**Mrs Fofana — and I am asking because I have to write it down either way and I would rather you said it than that I guessed. How does it feel.**"

It was a clerk of twenty-two years who had never met her and who had a folder under her arm and who asked it the way you ask a question you have put in a minute, and Yolanda Fofana did not look up from the page.

"It feels like the only one who can get out," she said.

She said it in about two seconds and she said it to the page in her hand and she did not add anything for about four, and Frances Tolley waited, because Frances Tolley waits.

"And I am not going to be able to tell you what that is like from October," she said. "**That is the answer to your question and it is the only true one and it is not an answer to anything else anybody is going to ask me about it, and I would like the minute to say that as well, because in about four years somebody is going to read that I said it and think it was modesty, and it is the opposite of modesty, it is the part I am least able to be brave about.**

"Three of the eight have already said they will stop if it turns into a job, and two of them mean it, and one of them does not and will find out in November. **I have been findable for nineteen years and I have never been the only one who could get out of anything. In fourteen years as a steward I have been the one they ring and the one they do not ring and the one who pays. This is the first thing in my life where the thing I am and the thing I can do are the same shape and it took a regulation from 2008 and a woman at a counter who told a woman what she was holding because a counter is required to answer a telephone.**

"And I am going to be here at sixty-one, and then I will not, and there is no clause anywhere in this city about what happens on the morning after one of them, and I am not going to be the woman who goes to a registrar about it, because a registrar has told me twice in six weeks that he has no power and he is right, and the last thing this borough needs is another person going to a counter and asking to be told what she is."

She gave the page back, and put her hand on the counter for a second as though the counter might move.

"And put one more thing in, and I am saying it once, so that it is in a document and not in a memory: **in about four years one of those eight is going to be in a room with a bad piece of work in front of him and four of the others are going to be in that room, and he is going to say something false and four of them are going to hear it and not one of them is going to be able to make him stop, because the only power this body has is to make a party answer.** And on that day one of the eight is going to stand up and walk out, and it is not going to be me, and whoever it is, it will be the last useful thing that body ever does, and there will be a minute about it, and the minute will not say that anybody made him do it."

There were four people at that counter. A woman of fifty-four in an apron went back down two flights of stairs and out into a street in the rain on a Monday evening, and the clerk typed five lines and read them back and then read them back again to herself.

**Monday the twenty-ninth of September 2014, ten past five in the evening, the shop.**

The bench is four feet eight and it is beech and it has been planed twice this year and it is the bench a man who has a shop works on, and Bea Osunde had shut the roller and had gone and Jonas Mercer had the pad out and the door open on the street for about four minutes because it was that kind of evening.

**He wrote two dates in biro on the bench, and the right hand did it, because on Thursday it had come back for about an hour, and he did not tell anybody that either.**

Not on the pad. On the bench, on the flat, along the grain, in the place where the plane had taken the ink up on Saturday and made a pale ghost of it. He wrote them big, about an inch and a half high, because he was not going to make a woman of seventy crouch down in a shop and read a biro line off a bench.

> **1 OCT**
> **11 OCT**

He put the pen down and looked at them.

**About six hours to the first of October, which is a Wednesday, and there is a clause about notice and a trust in Kestrel Hill that has answered a machine on the fourth of July and a person on the seventh and has not noticed that it has been notified, and a woman with a lever-arch file and a lanyard from 2009 with nothing on it is going to read a notice in about four weeks and do her job, and nobody asked her to and she is not going to be asked.**

**And twelve days to the eleventh of October, which is a Saturday, at ten o'clock at night, on a stair at a hospital, where the renewal begins at seven the next morning, an hour after the money stops.** He is not going to be in that corridor. He and Tomas Renn have each separately and correctly refused to discuss it in a shop and that is now the whole of the rule between them, and it is a rule about not making a plan, and it is the only instrument anybody in this city has produced in five months that is any use at all.

He knew the wood would take it. That was the point of a bench and the point of a Saturday and the point of a plane, and in about four years somebody will sand it back and oil it and the ink is in the wood and not on it and it will still be there, and it will be an inch and a half high, and nobody in this city will know what it is, and there is no rule anywhere that says anybody has to read it.

Bea Osunde came back through at about half past five to shut the lights and saw it and stopped and stood in the doorway for a moment.

"**You have written two dates on my bench.**"

"I have."

"In biro."

"In biro."

"Mr Mercer," said Bea Osunde, "I have been oiling that bench since about 1998 and I have never once been able to get a mark out of it, and that is not because of anything to do with oil. **That is because there is nothing in this borough that can be argued with a mark on a piece of wood that nobody is required to read.**"

She put the light out over it.

She was careful, which is the thing she is, and she left it, and she did not say anything else about it, and in about four years somebody will sand this bench back and find that the ink is in the wood and not on it, and will not be able to read it, and will not be required to, and that is the fourth mark this borough has produced this year that cannot be argued with: after a wet smear nine millimetres high on a chalk board in Gantry Ward, and a line in a room above a building society, and a year on a piece of paper in a post box.

---

He put the pad out on the bench beside the two dates and wrote four lines, standing, with the door open on the street, and the fourth of them is the only one that is about anything he is going to do.

> *Monday the twenty-ninth of September 2014, 17:20. Both dates on the bench. The first is a Wednesday and it is in about six hours and there is a clause about notice in it and a trust in Kestrel Hill that has answered a machine once and a person once and has not noticed that it has been notified, and a woman of sixty-three is going to read a notice in about four weeks with a lever-arch file and do her job, and nobody has asked her to and nobody is going to. The second is a Saturday at ten o'clock at night, on a stair, and a term read then begins at seven the next morning, an hour after the money stops, and I am not going to be in that corridor, and Tomas Renn and I will not discuss it, and that is the rule and it is the only instrument anybody in this city has produced in five months that is any use at all.*
> *The things I have not been able to put down since May, and I am not going to pretend I have, and none of them is mine: a district in this city that the layer does not draw, which is why it cannot be seated, which is why it cannot be on the body that eight of us sat in a Methodist hall on Thursday, and it has water points on it and eleven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds a year of them in a minute in May, and a woman of about sixty who keeps a book in pencil a mile from that wall and will not work it out into a number of people because then it would be a number of people. That is not a question for this shop. It is the next one, and it is going to arrive as a person and not as a form, and I do not know what happens to a person when there is no instrument that can hold one.*
> *And a card in an envelope in a drawer on a lower floor since the eighteenth of July about a stair, and nobody may open it and nobody may write into it, and I stood in front of that drawer on the twenty-first and did not open it, and a stair is a thing a person climbs and there is no form in this city that would hold one and that is the only reason in the world and it is not a good enough reason.*
> *And a paragraph number on a supplier's system that is going to be on a public report in the autumn in front of four hundred and twenty women, and a second tray on a shelf in Gantry Ward with two questions on it, and a man of forty-four in that building who signed a piece of paper in ninety seconds in August and does not know what any of the words in it mean, and nobody is going to ask him, and I have been told twice this month by two different people not to ask him and I am not going to.*

He put the pen down and Bea Osunde came and stood at the end of the bench and read the four lines over his shoulder, which she had not done since April.

"Well?" she said.

"It is the fourth time this year I have written down something I cannot do anything about," said Jonas Mercer, "and the first three times I wrote it down to a woman in a records office and a man with a fan on a windowsill and a woman in a kitchen, and all three of them wrote it down better than I have, and the fourth one is on the back of a delivery note in a shop and nobody else is ever going to see it."

"Then it is the same as the other three with worse handwriting." She straightened the pad square with the flat of her hand, which she has done about four times in five months and which is the closest she gets. "**Mr Mercer. I have been in this shop eleven years and I have watched about nine people come and go who were right about the thing they were complaining about.** You are the first one who is right about the thing and is not complaining. And I am not going to stand here on a Monday evening and tell you that you have done enough, because I have watched that said to two of them and I was one of the people who said it, and both of them stopped, and one of them left the borough.

"So that is my review and there is not one in it." She put her hand flat on the bench, on the wood, about two inches from the ink, and did not touch it. "**And when you have worked out what the next one is, come and tell me, and do not wait until October, because I am the person in this shop who will be here, and I am not a party to anything, and there is nobody in this borough I can ring.**"

"Neither can I," said Jonas Mercer.

"**Then that is the two of us, and one shop, and it is not nothing, and I have seen what happens in this borough when two people who cannot be required to do anything end up in the same room.**" She went and turned the last light off over the door and left the two dates in the grain of the bench, four inches from a ghost that a Saturday's plane had brought up out of the wood, and in about four years somebody will sand it back and find that a man who cannot be released by any person had put the only two dates in this account where nobody is required to read them, and neither of them is his, and one of them is a Wednesday tonight and the other one is a Saturday night, and the bench will keep them for as long as the wood lasts, and the wood is very good.
