# Chapter 0061 — Two Pounds Sixty

There is a figure in the Carrow Lane Housing Association's accounts that Jonas Mercer had not understood until about eleven o'clock on the morning of Friday the second of May, and it is two pounds sixty, and the reason he had not understood it is that he had spent three weeks thinking of it as a price.

It is not a price. It is a transfer, and it works in one direction only, and the direction it works in is the direction it always works in.

He did the sum on the crate in the yard at a quarter to eleven, sitting down, with the accounts summary Nina Calder had given him on Wednesday and had told him twice she would deny giving him, because the summary was a sheet of about forty rows with block letters and a column of money, and forty rows of a column of money is a thing he can do.

Two pounds sixty a week. Fifty-two weeks. A hundred and thirty-five pounds twenty a room for the year.

A hundred and twenty-two rooms. **Sixteen thousand four hundred and ninety-four pounds and forty pence.**

The schedule cost **sixteen thousand eight hundred pounds.**

**Three hundred and five pounds and sixty pence short.**

He looked at it for a while, and then he did the other sum, because he had been doing sums all week and it was the only thing he knew how to do with a number, and he did the other one and it came out at seventeen thousand three hundred and five pounds and sixty pence, and a hundred and twenty-eight payments would have covered the fee with five hundred and five pounds and sixty pence spare.

So the cost of the six people who would not sign, in money, for a year, is three hundred and five pounds and sixty pence, to the penny, and nobody in that office has ever worked it out, and nobody in that office is going to work it out, and he was not going to walk into Carrow Lane at half past eleven on Friday morning and put a number on a table that a chief officer with eleven years in post and no assistant had not worked out herself, which is what she had built a board in her corridor to find out whether he would do.

He took the accounts summary back to Nina Calder on the Friday afternoon and gave it back and asked her for one thing, which was the consumption figures for the four Carrow Lane blocks for the eleven days in March, and she gave it to him, and then she said:

“You are going to work out what sixteen thousand eight hundred pounds bought.”

“No.”

“You are going to work out what sixteen thousand eight hundred pounds bought, and you are going to do it in a corridor, because you always do it in a corridor, and this time there is a woman in a corridor who has been waiting eleven years to be asked that question by somebody who is not a board.” She put the file down. “Go on. Friday afternoon. And do not go on Saturday, because Saturday morning she has a void.”

---

## Two

Marguerite Oyelaran had not opened the file on the desk. That was the first thing Jonas Mercer noticed, and he noticed it because it was the thing he would have done.

“A void,” she said. “Block C. It went on Wednesday night, officially, and it has been going since Sunday, and I have been in it with a clipboard on Monday and Tuesday and Wednesday because there are thirty rooms in that block and twenty-two of them were still in it on Sunday morning.”

“Thirty rooms.”

“Thirty rooms, 1938 build, and the water is on a shared stopcock in a cupboard on the ground floor that belongs to a company that no longer exists in any form anybody can write a letter to. No key. There has not been a key since about 1998. Eight households in C have been carrying the water for thirty of them since 1998 in buckets because that is what you do, and I have known that since 2011 and I have never once put it in a letter.”

She put a sheet on the desk, and it was a letter, and it was dated the twenty-ninth of April, and it was from an agent and it had the word *isolation* in the first paragraph and the date the sixth of May in the second.

“They are cutting the shared supply off on Tuesday,” said Marguerite Oyelaran. “I am not going to fight it and I am not going to represent to twenty-two households that I am going to fight it, because I have looked at the lease and the lease is against me and I would lose and I would lose in about eleven minutes. So I have moved twenty-two households since Sunday morning out of my own void allowance and two of them are in a hostel in Anchor House that I am paying for out of a line that is empty until June, and the block is empty, and the stopcock is going to be isolated on Tuesday, and the freeholder’s agent has asked me to remove my meters.”

“How much is that costing you.”

“Do not do that,” said Marguerite Oyelaran. “You have come in here with that face and I have seen the face in this room before and I want to say the thing about the face before you do the sum, because you are going to do the sum and you are going to do it quickly and you are going to say it out loud, and I have spent eleven years saying sums out loud to boards and I would like to do this one first.

“The sum is this. I have thirty rooms that have stopped paying me two pounds sixty a week, which is three thousand and eight pounds a year — no. Work it properly, you are going to work it properly. Thirty rooms at a hundred and thirty-five pounds twenty a year is **four thousand and fifty-six pounds a year that has stopped coming in**, and I still owe sixteen thousand eight hundred, and what I am still collecting is twelve thousand four hundred and thirty-eight pounds and forty pence, **which means the gap between the sixteen thousand eight hundred I am paying and what I am collecting is four thousand three hundred and sixty-one pounds and sixty pence, and it was three hundred and five pounds and sixty pence on Monday morning before you walked in here**, and there is a void allowance in this association’s budget for the month of October that is nil, and I have said that sentence in this room before to a board and it did not help, and I am not going to say it again to you as though you could do something with it.”

Jonas Mercer did not say anything.

“You are not going to say it,” she said, “and I want that minuted in whatever you are holding, because you have now been in two rooms this month where somebody said a number and you did not spend it, and I do not know a single other man who could manage that, and I am not going to be able to manage it by July.”

---

The thing that happened next happened because a woman came up the stairs, and it happened at about ten past one on the Saturday morning of the third of May with Nell Ardent in the office and Jonas Mercer in the room, and Jonas Mercer did not arrange any of it.

“Can I come in,” said the woman, from the doorway, “or is this a closed meeting, because I have been in the corridor for four minutes and I have a shift at two.”

Marguerite Oyelaran said, “Come in. Sit down. That is Mr Mercer and he is not here about anything and he is not writing anything down.”

“I am writing something down,” said Jonas Mercer.

“You are writing the time down,” said Marguerite Oyelaran, “and that is different and I can tell the difference, and if I could not tell the difference I would not have eleven years in post and a sign on the door that says ask for Marguerite.”

The woman was about sixty. She had come up the stairs slowly and she was wearing her coat.

“I am in Block B,” she said. “Room eleven. I signed in January, at the door, you read it to me and I signed it and I have paid every week since, including this week, and I have got the money for the next eight weeks in a jar, and I would like to come out.”

Marguerite Oyelaran sat down.

“Come out of what.”

“Out of the agreement. I have read the thing. I went and read it — I got my daughter to print it and I read it on the Thursday, and the bit on page four is the bit I want out of. It says my signature is a consent mark and I cannot take it back for a year.” She put her hand flat on her knee. “I would like to come out on Friday.”

“Right,” said Marguerite Oyelaran, and she did not hurry, and Jonas Mercer watched her choose her words in a way he had not seen a person do before.

“Two answers, and I am going to give you both and you are not going to like the second one.

“On paper — in the agreement itself, the ordinary termination clause, thirty days’ notice in writing to the chief officer, the fee stops, I do not pursue any arrears, and the association is not entitled to anything back off you. You give me the notice this morning in your own hand and I will date it the third of May and it takes effect on the third of June. **In a fortnight you are out of the agreement.**

“On the plate — the thing under the block, the thing the money bought, the one with four fields and a rank in it — your signature is graded by date and it cannot be withdrawn until the twelfth of March next year. And I am not able to shorten that. I have telephoned the water people twice about it and I have had a lawyer look at it and the lawyer has been extremely kind to me and has told me that the clause works. **You cannot leave the term for a year.**”

The woman said, “What is the difference.”

“That is the difference,” said Marguerite Oyelaran, “and I am not going to be tidy about it, because you came up those stairs on your own and you have a shift at two.

“From June you are not paying me. I am not going to chase you and I do not want your money. But for ten months after the third of June you are still counted as having consented, and the Lattice grades you, and what your grade does is decide how far down your own water goes if the money ever stops. And it will not stop tomorrow, and it will not stop this year, because I have paid for four blocks and I have not missed a payment in eleven years. So for ten months you are paying me in your name and getting nothing extra, and there is no mechanism anywhere in this country to give that back to you, and I have looked, and I have had a lawyer look, and she used the word *consent* eleven times in an hour and I could not make her use it once more than that.”

Nell Ardent, who had been standing at the window with her back to the room, said:

“Ms Oyelaran, I am going to say a thing and you are going to be angry with me and I am going to say it anyway because I have been awake since Thursday about it. **If twelve of you come out on the same morning, it is a data set.** It is not a protest. Every one of those twelve is a person acting alone and correctly, and put side by side on a Monday morning they are a *withdrawal rate*, and a withdrawal rate is a number a landlord uses and a funder uses and a lender uses, and in about fourteen months somebody in this city is going to be able to say the Carrow Lane block was a bad asset, and they will be able to say it in a sentence with a number in it, and the number will be twelve, and none of the twelve of you will have ever agreed to be one.”

Marguerite Oyelaran turned round.

“No,” she said. “No, that is not what it is, and you are right about the shape of it and you have been in a Methodist hall on this road for a week and I will not have you do that in my office.

“A data set is when somebody else makes the list. **Those twelve people are making their own list, in their own handwriting, on their own doorsteps, on the same day, because on the same day they all found out on the same morning that they had each been thinking about it for a month and nobody had said anything to anybody.** That is not a rate. That is a hundred and twenty-eight people finding out at the same breakfast table what everybody else was thinking, and I have been doing this for eleven years and I have never once seen it and I am not going to stand in a room and tell a woman of thirty-nine with a client list of eleven that it is a data set when it is the first honest thing that has happened on this road since I put a tier on four blocks in March.”

“It is both,” said Nell Ardent.

“It is both,” said Marguerite Oyelaran, “and both of those are true at once, and that is the entire content of my job, and I have not got anybody to delegate it to, and I have got a meeting in ten minutes.”

---

She went out at ten past two for the void and Jonas Mercer and Nell Ardent were left in an office above a betting shop on Carrow Lane with a form on the desk that had ten fields on it and a blank rule at the bottom.

“Right,” said Nell Ardent, sitting down on the end of the desk. “You are not going to tell me why you have not spent her number, because she told me on Thursday that she wrote eleven thousand two hundred and eighty pounds on a board in her own hand to find out whether you would do anything with it, and you did nothing with it, and she is *pleased* about that, and I have been trying to work out what that is for three days.”

“No.”

“No, I did not think you were going to. I have been in this borough for nine days and I have noticed that you are the only person in it who is frightened of a number.” She put her hands in her pockets. “So I am going to say one thing and then I am going to go and read a lease I am not being paid for, and I am going to say it because on Thursday I had it in front of me and I did not say it, and if I do not say it now then I have sat in this room and taken the money.

“In the version of the participation agreement in my folder, which is dated the twelfth of April, the withdrawal date is the twelfth of April next year. In the version in the community room, which is the version that was actually signed, it is the twelfth of **March** next year. Eleven months either way, and nobody was deceived, and I am not going to say that anybody was deceived, and I have been an eleven-year lawyer and I have got eleven years of getting people to sign things and I have never once been able to un-sign one, and every one of them knew exactly what they had signed, and I could not tell you one of them from the last.

“That is the whole of what I have. Take it to your Monday. And do not put my name on anything, because I am acting for eleven households in a building you have not been to, and the second my name is on a piece of paper next to a form of Nina Calder’s is the day those eleven households are a thing I represent on a Tuesday as well as a thing I live beside on a Monday.”

---

Jonas Mercer went down to the meter room at about twenty past two on the Saturday afternoon, because there was nothing else to do and because he had been in a room with a great deal of money in it for two days and needed to look at a pipe.

It is a cupboard off the corridor with a fuse board and a stopcock and a plate on the door, and the plate said what it said in May, which is:

> **A TIER MAY BE CHALLENGED BY A DISTRICT OR BY A NODE IN THE SAME TIER. IT MAY NOT BE CHALLENGED BY A PERSON.**

He had a roofing slate in his bag because he had a roofing slate in his bag every day of his life, and he put it on the step and got his left hand round the edge of the cover, and the plate printed.

> **A NODE WITH A HOLDER THAT HAS NOT BEEN RENEWED FOR ITS TERM SERVES THE TERM IT WAS LAST READ INTO. AND NOT THE TERM SOMEBODY EXPECTED.**

He read it four times, standing up, in a cupboard, on a Saturday afternoon, with a woman of thirty-nine reading a lease two rooms away.

It is not a fault notice. He had had eleven of those in five weeks and every one of them stated a condition, and this stated a condition, and the condition was that **a term is only ever the term somebody last read into it.** Not the term that was bought. Not the term in the folder. Not the term anybody in a room in June is going to assume was in force. The last term that was read, by a holder, out loud, at a handover, to another holder.

Which is the west-clinic rule, and it is the Weir stand rule, and it is rule eleven of the collectors' rules at Bellow Market, and it is the rule that Mara Sowle asked for on the eighteenth of April when she was ninety-one and said *who is holding the door*, and it is in six places in this city and it is in all of them because a set of people found it out the hard way in March.

And it is in **Schedule 4**, and nobody in the city had noticed, and Jonas Mercer stood in a meter room above a betting shop on a Saturday afternoon and worked out that the schedule's continuity term for a node is the term somebody last read into it, which means that a block of thirty rooms that empties on a Wednesday and a stopcock that is isolated on a Tuesday is a node whose holder has not been renewed for its term, and is therefore **serving the term it was last read into**, and **not the term somebody expected** — and the term it was last read into is the one from the eleventh of March, when the block was full.

He wrote it on the pad with his left hand against the cupboard door, and then he read what he had written and put a line under it, and then he read that too.

> *Eleven March: four blocks, four hundred rooms' worth of nothing, priced on a block with voids in it.*
> *Ninety per cent occupancy in March. A hundred per cent from the twenty-first to the twenty-eighth of April, because of the surge, and the association took people in, and everybody was full, and the water went up thirty-one per cent against a term sized on eleven days in March when nobody was here.*
> *So the term is sized on a year in which these blocks are half empty and the bill is being paid in a year in which they are full, and the difference is not mine and is not hers. It goes on the metered recharge. It is in April's recharge. It is in four invoices going out on Wednesday to a hundred and twenty-eight people and it is not in a letter.*

And underneath, because he had learned in April that you write the whole sentence or you have not written anything:

> *Nobody in this city is going to stop me saying that number on Monday. And it is eleven thousand pounds and a bit a year for six points of water, and this is a hundred and twenty-eight rooms going up thirty-one per cent for eight days in April, and both of those numbers are true, and one of them is a fact about a district and the other one is a fact about a day, and if I say the first one in a room on Monday somebody is going to use the second one to bury it. So I have to be the person who says both, or I have to say neither, and I have not worked out how to do that and I have until Monday.*

He put the cover back on and went up the stairs and told Marguerite Oyelaran on his way out that the service riser at Saint Orra west was a wet riser, and she said *is it*, and he said *I do not know why I said that*, and she said *you said it because you have been in a cupboard*, and let him out.

---

There were twelve of the hundred and twenty-eight who had asked to leave the agreement by the Saturday afternoon, and eleven of those twelve had done it in one morning, in their own handwriting, in their own doorways, in the same hour, on Tuesday the twenty-ninth of April, having each been thinking about it since the Friday before.

Twelve of them will stop paying on the third of June, which is a fortnight away, and their notices are in a drawer in the back room, dated and initialled, and every one of the twelve is still on the rent roll and still on the meter recharge, and I have not told any of the twelve that the other eleven exist, and I am not going to. There is a figure in the middle of this and it is not the figure anybody is frightened of, which is that the association is collecting **twelve thousand four hundred and thirty-eight pounds and forty pence** a year off four blocks it is paying sixteen thousand eight hundred for, and the freeholder's agent is going to cut the water off the last block with any people in it on Tuesday the sixth of May, and in eleven weeks it will be worse and not better, and the void allowance is nil in November as well as October.

Nell Ardent said a mass withdrawal is a data set.

Marguerite Oyelaran said it is a hundred and twenty-eight people finding out on the same day.
