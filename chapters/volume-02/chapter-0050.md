# Chapter 0050 — Six Days Is Not a Term Any More

The renewal at Saint Orra west on the Monday night of the twenty-first of April was the shortest thing in the building and nobody made a ceremony of it.

Ezra Mbeki read the five lines out loud in the middle of the corridor at ten o'clock and Ruth Nwosu read them back and Mina Okafor read them back a second time, because it is her job and because she has said so twice to two different people and is not going to stop saying it. Then Ruth signed the box and Ezra signed the box and the clerk wrote the times down.

What they made was a term of thirty-two hours, running to six o'clock on Tuesday morning, and it was the fourth short term that corridor had run in nine days, and there was a reason for it, and the reason was in the handover book in Ruth Nwosu's handwriting and it was one line: *no six-day term until the rota is built again.*

Nobody had built the rota again. The surge had taken a night's rotas out of three weeks of them and four porters had done four nights each that they were not rostered for, and a woman of sixty-three had been on her fourth consecutive shift on the night of the seventeenth, and nobody in that building had sat down since the Saturday and written out six days.

Jonas Mercer read the handover book standing in the corridor at about half past ten and did not say anything about it.

It was the first time in five weeks that the door had been somebody else's problem, and he went home at eleven with a bag over his shoulder and lay in the room above the shop and could not sleep, and got up at half past four and drove to the hospital because he did not trust the length of that term and there was nothing else to do at half past four in the morning.

The plate in the middle of the corridor at six o'clock on Tuesday morning had one new line on it under the five, and the five were still there, and the new line was in the same flat register with a full stop after each word.

> **THIS NODE IS NOT ON A CONTINUITY SCHEDULE. A NODE NOT ON A SCHEDULE IS NOT DISCONNECTED. IT IS DEFERRED.**

He read it four times standing up.

It is not a fault notice. He has had eleven notices in five weeks and every one of them stated a condition and he has learned the difference between a condition and a piece of administration the way a man learns the difference between a wound and a scar. This said a condition. *It is deferred.* It did not say by whom, or until when, or what deferred meant, and it did not say what a schedule was, and there is no line in any manual in this city called a continuity schedule because the manual for the Saint Orra agreement is a 2019 document and a schedule is added to a document by a later document.

It was nine minutes' walk from the care entrance to the service manager's office and Jonas Mercer did it in about four, because he was carrying a plate cover in his left hand and his right hand would not close, and he could not have made himself take nine minutes over it at half past six in the morning.

---

Gunnar Halvorsen had been awake since four and had a printer's output on his desk with a staple in the corner and two telephones on a moving arm, one of which had rung four times between five and half past six.

“Sit down,” he said. “Do not sit down. I have got about ninety minutes before the seven o'clock and then it is the day team and I am not doing this twice. Read that.”

It was four pages, and it had a header that said it was an assessment and not a notice, and Jonas Mercer read it standing up.

> **CIVICCORE CONSORTIUM — CONTINUITY ASSESSMENT, SAINT ORRA WEST SITE CHAIN. Paragraph 11, Saint Orra service agreement, 2019: a node is in failure where its civic term has not been continuously in force for more than twenty-four consecutive hours.**
>
> **The following nodes on the Saint Orra west site chain are in failure: 6-44 mortuary van gate; staff stair 2; car park barrier 3; service riser 1. The care entrance 6-41 is not in failure.**
>
> **A node in failure is an unfunded node. An unfunded node is not disconnected. It is deferred, and a deferred node serves its last verified essential use, and if none is recorded, the node's maintenance. A node in failure may be placed on a continuity schedule by the district in which it stands, on payment of a continuity fee, at a tier. The order of tiers is set by the fee. A higher tier outranks a lower tier in any precedence question. An unfunded node is outranked by every node that is not.**
>
> **Fee for the 2014–15 year: Tier 1 £1,880. Tier 2 £4,200. Tier 3 £11,400. Per node.**
>
> **The party holding the standing continuity authorisation may place nodes on a schedule at any time.**

“Four thousand two hundred pounds,” said Gunnar Halvorsen, “is what I have got, and it is what I have got until the end of the financial year, and I have said that sentence out loud to about four people this week and three of them were in this building.”

“That is Tier 2 on one node,” said Jonas.

“That is Tier 2 on one node,” said Halvorsen, “and Tier 2 on the four of them is sixteen thousand eight hundred, and Tier 1 on the four of them is seven thousand five hundred and twenty, and I have four thousand two hundred pounds and a service riser, a stair, a car park barrier and a mortuary gate, and the last one of those is the only one of the four that anybody in this city would be frightened to have described in a newspaper, and I have got until midnight on Saturday night to say which one it is.”

He said it standing up, with his coat still on, and it was not a rhetorical question, and Jonas Mercer understood — standing in that office at ten past six on Tuesday morning with a bag over his shoulder and four pages on a desk — that a man he had known since the second of March had just asked him a money question.

He had been asked a technical question at about a hundred and forty hinges in five weeks. He had been asked a moral question in a net-loft kitchen and on a slipway and in a committee gallery. Nobody had ever asked him which of four things was worth four thousand two hundred pounds a year, and the reason nobody had ever asked him was that the answer had never been a technician's, and Halvorsen had known that, and had asked him anyway, because Halvorsen had been in post nineteen years and had run out of people to ask.

“No,” said Jonas Mercer.

“No which.”

“I am not going to tell you which node is worth four thousand two hundred pounds a year, and I am not going to tell you that the answer is all four, and I am not going to do the thing where I say the correct thing about the value of a mortuary gate and make you feel better about a budget.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only thing I have got, and I have thought about it for a second and a half in your office and I would like about forty, and I am going to tell you who should answer it and then I am going to go and do something that is actually mine, and you are not going to like either half of it.”

“Go on.”

“You have a person who stands in that mortuary yard at four o'clock in the morning,” said Jonas Mercer. “I am not a person who stands in that mortuary yard at four o'clock in the morning. I have never been in that yard in my life. I have read the plate on the gate from the pavement and I have read the works card, and the man who can tell you what a mortuary gate is for is not the site manager and it is not a technician and it is very probably not the man who drives the van either, because he has never had to think about it, and if the money decides then he is going to find out what he thinks about a gate in the first ten minutes of the first time he has to use it, and he is going to be standing in the rain with a body, and that is when the answer is worth something, and not one minute before.”

“It is not a vote,” said Halvorsen. “You cannot put four names in a box and count them. I have a site manager, a facilities manager who is on a lorry in Gantry Ward until Thursday, a trust chief executive who has a board meeting on Friday morning, and a mortuary porter, and I have to write to one of them by six o'clock on Saturday and I am telling you that none of them is going to be the right one and that is not an excuse, that is the job.”

“Then write to the porter,” said Jonas Mercer, “and find out that you were right, and I am sorry that it is a bad way to find out, and I am not going to be in the yard with you on Saturday night because that would be me again.”

Halvorsen looked at him for a moment and then sat down for the first time in the conversation and put his elbows on the desk.

“Sit down,” he said. “Twenty minutes. I have to write this letter and I would like somebody in the room who has read a paragraph eleven before.”

---

The second half of the four pages was the part that Jonas Mercer could not put down, and he did not put it down, and he took it into a corridor at twenty to seven and read it twice standing up and then a third time in a stairwell.

It is a schedule. That is all it is. Four pages, no letterhead argument, no case to be made. Paragraph 11 of a 2019 service agreement is a **contractual maintenance guarantee** and Oren Pell suspended a determination on the eighteenth of April on the ground that a contractor's contractual definition of failure is not a civic determination of a node's state, and both of those things are true, and the schedule does not disagree with either of them. The schedule accepts the registrar's finding. The schedule says: *we agree that you have not failed. We agree the contractor cannot decide that a node has failed. We have therefore found a different word, and the word is unfunded, and the word applies to the same four nodes on the same facts, and here is what an unfunded node does.*

It is not a lie. That is the thing Jonas Mercer stood in a stairwell at ten to seven on Tuesday morning and understood, and it took him about ninety seconds and it went into him like cold water.

Every sentence in those four pages is true. There is nothing in them to be angry at. A node whose term has not run continuously for more than a day is a node that somebody has to pay to keep, and the schedule says that plainly, and the schedule says the price, and the schedule says what happens if the price is not paid, and the thing that happens if the price is not paid is not that a door shuts.

**It is that a door stops being a door and becomes a maintenance access.**

He had read that sentence eleven times by half past seven and it had not once changed shape.

---

He got to the mortuary gate at about twenty to eight on Tuesday morning with a slate and a bar and a torch and did the only thing in the world he was actually qualified to do, which was to look at it.

The gate is a wrought thing about nine feet wide, two leaves, on a hydraulic closer, with a card reader on the bollard side and a latch on the wall side. The hydraulic was fine. The closer was fine. The closer had been serviced in February and there was a work sticker on it with a date and a name. The card reader drew power and answered. The latch threw and retracted. The card reader and the latch and the closer were all in a good state of repair and the gate was not going to fail.

He got the cover off the plate on the bollard. Four bolts, a cloth, a bar, and the right hand would not close on the spanner so he put a wedge of roofing slate under the cover and held it down with his forearm for eleven minutes, which is what he has done for a hundred and forty hinges in five weeks and which is now the only way his hand works at all.

Inside the cover there were six fields. Five of them had names on them.

**HOLDER. SCOPE. WITNESS. TERM. CLAIM. PRECEDENCE.**

All five were printed and dated and had somebody's handwriting in them, three of them from a commissioning card in 2011 and two of them from a schedule in 2013 that had a caretaker's initials against it, and Jonas Mercer had read them all standing up and every one of them was complete.

The sixth field was under them and it was shorter than the others and it had no printing on it at all, only a rule and a cursor, and it said:

**LAST VERIFIED ESSENTIAL USE: —**

That was the fault.

Not a seam. Not a hole. Not anything the Lattice prints. A field that has been on a plate on a mortuary gate since 2011 with a rule and a cursor under it and nothing in it, because a mortuary gate has had a hydraulic closer and a card reader and a working latch every single day for eleven years and nobody in the city of Nacre has ever had to write down what it is for, and the answer was always going to be *whatever somebody needed at the time*, and that was fine, and it was fine for eleven years, and it is not fine any more at midnight on Saturday.

He stood up in the mortuary yard at twenty to eight on Tuesday morning with a roofing slate under a cover and a field with a cursor in it and worked out, standing up, in the time it took him to get the cover back on, that the mortuary gate at Saint Orra west was in perfect condition and was going to stop being a gate on Saturday night at one minute past midnight, and that nobody in that building was going to be able to prevent it, and that he could not prevent it either, because a field on a plate is not a fault and a fault is the only thing he has ever been able to mend.

Then he wrote in the pad he carries, in his left hand, four lines.

> *6-44. Not a fault. Field six empty. LAST VERIFIED ESSENTIAL USE.*
> *Deferred node serves last verified essential use. If none recorded, the use is the node's maintenance.*
> *I cannot mend a field. I can put words in a field. Words in a field are a record and a record is made by a person who stands there.*
> *Ask Halvorsen who stands in that yard at four in the morning. He does not know either. Ask him to ask.*

And then he put the cover back on, and drove back to River Stacks, and did not go to the shop, and sat in the room above it with the pad on his knee until eleven o'clock, and at about eleven he telephoned the registrar's office and asked whether a record of a last verified essential use could be struck, and a woman said that the registrar would look at it on Friday, which is not an answer and was the correct thing for her to say.
