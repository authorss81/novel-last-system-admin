# Chapter 0114 — A Register With A Motive In It

**Friday the second of January 2015, ten past nine in the morning, the counter, the records office, second floor.**

**NINA CALDER HAS BEEN DESIGNING LISTS SINCE 1999 AND SHE HAS DESIGNED THE ABSENCE OF ONE BOX IN MARCH, AND ON THE SECOND OF JANUARY SOMEBODY PUT A SHEET OF A4 ON HER COUNTER IN AN ENVELOPE AND ASKED HER TO PUT A BOX ON IT.**

She is about fifty. Nineteen years. She has a tray of correspondence on the counter most days of the week and a board in a vestibule eleven feet by nine with a slot in the counter that has been empty since the eighteenth of July, and she is the only person in this city who has said out loud, three times, in public rooms, in the same words, that the absence of a name box is a decision and not an oversight.

The board has nineteen entries on it. It had sixteen in September and it had been still since the fourth of August, and it grew three times in nine weeks — a meter room, a riser space, a yard light — and every one of the three is correct.

**A register that only grows when somebody is hurt is a register with a motive in it. A register that grows when nobody is hurt is a register somebody can hold over you.** She said the second half of that out loud in a corridor on Monday the first of December, and added that if anybody in this borough ever uses that board against anybody, they will not use the meter room. They will use the bulb.

**It is the second of January, and the board has not moved since the twenty-sixth of November, which is thirty-seven days, and thirty-seven days is the longest it has gone since the fourth of August, and she has been right for five weeks, and the bulb is still a bulb, and she has been enjoying it, and enjoying it is not a thing she is allowed to write down.**

---

The envelope came in on the Friday morning post and had a stamp on it and a name on it, and it was addressed to her by name, which is not a thing anybody in this borough does, and it had a reference in the corner in ballpoint and the reference was wrong, and she noticed the reference before she noticed anything else.

She opened it at the counter because that is where she opens things.

Inside there was one sheet of A4, printed, folded once, and it had a heading on it in the sort of face that comes off a disc, and the heading said:

> **QUERY — M. FURNEAUX, resident. Carrow Lane, rear block.**
>
> *I have read the register in the vestibule. I am not writing to complain about anything on it and I would like that said first, and I think you may find that unusual.*
>
> *The register says what was done. It does not say why. There is nowhere on that page, or on any copy of it I have asked for, for anybody to have written down in one line what the reason was for the thing that was done. I asked at the counter on the ninth and the young woman explained to me, kindly, that there is no box for it.*
>
> *I am not asking for a name. I have read your page about names and I understand the argument and I have been careful about it for about a month, which I would like noted.*
>
> *I am asking for a reason. One line. Free text. Against each entry. Not a drop-down. Not a category. What somebody decided and why, in a sentence, in the hand of the person who decided it, dated.*
>
> *Reason for this request: in about four years I would like to be able to sit in a kitchen and explain that board to somebody who is frightened of it, without inventing anything, and I cannot do that now, and the reason I cannot do that now is that nobody has ever written down why.*

**It is a completely ordinary letter. It comes from a man of sixty-one at the back of a block of thirty rented rooms, and it is signed, and it is the most sensible letter anybody has put in front of her in a year, and and it is the single most dangerous letter anybody in this borough has handed to that counter since March.**

She read it four times standing up.

**Because a reason column is a sentence column. And a sentence column is a name box with the serial numbers left off.**

A serial number cannot say anything. `T3/11400/0304, one half, Tier 3` cannot be argued with because it cannot be read out. But *re-asserted on notice of 1 Oct 14 with the consent of no person required* can be read out, and there are nineteen of them, and if there is a reason against each one then there are nineteen sentences on a wall in a room people walk into, and the sentences will be about decisions, and decisions are made by people, and in about four years somebody will be able to say *the person who decided that*, and there will be a way of asking which person, because a sentence has a shape and a shape can be asked after.

**The serial numbers stay because the serial numbers are a machine. The sentences are about us.**

---

**Jonas Mercer came in at about half past nine on the Friday and she had the letter face down on the counter, which she has never done before and did not do on purpose, and he saw it, and she saw him see it, and neither of them said anything about it for about a minute and a half.**

"You have had a bad post," she said.

"I have."

"That is not a question and I have not answered one and we both know how that goes." She put her pen down. "Sit down, Mr Mercer, you are going to want to sit down for this one and you are not going to enjoy it."

He sat down on the wrong side of the counter, which he has done about nine times this year and which she has never once corrected.

"Read that."

He read it. He read it twice, which she was not expecting.

"**He has not asked for a name,**" said Nina Calder. "He says so. He says he has been careful about it for about a month. He has read the page that went out unsigned in the third week of November and he has come back to a counter and worked out, on his own, in his own front room, that the argument I made in November applies to a reason and not to a number, and he has written me a letter about it with his name and address at the top, which nobody in this borough does, and it is the politest thing anybody has put in front of me this year."

"And it is the most dangerous thing anybody has put in front of you this year."

"**Yes.** And both of those are true and they are not in tension and that is what I have found difficult since about eleven o'clock this morning."

---

**He asked his one question. It was nine words and he asked it at a counter, and a counter is required to answer and a counter is not required to make one, and it is the only question he asked that day.**

"Would a reason column have to be filled in by hand."

"**In his letter, yes. He has been extremely specific. He has said *in the hand of the person who decided it, dated*, and he has said *free text*, and he has said *not a drop-down*, and he has thought about it more carefully than anybody who has ever asked me for a box in nineteen years and that is the part that is going to take me apart.**" She turned the sheet round so the heading was facing him. "If it is free text then it is a sentence, and if it is a sentence then somebody has to be in a room with nineteen entries and nineteen reasons, and there are only about four people in this borough who could fill in that column for nineteen entries, and one of them is me, and one of them is a registrar, and one of them is a chair, and none of them can be made to."

"So it is a rota."

"**It is a rota of sentences.**" She said it flatly. "And I have been designing lists for nineteen years and I am going to tell you what a list of sentences is, and I have never had to say it out loud before, and I have been not saying it since Friday. **A list of sentences about decisions is a list of people.** That is all it has ever been. That is what every register in the world is when you take the numbers off it. A serial number is a person you have not met yet."

---

**And then she refused, and she did it at a counter, in a public room, in about eleven seconds, and in the right order, and it was the seventh.**

"**No.**"

She did not stop there and she did not soften it and she did not go and get a form.

"Mr Furniaux. I am going to say four things and I am going to say them in this order, and the order is the whole of the argument, and you may take it or leave it, and you will not be able to take it or leave it more than once, because I have seven of these in nineteen years and I have never let anybody come back to one and nobody is going to come back to this one either."

"Somebody has—"

"**No, you will wait.** One. You have asked me not to add a name box, and I have not, and I am not going to, and I have been telling people that out loud in rooms since March and about four hundred people have added their own names to my page instead of mine, and that was the design and not an oversight. Two. You have now asked for a reason, and a reason is a sentence, and a sentence is a thing a person is found in, and a serial number is a thing a person is *not* found in, and you have thought about that for a month and I have thought about it since the second of April, and I have not come to a different place. Three. The only instrument this borough has ever offered anybody who needs it is a list, and I have watched the whole of that go wrong twice this year, and the first time it went wrong nobody meant any harm by it, and I was in the room. Four. **A form is the last thing a person writes and the first thing an institution keeps.**"

She put the letter down flat.

"**That is the seventh. It stands. There is no version of this in which it is reconsidered, and I would like it minuted if there were anybody here to minute it, and there is not, and that is also the design.**"

---

Nobody argued. Jonas Mercer did not argue, and he has never once argued with her about anything, and it is worth saying that about him because it is a measurement and not a compliment and she has said so to him four times.

"**You could have said no in nine words,**" he said.

"I could have said no in one, and I have said no in one three times, and it is a lie every time, and every one of those three lies has been a kindness to somebody who did not deserve it and cost me a day." She squared the sheets. "You want to know why I said it in eleven seconds and not one. **Because one word is a door closing and eleven words is a door with a reason in it, and the whole of nineteen years is the second one, and in about four years somebody is going to come to this counter and ask me to give my reasons on paper, and I am going to say the same eleven words in the same order, and that is all the difference there has ever been.**"

"**And you have not written it down.**"

"**No. And I am not going to. And I would like you to notice that I have not, and I would like you to notice also that you have not either, and there are two of us in this borough who could put that in a file before lunch and neither of us is going to, and that is the whole of what this borough has instead of a rule.**"

---

**Friday afternoon. The request goes back.**

She put it in an envelope herself, and she did not put a line on the front of the envelope, and she did not put a reference on it, and she did not put anything inside it — no covering note, no refusal letter, no form, no box, no name, no reason, no date of her own. The letter went back exactly as it came, folded once, with a stamp on it, and the stamp was the same denomination as the one that was on it.

"To answer your question," she said, to the room, to nobody, "it goes back on Monday, because there is no post on a Saturday, and it will come back into a tray on Monday and nobody will know that it came back and nobody will know that it went out, and that is not an oversight either, and in about four years he will write to me again because a man does not give up after one, and when he does I am going to have to say no a second time, and it will be in the same words, and it will not be the eighth of anything, because it is not a list."

---

**And the arithmetic of it, which she did on her own pad on the Friday night and which nobody has ever seen, is the reason she has not slept much.**

Nineteen entries. One line each.

**Nineteen sentences, written in four hands at most, in a room eleven feet by nine, over about four years, by people who will have forgotten most of what they were thinking about by the time they are asked why.** And the sentences will be about *consent of no person required*, and *narrowed to its maintenance*, and *the survey instruction lapsed*, and *the bulb is out* — and every one of those sentences is a fact and every one of them is a person's decision and a person's day, and a person who can be asked about a decision on a wall is a person who can be asked about anything else they ever did.

**And the reason column would be a column she has to defend, and she has six refusals in her in nineteen years and she does not call them that and has never once called them that, and a seventh one is going to be very hard to hold on a wall by itself with nothing next to it.**

She wrote the six out once, in order, on the pad, on the Friday night, and then she crossed the list out and wrote under it: *seven. Stop counting.*

---

**Saturday the third of January 2015.** Nothing happened on the Saturday and that is the first time in five weeks that nothing has happened, and she sat in the records office with the radiator on and did about four hours of other work and read a report about a private clinic's residential floor in Kestrel Hill, which was put back on a schedule on the twenty-ninth of October at Tier 2 and which nobody was asked about, and which is one of the four differences between two public records and which she can explain three of.

---

**Sunday the fourth of January 2015, ten past two in the morning, the vestibule, ground floor, the Old Ward.**

Jonas Mercer was in the vestibule because he could not sleep and because it is eleven feet by nine and it is the only room in this borough that asks nothing of anybody at two in the morning.

There is a counter on the left with a slot in it for post, and the slot has been empty since the eighteenth of July. There is a board on the wall at the end of it, and the register on that board has nineteen entries on it, and the nineteenth is a bulb.

**And under the nineteenth line, there is a blank line.** No rule, no caption, no label, nothing in a box, nothing in a hand. It was blank on Monday morning and it is blank at ten past two on Sunday morning, and it is the only fixed point in the building.

Above it, where anybody coming up the ground-floor corridor has to walk underneath, the two conditions from August were still there, and they are still not the same document, and they are still not reconciled, and nobody in this borough is going to reconcile them: **the city's own paper, printed the fourteenth of August and initialled N.C.**, and **sixteen inches below it an enamel strip with nothing on its face at all, undated, unattributed, with two initials and one word in pencil on the back of it, and the word is *not mine*.**

He stood in front of the board for about eleven minutes and did not touch it, because there is nothing to touch, and because a man who puts his hand on a public board at two in the morning is a man who has started something.

**At ten past two on the morning of Sunday the fourth of January 2015 the Saint Orra west term had three hours and fifty minutes left in it, and the gate on that term opens at seven, and the rota has still not been rebuilt, and the eleven o'clock to half past one gap is still published and still unfilled, and Bram Osei's Thursday is still refused on a board in his own words with *no reason I can give* under a *no reason given* in somebody else's hand, and Priya Ranganathan's card is still on that board because she put it there, and the man who signs the weekly verification stopped in October because a job in November has a department in it, and nobody has rung him.**

**And the handover was on the night before, at ten o'clock, and there is no date written down anywhere for that Saturday night, because a person is not required to be told and there is nobody in this borough who is required to write it.**
