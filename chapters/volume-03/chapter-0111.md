# Chapter 0111 — The Second Time Somebody Is Asked, Again

**Thursday the eleventh of December 2014, about half past four in the afternoon, a kitchen off a landing, Wren Court, Salter's Yard, Tideglass.**

The kitchen is eight feet by six. Two gas rings and one of them does not light, and the table with the cloth off because the cloth goes on a Sunday and comes off on the Sunday and she is particular about that in a way she has never had to be particular about anything, and a chair with a wobble in the front left leg, and a mug on the draining board that has been there since Friday.

A room has a minute.

**A kitchen does not have a minute, and that is the whole of the difference, and it is the reason this is the second time this account has gone inside a person who is on no board and is paid nothing, and the reason it is allowed to happen twice in three months and not once in a year, is that a kitchen is not a room and a close one costs a chapter and the chapter is worth it when the person is not in a room and cannot be got into one.**

There is a salt and a pepper and a tin of something and a rent book on that table and a plastic folder of council letters that came in the post on Monday and that she has not opened and is not going to open, because they are the fourth kind and there are only three kinds and the fourth kind is a letter that wants a date in a box.

Her name is Yolanda Fofana and she is fifty-four and she is the ninth seat of a body of nine that stands at seven of nine, and she has been the named steward of a building of thirty-one rented rooms since about 2001, and she has done it for nothing since the fourth of July, and before the fourth of July there was a participation fee, and the fee did not stop because anybody sat down and decided it should stop. It stopped because thirty-one rooms stopped paying it in the same week, in June, and nobody could find the person who had decided that, and she has never told anybody that she was the one who found out first.

She is the only one of those nine anybody can write to. She has said that out loud, twice, in rooms, and both times it was true, and neither time was a boast.

---

The knock was at half past four and she knew who it was before she opened it, because there is one person in this borough who comes to this door and he has been coming since the fourth of September, and she had put the kettle on at four because it is December and because she is fifty-four and because a person who is going to say something to you ought to be able to have a cup of tea while he says it and has not had to ask twice.

"You have not telephoned," she said.

"No."

"You have not written."

"No."

"**Then you have come up forty-one steps to tell me something in a kitchen, and I have made you a tea, and you are going to have to say it in about nine minutes because it goes off at five.**"

He had a pad in his hand and he put it down on the table, face down, which she noticed and did not comment on, and there is a thing about him that she has known about since September and has never once said to him, which is that he is the only person in this borough who has ever put a piece of paper face down on her table.

"**I am going to ask you one question,**" said Jonas Mercer, "**and a kitchen is not a public room, and I know that, and I am going to use one anyway, and I am telling you I am using one, because if I do not tell you then you cannot decide what to do about it.**"

"You have been telling me things like that since September and every single time it has been the right thing and I have never once known what to do with it."

"Yes."

"Then ask it."

He asked it, and it was eleven words, and it was: **whether she had decided what she was going to do about two empty chairs.**

And she put the tea down.

---

**And then she did the thing she has done four times in nineteen years, and she did it before she had finished the sentence he had started, and it is worth setting down what it is because nobody in this borough has ever described it and she has never described it either.**

She did not refuse him. That is the whole of what she is about to do and it is not a small thing and she does it harder every year.

She took about four seconds, and she looked past him at the door of the kitchen, at the landing, at the stairs going down, and then she said: "**You have asked me that in a shop in September and you have asked me that in a records office and you have asked me that in a hall on a Friday with nine chairs in it and two of them empty, and this is the fourth time, and I want to say to you that the answer is the same four times and that I have not once been asked whether I would rather you did not ask.**"

"I am not going to argue with you."

"**I know you are not going to. That is the difficulty and it has been the difficulty since about the eleventh of September.**" She sat down on the side with the wobble in it. "You have never argued with me about anything, and neither has anybody else, and the whole of what that is is that nobody in this borough is permitted to argue with me about a thing I have said no to, and it does not turn out to be the same thing as nobody arguing with me because I would win."

That was true, and she let it sit, and the tea went cold about a degree and a half.

"Now sit down, because you have not got long and I have not got long and the kettle is on a timer and this is a kitchen."

He sat down on the box side, which is the side that is not the chair.

---

"**No,**" said Yolanda Fofana. "**I have not decided anything. I have not decided anything about two chairs and I have not decided anything about that minute and I have not decided anything about a piece of paper in my bag, and I would like it noticed that you have come up forty-one steps to ask a woman of fifty-four whether she has decided something, and that nobody in this borough has ever asked me that, and that the honest answer is that there is nothing to decide, because there is no power anywhere that makes it come out different whichever way it goes.**"

"Then what do you want me to do."

"**Nothing. That is not a mood. That is the whole of the work and I have been doing it since the fourth of July and I have got very good at it and I would like somebody to write down that I am good at it, and I am not going to ask you to, because a piece of paper in this borough is a lever and I have watched two people get lifted off the ground with one this year.**"

She took the mug and looked into it and did not drink it.

"And I have not read the minute of the third of December in a way that anybody in this borough may discuss."

He did not say anything at all to that, and she went on, and it is worth saying that about him, because it is the third time in four months he has gone quiet at the exact sentence and let a person finish, and she has noticed every one.

"**You cannot discuss a minute with me. Not because you would not. Because once two people have read the same minute and talked about it, that is a thing that happened, and then somebody can ask me what I think about it, and nobody in this city is able to stop somebody asking me what I think about it.** I have been on this board since September. I have been in about eleven meetings. Somebody has asked me what I think about a thing in about nine of them, and I have answered, and every single time I have answered I have been a person with a view and I have never once been able to take it back. You cannot un-say a thing in a room. I know that better than anybody in this borough now and I know it because of you and a woman of thirty-four with a trolley and a date on a street, and you did not do that for me, and I have not got a word for what you did do it for, and there is no word in this borough for what you did it for."

"There is not."

"**Then I will leave it there and we will both get on with the afternoon.**"

---

**And then she said the thing nobody has said in a room in this borough, and she said it in a kitchen, and there is no minute for it, and there never will be, and it is the reason this chapter exists.**

"**Listen to what I am about to say and then you can go, and I have thought about whether to say it for about four days and I have decided to say it to a man in a kitchen and not to a room, and that is a decision and it is mine and it took four days.**"

"Say it."

"**A person who is findable is a person who can be asked.**

"That is all of it. That is one sentence and it has been true for nineteen years and nobody has ever said it out loud to me, including you, and you have been coming to this door since the fourth of September and you have asked me nine questions and every one of them started with the word *are* or the word *can*. *Can you. Are you.* Nobody has ever come up those forty-one steps and asked me what it *costs*."

"The what costs."

"**Being the one they can ask. That is what costs.**" She put the mug down. "**And a person who can be asked has to have an answer ready. That is the half nobody has ever thought about, including me, until about eleven o'clock on Tuesday night, and I have been a person who can be asked since about 2001 and I have had fourteen years to get good at it, and I want to tell you exactly how I do it, and I am telling you because you are the only person in this borough who writes things down and I would like there to be one place where it is written that I did it this way on purpose.**"

"Say it and I will not write it."

"I know you will not. **I am going to have an answer ready, and it is going to be no, and it is going to be a very short no, and it is going to be in about nine seconds, and it is going to be about a thing and not about me, and it is going to be the same no every time, and nobody will ever be able to tell whether I have thought about it or not, and that is nineteen years of practice and it is the only thing I have that is worth anything in this building.**"

The clock on the wall of the kitchen, which is a kitchen clock with a picture of a cat on it and which loses about four minutes a week and which she winds on Fridays, said something that was not right.

"**And I have not got one ready for this,**" said Yolanda Fofana.

The landing was very quiet. Somewhere below, a door went, and then another one about a minute later, which is what thirty-one rooms do at that hour on a Thursday in December.

"I have not got one ready and I am not going to get one ready in a room. I am not going to get one ready on a Wednesday in a hall in front of nine people who cannot make me and four people who will not, and I am not going to get one ready for you, and I have not got one ready and I have been asked four times since September and I have said no three times and this is the fourth and I am going to say the same thing and it is going to be the same no, and it is going to be a no about a thing, and it is going to be about nine seconds, and it is going to be the same refusal as the twenty-fourth of September and the reason is the same reason and it stands.**"

He said: "Frances Tolley's is the same shape."

"It is exactly the same shape and it is a completely different refusal and you may put those two next to each other if you want to and I would rather you did not, because the day two people who have said no start being a pattern is the day somebody in this borough writes *policy* on a card and puts four names under it."

**Nobody argued with her. There was nobody in the room to argue with her, and that is also the sentence.**

---

They sat there until about ten to five, and the tea went cold, and neither of them mentioned it.

"**The letter,**" said Jonas Mercer.

"**It is in the bag.**"

"I know where it is. I am not asking where it is. **I read that sheet standing up on a trestle table on the third of December and I have not read it since and I am not going to read it again, and if you want me to have it you cannot give it to me, and if you do not want me to have it then it goes on a table in a hall, and both of those are available to you and neither of them is available to me.**"

"I have read it." She said it flatly. "I have read it three times and it is one side of A4 and the handwriting is not a clerk's and she has put her name at the bottom of it in her own hand on purpose, and I have understood every word of it, and I have not decided what to do about it and I have told you I have not decided and I am not going to decide in a kitchen to a man who writes things down."

"You do not have to decide in a kitchen."

"**I have to decide somewhere, and there are about four places in this borough where a decision like that can be made and three of them have a minute.**"

---

**And here is the thing about her that nobody in this borough is entitled to be told, and that is not a rule anybody made, and that she has said out loud twice that she is not going to be told anything about, and that is why it is true.**

She is not the only door in that building. She is the only *person* in that building anybody can write to, and that is a different thing and it is the only one she knows about, and the other one is that a body of nine seats has one power, which is to require a party holding continuity to answer, and that she is the only one of those nine who cannot be removed by any of the other eight, and therefore the only one whose departure could empty the body, and there are three people in this city who know that and none of them is in this kitchen.

**She does not know it. She has said out loud twice that she will not be told, and she is right, and nobody in this borough is going to tell her, and there is no instrument anybody could produce that would make it right to tell her, and that is the correct position and it has been the correct position since the second of June.**

And nobody in that building of thirty-one rented rooms may be told that she is a door, and nobody in this borough has ever said it to her, and she is fifty-four and unpaid and findable, and on Thursday the eleventh of December 2014 she told a man in a kitchen the sentence that nobody in this borough has said in a room, and he did not write it down, and there was no word between them at the end of it, and that is the whole of what happened in the kitchen.

---

He went down the forty-one steps at five o'clock because she told him the tea was going in the sink whether he had finished it or not, and she stood in the doorway with her arms folded, and the stairwell door has been broken since March and nobody has had it fixed.

At the bottom he stopped and looked up, and said, "Mrs Fofana—"

"If you are going to say something else I shall come down these stairs, and I am fifty-four, and I have come down them once already tonight."

"I was going to say that the register has not moved since the twenty-sixth of November and there are nineteen entries on it and the blank line is blank, and I do not know what your two chairs have to do with that, and I thought you would want to know it on a Thursday."

"Right," said Yolanda Fofana, from about four floors up. "**Then that is what the fourth time was for. Go away.**"

---

**And the piece of paper stayed in the bag, in a handbag, on a table in a kitchen off a landing, in a building of thirty-one rented rooms in Tideglass, for another five and a half weeks.**

**It is the only piece of paper in this borough that is addressed to a room and is signed, and it is the only one anybody in this borough has ever had in a handbag, and it did not go in a minute, and it was never going to.**

**And on the twenty-first of January a sheet of A4 went into a Methodist hall in daylight, in the hand of the woman it was about, and it came back out of that hall with nine seconds on the top of it and her name and the street and the date, and the letter never went near the hall at all: it has been in a handbag in a kitchen off a landing since the third of December, and neither of those two things was a decision anybody took, and both of them are the only ones available.**
