# Chapter 0102 — A Person Who Is Not Counted

**Wednesday the twenty-ninth of October 2014, twenty past six in the evening, the furniture workshop, Wren Court, Salter's Yard, Tideglass.**

There are two heaters in that workshop and only one of them works, and the working one is at the back by the saw bench, and so the eleven of them sit in the back half with their coats on and their hands round mugs, and the front half is cold and dark and smells of sawdust and linseed oil that has been there since about 2009.

Cynthia Akintola arrived at ten past six and put her bag on the third chair from the left, which is the chair she has sat in since about 1996, and took the tin out from under her arm, and set it on the bench.

**The tin is a biscuit tin with a picture of a dog on the front of it that came out of somebody's Christmas in about 1988.** It is nine inches by seven and it has a rubber band round it, and under the rubber band, on the lid, there is a piece of card with a number on it, and the card is not the number of money.

She unscrewed the lid, counted what was in it without taking it out — thumb along the rims of the coins, then the notes, then the notes again — and wrote the figure on the back of a wage envelope on her knee, and then did a sum she has done every Wednesday for nineteen years, and then took the card out from under the rubber band and looked at it for a while.

**Nine hundred and eighty-six.**

She knows what the tin should hold if it has had £11 a week put into it every week since the Wednesday in March 1995, and then £13.60 from June 2014, and she knows both numbers and she has never asked anybody why one of them moved. It is not the number on the card. The number on the card is what she has actually written on the card, on and off, since March 1995, and it is a number of Wednesdays, and it has been on the card since about 1996, and she has never once put a pound sign on it or written a note about what it is.

**The two numbers do not agree and they have not agreed for years and the difference is about four hundred and thirty pounds.**

She knows exactly how the difference happens, because it is her arithmetic. She counts a week whether or not the money went in. If somebody is away, or short, or late, or has not been in the building for a fortnight with a relative, that Wednesday is still a Wednesday and it still goes on the card. There have been perhaps thirty of those in nineteen years. Some of them were paid later and some of them were not.

**Nobody has ever asked her about the difference. It is hers. It is the only thing about that tin that is hers, and she has never mentioned it to the steward and she is not going to.**

"Anybody want a tea," said somebody behind her.

"I'll have one when the count's done," said Cynthia Akintola.

She put the card back under the rubber band and screwed the lid on and put the tin on the shelf, and then sat down, and then the door at the front of the workshop opened and let in about four seconds of cold and let in a man.

---

He was thirty-one and he had a clipboard and a soft cap and a jacket with the name of a firm on it that Cynthia Akintola could read, and he stood in the front half of the workshop in the cold for about four seconds while his eyes went round the room and found eleven people and did not go to any of them.

"Sorry to come in on a Wednesday," he said. "I know that's a bad time. Is there anybody who— **actually, no, never mind. Is there anybody here who is responsible for the water.**"

The room did the thing rooms do. Two people looked at two other people. Somebody said, "Yolanda's upstairs and she'll be down in a minute," and somebody else said, "Yolanda's not, she's at the meeting on Carrow Lane tonight," and the first person said, "Right," in the voice of a person who had made a mistake and was going to have to be the one to make it again.

**Cynthia Akintola put her mug down.**

"He's not asking you," said the woman on the end of the bench, who was sixty-eight and had been in that building since 1988 and had signed since the first week. "He's asking whoever does it."

"Everybody does it," said the man. "That's the point of the form."

That stopped the room, and it stopped it not because it was clever but because it was the plainest thing anybody had said in that workshop in about four years.

He came to the back half then, and he did not sit down, and he put the clipboard on the bench on top of a stack of offcuts, and turned it round so it was facing the room, and it was facing the room because he had been turned round by somebody in about 2009 who had never thought about it again.

"Three things," he said. "That's the whole form, and I've got forty-one of them in the van, and I don't suppose anybody has ever been told that. **One: the state of the supply at this point. Two: the number of dwellings drawing from it. Three: the name of the person responsible for the supply at this point.**"

"State's fine," said Cynthia Akintola. "There's water. It comes on about six in the morning and it goes off about half nine at night and if it's not doing that then somebody's poured something in the tank, because there's a tank on the roof, and the tank's got a float on it like a loo cistern and I have been up that ladder looking at it about four times in nineteen years. It comes on and it goes off and that's the state of it."

He wrote that down. He wrote it in the box marked one and he wrote it in about nine words and it took him about eleven seconds, and he did not ask her a single follow-up question, and she noticed that.

"Two," he said.

And Cynthia Akintola took her hands off the bench and put them in her lap, and it took her about four seconds, and about nine of the eleven people in the room watched it happen and not one of them said anything.

"**No,**" she said.

The room did not move.

"**I can tell you what is in the tin and when it went in and I cannot tell you how many of us there are, and if I could it would be a list, and there is not going to be one, and nobody has asked me for one in nineteen years and I am not starting on a Wednesday.**"

That took her about nine seconds and she did not raise her voice once in it, and she did not stop in the middle of it, and at the end of it she picked her mug up and had the tea, which is the only punctuation she put on it.

The man with the clipboard did not argue. He did not ask why. He did not put his head on one side. He looked at the second box for about two seconds and then he wrote **NO** in it, in capitals, and it is the only capital letter on that page.

"That's fine," he said. "I've had that at about thirty of them. It's not — I mean, it's not a problem, it's just a form, and I've got thirty nones on forty-one sheets and there is no box on it anywhere for thirty."

"Right," said the woman of sixty-eight.

"Right," he said. "Three, then. The name of the person responsible for the supply at this point."

"I don't know," said Cynthia Akintola. "**And that one isn't mine to give you either, and I'd be doing it to somebody who isn't in this room, and she's got a shopfront and a lease and she is the only one of us anybody can write to, and I am not going to be the reason somebody wrote her name on a form in a van.**"

"That's fair," said the man.

Nobody in the room said anything for about four seconds, and then the woman of sixty-eight said, "It's a Wednesday, love," in the voice of a person who was not asking for anything, and the man said, "I know it is. Sorry. I'll be off, then," and picked the clipboard up, and stopped, and said:

"Actually — do you want me to write that you told me no? Because there's nowhere to put it, so I just want to say, I am writing *the resident declined to give a number* and I am writing it in my own file and not on this, and I'll tell you what I am writing so you can stop me."

"You can write it," said Cynthia Akintola.

"**I am writing it like this. One thing, one time, one place. What was asked and what was given and what was refused. And then a line that says what it isn't — that it isn't a refusal of anything, and it isn't a number, and it doesn't establish that anybody here is or isn't anybody. And that you can refuse, and I'm writing that I asked you twice and you said no twice.**" He did not look at her while he said it. "That's it. That's the whole of it. It's on my own pad and it goes in my own file and there is no box on your form because there is no form."

"You could put a box on it," said somebody at the back.

"No," said the man. "**I have had this form for three years and I have never once known what to do with a no, and that is not the council's fault and it is not mine, and if I put a box on it then I have made a register of people who will not say things, and I am not going to do that on a Wednesday either.**"

He put the cap on and went out into the cold, and the door shut, and the room carried on.

**And the job did not.** That is the part that would be got wrong afterwards. The two of them who had looked at each other went back to what they had been doing. Somebody put a cup on the saw bench and it was the wrong cup and nobody minded. The man of sixty-one who does the second floor asked somebody whether the van was still outside and was told it was, and said *good*, and then said, "I told him to come Thursday," and nobody asked what he had told him or what it was about.

Nobody thanked anybody. Nobody said anything about what the man had asked. It took the room about four minutes to go back to where it had been and it never entirely got there, and the tin sat on the shelf with a picture of a dog on the front of it and a card under a rubber band, and **nine hundred and eighty-six was on the card and four hundred and thirty pounds of a difference was in the air of that room and did not go anywhere.**

---

She stayed until about half eight, which is what she does, and she did the tea things at the end because the tea things are also hers and have been since about 1996, and the woman of sixty-eight and the man of sixty-one and the two of the younger ones stayed for about another hour, and there were nine of them and then there were seven and then the heaters went off at half eight because they go off on a timer.

**Cynthia Akintola is sixty-one.** She was sixty in the March of this year. She has been in Salter's Yard since 1995 and in that room since about 1996 and she has never had a job in it and has never been asked for one and has never put her name to anything at all except about four hundred sheets of paper with a figure on them.

There is a board of nine seats in this city now, since September. She knows it exists. Everybody who comes up that stair knows it exists, because the steward says the word *board* about once a fortnight and then says something else.

**The steward is the ninth seat. The steward said so herself, on the stair, in words, and she said the whole price of it out loud, and one of the things she said out loud was that she was not going to be paid and would not be paid until she was sixty-one, and that after that she would not be.**

Cynthia Akintola did not say anything about that on the Wednesday and she has not said anything about it since, and she has thought about it perhaps four times, and the four times are all the same thought and it is this.

**The steward gave it up at sixty-one. Cynthia Akintola is sixty-one in the March.**

There is nobody she could say this to. That is not self-pity, and it is not a complaint, and it is not a claim. It is a fact about a Wednesday in a workshop, and it goes down the same as the four hundred and thirty pounds and the nine hundred and eighty-six, and it stays hers, and she is not going to be the person who puts it in a letter to a council about a board of nine that has one power and no money and cannot require anybody to do anything at all.

What she did do, on the walk up the stair, was take the card out from under the rubber band and look at it again, and then put it back, and then say out loud, to nobody, in a corridor, four words:

"**It's weeks. It was always weeks.**"

She had said it to the man in the van. She had told him, as it turned out, before he had finished asking. She had put a number into a room of eleven people that was a number of Wednesdays, and it was the only number about that tin that anybody has ever been told, and the man had written **NO** in a box, and the two of them were the same fact seen from two ends: **she could not give him a number of people, and the number she had was not a number of people, and she has been keeping the right one for nineteen years without knowing that anybody in this city has anything to say about it.**

Nobody had said anything. That was the correct outcome. A person may refuse to be counted and the count then says so, and it had said so, in a van, on a clipboard, in capitals, and there was no box anywhere in this city for the other half of it, which is the half where the person who counted nothing goes home at half eight to a flat with a mug in it and a tin on a shelf.

She got in. She had not eaten since one o'clock and she was not going to eat now. She washed the mug out because the mug goes back on the shelf and the shelf is hers, and she put it upside down, and she stood for a second in her own kitchen with the light off.

**And that is what a service floor is made of.** Not a determination and not a determination's price. A woman of sixty-one with a biscuit tin and a number of weeks on a piece of card under a rubber band, and a difference of about four hundred and thirty pounds that nobody has ever asked her about, and a stopcock that turns, and a plate in a cupboard that has said since before the war that the point is not the payer's.

**She is on no board. She is in no field. She is on no rota. She has never been asked anything by anybody, including in a hall, and on Wednesday the twenty-ninth of October 2014 she refused a form in nine seconds and the room carried on and the job did not and the refusal cost nobody anything at all, and it was the cheapest refusal anybody in this city has produced this year, and there is no instrument anywhere that can record it, and the man who recorded it did it on his own pad with no box on it and did not know that he had written the fourth page.**

**And the woman who has been counting since 1995 does not know that is what she has been doing.**
