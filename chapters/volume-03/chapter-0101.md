# Chapter 0101 — What Came Back From Kestrel Hill

**Monday the twentieth of October 2014, twenty past nine in the morning, the vestibule, ground floor, the Old Ward.**

It is eleven feet by nine and there is a board on the wall at the end of it and a counter on the left with a slot in it for post, and the slot has been empty since the eighteenth of July.

The register of narrowings was on the left of the board and it had **seventeen** entries on it.

It had sixteen for the whole of August and the whole of September, and on Friday the twenty-sixth of September a records supervisor had stood in this corridor and said, correctly, that a register which only grows when somebody is hurt is a register with a motive in it, and that if it started again in October then somebody had broken something. Somebody had broken something, and it was not a person, and it was a sentence and a plate, and it had taken three weeks.

Jonas Mercer stood in front of it with his hands behind his back and read the seventeenth line four times.

> **17.** *2 Oct 14. Node, Meter Room 3, Salter's Yard, Tideglass. Building of 31 rented rooms. No civic address. Claim ref T3/11400/0304, one half, Tier 3. Re-asserted on notice of 1 Oct 14 with effect from that date. Consent of no person required. Holder field: none. Party: as re-asserted. Class per determination of 28 Aug 14. Entered on a public form by a person. No name.*

The last line was not part of the form. There was no line on the form for it and there had never been one, and Nina Calder had said out loud in a Committee room in August that the absence was a decision and not an oversight, and had said it twice, and had meant it every time.

**A record of shapes with no name box on it, and a person has written the sentence *entered on a public form by a person* at the bottom of it in a hand, because the space was there and because nobody in this city has ever once refused a space that was there.**

He worked out which building it was in about eleven seconds.

He had not asked anybody. He had not opened anything. He had read a line that names no person, and it had given him a street he had walked to twice and not gone up, and a woman in an apron on a landing, and a meter cupboard under a stair with a plate on it that had said since before the war that the point is not the payer's. Eleven seconds, standing up, in a corridor, with his hands behind his back.

**That is what a form is for, when a form is written by a careful person: it does exactly what it was designed to do, and the care was in the design, and the design was right, and it has been used to find a building, and there is nobody to blame in the whole of this except a man with a register who asked for a state and got one.**

Above the register, where anybody coming up the ground-floor corridor has to walk under it, the two conditions that went onto that board in August were still there. The one in the flat register on the city's own paper, printed 14/8 and initialled N.C., with a paragraph in a Committee room explaining why a records supervisor initialled hers. And sixteen inches below it the strip with nothing on its face at all, undated, unattributed, with two initials and one word in pencil on the back of it that somebody wrote there and the word is *not mine*.

**Two conditions on one board with opposite provenance, and both of them still standing, and there is no document anywhere that says how they are related and there is not going to be one.** The 1 March line was higher up the same enamel strip and had not been touched in seven months and was not going to be read out loud by anybody.

And at the bottom of the register, under the seventeenth line, there was a blank line. No rule, no caption, no label. It was blank on Monday morning and it was blank on Monday afternoon and it stays blank.

---

He asked his one question at the counter at ten past nine, with a queue of three behind him, and it was the only question he asked anybody that morning, and it was four words and it had taken him about eleven seconds to make it that short.

"Who entered line seventeen."

The woman at the counter was about thirty-four and had been there two years and looked at the board, and then at him, and then at the board again, and she answered the way a counter answers, which is to say the true thing, because a counter is required to answer and a counter is not required to make one.

"**Anybody can.** It's a public form. It's on a public board in a public corridor. There is a signature box on the other side for a person entering a state at the request of a party, and that one is over there, and that one is dated and initialled, and nobody has used it since July." She turned the clipboard round. "There's no box for the person who picks up a biro. There is no box and there is not going to be one, because the records supervisor designed it that way in March and has said so out loud three times."

"And you did not ask me when it went on, so I am going to say that anyway, because it is the true thing and a counter is required to answer and a counter is not required to make one. **It went on on Wednesday morning.** It was not on it on Monday and it was on it on Wednesday and I did not see anybody do it, and I am on this counter from eight until half past four and I was on it on Wednesday, and if you were asking me whether I did it then no, and if you were asking me whether I saw who did then I did not, and if you were asking me whether it is possible that it was done at eight o'clock in the morning by somebody with a lanyard then it is."

Nobody in the queue said anything. The queue did what queues do.

"That is the correct answer," said Jonas Mercer. "Thank you."

---

He found Nina Calder at about half past ten on the second floor with a tray of correspondence on her knee and a piece of paper on the tray that was not correspondence.

"I am not going to tell you who wrote it," she said, before he had said anything at all, "and if you have come up here to ask me that then you have come up here to waste a morning, because the answer is that the register is a public document on a public board and there is no field for the person who picks it up, and I built that on purpose in March, and I have been asked about it four times this year and I have given the same nine words four times, and you are about to be the fifth person and you will get the same nine words."

"You already gave them to me."

"Then you have come up here to waste a morning for a different reason."

Jonas Mercer looked at the tray.

"I can find it."

"I know you can find it. **I worked that out in about four minutes on the Wednesday afternoon and I have not been to that street since 2009 and I have not telephoned anybody and I have not written the street down.**" She did not look up. "A design is not a promise. That is the whole of what I have got and I have been saying it since May. Everything on that board is safe because nobody can be argued out of it, and it has been argued with eleven times this year, and this week it got used, and it was used by a careful person doing exactly what it was built to do, and there is no version of that form where that does not happen, and I have known that since the day I drew it."

"Who do you think wrote it."

"**I do not know and I am not going to find out, and you are not going to tell me, and if you tell me I will put it in the bin tray on Monday and you will be able to have it back on the Tuesday and neither of us will have learned anything.**" She turned a page. "Go and find out what came back from Kestrel Hill instead. That is the thing that is in this room and nobody in this room is going to be told about it either."

---

**What came back from Kestrel Hill is publicly visible in three places and in three places only, and Jonas Mercer assembled all three of them in one day, on foot, without telephoning anybody, which is the only way it can be assembled.**

**One. The register has seventeen.** The seventeenth line is quoted at the top of this chapter and it is the whole of what is on it, and nobody in this city can say who put it there, and the record says a state and not a person, and is therefore correct.

**Two. The counter at the records office, second floor, which is four floors above this vestibule and is not on the ground floor's opposite.** Any person may ask for a copy of a document received by a party holding continuity. There is no charge and there is no form and there has never been a question asked of a person standing at that counter. On Wednesday the twenty-second of October, at about eleven, a woman of about thirty-four produced, from a folder of about nine, a sheet headed **NOTICE OF RE-ASSERTION**, stamped **RECEIVED 21 OCT 2014**, and set it down flat, and said: *that one came in on Monday and it is not one of ours, it went straight to the drawer, and I am not supposed to hand those out and I have handed you that one out because you have been in here four times this year.*

It was one sheet. It was dated the fourteenth of October, which was five weeks after the notice it was answering, and it was signed for a trust in Kestrel Hill, and it gave a claim reference, a tier, a node reference, a service district, and an effective date, and it re-asserted one half of a claim at Meter Room 3, Salter's Yard, at Tier 3, with effect from the first of October 2014.

**There was no signature on it on behalf of any person. There was no field on it for one. There was nothing on it that was anybody's consent, because the clause it was made under says that the consent of no person is required, and the clause is not a trick and is not a flaw and is a rule in a schedule that has been in force since the first of March and that nobody in this city has audited.**

**Three. The two entries for Meter Room 3, which are still two, and which cannot be amended, and which the reading rule of the twenty-first of August says are to be read with each other.** They were on the file in the same folder, and they were read with each other that morning by Jonas Mercer and by nobody else, and what reading them together produced was the whole of what had happened since the first of October, and it will be Chapter 104.

**What is not publicly visible: what the clerk of sixty-three said. What the trust's board did or did not decide. Whether any person has been told anything. And whether the register of narrowings will be seventeen again in November.**

Nobody in this city is entitled to know what the clerk said, and the reader is not going to be told either, and that is not a withheld secret. It is a woman of sixty-three doing her job in October, unasked, and it will be about eleven weeks from now that anybody finds out that it happened, if it happens at all.

---

**Wednesday the twenty-second of October 2014, four minutes past five in the afternoon, the shop.**

The telephone rang four times, which is what it does.

He did not say his name.

"Shop."

A pause, and then a woman's voice, about fifty-four, and it was not the voice from the nineteenth of September, and it was the first time in five months that anybody had given him a name on a telephone.

"My name is Beverley Annis," she said. "I am the principal of the managing agent that has four hundred and ten flats, and I am not the woman who telephoned you on the nineteenth of September, and I have been trying to decide how to start this for four days."

"You have about four minutes and I am in a shop."

"Then I shall be quick and I shall be bad at it. **A form came back on Monday. It is stamped the twenty-first. It re-asserts one half of the claim at Meter Room 3 at Tier 3 with effect from the first of October, and it came back with no name on it anywhere, and there is no field on it for a name, and I have been an agent for twenty-six years and I have never had one of these come back with nobody's name on it, and I have telephoned a shop because there is nobody in this city I am allowed to ring.**"

The shop had a till going and a extractor fan and Bea Osunde was at the back of it with an ash on her shoulder, and neither of them said anything.

"You went into that cupboard on the third of June with a torch," said Jonas Mercer.

"I did."

"And you put a description in the holder field instead of a name."

"**I put *the named steward of the building*.** I did not put a name. And I want you to understand that I did that because I was not going to put a woman's name on a plate in a cupboard under a stair without asking her first, and I have thought about it every working day since, and I would do it again, and both of those are true at the same time and I have not been able to put them anywhere that would let them both stand."

"That is the reason the form came back with no name on it."

"Yes."

"Was it a reason you were told about?"

"No. Nobody told me. **Nobody has told me anything.** My clerk read the letter with the year on it and wrote a line in her own file about what she saw, unbidden, and she is twenty-nine and has been here fourteen months and she was not told why she was looking at a stopcock, and she wrote it down because she is the kind that does. And on Wednesday last week I went to the Old Ward because I wanted to see the record that put my firm's node in a class, and it is a public board, and there was a space at the bottom of it, and I wrote in it what I saw." A pause. "I did not put a name. I put what I saw. And I want to know that somebody has read it."

"I have read it."

"Then that is the whole of what I wanted and I am going to hang up now before I say something I have not thought about."

"Wait. Does she know."

"Does who know what."

"Does the person in the holder field know that she is in the claim again."

There was a silence on the line of about four seconds long, and it was not a bad connection, and neither of them filled it.

"**No,**" said Beverley Annis. "And she is not going to be told, because she is not to be told, because that is the position of this city since April and it is not going to change because a form came back with nobody's name on it. I have read section nine of that Schedule twice this month. I am a party. I am allowed to read it. **And nobody in this city may be required even to be told, and I am not going to be the person who breaks that, and neither are you, and I have rung a shop to establish that.**"

"Then there is nothing for me to do," said Jonas Mercer.

"No. There is not. And I did not ring to ask you." Her voice did not change and it did not go up. "**Mr Mercer, the four meters are not going into that cupboard.** The freeholder has withdrawn the standing instruction of the fourteenth of April. I am telling you because there is a woman in a block in this borough who has been refused that letter twice in sixteen years and she is seventy-two and she does not know that any of this has happened, and I am not going to be the person who tells her, and I could not sleep for a week, and I wanted it said out loud to somebody today and there was nobody."

She put the phone down.

Jonas Mercer stood in a shop with a telephone in his hand for about eleven seconds, and then he put the pad out on the bench, and then he did not write on it, and then he picked it up again and put it away.

**He did not write it down. That is the first thing he has chosen not to write since the third of April, and it took him about eleven seconds, and it is the first time in seven months that a person has told him something and not told him not to tell it to anybody, and the reason he did not write it is not discretion. It is that in four years there is going to be a piece of paper in a file in a building in Salter's Yard that says a managing agent put a description in a box in a cupboard in June, and the box is what kept a woman out of a claim for four months, and it will be in the file next to a meter round and a rent ledger and a woman of fifty-four who is findable, and it will be the only sentence in that file about the sixth of June and it will read as though it had been intended.**

Bea Osunde came through with the ash on her shoulder and put it on the bench and looked at him.

"You've been on that telephone a long time and you haven't written anything."

"I haven't."

"**In seven months.**"

"In seven months."

"And you are going to tell me about it on Saturday when you have got a pad out and you have got your elbow on it, because you cannot keep a thing like that, and I have watched you not be able to keep a thing like that since March." She straightened the ash against the edge of the bench. "So write on Saturday. And whatever it is, it is not the woman's name and it is not the letter's, because I have read about that letter and I know what a piece of paper is."

Then she went and turned the light out over the door, and the shop went dark from the front, and the last thing under the light was the bench.

---

The bench is four feet eight and it is beech and it has been planed twice this year and it is the bench a man who has a shop works on, and the two dates are still in it.

**1 OCT and 11 OCT, an inch and a half high, in biro, along the grain, on the flat, in the place where a Saturday's plane had brought up a pale ghost of a four-month-old date.** In four weeks a man with a plane or a scraper or a piece of sandpaper will find them, and in about two years somebody who has been told about them will sand them back and oil the top and they will still be there, because ink goes into beech and does not come out of it, and they will be an inch and a half high, and no rule anywhere in this borough says that anybody has to read them.

Both of them had gone by.

**The first had gone three weeks and nobody in this city who is allowed to know has said what it was, and the second had gone eleven days ago at ten o'clock at night on a Saturday on a stair at a hospital, and he was not in that corridor, and nobody has told him anything about it, and he is not going to ask, and Tomas Renn is not going to tell him, and that rule between the two of them has now been in force for three months and has never once been tested and has never once been broken and is the only instrument either of them produced this year that is worth anything.**

He put his hand flat on the bench, about two inches from the ink, and did not touch it, which is what he does, and which is the only thing about him that Bea Osunde has never once asked him to stop doing.

"Both of them have gone," she said from the doorway.

"Yes."

"And you don't know."

"No."

She looked at the wood for a moment longer than she needed to, and then she went and shut the roller the rest of the way down.

"**Well,**" said Bea Osunde. "**That is the first thing you have written on that bench that is true as well as permanent.**"
