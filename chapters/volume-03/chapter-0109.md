# Chapter 0109 — Six Vacancies

**Wednesday the twenty-sixth of November 2014, ten past four in the afternoon, a corridor, second floor, the civic offices.**

This account gave a chapter on the twenty-ninth of October to a woman of sixty-one who is on no board, in no field and on no rota, and there is a rule about that and the rule is not a rule, it is a debt.

**The person who empties a seat in this chapter is not in this chapter.** If she were in it, then the last thing a person at the bottom of a stairwell in a building with no civic address would be given this year would be a scene in which somebody else's resignation happened to her. She has been somebody else's event since 1995 and she has done it very well and she is sixty-one, and Jonas Mercer is not going to be the reason it happens to her twice in eight weeks.

So he is here, in a corridor, with his one question, and he has been saving it since the twenty-eighth of October, and he has not spent it in a public room, because a corridor is not a public room, and that is a line he is drawing himself and Frances Tolley is entitled to see him drawing it.

"Frances."

"No."

"You have not said what the question is."

"**I know exactly what the question is, and I have known since the second of October, and I have had it in my bag in my own hand for about three months, and I have been waiting for somebody to ask me so that I could say no to a person instead of to a file.**" She had the file under her arm and she did not put it down. "No."

"I am not going to argue with you."

"I know you are not. That is what makes it tiresome." She shifted the file. "Mr Mercer, I have been a clerk for twenty-two years and I copied four pages out by hand on a Thursday afternoon in May because I did not trust myself not to improve them, and I have read it about nine times, and I have not told my husband what is in it, and there are three people in this city who know and one of them does not work here any more. **And you are not one of the three and you are not going to be, and the reason is that you have asked me nine questions since March and every single one of them has been about a document.**"

"That is a reason and it is the first one anybody has given me."

"It is not a reason," said Frances Tolley. "**It is a pattern. And in about four years it is going to be the only thing anybody in this borough knows about you, and it will be that you asked a person with a handbag the same question four times and got the same no.**"

He said, "That is fair," and meant it, and went down the stairs.

---

**Half past five, the Methodist hall, Carrow Lane, Tideglass.**

There were nine people and nine chairs and two of the chairs were empty, and one of the empty chairs had been empty for forty-nine days.

**Jonas Mercer had not been asked.** Nobody asked him. He came at twenty-five past five and stood against the left-hand wall with his hands behind his back, and the woman who keeps the minutes looked at him and then at Ibrahim Sesay, and Sesay shook his head, and she wrote in the minute that a person was present at the invitation of nobody, and Jonas Mercer read that sentence four days later and did not know what to do with it and has not decided yet.

**Four of the nine had said they would stop if it turned into a job.**

Yolanda Fofana had said it on the twenty-ninth of September in a records office, in about nine seconds, and had given the arithmetic: *three of the eight have already said they will stop if it turns into a job, and two of them mean it, and one of them does not and will find out in November.* It is now the twenty-sixth of November and there are four of them, and the fourth is Gloria Nye, who said it on the eighth of October in eleven words and then apologised for saying it, and who has not withdrawn it.

**And there is a sentence in this account about a body of nine with one power that is not anybody's and is not in this room.**

There is a fourth page of a regulation from 2008. It has been read three times in this city by three people and told to nobody, and one of those three read it once and gave a reason in a public room, and one of those three read it twice and would not read it out in a corridor, and one of those three copied it out by hand in a single afternoon in May because she did not trust herself not to improve it. It is not in this hall. It is not in anybody's bag that anybody in this hall can see. And this chapter is not going to put it in this hall. **There is nobody in this hall tonight who knows what is on it, and there is a woman in this hall whose position in this body is not a thing anybody in this room can be told about, and she has said out loud twice that she is not going to be told anything, and she is right, and this chapter is not going to tell her and is not going to work it out in front of you.**

---

Stanley Umeh said it at nine minutes past six.

He is fifty-eight and he has been a market porter and the keeper of a lock-up at Bellow Market for thirty years, and his answer on the first of September was *the shutter and the padlock and what the padlock is for*, and he had not said anything in twenty-five minutes, and then he said the thing, and he said it the way he has probably said everything for thirty years, which is to a point about eleven inches in front of his own shoes.

"**Right. I'm going.**"

"Stanley—"

"**No, let me say it while it's the same as when I sat down, because if I wait ten minutes I'll have talked myself into it and I've been talking myself into it since about the middle of October and I'm fifty-eight and I've not got the months.**" He put his hands on his knees. "Thirty years I've had a shutter and a padlock. In this borough. Since about 1984. And nobody's ever asked me what the padlock's for and I've never needed anybody to, because a lock-up is a lock-up and I am the man nearest the thing and that's the whole of my job and it doesn't want a minute.

"And there's a minute now. And there's a wall in the vestibule of the Old Ward with seventeen things on it and a blank line, and there's a woman in a records office who says a form is the last thing a person writes, and she's right, she's completely right, and there's a book and a number in the market that I have never once had to put in a book. **And this body has got a minute and a publication and a thing that goes to the Assembly, and a minute is a service, and I have watched this borough turn about forty men I know into services and I did it too, twice, and both times I was right to and I have not felt good about either.**"

He stood up. It took him a while and he did not use the chair.

"I'm not angry with anybody. I'd like that said. **And I'm not stopping because of you. I'm stopping because of me and because of thirty years, and because I'm the fourth one of you who's said it and three of us mean it and one of us doesn't and I'm not going to be in a room on a Friday evening with three-quarters of myself.**"

"Stanley," said Gloria Nye. "You are going to be exactly where you are. That is the thing about this body that nobody understands and that I have been explaining at people for three weeks—"

"**Your chair'll be empty in a fortnight, Gloria, and you'll be in it, and you'll be a service, and there's nobody who can stop you and that's the design and you're in it on purpose.**" He said it without any malice at all. "I watched you say that in September and I thought you were proud of it. I don't think you're proud of it. I think you said it about eleven times."

Nye did not answer that.

**And Ines Tarrant, 44, car park supervisor, eleven years, whose answer on the first of September was *nobody has ever asked me what it is for and I have assumed it is a barrier*, said that she meant it too, and then did not stop, and everybody in the room could tell the difference, and nobody said a word about it, and it is not going to be resolved this year.**

She said: "I said it in October and I meant it and I have thought about it every day, and I am not stopping, and I would like somebody to ask me why, and nobody is going to, because nobody in this room can require me to answer a question."

Nobody asked her why.

**And Winifred Crabbe, 62, linen supervisor, nineteen years, said the thing that everybody in the room had been thinking since the middle of October and that nobody had been willing to say out loud, and she said it to the wall card.**

"**There is a card on that wall that says a body which cannot be required to do anything cannot be told to stop. And Stanley has just gone. And that is not a contradiction, and I want it in the minute that it is not a contradiction, because in about four years somebody is going to write one of those two things at the top and the other one at the bottom and it is going to read as a hypocrisy, and it is not a hypocrisy, it is the design.**" She took her glasses off. "He went because he wanted to. That is the only way anything has ever left anything in this borough."

The woman who keeps the minutes said, "It will say that, and it will say that you said it, and one of the two lines is going to be a date."

"Thank you."

---

**And then they did the thing that a body of nine does, and Jonas Mercer had never seen a body do it before and had not known it was possible, and it took about eleven minutes and it was the whole of the evening.**

Nobody tried to keep Stanley Umeh. **Nobody argued with him, and nobody asked him a second question, and nobody said *are you sure*, and nobody said *can it wait until after the twenty-fourth of November*.** A person named without being asked may decline afterwards in his own words and nothing follows from it, and that sentence is the whole of the power this borough has been able to give anybody in a room since the third of April, and it has now been used for the first time in a body that has a name and a minute and a publication.

What the room did instead was this.

The woman who keeps the minutes read out Stanley Umeh's answer to the question he had given on the first of September — *the shutter and the padlock and what the padlock is for* — in his own words, all three parts, and then read out his four lines from the twenty-sixth, and then asked the room, not him, because he was by the door with his coat on and nobody was going to keep him.

"So: **the seat is not vacant, it is about a lock-up, and it is the eighth of nine, and it is answered for by a person who is nearest the thing.**"

"And nobody can fill it," said Dermot Fahey.

"**Nobody can fill it, and nobody can unfill it, and the only thing anybody in this room can do is write down what it was, and that is on page three and it takes eleven seconds.**"

"It takes about forty," said Ibrahim Sesay. "There is a box and the box is *district or facility* and there are two of those words and one of them is going to be wrong and I have watched this borough for thirty-one years and I know which one is going to be wrong and I am not going to say which in this room."

"Then we will write *the lock-up at Bellow Market, and the shutter, and the padlock, and what the padlock is for*, and we will leave the box, and the box will stay empty, and in about four years the empty box will be the only interesting thing on the page."

Nobody argued with that either.

**And then Yolanda Fofana said the thing that has been sitting under everything since the second of June, out loud, in a room, in about eleven seconds, and she said it as an observation about a document and not as a motion, and nobody treated it as a motion, and everybody in the room treated it as exactly what it was.**

"**Miss Calder wrote in June that for this to be nine people and not nine chairs, one of us has to be able to be put in a room and told to stop, and removed in front of the other eight, by people who did not put him there. And there is no power in this city that can do that. There never was. She did not say it could not be done because she wanted it done. She said it because a body without that is nine chairs and she would rather have nine chairs and know it.**" She did not turn round. "Two chairs are empty now. That is not a step towards anything. It is a Tuesday."

---

**Friday the twenty-eighth of November 2014, twenty past nine in the morning, the counter, the records office, second floor.**

The minute of the twenty-sixth was on the counter list, and the woman read out the two lines that mattered, which is all he wanted.

> **Item 3. Seat eight. The person answering for the lock-up at Bellow Market, the shutter, the padlock and what the padlock is for, has stated that he will not continue. No reason was recorded at his request and none was given. He was not asked a second question. The seat falls vacant and remains vacant, and no person may be required to take it.**
>
> **Recorded: that this body now stands at seven of nine; that it stood at nine of nine from 4 September to 8 October and at eight of nine for forty-nine days; and that it has no power to fill either vacancy and will not seek one.**

"Eight of nine for forty-nine days," said Jonas Mercer.

"That is what I read."

"Yes."

Then the woman at the counter said, as a counter does, something she was not asked for.

"Mr Mercer. **The ninth seat read the minute on the Wednesday night. She was in the room when it was read out, because she is in the room at everything, and then she took a copy, and copies go to anybody who asks.**"

"And?"

"**And she has not come to this counter.**"

The counter opens at eight. It is four floors from a records store on a lower floor where a sealed envelope sits in a drawer, and a woman of fifty-four who is the only person in this city anybody can write to, and who has said out loud in a hall and in a kitchen and at a counter that she is not going to be the woman who goes to a registrar about what she is.

**Jonas Mercer went and stood on the pavement outside the civic offices at about ten past nine on the Friday morning, and then at ten past ten, and then at half past ten, and then he gave up at eleven and went and did four hours of something else.**

He did not know, on Friday, whether she was coming.

**He did not know on the Friday, and he has not known since, and he wrote that down, and the pad says this:**

> *Friday 28 November, the records counter. Seven of nine. I asked for the minute and I got two lines and the arithmetic, which is what I asked for. The ninth seat took a copy on Wednesday night.*
> *I stood outside that counter for about fifty minutes on Friday morning. I did not see her. I did not know whether she was coming, and I still do not know, and I have not rung her, and I am not going to ring her, and that is not a rule I have made for her sake. It is that in about four years there will be a piece of paper that says a man telephoned a woman to ask her whether she had read a minute.*
> *And there is a fourth page of a regulation from 2008 that three people in this city have read, and one of the three copied it out by hand in a single afternoon, and I asked her about it in a corridor on Wednesday and she said no before I said what the question was, and she was right, and I did not argue, and I have written down that I asked and that she said no, which is the fourth time this year and it is the first one I have wanted to argue about.*
> *Two chairs. She said it is a Tuesday. Nobody in that room knows what is on the fourth page and neither do I, and there is a woman in that room who could not be told if she did know, and that is the correct position and it has been the correct position since the second of June, and two chairs is not a step towards anything.*
> *A man of fifty-eight went out of a room on Wednesday night because he would not be a service, and the whole of the power this borough has been able to give anybody in a room since the third of April is that he was allowed to, and it is not nothing, and it is one power more than this body had on the fourth of September, and one power fewer than anybody in this room would want.*
> *The last thing this borough needs is another person going to a counter and asking to be told what she is. Including her. Especially her, this month, and I am not going to be the one who sends her, and I do not know whether she has decided anything at all, and I am going to have to carry that.*
