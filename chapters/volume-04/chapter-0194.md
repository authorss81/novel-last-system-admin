# Chapter 0194 — The Shutter

Wednesday the twenty-third of September 2015, from about half past six in the evening, a parade of shops in Tideglass.

The shape of this city went out on Friday morning into four places and was on forty walls by that night, and it has not stopped going up since. About nine people are standing in front of one of them. The road on this parade was mended this morning. The last thing that happens on it tonight takes about a minute and is not on anything.

**THE ONLY LATTICE NOTICE ON THAT PARADE THIS EVENING, printed and fixed to a post at about the height of a person of average height, and it defines nothing, and nothing on it defines *holder*:**

> **A DRAWING OF A THING IS NOT THE THING.**
> **NO PERSON IS REQUIRED TO HAVE AN OPINION ABOUT IT.**
> **NOTHING ON THIS DRAWING IS A RECORD OF ANY PERSON.**

It was an ordinary evening on an ordinary parade. A crew had been in the trench here on Monday and on Tuesday and had backfilled it on Tuesday night, and the trench had been taken up, and both barriers had gone on Tuesday afternoon, and the piece of A4 had gone with one of them because it had been gummed to it. The water came back on Tuesday afternoon and nobody wrote down when. The road was made good again at about eleven this morning and a roller went over it twice. The stopcock key went back to the water side and the man of about thirty-eight was not on the parade at all.

A bus went past. A shutter came down across a shop at the end, the long slow kind, and the man inside it kept going. A man in a high-visibility jacket who had been at work was going home. A woman with a carrier bag. Two women talking outside the chip shop about a bin. A man with a bicycle who could not get on it because of the crowd.

And on the shutter of that shop, above the height of a person's head, a sheet of paper about two feet by three feet had been gummed to the metal, and about nine people were standing in front of it, and not one of them had been standing there an hour earlier.

The correct outcome of a shape becoming public is that it is discussed by the people it does not help. The woman of about forty-six put it out on Friday morning at about nine and has said, to everybody who has asked her, that it is not a scandal and that it is a drawing, and she is right about both halves of that, and she is not available to be asked about it by anybody, and there is nobody in this city she could hand it to.

Here is what about nine people said about it in about ten minutes, and every sentence was correct.

The man in the high-visibility jacket wanted to know whether this parade was one of the eight.

"It's not a street, it's a district."

"Right. Is there a district for this bit of it?"

"There's no district for a bit of it."

"That's what I came back for."

A woman with a carrier bag wanted to know what it does for people who cannot get to work.

"It doesn't do anything. It shows where the terms start and where they stop."

"So it's a map."

"It's a drawing of a map, and there is no person on it anywhere on it, and that is not an oversight, and if there were one on it I would not want to be the one standing here reading it."

Somebody said they had known all of this for a decade, and that it had been on the wall of a room in this city that people walked past every day without being shown it, and that the only new thing in it was the middle of August.

Somebody else said: "Then the middle of August is the whole of it. That is the first thing anybody in this city has ever written down that would work, and it is a name."

"Aye. And you cannot unring a telephone."

"No."

A woman asked whether she could get an answer out of it.

"No."

"Then it is a poster."

"It is."

"Right."

Not one of the people on that pavement had been on it on Friday morning, and not one of the nine in front of that shutter is a person the drawing says anything about, and the man who stopped a clause on Friday is not one of them, and neither of those is a complaint.

**AND THEN A MAN WITH A BICYCLE PUT HIS FINGER ON THE MIDDLE OF THE SHEET AND ASKED WHAT THAT SQUARE WAS FOR.**

There is one square on that sheet with nothing in it. It is not a district and it is not a seat. There are eight districts on that sheet and all eight of them have a term in them and a name above them, and the square he is asking about is not one of the eight, and the line that comes into it from the district above it goes nowhere at all, and it is the same square that was on a different copy in a folder in a woman's hands on Friday morning, and Jonas Mercer read it while she was still talking and did not say so then and has not said so since.

"I don't know," said the man in the high-visibility jacket.

"That one's empty."

"Aye. It's empty."

Nobody in this borough is going to be able to say what goes in it. There is no body that can require anybody to say. A man of thirty-two is standing about ten feet away with his hands in his pockets who has read that square twice and could not tell them, and he is not going to, and the woman who put the sheet out does not know that she is holding the answer to it, and every one of the forty on the walls of this city has the square, and not one of them has anything in it.

A man with a carrier bag said: "Is that you, on the Friday?"

"I read it."

"You're the one that stopped it."

"I read it and I did not apply the sixth clause, and I said that out loud on this pavement on Friday, and nobody in this city with a department has said one word about it since."

"So what happens in November?"

"There is a date on the last page of the document and there is a line under the date and there is nothing in the line. That is the whole of what I know about November and I am not going to say any more than that, because nobody with a department has asked me anything and I am not going to be one of the people who tells a street what a date means."

"So what do I do?"

"I could not tell you. There is nothing in it about what to do. If I told you what to do I would be making it up, and I have not made anything up in this borough in nine months and I am not going to start on a Wednesday evening."

Nobody on that pavement had an answer to that, and the man with the carrier bag said "fair enough" and went to the chip shop, and it is the only thing anybody asked him all evening and he answered all of it.

The light went off the fish shop at seven and about half the people went home, and the argument about the square went with them. What was left on the pavement was a man with a bicycle, a man in a high-visibility jacket, and two people who had not been in front of the sheet at all and who were not looking at it.

A man of about twenty-seven came up the parade with a flatbed and a clipboard, and about six sheets of the drawing rolled up along the rail, and a roll of gummed tape, and he put the flatbed across the end of the parade where the last bus comes out and stopped it, and nobody asked him to and nobody told him to.

He had done this all week. He had been to about nine buildings and he had a list of the ones that had wanted it and a list of the ones that had not, and both lists were in his own writing on a clipboard, and the clipboard was his own, and there was no arrangement anywhere that had put him on this parade on this evening.

The shop at the end had one on its shutter already, so he went along to the next one, which was down and had nothing else on it. It is the better surface, because that shutter goes up at half past seven every morning and a sheet gummed to it will be standing at eye level by the time the parade fills up, and he did not say that out loud because there was nobody to say it to.

He gummed the sheet along the top edge and pressed it with his palm from the middle out, and it came off the metal twice, and on the third time it took.

He could not reach the top of it and hold it flat. He could not do both with two hands, and there was nobody on the parade to ask, and asking somebody would have meant standing in a doorway and explaining himself, and he had been doing this since Friday and he had got used to not having to.

He came down the left side of it, and the corner at the top was still loose, and he could not hold it and get at it at the same time.

"Hold that."

It was a woman of about sixty-three with a bag in each hand who had come past with her shopping and had stopped, and she took one corner of it between two fingers and a thumb, about eight inches down from the top, and held it there, and did not read it, because she was holding it and she could see about four words of it and she did not turn her head to try to get more.

He got the last of the gum down the side, and the corner she was holding stopped moving.

"That's it."

"That's it," she said, and let go, and picked her bags up, and went to catch the bus, and it had taken him about a minute and her about a minute, and neither of them was asked anything, and they did not say anything else to each other.

Nobody came out of the shop. Nobody paid either of them. There is no list and no field and no minute and no hour recorded anywhere, and neither of them is on anything, and the reason there is no arrangement anywhere that could have sent a woman of sixty-three to hold a corner of a piece of paper on a parade of shops on a Wednesday evening is that there is no body in this city that can require anybody to do anything, and both of the people who did it had not been asked to and are not going to be asked about it.

It is on a shutter now, and it is correct, and a thing on a shutter is the first thing in nine months that a person who was not standing there can read about afterwards, and whether that is a proof or a story is still open, and nobody on that parade was going to settle it, and he was not going to settle it, and it is not going to be settled.

The rest of the evening on that parade was a bus and a bin and the two people who were not looking at the sheet. Nothing was minuted and nothing was arranged and the order was not renewed and it was not ended, and the clause is still where a man of thirty-two put it in daylight on Friday, and it is holding, and holding is not settled, and nobody in this borough has heard one word about it from either of the two places that could.

A gate on the west side of this city goes up at seven on a Sunday morning and stops at six on the Sunday morning after that, six days long and not a week, with no date on it anywhere in this city, and nobody is required to know which day it is, and nobody in this borough is going to be told.

A person who is not counted is not a person who is helped, and the shape of this city is on forty walls with no name on any of them, and being on forty walls is not being counted, and the day anybody in this city works out which of the eight districts a person is standing in is not the day anybody asks them how they are.

There is a square on that sheet with nothing in it and nobody knows what goes in it. There is a date on the last page of a document in a building and a line under the date with nothing in the line. Both of them were there on Friday morning and both of them are there now, and there is no instrument in this city that could put a name in either, and nobody is going to be asked.
