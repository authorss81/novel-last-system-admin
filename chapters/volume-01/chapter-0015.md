# Chapter 0015 — The Price of an Open Door

The woman’s name was Lorna Vessey. She was sixty-one, she had come in with pain at rest, and the slot in the cardiac suite that could have told them whether her heart was going to keep doing that was scheduled for twenty to four and had been booked since Tuesday.

Mina Okafor stood at the top of the ramp with the transfer card in her hand and the patient waiting behind her in a chair under a hospital blanket, and she had been doing the arithmetic for nine minutes.

“The card is real,” she said. “Her claim is verified. Her bed is assigned, the suite is staffed, and the receiving shift is named. I have the name of the nurse who is going to meet her. It is not a formality, I checked it myself, it is a person.”

“Then the door opens,” Jonas said.

“The door has been shut since eleven this morning and the plate says *holder not named* and the holder list for this shift says *not named*.” Mina turned the card over. “And the day has no name on it, because there is no day watch. There is a clerk and a care assistant and me, and none of us is the holder, and one of us cannot be made into the holder by anybody standing on this ramp.”

The daughter was standing at the foot of the chair with one hand on her mother’s shoulder. She had a coat over her arm and a phone in her hand with the appointment time in it.

“We were told at ten to two that the gate was in service,” she said. “The woman on the ward said the door was fixed. There was a green light on it.”

“There was a green light on it,” Jonas said, which was the entire architecture of his failure in two words, and he heard himself say it and kept going because that was what the bond had asked of him at half past eleven the previous morning in a shop in River Stacks. “My name is on that gate. The plate is closed because I have not acknowledged this shift, and it will not open for a clerk or a porter because neither of them is named in a term they can refuse.”

He had three and a half days of the term left and six hours of it in front of him.

Sanaa came out of the treatment bay and looked at the patient and then at the sky and then at him.

“Tell me she is not an emergency,” she said.

“She has chest pain at rest twice this morning.”

“She has had chest pain at rest twice this morning and she is now on two milligrams of something, comfortable, warm, with a monitor that has not changed in an hour.” Sanaa crouched by the chair and put two fingers on the back of Lorna Vessey’s hand. “Mrs Vessey. I am going to call you stable. Do you understand what that means for you this afternoon?”

“That I wait.”

“That you wait, and I do not lie on your behalf to stop you waiting.”

Jonas felt the shape of the next minute arrive before it happened, and there was exactly one door in it, and it was the emergency maintenance card, and it would have opened the ramp in under a minute if somebody with clinical authority stood in front of the plate and said *this is an emergency.*

He looked at Sanaa.

She looked back at him with a completely level face, and he understood that she had thought of it too, and that she had thought of it first, and that she had watched him think of it.

“No,” she said.

“It is not a lie if the pain recurs.”

“It is a lie now. It is a lie now, in this corridor, at this minute, and it will be a lie the moment the door opens, and the person it lies to will be whoever comes after her at six o’clock with a real heart.” She stood up. “I have watched a triage decision get made by whoever is holding the plate. I am not going to let the plate be held by a man who slept through the day.”

The suite called at twenty to four.

Mina answered it and put her hand over the mouthpiece and said, “They want to know if she is in the lift.”

And Jonas, standing on the ramp of the Saint Orra west clinic with his right hand dead to the knuckle and his name printed at the bottom of a schedule, understood what his repair had done. It had not closed a door. It had built a door that opened for him and for nobody else, and the difference between those two facts was a patient sitting in a chair in the sun.

“Go,” he said to Mina. “Tell them she is not there.”

The slot was released at fifteen forty-one.

“Standby list,” Mina said afterwards, in the flat voice of a clerk who has written this line before. “Next available is the fourteenth. Nine days. It is in the record, and I have not softened it, and I have not entered a reason that makes it look better than it is.”

“Write *gate not held by a named bearer*,” Jonas said. “Not *clinic delay*.”

“That is going in.”

Ruth Nwosu was not there, and that was the entire size of the failure. She was six and a half hours away from the start of a shift, and there was nobody else, and every name that would have been in that slot was a name the schedule did not have.

The handset on the counter in River Stacks had been lighting up since twenty-four minutes past three, and he had slept through it twice, and then it rang.

He woke with his mouth dry and his coat on and the light going on and going off, and looked at the time, and looked at the number, and knew before he answered that the call was not a fault report because fault reports do not ring the personal handset at a quarter past three on a working day.

“Jonas,” said Sanaa.

“I am awake.”

“The door says you are the door.”

The transfer crew had waited on the ramp. The cardiac suite had released the slot and put it on the standby list, where it would sit until somebody with a worse heart than Lorna Vessey needed it at four o’clock, and it would stay there for nine days because releasing a suite slot is not a decision anybody makes twice.

He was on the ramp at ten to four with his boots still laced and a paper cup of station coffee that he spilled down the front of his coat.

He stood in front of the plate with his left hand raised, and he did not put it on the plate, and for about four seconds nobody in the corridor said anything at all.

“Nineteen minutes ago the registrar’s office got a copy of your acknowledgement of last night’s shift,” Mina said, without looking up from the card table. “They have not written to you yet. They will.”

“I know,” Jonas said.

“You are suspended from initiating gate use. That is a standing order with a review on it, and putting your name on a shift is initiating gate use with your body two kilometres away, and I am the clerk who has to write down that a patient came through a door that a suspended technician opened.”

“I am opening it,” he said. “Write that down in those words.”

Mina wrote it down in those words.

He put his hand on the plate at fifteen fifty-eight and the ramp door opened at sixteen oh three, and Lorna Vessey went through it in a chair with her daughter beside her, and the plate recorded the route as *issuer-held*, because that was the only line it had, and he had made the choice with his eyes open and his name on the record and there was no version of the next ten minutes in which any of that was reversible.

Sanaa came out and stood at the bottom of the ramp.

“Say it out loud,” she said. “Say what just happened, because in four minutes there will be six people in this corridor who each have a third of it and I am not going to be the one who assembles the story.”

Jonas turned around. Mina was at the card table. A porter was holding the door. Ezra Mbeki was at the far end of the corridor with his hand flat on the wall, and two ward nurses had come out of the bay, and Lorna Vessey was on a bed in the bay with her blanket still over her knees, and none of them had been in the room when the decision was made.

“The gate I repaired at six yesterday morning worked exactly as it was written,” Jonas said, “for the person who wrote it, and for nobody else. The door was closed from eleven o’clock yesterday morning until four this afternoon. In that time one supply run and one fall-risk patient used it, and both of them were allowed through by my name. At twenty to four a transfer with a verified claim, an assigned bed, a named receiving nurse and a booked theatre slot arrived, and it could not come in, and I was asleep, and the reason I was asleep is that since the morning I mended this gate my name has been the only one the plate has ever been offered for a holder.”

He was aware, while he was saying it, of Ruth’s absence as an actual physical quantity in the room. The woman who would have stood on that corridor with a key was not there, and would not have been there, because the schedule he had signed said that at this hour nobody was.

“The slot was released at fifteen forty-one and goes to the standby list,” Mina said. “Next available is the fourteenth. Nine days.”

“Write it there,” Jonas said. “On the gate log, on the conflict record, and on the review sheet. Not as a footnote. Write *released slot*.”

Mina wrote it. He watched her do it, and the pen sound was the loudest thing in the corridor.

“The second thing,” he said, “is that the same page made a different mistake on the other side. At twenty to three this morning a porter brought a fall-risk patient back from a scan alone at that hour, because the plate said the route was open and the issuer was on duty. I acknowledged the night shift at nine minutes past nine last night at my kitchen table in ninety seconds. The patient was on the floor for eleven minutes before a cleaner found him. There is a safeguarding form and it is going in the same file.”

Nobody spoke. The porter at the door took his hand off the frame and looked at the floor.

“The third thing is the only part I can actually offer you,” Jonas said. “Ruth Nwosu can stop that gate at any single use, without giving a reason, and I am not allowed to ask her for the reason afterwards. Sanaa can say in writing that a patient is an emergency, and the plate has to open for her, and I cannot overrule her. Mina can refuse to verify a claim, and a transfer stops at the card table. Any of the three of them can do that without me and without the department, and every one of them can do it to my face while I am standing here. From tomorrow, the people who can stop the next use are not technicians. It is the three people standing in this corridor.”

“That is a good answer,” Sanaa said. “It is also the first sentence of the speech you will give the department in about ten minutes, so I hope you mean it.”

“I mean it.”

They had the meeting in the corridor at six, on a folding table, with the bonding paper and the log and the safeguarding form and the released slot, and Jonas said the same three things again and answered questions for an hour and ten minutes, and no one shouted at him, and the absence of shouting was the worst part.

At ten past seven, Sanaa took the certification form off the bottom of the stack, read it, and signed the top half of it.

**MECHANICAL AND OPERATIONAL CONDITION OF THE WEST CLINIC CARE ENTRANCE — CERTIFIED.**

Then she turned the form back over, took the pen, and wrote on the line underneath it in her own flat unhurried hand:

**I do not certify this permission. The gate opened. The service did not. I was not present at 15:40 and I was not asked to be, and the term that let it happen was signed by a man who is asleep during the hours in which it fails. Ask me again when a named person is holding this door at four in the morning and has said in writing that they will be there.**

She put the pen down.

“Photograph it and put it on the board,” she said, “because the board is where the people who have to be here will look, and I do not intend for them to have to take my word from a technician who cannot hold a screwdriver.”

Lorna Vessey’s daughter came down at half past seven, when Jonas was alone on the ramp with a bag of cold chips he had not eaten.

“You’re the one who fixed it,” Nerys Vessey said. It was not a question and it was not an accusation either. “The door was open. It said the door was open. Everybody told me the door was open. And when we got here it was shut, and the woman on the machine said it was because of a name that wasn’t on the rota.”

“Yes.”

“Do you know what I’m going to have to tell my mother?” Nerys Vessey looked up the ramp at the closed door. “I’m going to have to tell her that there was a door and it was open and nobody was standing behind it. And she’s going to ask me what that means, and I’m going to have to say *nothing happened*, and she is not stupid, she worked in a factory for thirty-one years.”

“I am sorry.”

“Don’t be sorry at me.” She shifted the coat on her arm. “Be sorry at the rota. And fix it, if it’s fixable. Nine days is a long time to be frightened.”

Then she went up to her mother, and Jonas sat on the ramp with three and a half days left on the term, and put his left hand over the inside of his right wrist, where a thin grey line had opened across the skin sometime in the twenty minutes between six in the morning two days ago and now, and which was warm, and which was not going to close because he slept.

The public Old Ward board refreshed at nine. The service log for the Saint Orra west clinic care entrance was on it in the same clipped language as everything else, and it was readable from the pavement, and among other things it said, in a line that did not use anybody’s name except one:

**SERVICE WINDOW 4. 15:41 — BOOKED CARDIAC SLOT RELEASED. NEXT AVAILABLE 14TH.**
**ISSUER: MERCER, J.**

Nerys Vessey found him standing in front of it.

“Is my mother’s name on that?”

“No. Only the window and the slot.”

“Good. Keep it that way.” She looked at the line for a while. “The slot is the city’s business. She is mine.”

“It is still hers on the ward record and it will be in the clinical note.”

“Then it will be in the clinical note, which nobody walks past on a pavement.”

He stood in front of it for a while in the cold. Somewhere behind him, on a ramp in about three hours, a woman named Ruth Nwosu would come in at ten o’clock for the start of a shift that somebody finally had written down, and would read a page she had already refused to soften, and would turn a key that opened a door she had spent nineteen years being told she was not officially responsible for.
