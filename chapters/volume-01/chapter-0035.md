# Chapter 0035 — The Appeal and the Price

The price of an appeal in the city of Nacre, in the spring, is four things and they are all printed on a form and none of them is money first.

Nell Ardent read the form out in the laundry room at ten past eleven on Monday morning with the whole of it turned round so that the association could see it, and she read it twice because the first time she was doing her job and the second time she was making sure they had it.

“The first thing is time. The panel sits on the twenty-third of April. That is twenty-three days’ notice, the minimum is fourteen, and the extra nine are not generosity — they are because the registrar’s office knows what happened on the twenty-second of March at the old canal and does not want it twice.

“The building is closed and it stays closed until the panel sits. That is the price, and it is paid whether we win or not, and the borough has bought fourteen nights at the Anchor House, the last of them the night of the twelfth, and the thirteenth is nobody’s problem yet. Somebody in this room is going to have to notice that in about three weeks, and I am telling you now so that you cannot say afterwards that nobody warned you.”

“The second is representation. The panel will hear an appellant represented by a solicitor, an officer of a recognised civic body, or a person the panel accepts is able to instruct themselves. That third one has a bracket round it in the regulations and there is no practice direction attached to the bracket and in eight years of doing this I have seen it used twice and lost twice.

“The third is the deposit. Three hundred and forty pounds, taken before the papers are accepted, returned if the order is set aside. And there is a bond as well — two thousand, against the council’s costs if the order is upheld and the building has to be stayed shut. You do not pay the bond today. You pay it if you win. You will not win, and so you will pay it, and I want that in front of you now, in this room, before anybody signs anything.

“And the fourth,” said Nell Ardent, “is that the panel requires the appellant to state, in their own words, what the safety position would be if the building stayed open.”

“That is a strange thing to ask,” said Yasmin Rohan.

“It is a remarkable thing to ask and it is the whole case, and I am going to take the next four minutes to explain it to you, and then I am not going to speak again unless I am asked, because I am in this room as a favour and a favour is worth exactly as much as the person who granted it.” Nell Ardent put the form down on the table. “Because the order is right. I have read the survey and I have been to the yard and Oren Pell is correct about the threshold. If you appeal by saying the building is safe, you will lose. If you appeal by saying he has been unfair, you will lose faster, because a panel is not a court and it is allowed to be fair and wrong.” She put her pen down. “Which leaves the fourth thing, and I am going to make you say it out loud now instead of on the twenty-third of April in front of four people. The building is shut. The store is on Marsh Lane behind a key that belongs to a rota which starts at seven in the morning. So tell this room what happens to that bank at two in the morning, and then say it again in April.”

The laundry room was quiet, and outside the window the van from Marsh Lane was still there, which it was not supposed to be, because the hauler had come back for the crates.

“We cannot answer that question,” said Ferdy Oduya, from the wall. “That is the entire point and you have all missed it. There is no answer. In January there was an answer and the answer was a basement with a key and two of us and a torch. There is not an answer now. That is what the order did, and it did it in twenty minutes from the signature to the van.”

“Then that is what you put,” said Nell Ardent. “You put *there is no answer and we are not prepared to pretend there is, and we are asking the panel to say so on the record, in public, with our names on it, because the next time it happens somebody has to be able to say that the lowland knew and said it and was not asked.*”

“That is a defeat dressed up.”

“It is a record dressed up,” said Nell Ardent, “and I know the difference, and so will they, and it is worth more than the deposit.”

Yasmin Rohan took her keys off the belt and put them on the table, all eleven of them, in a ring, and put the ledger on top.

“Two things,” she said. “One. The clinic.”

“The Thursday session moves to the Methodist hall on Carrow Lane and the next one is in three days, and the hall is nine hundred metres from the front door of this building and up nine steps, and the list is forty-one patients and nine of them are over seventy-five and three of them are housebound, and I have already had to tell two of them on the telephone and one of them cried and one of them said that is fine, and both of those reactions were correct and I did not know which one was coming.”

“Can the loft do Thursdays?” said someone at the back.

“The loft does Wednesdays and the loft is up a towpath in a district that is not on any of your maps,” said Pia Santos, from the wall, where she had been standing for twenty minutes without speaking. “And before anybody asks me to carry a message up that path, here is what I am carrying and it is the only thing: *no*. I have said it twice this week to Yasmin and I am saying it in this room. The Weir does not hold the store, does not hold your clinic, does not hold your beds, and does not go next to you on a piece of paper. If your building and our street end up in the same file, then the next thing that happens to both of us is a count, and the count will be done by an office that will use my mother’s door number for the whole of it, and there will be a number under it and the number will be wrong and the wrongness will be in the file for ever.”

Nobody contradicted her, and Jonas understood, standing at the back of a laundry room, that a nineteen-year-old courier had just made the correct procedural argument to a room of eleven adults and a lawyer and he had not made it himself in three weeks.

“Two,” said Yasmin Rohan. “CivicCore wrote to me on Thursday afternoon.”

The room changed.

“They have offered to hold the store contents under the continuity contract, on their own premises, with their own keyholder, for a weekly sum, and they have offered to stand the cost of the Thursday clinic at Carrow Lane for eleven weeks, and they have offered both of those things in one letter with a reference number on it, and the letter says that the arrangements are without prejudice to the appeal and do not constitute an admission.”

“Is that not good?” said Ferdy Oduya.

“It is very good,” said Yasmin Rohan, “and I am going to write to them this afternoon and decline it in one sentence, and the sentence is: *the lowland declines, thank you, because in the fourth year of this we would be a client, and I have been to a lecture about what a client is, and I would rather have a store I cannot open than a store I can only open if we can pay.*” She pushed the keys across the table. “And I want that read into whatever the panel reads.”

Nell Ardent picked up the letter, read two lines of it, and set it down.

“They will not press it,” she said.

“They always do not press it,” said Yasmin Rohan, “and that is not a complaint about them, it is the difference between them and everybody else, and I have noticed it and I am recording that I noticed it, and it does not make them the answer.”

Oren Pell came at four and sat in the laundry room with his clipboard and did not sit in it, and Jonas was there because Yasmin Rohan had told him at one o’clock that she was not going to keep him out of a room for a second time after she had kept him out of one, which was a sentence he did not know what to do with.

“The Weir question,” said Yasmin Rohan. “Can they be a party?”

“No,” said Oren Pell. He put his hand flat on the table, fingers spread, and bent one of them down. “I asked the panel’s clerk at eleven this morning, before I came, and she said it in one sentence and I am not going to improve on it. *A party is a person or an institution with a name on a register.* That is her sentence and that is the whole of it.

“This one.” The first finger. “No address, anywhere, in any system. This one.” The second. “A classification going to a review tomorrow that says both its nodes are plant. And this one.” The third. “A term that expired on the twenty-first at half past one and has not been renewed, so there is nobody there to name even if you could name the street. I have been trying to give that council a thing to serve for thirty-two years and I have never once had anything to put in an envelope.”

“Could their evidence be read?” said Jonas.

“No,” said Oren Pell. “And do not try, and I am going to tell you why, and then you can be angry with me. In eleven days, with a plate working and a term alive and a rota on it, that tap ran itself from Monday to Thursday because the mains does not answer to a rota, and nobody has any remedy and the rota cannot stop it. That is the best evidence anybody in this city has that an unheld node is not a service. And I cannot put it in front of a panel, because the term that was broken on the twenty-first of March was one I did not write and cannot be produced, and the department will say the record is unreliable, and the department will be right to say it, because the record *is* unreliable, and you know precisely why it is unreliable and so do I and it is a tin in a loft and I am not asking and I never will.”

“Then we appeal on arithmetic and you tell us we lose,” said Yasmin Rohan.

“I tell you the panel cannot hear arithmetic,” said Oren Pell. “I do not tell you we lose, because I do not know that, and I have been wrong about a building twice and I would rather be wrong a third time than be right and have said so.”

Jonas took the sheet of ruled paper out of his inside pocket. Pia Santos had written it herself, on the Saturday morning at the bottom of the slipway, on the back of his own copy of the series 6 form, and had made him fold it into four and had watched him fold it. It was not the loft’s paper. The loft’s paper was one side of a sheet, on four paperclips under the glass canopy in the Old Ward, and he had never been near it. This had four names against three positions and a ring drawn round a blank space, and under the ring, in his own hand, because he had copied it off a kitchen wall out of memory on the way back down the path: **THE 22ND, AT 03:00, ON A BANK. NO NAME.** It was the only thing in his coat he was not allowed to write into a file.

He did not put it on the table.

“There is a piece of paper in that room,” said Yasmin Rohan. She had not looked at it and had not needed to. “I have heard about it. A woman on a street in a district with no address wrote down three positions and drew a ring round the place where a name would go, on purpose, so that everybody could see that there was not one. And you have been carrying it around since Saturday.”

“Yes.”

“Do not bring it in here,” she said. “Not to me, not to the panel, not into the file. If your paper and my appeal are in one bundle, they are two examples of the same problem, and the answer to both of them is going to be *a name*, because a name is the only thing this system knows how to ask for, and if they put a name on your street then your street is a customer or a ward or a case, and if they put a name on mine then mine is a landlord’s tenant with a defect, and the panel will find against me in about nine minutes and find for them in about nine minutes and we will both be right about our own street and lose on the arithmetic.”

“That is exactly right,” said Jonas.

“Good, because I have known it since Tuesday and I have been waiting to see if you would,” said Yasmin Rohan, “and you did, in about four seconds, which is the fastest anybody has got there this month, and put it down that you were the fastest, because I am not going to soften anything else.”

“There is one more thing in that file,” said Oren Pell, standing up, “and it is the one I came to say, and then I am going because I am not a member of this association and I have no standing in a laundry room.

“On the twenty-third of April the panel will ask who is instructing counsel, and it will require a name, because a panel that hears an appeal from eleven unnamed households is not an appeal, it is a complaint. And you can put a person, and it does not have to be you, and I am telling you that as a registrar and not as a friend, because the moment a name goes on that form it is a civic record and that person becomes a person who can be served with the enforcement, at their own door, on their own, for as long as it takes.”

“I know,” said Yasmin Rohan.

“Eleven flats,” said Oren Pell. “Forty-one patients on a Thursday. Two thousand four hundred sandbags. And one name.”

He put his clipboard under his arm and went up the stairs, and the nine steps, and out into a yard where the van from Marsh Lane was still standing with its engine off.

Selah Mora was the last one out. She had been in the hostel corridor in a street off Salt Row since nine o’clock the previous night with a list of eleven addresses and a pen, because eleven people sleeping in a hostel is a return-of-care problem, and she had made herself the person who had it, and nobody had asked her to. She had read the return-of-care limb out loud to three of the night staff and made them write it down, and the second one had written *rest* instead of *return of care*, and she had made him do it again.

At the bottom of the stairs she stopped and turned round.

“That is the shape,” she said. “That is the shape of every single one of these, and it is always the shape, and I have been watching it for eleven days from a corridor where a man of twenty-six hands over a key to a woman of sixty-eight every night at ten o’clock. Eleven flats, one name. Fifty-nine doors, no name, a ring round the gap. One clinic and a hundred and eighteen thousand pounds that a woman will not let anybody compute. It is the shape, and every time the shape arrives it is always a person who is paying, and it is never the person with the lever.”

“Selah,” said Jonas.

“I am not finished. The ninth is in nine days, and forty minutes of *matters arising*, and I am going to ring him tomorrow and I am going to read him the line off your board and I am going to make him say the words *not yet agreed* out loud in a telephone, so that on the ninth, when there are nine other things on that agenda, at least one person in that room has said them today. And that is all I have got and it is a telephone call and it is not a cheque.” She put her bag on her shoulder. “And I am not going to be one of four women hauling two thousand four hundred sandbags to a lock-up on Marsh Lane at eleven o’clock at night, and I know the woman there is watching me decide, and I have decided.”

Yasmin Rohan opened the ledger to the second page, and took her own pen out of her own coat pocket, and wrote at the bottom of it, in the fourth line of the form, one name, and underlined it, and did not read it out to anybody.
