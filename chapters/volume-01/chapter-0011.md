# Chapter 0011 — The Bond

The shop at River Stacks smelled of cut wire, hot dust, and coffee that somebody had made too strong at six in the morning. Jonas arrived at ten to nine with a night he could not account for behind him, and with the appointment notice in his coat pocket, folded for the fourth time that week.

He had slept for perhaps three hours. The afterimage the bridge plate had burned into the backs of his eyes in October was back, and it came with a dull taste he could not rinse away. Twice in the night he had woken convinced he heard a plate give its blunt ceramic note, and both times the sound was his own jaw.

Tomas Renn had put a straight-backed chair in the middle of the shop floor and a sheet of paper on it.

“You slept,” Tomas said.

“Some.”

“You look like a man who slept.” The shop steward turned the paper so it faced Jonas. “Read this standing up. You look worse sitting down.”

Bea Osunde was at the long bench with a coil of control cable and a pair of wire strippers she was using as a pointer. She had been grade II for nineteen years and had the department’s particular gift for making a bad idea feel embarrassed. Two other technicians kept their heads down over a dismantled relay cabinet, listening with their hands.

Nina Calder stood by the parts counter with a folder under her arm and the expression of a woman who had already read the paper and did not like it.

“What is it?” Jonas said.

“It is the thing you sign before you make a civic repair.” Tomas did not move from the chair. “I did not write it alone. Two other stewards wrote it, and Nina typed it, and a public-interest lawyer read it to make sure it is not clever.”

Jonas picked it up.

At the top it said **RESPONSIBILITY BOND — NDC SHOP COPY**. Under that, seven numbered clauses. He had time to read the whole thing twice, which meant it was short enough to be a promise and long enough to be a weapon.

> **1. SCOPE.** One local permission term, at one named hinge. No citywide change, no cross-node change, no root change. The hinge is named on the line below and may not be changed after signature.
> **2. WITNESS.** At least one named living person who has read this bond and can state, from memory, what the term permits, when it ends, and what claim follows. The issuer may not be the only witness to his own change.
> **3. EXPIRY.** The term ends at the stated hour. After that hour the term is closed, and it reopens only when the named bearers say so, in person, out loud, together.
> **4. CLAIM.** The issuer carries the maintenance of the term he issues, from the moment he issues it until either a named successor steward accepts that maintenance, or the term expires. The claim is not satisfied by intention, apology, or good conduct.
> **5. REFUSAL.** Any named bearer or witness may stop the change, or any later use of it, without the issuer’s agreement. A refusal does not have to give a reason the issuer accepts.
> **6. IF THE ISSUER IS WRONG.** The term returns to the state it was in before he touched it. His name stays in the record as the person who wrote it. The shop steward or the department may suspend his civic repair authority until a review, and the review is not his to postpone.
> **7.** This bond does not affect the issuer’s civil-service appointment.

Jonas read the seventh line twice.

“That one is mine,” he said.

“It is,” Tomas agreed. “It is also the reason I need this read out loud in a room with witnesses in it. A man who believes a bond can revoke his own job will write anything you ask for and mean none of it.”

He reached behind the chair and put a second document on the seat beside the first.

Jonas recognized it at once. Four lines, an institutional seal, three names. *Selah’s receiving shelter. Mina Okafor, clinical duty. Mara Holt, night watch.* It was the transport record he had signed two days earlier, the morning after Oren sealed the old-canal building, because Tomas had put a blank personal guarantee in front of him and he had refused to sign that instead.

“Put them side by side,” Tomas said. “Read me the transport record.”

Jonas read it. It named a route, a vehicle, a handoff, and three custodians. It had no term. It had no expiry. It had no line saying what happened if the route failed at three in the morning, and it had no line saying who could say no.

“Now tell me which one you would be holding at three in the morning if the door you mended shut on somebody.”

“The transport record,” Jonas said.

“Why?”

“Because it has names in it.”

“It has institutions in it. That is not the same as a person who can refuse you.” Tomas took both sheets and put them back down, side by side, and the two pieces of paper looked to Jonas like two people in a corridor, one of whom had agreed to be there. “That is the difference. The one you signed the day before the bridge test moves a stretcher. The one in front of you lets you write a rule. People understand the first and get frightened by the second, and the frightened ones are the ones who are on the corridor when it stops working.”

“Fine,” Jonas said. “Give me a pen.”

“Give me a sentence first.”

Jonas had already composed it. He had been composing it on the e-bike, in the shower, in the four minutes of sleep he had got. It had a good shape to it.

“I, Jonas Mercer, NDC grade III, guarantee that the Saint Orra west clinic gate will remain in service for the care of any person who requires it, until the underlying fault is resolved.”

He said it out loud because Tomas had asked him to say things out loud all morning, and the shop had gone quiet in the way rooms do when everyone is waiting to see whether a person means it.

Bea Osunde put down her wire strippers.

“That is a nice sentence,” Bea said.

“Thank you.”

“It is also four words wide.” Bea came over and pointed at the middle of the page without touching it. “Any person who requires it. So who decides who requires it? Until the fault is resolved. Resolved by whom, on what day, and what happens to the gate in the meantime if you are the person who is dead, or asleep, or on the other side of the city, or simply not feeling it? You have given me a door that is open forever, for everyone, and you have not put your name on the only thing in the sentence that can shut it.”

“It was meant to be safe.”

“It is the opposite of safe. It is safe for you and it is enormous for everyone who has to stand next to it.” Bea went back to her cable. “And it is not a term. A term has an end. You wrote a promise and called it a door.”

Jonas felt heat climb out of his collar. He looked at the paper and could see, now that Bea had said it, that his sentence had no bottom to it. He had written it in the shape of a guarantee because a guarantee sounds like strength, and because a term, with its hours and its conditions, sounded like a set of small doors that could all be closed at once.

Nina opened her folder. “There is a plate on your test bench,” she said. “Decommissioned, pulled out of the Old Ward store in March. Clinic type. It will answer a credential if it is asked properly.”

“Asked properly how?”

“That,” said Nina, “is the question, and you have not answered it, so go and find out.”

The bench plate was a square of chipped ceramic in a steel frame, older than anything in the shop and greener than it should have been. It had come out of a records room with a paper tag wired to it and no destination. Jonas set it on the bench, clipped a grounding strap to the frame, and put the brass probe against the ceramic.

“Anchor me,” he said.

Bea moved to his left shoulder. “I am here. I am a person, not a plate.”

He put his maintenance credential against the field and said the sentence he had written, all of it, slowly, the way a person reads a consent form to somebody who is about to sign it.

The plate did not think about it. It did not weigh the offer or find it generous. It went ceramic white and put three short lines across his maintenance lens, and then it put a fourth line under them that he had never seen before.

> **A promise with no end is not a term.**
> **No bearer named. No expiry.**
> **Nothing to hold.**

Jonas waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it.

The field stayed blank. The plate had not opened anything, refused anything, or recorded anything at all. His credential had gone into a gap and come out the other side, and the door on the bench did not move, because there was no such thing as the door on the bench. There was a hinge with no service behind it and no one to answer for it.

Then the taste arrived. Copper, thick, and the pressure behind his eyes came up like water in a pipe, and Jonas found that his right hand had gone cold from the wrist to the second knuckle. He took his hand off the ceramic and put it flat on the bench until the bench stopped moving.

“You were somewhere,” Bea said.

“There was no term to read. There was only an empty field.”

“Right,” Bea said. “Try the narrow one. Say it with a person and an hour in it and see whether the plate is happier.”

Jonas tried the narrow version. He said: the west clinic care entrance may be held for a verified clinic transfer by the named watch on duty, from the first hour to the sixth hour.

“That is better,” Bea said, and then asked, without any particular emphasis, “and on the sixth hour, who is it?”

“Anyone on the rota.”

“There is no rota.”

“Then the watch.”

“The watch is one woman with a key and a contract that ends at eleven and a board that says she left at six.” Bea folded her arms. “So answer me properly. It is six o’clock. She has gone home. Who opens it?”

Jonas opened his mouth and found that he had nothing, and that what he had was worse than nothing, which was the phrase *I will*.

Tomas picked up the pen and put it on the bench between them.

“Write down what happened,” he said. “Not what you promised. What happened.”

Jonas wrote: **No bearer available at 18:00. Issuer proposed to cover. Issuer is one person. Issuer sleeps.**

Tomas read it upside down. “That is the whole job in four lines. You did not discover it with a machine. You discovered it because a woman asked you a question about a clock. Now put the four lines in order, and put a number on each one, and read them out.”

Jonas wrote the seven clauses in Tomas’s order, and Tomas corrected him twice. When he reached the claim, Jonas tried to write that the claim was discharged by good and diligent maintenance.

“No,” Bea said. “Good and diligent is a compliment. A claim is something somebody gets.”

He wrote it the way it was written on Tomas’s page, in the flat language of a person who has already been in trouble: *not satisfied by intention, apology, or good conduct.* Then he wrote the appointment line at the bottom, in his own words, and underlined it, and Tomas let him.

“It does not protect you,” Tomas said, watching him write it. “You understand that it does not protect you.”

“It protects the door from me,” Jonas said. “That is the part I would have written wrong.”

Nina made him read the whole thing twice more, and then she made him say the hinge out loud and write it, and then she made him cross out the words *and related service* that he had tried to add to the scope line, because a hinge is a place and *related service* is a mood.

At five past eleven the door at the front of the shop opened, and Ruth Nwosu came in out of the cold with her coat still buttoned and her watch still on.

“The board has me off at six,” she said, before anyone asked. “I start at ten. I have done that for nineteen years and not one hour of the night is in any record, and I am not here as a favor to you. If that page goes onto that gate with my name on it, somebody in an office is going to have to explain why a duty that has happened every night for nineteen years was recorded as having stopped at six. I have come for an hour, the shop’s dispute fund is paying it, and it is not going to be you, because a technician paying it out of his own pocket is the exact thing this document is about.”

Bea found her a chair. Ruth read the seven clauses twice, slowly, and then read the third one again, and Jonas watched her mouth move on the word *outloud*.

“Clause five,” she said. “I want it written that I can stop it at any use, and that I do not have to tell you why, and that you cannot ask me afterwards what my reason was.”

“You can have that.”

“I want it in my words, not yours.” She took the pen from the bench, wrote a line in the margin in block capitals, and signed her name under it. **I CAN STOP IT AND KEEP MY REASON. HE MAY NOT ASK ME FOR IT AFTERWARDS.**

“Is that all of it?” she said, and Jonas told her what the claim on the term would have to be, which was that if a term failed without a bearer present the route would narrow to emergency only, and the issuer would answer for the delay.

Ruth looked at him for a while. “That is the sentence you did not write,” she said. “That one is worth reading to people.”

She signed the witness line. Bea Osunde signed the second witness line, and then went back to her cable, and told Jonas that if he ever signed another repair with *and related service* in the scope she would file a grievance purely for the pleasure of it. Nina Calder countersigned as records, and stapled the shop copy to the yellow board by the door, where every technician who came in would have to walk past it, and wrote the time and the date on the board’s margin in her careful hand.

Tomas removed the chair from the middle of the floor.

“Accepted,” he said. “Not approved. Accepted, with a witness who has put a condition on it and a shop copy on a board, which means that if you go behind any part of it, the board is the first witness the department calls.”

Jonas looked at the yellow sheet. His name sat on the issuer line, the claim line, and the appointment line he had insisted on. He had a document that proved he intended to take responsibility and, in a way he had not expected, a document that proved he had tried to keep himself beyond the reach of one.

“Go and find out what the gate is for,” Tomas said, taking the bench plate and putting it back under its paper tag. “Not what it is failing at. What it is for. Then go back and ask three people, and make sure two of them disagree with you.”

“Why two?”

“Because if three people agree, one of them has not understood the document yet,” Tomas said, “and that one will be you, six days from now, at an hour when nobody is available.”
