# Chapter 0026 — The Terms of a Pump

They met at nine on the Monday night with the tide table on the table and the green form in Margo Doré's hand, and it went on until two in the morning, and at no point did anybody stand up and leave, which Jonas understood afterwards to have been the only reason anything was decided at all.

The first hour was arithmetic, and Jonas did the arithmetic because he was the only person in the room who could be trusted with a sum and not trusted with anything else.

“Saturday’s figure was seven thousand one hundred and thirty, and I want to take it apart, because the parts of it do not all exist at the same time and I do not want anybody in this kitchen deciding against a number that was never real.”

He wrote it on the back of the tide table in his bad left hand.

> **MARINE CREW, six, out of town, four days — 3,600**
> **PUMP, 4-inch diesel, four days — 1,900**
> **STONE, 4 tonnes, to the top of this street — 340**
> **SKIP AND LORRY, rotten sill and silt — 410**
> **FLINT, HAUNCHING, GASKET, 40 BAGS OF BEDDING COMPOUND — 280**
> **COUNTY MASON, two days — 600**
> **— seven thousand one hundred and thirty**

“Seven thousand one hundred and thirty,” he said. “And two of those six lines only exist if a name goes in box four of that green form, because the pump and the mason come with the county, and the county does not lend a pump without somebody to serve notices on. So the honest version is two prices. Everything except the pump and the mason is four thousand six hundred and thirty. Everything is seven thousand one hundred and thirty.”

“And we have nine hundred and thirteen,” said Margo.

“We have nine hundred and thirteen. Seventy-two in the loft tin, seven hundred and forty in the water tin, and a hundred and one in the church hall that is harvest supper money and has been in the tin since September because there has not been a harvest supper.”

Aurrie Nkemdirim made a noise with her mouth that was not quite a laugh.

“Ten years,” she said, “of people saying to me, *you could always have asked.* Ten years. And when I finally produce the piece of paper that you have all been waiting eleven days for, it says that we are three and a half thousand pounds short, and that the only way to be three and a half thousand pounds short is to be six people instead of a crew.”

“Yes,” said Jonas.

“Say it properly, though,” said Perdita Sow.

He looked at her properly. She was forty-four, she was on her third cup of tea, she swept roads for the borough and had swept the weir road on Sundays for eleven years because it was not on her round, and nobody had ever given her a map that had this street on it, and she had found out about the meeting at four o'clock that afternoon because she was in the boat shed when Bo was there.

“I am not the department,” Jonas said. “I am a technician with a debt. Nobody on this street has to take my word for anything and I have been wrong in front of you in public twice this week.”

“The man in the coat,” said Bo.

“The man in the coat was me,” Jonas said, “in a different coat, in a department van, in 2019. I said the gasket was the wrong size. It was the wrong size. I did not know that, and I said it in a shed, to a boy, in a hurry, and the stand was off for six weeks, and nobody said why to anybody afterwards, and I have been on this street five times since and nobody has mentioned it, which is worse.”

He put the pen down.

“And Hector asked me for a cigarette outside a hardware shop that same year, and I did not give him one. He told me that on Sunday, on a towpath, with a look on his face, and I stood there and let him tell me that a man had my face, and I did not say a word. That is four days I have been carrying that round with me, and I am putting it down here because I have just said the other thing out loud and I am not able to carry two of them.”

“Did he know?” said Aurrie Nkemdirim.

“He knew it was somebody,” said Jonas. “He has known for six years and he did not put it to me, and I did not give him anywhere to put it, and that is mine and not his, and I would like it written down in whatever it is you write down.”

The arithmetic sat there for a while.

“So it is the six of us,” said Hector Mbatha. He had his coat on and had not taken it off since eight o’clock, which was the way he did a long night. “That is what we have established. Four thousand six hundred and thirty is the price of everything except the two things that require a name, and we are three thousand seven hundred and seventeen short of that, and there is a pile of stone arriving on Tuesday for three hundred and forty which is somebody’s idea of a joke.”

“No,” said Margo. “I am not accepting that arithmetic and neither should you.”

She put the green form on the table, face up, with the pen beside it, and she did not look at it while she said it, and everybody in the room understood that she had known since about half past nine exactly what she was going to say and had spent two hours letting the room arrive at it.

“I am not going to write my name in box four,” she said, “and I am going to tell you why in one sentence and then I am not going to say it again tonight, and Hector is going to say the same thing in a different sentence because he has been holding it since seven o’clock and I can see him holding it.”

“Go on,” said Jonas, and then said, “No. Wait. Before anybody says it — I am not going to sit here and listen to two people refuse a pump and then be the man who points at the six of you and calls that a decision. I want the refusal to cost something on the page before it costs anything on the bank. Write the sentence down. That is all. That is the only thing I am asking.”

Margo Doré picked up the pen and wrote on the green form, in box four, on the line the county had printed:

> **M. DORÉ WILL NOT ACCEPT. A PERSON WHO CANNOT BE REACHED AT 03:00 MUST NOT SIGN FOR MACHINERY.**
> **M. DORÉ, KEEPER OF THE ROT A, 63.**

“That is wrong,” she said, “the rota is spelled wrong, and I am leaving it wrong, because I wrote it in a hurry and because if somebody in a county office corrects my handwriting they are editing my refusal, and I do not want my refusal edited.”

“Margo —”

“You will let me finish, because you have not once in ten days interrupted anybody on this street except yourself.” She put the pen down. “It is not that I will not wake up. I am sixty-three and I have done the trap at low water for six years and I will be awake at three in the morning, God knows I will. It is that the form asks me to be responsible for a machine, and I do not want that, and I want to be exact about why, because in about four years somebody is going to say that Margo Doré was frightened off a pump by sentiment and I will not have it said about me.

“If my name is in that box, then on Tuesday there is a pump on this street, and on Tuesday night there is a machine out there that somebody has to lock, and if it is stolen the county want to know who was responsible, and if it floods and the inlet blocks and it does not pump on the ebb, the county want to know who was responsible. And every night for four nights, the person who is responsible for that pump is a person who is out on a bank in the dark in a flood year watching a lamp. And if I sign that form, then on the fourth night, when I am standing there at ten past three in the morning being fifty-eight years old, I will be doing it *because I signed a piece of paper*. I will not be doing it because I am the person who does it. And the difference between those two is the whole of my life, and I have watched the other one kill a man.” She turned her cup round. “So no. And I want it written down that it was not the pump. The pump is a good pump. I did not want the responsibility, and I have wanted a lot of things I have not wanted recently.”

Hector Mbatha stood up, which he had not done in nine years, and it took him a moment, and nobody helped him and he would not have let them.

“I have wanted a number since 1994,” he said, “and I have said so in this kitchen twice tonight, and I am going to say the last sentence about it now and then I am going to sit down for the rest of the night. If the county’s name goes on this street in a box on a green form, then this street has an owner. And an owner is somebody with a duty, and a duty is somebody who has to be on a bank at three in the morning, and I am seventy years old, and I have a mother in number twenty-two, and I will not be that person, and neither should anybody here, and if a name goes in that box the honest truth is that none of the six of us can do it, and we all know it, and the form does not have a box that says *nobody here*.”

“Who would?” said Aurrie.

Nobody answered for some time, and then Aurrie Nkemdirim said, in a completely level voice, “Me. Obviously me. I have wanted a district since 1994 and it turns out that a district is a box with a person’s name in it, and there is a person here who has wanted one for thirty-two years and has a building with a committee and a minute book and a roof, and I would put my name in it tomorrow morning and I would be entirely honest about wanting to.”

She stood up from the table and came round it, and she was not dramatic at all, which was the thing that undid people about her.

“But I am not going to,” she said, “and here is the reason, and it is the reason I have been not-saying for eleven years and I am saying it now because if I do not say it in this kitchen tonight I will say it to a county officer in a corridor in a fortnight and I will have forgotten that I was ever frightened of it.

“The day my name is in box four, this street has an owner. And an owner is responsible. And on the twenty-second, when the water comes over the path and the bank is running, the person who has to be on that bank at three in the morning is *the owner*. Not the street. The owner. And then it is not a favour and it is not a rota and it is not six neighbours and it is not Bo on a water rota since he was eleven. It is one person who owns a thing, being legally and morally and practically the only person who can be out there, and who will be out there at three in the morning on the night it comes, every night, for as long as she owns it, because that is what owning is. And I do not want that, and I have not wanted it since about half past nine tonight, and the reason I have not wanted it is that the only thing this street has ever had is that it belongs to nobody, and everybody here, and I mean everybody including Bo and including Margo, has been able to say *that is not mine, and I am not going to be the one who is standing on the bank at three in the morning*, and that has been the entire civic technology of this street since before I came to it.” She put both hands flat on the table. “I would rather have no pump and keep that.”

Nobody in the kitchen said anything at all for a length of time that Perdita Sow later described as about forty seconds and that Jonas described, in his own account, written on a page nobody read, as the longest he had been in a room in his life.

“Then there is no pump,” said Bo.

“There is no pump,” said Margo. “And no mason, because the mason comes with it. Four thousand six hundred and thirty against nine hundred and thirteen.”

“Three thousand seven hundred and seventeen,” said Jonas.

“No it is not,” said Margo. “It is one thousand and thirty, because we are not hiring six men out of the city, and the six of us are not on a wage, and that is the entire point and it is also the reason the sum is what it is. One thousand and thirty against nine hundred and thirteen. A hundred and seventeen pounds short.”

It was Bo who found it, and it took him most of the night.

“No it is not short,” he said, at about half past twelve. “The skip is four hundred and ten and it is for the rotten sill and the silt. If the chamber is not pumped, we are not cutting the sill, and we are not paying for the skip. And the stone is not bought until somebody has cut the bank back, and the bank is dry work, and dry work is a morning with five people and a barrow, and there is no hire in that. So here is the whole of what is actually on the table tonight. Stone, three hundred and forty. Flint, haunching, gasket and forty bags of compound, two hundred and eighty. That is six hundred and twenty. And we have nine hundred and thirteen.”

“We have two hundred and ninety-three pounds and no pump and no sill,” said Margo.

“We have two hundred and ninety-three pounds and a bank that does not boil on the twenty-second,” said Bo.

That was the decision, and it took another hour and a half, and what decided it was not the money.

It was Margo Doré, at about one in the morning, when somebody — Jonas, probably, though he was not the one who said it first — asked who was going to be on the bank.

Not the work bank. The bank in the other sense. The one with a boil on it and four tonnes of stone going onto it on Tuesday, cut back two metres, above a chamber that could not be pumped, on a street where a flood was coming on the twenty-second.

“That is what the paper says,” she said, and went and got the sheet Bo had pinned up on Saturday with the four names on it, and held it up, and read the bottom line, which was the one nobody had ever filled in.

> **THE BANK — anyone who will take it, and there is no name on that line because there is no name yet.**

“There is no name on it,” she said, “and that is not an oversight, that is the finding, and it has been a finding since the middle of the month and I have been putting it off saying it out loud because the moment I say it somebody in this room is going to be a hero. So I am going to say it and then I am going to make sure nobody is a hero. On the twenty-second there will be a flood on this street and there will be a bank with four tonnes of stone on it that has been cut back two metres, and it will be night, and there will be nobody on it unless we decide tonight that there is. And I am telling you now that I am not going to be asked, and I am not going to put my name on that line, and I will not be on a bank at three in the morning in a flood year because I have said no once tonight to a machine and I am not going to be asked again an hour later about a different thing while I am still in the room.”

Hector said nothing, having already said his.

Wesley Kpomah said, “I will be on the stone in the day. I will not be on it at night and I am not giving a reason and I am not being asked for one, and write that on the paper so that nobody has to ask me on the night.”

Aurrie Nkemdirim said, “And I will not.”

And then it was four o’clock in the morning, and there were six seats on the wall, and the people who had spoken about them were the three who had said no, and Margo, and Bo, who had been in the room since half past six on the Friday before, and Perdita Sow, who had not spoken once in the four hours she had been sitting at the end of the table. She put her cup down.

“I will be on it,” she said.

Hector turned round in his chair. “Perdita —”

“I have swept this road on a Sunday for eleven years,” Perdita said, “and it is not on my round, and I have never been given a map with it on it, and I have never once been paid for the Sundays, and I am not doing this because I am brave. I am doing it because I have been coming down here on a Sunday for eleven years for a hedge and I have got into the habit, and a habit is not the same as a decision, and I would rather be a person with a habit on the twenty-second than a person who was brave once in a kitchen.” She shrugged. “And I will be on it in the daytime, and I will bring my own lamp, and I am not letting anybody else hold it, because it is a lamp and I have had a lamp on that road for eleven years.”

Which left one seat and nobody in it, and Jonas, who had been standing at the edge of this for four hours with his hands in his pockets because he had understood by about the second hour that he was furniture, finally sat down.

“I am going to say the thing that is mine to say and then I am going to go and sleep for three hours because I have to be on a bank at five past five tomorrow morning to be useful, and the only useful thing I know how to do is hold things and read plates.”

Nobody told him to stop.

“The eighteenth. Four o’clock in the morning. The tide is out. The bank is dry. That is when the paper gets read out loud in this kitchen to whoever turns up, and if there is nobody, the work does not happen, exactly as Aurrie said on Saturday. The work that happens is the bank, and the bank is not the barrier, and I want that written on the paper in the middle in the same size as everything else, because in four months one of us is going to come back here and tell a county officer that the barrier on the lowland dip has been repaired, and it will not have been. It will have had four tonnes of stone and a new sill packed in compound and a bank cut back, and the flap will still be the same flap, and the chamber will still flood at four hundred an hour at the spring, and it will be the twenty-second before anybody is out there.”

He put his hand on the paper and stopped, because there was a blank space in the middle of it and he had been about to write in it and he did not know what to write, and the room waited, and he understood that they were waiting on purpose.

“I am not going to write a name on that line,” he said, “and I am not going to be on that bank, and I would like it minuted that I offered, in a kitchen, on a street with no address, once, and was refused by five people separately, and that the five refusals are not a failure of the meeting.”

“That is not a minute,” said Margo, “that is a thing you have just said, and you have said it about yourself, and nobody here has asked you to. Take it out.”

“I will take it out.”

“Then write the date and the tide and the number of seats,” Margo said, “and leave the rest of it blank, and put a line round the blank bit so that somebody can see that it is on purpose and not because a man from the city did not have anybody to write.”

And he wrote, in the middle of the page, in a ring of pencil, and it is the only thing in the paper that anybody on the Weir has ever read out loud to a stranger:

> **THE 22ND, AT 03:00, ON A BANK. NO NAME.**

And then the light came up in the window, because it was twenty to four in the morning, and Perdita Sow looked out of it and said, “That is the early, and the early is the one that takes the water first,” and everybody in the kitchen stood up at once, and the six of them went down the path in the grey with torches to clear the silt trap, and Jonas Mercer went home to sleep for three hours, and got two of them, and was on the bank at ten past five with his left hand in his pocket and a strip of paper in the other one that said a tide time, and did nothing whatever for anybody that he was any good at.
