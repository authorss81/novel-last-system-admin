# Chapter 0019 — Another Map

Pia Santos met him at ten past six at the bottom of the towpath with her bicycle against a railing and three bags on the handlebars and a look on her face that had been assembled in advance.

“You have not changed your jacket,” she said. “That is the first thing. The second thing is that you are not carrying the roll.”

“It is at the shop.”

“Good. Do not go and get it. I have watched men carry a roll into a place and the roll turns the place into a job.” She took her gloves off and put them in her jacket. “Here are the four things, and I am saying them here, on the towpath, where there is nobody, so that if you break one I do not have to explain it to anybody.”

“All right.”

“One. No names. Not the children, not the old people, not who lives in which building. You will not take a list, you will not ask for one, and you will not be given one, and if a name comes into your head you will leave it there.”

“I have never wanted a list from you.”

“Two. No photographs. Not of faces, not of the street, not of the stand, not of the plates. If you need to remember something, you will remember it, and if you cannot remember it, it is not yours.”

“Agreed.”

“Three. No promises. If somebody here asks you for something, and you are about to say *yes, that is possible*, you come and find me first and I will decide whether you are allowed to say it. I am not being dramatic. I have watched a man say *yes, that is possible* to a woman with a water problem and the water problem became a district code in eleven weeks and my mother was given a letter with a number on it that she did not want.”

“Nothing without the people here agreeing to it.”

“Four.” She looked at him, and for a moment the professional hardness went out of her face and something older and more tired looked through. “I am not lending you the old-canal folder. Not because Selah would stop me — Selah would have to be unconscious — but because it is not the thing you want and it is not mine to lend. It is a book of people who were frightened in a specific week. If you open it you will learn four names and think you know something.”

“I am not going to open it.”

“I know. I told Selah last night, on the way down here, and she said that if you turn up on your own down there she will put the kettle on and that is all, and I have decided to tell you that so that you do not think the two of us are running something.” Pia took her gloves back out. “That is all four things. Now you can come up the Weir, and when we get to the loft I will say your name to one person and you will not say it to anybody else unless they say it first.”

They walked up a street that Jonas could not find on any of the four maps he had checked the previous afternoon.

It was three hundred meters long and it was not a street so much as a working space that people had been using as one. A boat shed with a chain across its doors had a queue of nine outside it in the last of the light, which was a fish seller, a bicycle repair, and a man who was doing neither of those things and standing in the same place because it was warm and the shed takes up a cold wind. A washing line ran from a gantry over the path, and somebody's washing was caught in it at head height, and a boy of about nine ducked under it without breaking stride.

The houses were low, brick, and built in the way of a place where the ground is cheap and the water is not. Most of them had a blue-painted door. Roughly one door in three had a brass token hanging from a nail beside it, worn pale, with three lines marked on it in blue tape.

He looked at one and then looked at another, six meters further on, and did not ask.

The net loft was a long low building at the top of the Weir with a corrugated roof and glass in the upper half of every window, and through the glass you could see a room with a rug on the floor and eleven small people on it and a woman sitting on a low chair with a mug of tea and a stack of paper cups in a shoe box.

They came in through a side door into a kitchen that smelled of toast and chlorine.

Aurrie Nkemdirim was sixty-one and had the manner of somebody who has run a small thing badly funded for a very long time. She took one look at Jonas, and looked at his right hand, and said, “You cannot use that properly.”

“I cannot use it properly.”

“Then you are not doing anything with it today. Sit down if you want to sit down. Do not pick up the baby, his other hand is stronger than yours and he knows it.”

“You are Aurrie,” Pia said, and that was the entire introduction, and Jonas understood that this was not rudeness.

“I am. And here is the situation, and I am going to say it plainly because you have a card with a deadline on it and I do not have time for the other kind of conversation.” She put the mug down. “The stand at the top of this road has been running twenty-two minutes a day for six weeks. It used to run forty. The rota is forty slots and we have not had water for half of them since the middle of the month, and the reason the rota has not been cancelled is that a cancelled rota is a thing you can find on a form, and a short rota is not.”

“I have not come about the stand.”

“You have come about a node on a card, and the node is why the stand has silt in it, so you have come about the stand. Have a cup of tea and I will tell you the whole of it, and then you will tell me something, which is the arrangement and I do not care that you are not used to it.”

Margo Doré came in from the yard while the tea was being poured, sixty-three, with a clipboard that had a cardigan sleeve over one corner to keep the rain off. She put the clipboard on the table and did not take her coat off.

“The trap is packed,” she said. “It packs every time the surge comes up over the embankment and it never washes itself out, because the lowland main is dead level for two hundred meters and the tide has to do the work and the tide only does it once a fortnight. We clear it by hand at low water. Four of us. It takes two hours and it is horrible and it is not on anybody’s rota but ours.”

“Can it be cleared differently?” Jonas asked.

“No. Not without money, and we have been told there is no money for a standpipe that is not in the index, which is correct, because the standpipe is not in the index.” Margo took the cardigan off the clipboard. “What has changed is the coupling. That is the boy’s part. Bo has replaced it four times this year and every time it is the same failure, because the length of pipe above it is wrong, and because every time somebody upstream puts a hose on the branch and forgets, and because the old main is iron and it is going.”

“Bo,” Aurrie said, toward the kitchen.

A boy of seventeen came in drying his hands on a tea towel. He had a callus on the right palm shaped like a crescent and he looked at Jonas with the flat assessment of somebody who has been talked over his entire life.

“If I cut back four hundred millimeters and put a proper compression coupling and a gasket on there, it will hold for two years,” Bo said. “It is not allowed.”

“Why not?”

“Because the tool is the loft’s and the loft’s tool is the rota’s, and I am not on the tool rota, and Aurrie will not put me on the tool rota, and the last person who fixed it without being on the tool rota was a man from a van in a coat who broke the stand for six weeks and told us the gasket was the wrong size.”

Bo said this without heat, which made it worse.

“You are not on the tool rota because you are seventeen,” Aurrie said.

“I am on the water rota. I have been on the water rota since I was eleven. I did the clearing at low water on Sunday with my hands in it to the wrist.” He looked at Jonas. “Are you the water department?”

“No.”

“Are you a doctor?”

“No.”

“Then you are a man with a deadline,” Bo said, “and I do not know what you want from me, and I have got a stand that has to be off my rota by six.”

In the room with the rug, the children were watching a woman build something out of cardboard. Four of them had brass tokens on strings. One of the tokens had the three lines marked in blue tape and had been marked so many times that the brass underneath had gone pale, and the child holding it was about seven, which is the age of a girl who came up a service lane in a green shuttle the same week he was, with a token in her fist, and Jonas did not ask, and did not look twice, and felt the not-asking sit in his chest like a swallowed coin.

“Who gave them those?” he said, to no one in particular, and Aurrie said, “Everybody has one. Do not ask why. It is a great deal of work to explain and it will not make you any more careful.”

On the wall by the kitchen door, above a shelf of cleaned cups, there was a plate.

It was a civic plate, of course. Everything was. It was fastened at a slight angle, as if it had been put up quickly by somebody who did not expect it to stay. The lower half of its face had been scraped — properly scraped, with a steel edge and patience — and the scraping had gone through the ceramic and left a pale scar, and the remaining text was in two different hands and two different decades.

> **[scraped] LOWLAND WARDS — WATCH ESTABLISHED FOR THE FLOOD YEARS**
> **ANY HOUSE MAY CALL. THE WATCH ANSWERS.**
> **[scraped]**

Aurrie saw him looking at it.

“It was there before I was,” she said. “Otie read it out to me once because she thought I would like it, and I said it was a nice idea, and she said *it was not an idea, it was a rota*, and then we had a fight about a kettle.” She put the tea towel down. “I do not know who scraped it. I have never known. It is not one of our things. It is a thing somebody did and then somebody undid and it is on my wall because the wall is where the loft keeps plates.”

“Who is Otie?”

“Ottoline Beck. She is seventy-one and she was a district nurse in the lowland for thirty-one years and she does a clinic in that room on Wednesdays from ten until two, and if you want to talk to anybody on this street who knows anything about a service, it is her, and she will talk to you for free, and she will also tell you that there is no district here, and she has been saying so since before the rest of us started.”

Pia put her hand on Jonas’s sleeve, which was the first time she had touched him, and it was not affection, and he understood that.

“Enough,” she said. “You have a face. I have watched four people get that face in this building and every one of them was about to do something generous to us so that they could go home with a story.”

“I was going to ask whether Ottie would let me look at the plate.”

“You were going to offer to clear the trap on Sunday.” She took her hand off his sleeve. “You were going to say *I can do the two hours of it, I have done harder things than that in mud*, and then in three weeks you would have to be here on a Sunday and you would not be, and we would have lost a Sunday.”

“That is what I was going to say.”

“Yes,” Pia said. “So do not say it. If you want to come back and be useful, come back useful. If you cannot, do not come back at all, and we will not think less of you, and you will not be owed anything, which is the part you will not like.”

They walked out to the top of the Weir at a quarter past six, and the water stand was a green pillar with a tap and a bucket on a chain and a rota board nailed to the fence beside it, and the rota board had forty names' worth of slots and a great many of them marked with a small cross.

Past the stand, the ground went flat and then dipped, and at the bottom of the dip the lowland main came up out of the ground in a concrete chamber with a hinged flap gate that was half in the water and half out, and beside it a bank of packed earth and gravel ran back toward the exchange, and the bank had a dark wet patch on its inner face about the size of a doorway.

Bo came out with a torch and shone it on the wet patch and did not say anything at all, which was the most eloquent thing that had happened to Jonas all day.

“That is a boil,” he said finally. “That is water coming up through the fill behind the bank. It is not the trap. The trap is a maintenance job. That is a different job and it is a bigger one and it belongs to about nine households and this exchange and a standpipe that forty people are on a rota for.”
