# Chapter 0025 — A Term at the Water Point

The stand was a green pillar about the height of a door with a bucket on a chain and a spout at the bottom, and it had been painted so many times that the paint had a texture like a cake, and on the back of it, at about the height of Jonas's hand, there was a plate.

He found it on the Monday morning because he went round the far side to look at the bracket he had cut back on the Thursday, and the pillar was in the way of the bracket.

He got the silt off it with the brush and his left thumb while Margo Doré stood on the other side of the pillar holding the rope of nothing in particular, and the plate came up the way the one in the chamber had come up, in pieces.

> **6-L/19**
> **LOWLAND WARDS — WEIR STAND, DRAWING POINT**
> **WATER IS DRAWN ONLY BY THE PERSON NAMED ON THE ROTA FOR THAT HOUR.**
> **IF THE SILT TRAP IS PACKED, THE STAND IS CLOSED.**
> **HOLDER: —**
> **CLAIM: not legible**

“They have not touched that in my lifetime,” said Margo, and then stopped, and started again, because she had caught herself. “That is not true and I nearly said it, so let us be exact. I have never cleaned that face. That is a different sentence and it is the true one, and I do not know why I nearly said the other one.”

“Two of them,” Jonas said. “In one morning. One at the bottom of the dip and one at the top of the street, and they are the two ends of the same water, and I am not going to tell you what I have told a registrar’s office, because you already know it and I have said it out loud in this kitchen once and I am not going to say it twice.”

He put his hand flat on the pillar beside the plate and looked at it, and then he did the thing he had done six times in his life and had never once done in front of anybody who did not already know what it looked like, which was to stop being a man in a kitchen and put himself against the object and let the rest of it go.

Bo was on the bank side of the stand with a spanner in his hand, and Jonas had said the sentence that made the reading legitimate, which was: *I am going to look at this plate and I am going to tell you what I see out loud, and if I see something I do not understand I am going to say I do not understand it, and you are the witness, and you can stop me at any point by saying my name.*

“You have said my name once in your life and it was to tell me off,” said Bo.

“Then it will be familiar.”

The Margin came up out of the plate slowly, the way it does when the hinge is old and there is a great deal of silt in the ceramic, and it was not a picture and it was not a score. It was a relationship, made visible, of the kind he had learned to see on the fourth day of his life: two lines coming out of the same field and not knowing which one came first, and neither of them wrong, and the plate in between refusing to choose.

“Right,” he said. “I can see two. There is the claim on this face — a person on a rota, drawing, and closing it when the trap packs. And there is another one coming in from the side, and it is not a person, it is a *premises* claim, and it comes from a plate that is not on this pillar and is not on this street, and it is the kind of claim that comes from a stopcock and a mains and a building that somebody owns, and it says: water may be drawn at this point by the occupier.”

“That is the county main,” said Margo.

“That is a mains claim. And the two of them have no order between them. That is the fault class. It is a seam, and it is the same fault as the clinic door, and the same as the bridge, and I have not got a tool in this world that can make one promise come before the other one.”

He came off the plate and had to put his right hand on the pillar to steady himself, because the bleed came up out of it the way it always did, with a metallic taste and a pressure behind the eyes, and the mark on the inside of his arm was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the temperature of the morning.

“What happens now,” said Bo, “in the actual world, on this street, when two promises do not know which one is first.”

“A stopcock opens,” said Margo, “and the plate asks the holder, and there is no holder, and so nothing moves, and a man from the county stands at the end of a hose waiting to fill a cistern for a compound he is mixing two streets up, and he waits, and he goes and finds a man with a wrench, and the man with a wrench puts his thumb on the wheel and the water comes out, and the plate has never been told that it happened, and neither has the rota, and on the board it says forty and there is a cross on it and the water came out anyway.”

“I have not seen that happen,” said Aurrie, from the path.

“You have not seen it happen,” said Margo, “because it has not happened. Yet.”

And that was when Jonas said the thing that started the whole rest of the day, and he said it badly, because he was tired and because he had said three good sentences in a kitchen six months ago and it had cost a cardiac slot.

“I can mend one field,” he said. “One. At this point. Not the holder, not the claim, not the term, not the water, none of it. The precedence. I can put in one line that says which of these two promises answers first when a man from the county puts a hose on a stopcock that is not his, and I have got the words, and they are: *the holder on the rota decides. A claim from outside the rota does not override the holder and does not cancel the rota.* And I would like a witness who can say what it permits, when it ends, and what claim follows, and I would like a holder who has read it, and I would like to say in front of all of you what I cannot do, which is I cannot make the water come back, and I cannot make the trap clear itself, and I cannot make a person who does not want to be on a rota be on a rota.”

“Which field,” said Margo, “exactly.”

“Precedence. One field. At this plate. And then it stops, and whatever is still broken is still broken, and I will write that down.”

“And the claim,” said Margo. “There is always a claim.”

“The claim is on the plate already and it is illegible. The one I would be putting there is the same one I have got at a clinic: the issuer keeps it true until a named successor accepts that, or it ends. And I would be the issuer, because the instrument comes out of me, and it runs for fourteen days, or until the barrier work is finished, whichever is sooner, and there is no carrying forward, and it can only be renewed by the holder on the rota reading it out loud to the next holder on the rota. Not by me. Not by the department. Not by the county.”

“Not by you,” Margo repeated.

“Not by me,” Jonas said, “and I want to say the next part, because I know what it sounds like coming from a man with a plate and a seam in his arm. Fourteen days is not long. Fourteen days is the barrier work and one more Wednesday clinic and the fortnight after. I know that is short. I am telling you it is short on purpose, because the alternative is that I put my name on a piece of civic paper for two years on a street where the last one of those got scraped off with a steel edge, and I have no standing here and neither have you, and the shortest term I can write is the shortest one I can be trusted to have written.”

“You can be trusted to have written a short one,” said Aurrie, and then, immediately, as if she had heard herself: “and that is not consent, that is a statement about you, and we will do this on the same basis as everything else, which is slowly.”

His handset went at eleven o'clock, in the rain, at the top of the street, and it was Tomas Renn from the shop, which meant it was not social.

“Say what you are about to do.”

Jonas said it. He said the plate, the field, the holder, the witness, the fourteen days, and the issuer, standing in the rain with Bo four metres away pretending to be interested in a spanner.

Tomas Renn listened to all of it.

“That is a stop,” he said.

“All right.”

“No, I have not finished. That is a stop and I am stopping it and I am not doing it because I think the field is the wrong one or the words are the wrong words, and I have not got an opinion about either, and if I had one I would not be on this telephone, I would be on the train.” The line went quiet for a second, the way a line does when a man with a telephone in each hand is deciding something. “I am stopping it because you have a bond on the yellow board in my shop with seven clauses on it, and clause one says *one local term at one named hinge*, and the named hinge is the west clinic care entrance, and you have just described a second one to me on a telephone at eleven o’clock on a Monday, and the issuer in both sentences was you, and the witness in both sentences was going to be somebody you chose on the train.”

“It was going to be Margo Doré and Ottie Beck.”

“It was going to be somebody you chose on the train,” said Tomas. “I am not doubting Margo Doré. I have never met Margo Doré. I am telling you what the instrument says and I am telling you that you have just used a bond to authorise work it does not cover, and if I let that stand then in four months there will be a plate on a bridge in Gantry Ward and you will have told a woman on a telephone at eleven o’clock that you were doing it, and the whole of this shop will be made a decoration.”

“That is not fair.”

“It is completely fair, and it is also the reason you are useful, and I am not going to be the man who lets you stop being useful because I got in a mood.” A pause. “Now. Here is what I will do. I am not extending your bond. Your bond is named to one hinge and it is going to stay named to one hinge, and if you want a second instrument you go and get one, and I will not countersign it, because I have countersigned one and I am still learning what I signed. What I will do is this. Bring Bea. Not tomorrow. Today. She is in the van and she is nineteen years a line technician and she is the only person I know who can read a term and say back what it permits and what it costs, and she is not a witness you chose on a train, she is the person I would send. And when she is there, you do not put your name in the issuer line. Somebody who is not you issues that term. And if you cannot find a person on that street who will put their name to a civic paper, then there is no term, and the stand stays as it is, and you go home with your arm, and that will be the correct outcome and not a failure.”

Jonas stood in the rain on a street with no address holding a telephone and could not think of one single thing to say.

“Tomas —”

“Say it to Bea, not to me. She will be there at two. And Jonas —” Tomas's voice changed, and it was not softer, it was the voice of a man about to say the thing he had been carrying. “Do you remember what Bea said to you on Thursday, in a yard, about adding a name to a rota to solve a Tuesday?”

“She said a rota stops meaning *these people, today, and they can put it down* and starts meaning *the person who must not leave*.”

“She said it in a yard and she was talking about a wrench and a boy of seventeen, and she was talking about a hinge, and she has been talking about a hinge for three days and neither of you has heard her.” He hung up.

Bea Osunde came at two o'clock in a department van with a bad exhaust and a folding rule in her pocket, and she would not let anybody carry anything for her, and the first thing she said on the Weir was to nobody in particular: “Right. Who is the issuer.”

“Not decided,” said Jonas.

“Then nothing happens,” said Bea, “and I have driven ninety minutes for nothing, and I have done that before, and I would like to say the rest of it now so that it is on the table before you all decide I am a villain.” She took the folding rule out and did not open it. “I stopped this once today on a telephone and I am going to say why, and then I am going to look at whatever it is you have written, and if I do not like it I am going to stop it again, in front of all of you, and my name goes on the minute of it if you want a minute.”

“Go on,” said Aurrie.

“He has written a name in the holder line.” Bea looked at Jonas and did not soften it, and Jonas, who had not in fact written a name in the holder line, said nothing at all, which was the most honest thing available to him. “He wrote it on a train, or he was going to. That is the whole of it. There is a rota on that fence with forty slots on it and thirty-one crosses and two names on it that have been crossed for a fortnight, and the man standing here is about to write one of them into a civic plate in his own handwriting, and the moment that name is in a plate it is not a slot on a board any more. It is a *holder*, and a holder is somebody who can be asked, and being asked is not the same as being the person who said yes, and there are nineteen people on this street who will not be able to tell the difference ever again, and in four years that name will be on that plate and the man will be forty-one and have a bad shoulder and there will be no way out of it that does not require somebody to be cruel to him.”

“That is Thursday’s thing,” said Bo.

“That is Thursday’s thing and I have been carrying it up here in a van since eleven o’clock,” said Bea, “and the reason I am here is that on Thursday I said it to a man about a pipe and he nodded, and nodding is what people do when a thing does not apply to them, and this morning it applied to him exactly.”

“All right,” said Margo Doré, and put her clipboard on the post of the stand, and did not sound like a woman conceding anything. “Then tell me what you want, because you have come ninety minutes and I have to clear the trap tomorrow afternoon if the crew is coming on Tuesday, and the two of you are standing in the rain solving my board.”

“I want the holder to be the board,” said Bea.

“The board is not a person.”

“The board is a practice that a person has kept for thirty years,” Bea said, “and I have been nineteen years in a department where the difference matters, and I am telling you as a mechanic that the difference between *that man holds it* and *whoever is on the rota holds it* is the difference between a fault you can find and a fault you cannot, because one of them can be handed over at eight o’clock in the morning in a kitchen by a man with a bad shoulder who has decided he is tired, and the other one cannot, because there is nobody to be tired, there is a name in a slot.”

Nobody spoke for a moment. Out at the fence, on the green board, thirty-one slots had a small cross on them and nine had not, and one of the nine was Wednesday.

“If the holder is the board,” said Margo, slowly, “then the holder is whoever is in the slot, and it changes eleven times a day, and it changes when somebody comes, and the plate has to be told who is in the slot, and the plate is bolted to a pillar two feet from the board and the board is on the fence and the fence is eight feet away, and I will not paint numbers on that pillar because the last time anybody painted a number on anything on this street it was to do with a survey and I am not doing that.”

“You do not paint anything,” said Bea. “You say it out loud.”

“Say what out loud.”

“The holder reads the term. At the top of the rota, at the top of the hour, the person in the first slot reads the five lines off the board to the room, and the room says them back if it wants to, and then they draw, and when they are finished they cross the slot. And if the slot is empty, the stand is shut, and the board says the stand is shut, and the board is on the fence where a person waiting for water can see it before they fill a bucket.” She put the folding rule away. “That is not a magic ceremony and it is not a civic term, it is a rota, and you have been doing it for thirty years, and what has changed since Thursday is that now it is in the plate as well as on the fence, and the plate and the fence must agree, and if the plate and the fence disagree then neither of them is worth anything.”

Margo Doré took the cap off her pen, and put it back on, and took it off again.

“It is not in the plate as well as on the fence,” she said. “It is in one place, and the plate is the one place, and the fence is a piece of wood with forty slots on it that I inherited, and if the plate is going to be the truth then I am going to have to write the slots on the fence in a way that a person can see from the end of the queue, and that is a piece of work, and it is work I will do, and nobody is going to help me with it because nobody on this street has ever helped me with the board and I am not expecting it now.”

“I will hold the board,” said Bo.

“You will hold the board and you will not be on the tool rota and that is still a different thing from the stand.”

“I know the difference,” said Bo. “I have known the difference since I was eleven.”

“No you have not,” said Margo, “but you will.”

The mending took forty minutes and most of it was talking.

Jonas set out the limit before he touched anything, because the West Ward terms he had made, and Selah had made him say the six words out loud in a kitchen, and because he had learned at a plate at five in the morning that the words were the part that mattered. *One field. One point. This plate. The holder decides which promise answers. The holder may refuse any draw and may close the stand at any time without giving a reason, and a refusal ends that draw and not the rota. The claim is that the holder keeps it true, and the claim is discharged when the silt trap is clear. It runs fourteen days or until the barrier work is finished, whichever comes first, and there is no carrying forward, and only the holder on the rota can renew it, by reading it aloud to the next holder on the rota.*

“The holder on the rota may refuse,” Bea said. “In writing, on the board, where I can see it from ninety miles away in a department that is going to be extremely interested in this street by the end of the month.”

“In writing on the board.”

“Where does the issuer go?”

Margo Doré took the pen and looked at the board on the fence for a while, and Jonas watched a woman of sixty-three decide to put her own name in a box on a street that had spent thirty-two years making sure nobody would.

“Not me,” she said at last. “Margo Doré, keeper of the rota. Not a name that can be found. A role with a person in it today, because if I put my name in a plate it is a name in a plate, and Bea is right, and I am sixty-three and I would rather be found by a rota than by a record.”

“Can it say that?” said Jonas.

“It can say it if a term is a thing that can be said out loud,” said Aurrie, “and if it cannot then half of this city is not a term, it is a list.”

He had put the role in first, on his own, without asking her, and the plate had refused it in the way plates refuse things, which is by printing the line back with the field where the name should have been simply empty and no explanation at all.

“That is the first thing I have learned about plates in my life,” said Margo Doré, looking at it. “I have wanted a thing that says *the person holding this* for thirty-two years and I have just been told, in a sentence with no feelings in it, that the city does not have a way of writing that down. Put my name on it. I am sixty-three and I am not going to be a category, and if the only price of being a category is that I never get named, then that is the price of this street, not of me.”

So the line in the issuer field, when the plate had taken it, said: **ISSUER: THE HOLDER ON THE WEIR STAND ROTA, KEEPER M. DORÉ.** And Jonas, who had put four names into issuer fields in eleven days, stood there and watched somebody else's name go into one and felt it arrive in a place in his chest that he did not have a name for either.

He anchored on the plate and put the brass probe on the ceramic and it took, slowly, and not because he was doing it well, but because the field was old and the terms on either side of it were patient and had been waiting a long time for anybody to say which of them came first.

The mark on the inside of his arm opened, and went past the elbow, and came back about two inches into the upper arm, which was new, and he was aware for about four seconds of the sound the rain was making on the green pillar and of nothing else at all.

Then the plate printed, on a narrow strip, in the flat register:

> **PRECEDENCE MENDED. THIS POINT ANSWERS TO THE HOLDER ON THE ROTA.**
> **A CLAIM FROM OUTSIDE THE ROTA DOES NOT OVERRIDE THE HOLDER AND DOES NOT CANCEL THE ROTA.**
> **AN EMERGENCY DRAW FOR CLINICAL REASON IS PERFORMED AND ENTERED ON THE ROTA.**
> **THE HOLDER MAY REFUSE ANY DRAW. A REFUSAL ENDS THE DRAW AND NOT THE ROTA.**
> **CLAIM: THE HOLDER KEEPS THIS POINT TRUE UNTIL THE SILT TRAP IS CLEAR. UNPAID.**
> **TERM: 14 DAYS FROM THIS READING, OR THE COMPLETION OF THE BARRIER WORK, WHICHEVER IS SOONER. NO CARRYING FORWARD. RENEWED ONLY BY THE HOLDER ON THE ROTA, READING IT ALOUD TO THE NEXT HOLDER ON THE ROTA.**
> **ISSUER UNCHANGED. THE ISSUER OF THE PREVIOUS TERM IS NOT A HOLDER OF THIS ONE.**

Ottie Beck read it over his shoulder and said the last line out loud, and then made him say it again, and then wrote it on the back of her own hand in biro because she was the sort of woman who did.

“Unpaid,” she said. “You have left her a claim.”

“She was carrying it already,” Jonas said. “She has been carrying it for thirty years. All I have done is put it on a plate so that it is on the record, which is the only thing anybody can do about it.”

“That is true and it is also the laziest sentence anybody has said on this street this week,” said Ottie, “but you said it to a woman who is sixty-three and who has carried it, so I will let it stand.”

The refusal came at four o'clock, and it came from a man with a terminal rotation, and it was not dramatic.

Wesley Kpomah came off the early and read the five lines off the board at the top of the hour, as the plate required, and then said, “No. I am not doing Wednesday. I have got a rotation in the second week of the month and I am not telling anybody why, and I am not going to pretend to a plate that I am doing a duty I have already told two people I am not doing.”

“That is a refusal of a whole day,” said Margo.

“That is a refusal of a whole day.”

“And under this thing you do not have to give a reason.”

“That is what I have understood for the last ten minutes,” said Wesley, “and I would like it noted that I have not needed it.”

Margo Doré crossed the Wednesday slot out on the board with a stub of the same pencil she had used for thirty years, in one stroke, and did not write anything beside it, and did not say anything, and the board said forty and it had thirty-two crosses on it and the stand ran for twelve minutes that day instead of twenty-two, and the ten minutes were not made up, and went off the board in the sense that the space where they should have been was simply not there.

And Jonas Mercer, who had come to a street with a coupling in a bag on the Thursday and had ended the Monday with a civic term in a plate bearing somebody else’s name, and who had spent eleven days in a city learning that a man who makes a term becomes responsible for it, stood at the top of the Weir at half past four in the rain with a bronze probe in his left hand and his right hand entirely numb to the elbow, and found that he could not work out whether he had got something off himself or put something on somebody else, and that the only honest thing he had to do about that feeling was to keep it and go home.
