# Chapter 0021 — Thursday, After Six

The Old Ward board is four feet high and glass-fronted and stands under a canopy between the ferry office and the Assembly's public doors, and at ten past five on a Thursday in the third week of March it had four lines on it in a single day that Jonas had not written.

**WEST CLINIC CARE ENTRANCE — SERVICE LOG.**

**06:00. HOLDER: B. OSEI. READ AND SIGN AT THE DOOR.** There was a tick in the box beside it, small and pressed hard, in the heavy hand of a man who signs his own name four hundred times a week and does not enjoy it.

**11:00. ROUTE CLOSED. NO NAMED HOLDER.**

**12:41. OPENED BY DECLARATION — ON-CALL CLINICIAN. GAP HOURS. RETURNED 13:02.** The hand under that one was small and upright and pressed hard enough to score the enamel, and Jonas stood and looked at it for longer than he meant to, because he had never seen Sanaa Iqbal's signature on a public surface in his life and it turned out that he had wanted to.

**13:30. HOLDER: E. MBEKI. READ AND SIGN AT THE DOOR.** Another tick, a different hand, younger and slightly too fast.

And along the bottom, printed and unchanged and printed every day for six days by something that did not have an opinion about any of them:

**TERM TO EXPIRE 06:00, WEDNESDAY. RENEWAL AT HANDOVER ONLY.**

He read it from the wrong side of the glass, standing in the way of two people going in to the ferry office, and then stepped back and read it again from the correct side, and it did not say his name anywhere. Not in the issuer line, not in the maintenance line, not in the claim line that he had put there himself in the flat register on a strip of paper and mounted under the plastic sleeve of a notice he had built in a yard with a planer.

Six days. The term had six days left on it and in five of them the door would be held by a porter, and on the sixth, by nobody at all, and the only thing that could change that was two people reading a page out loud to each other at a handover, and he was not one of them and could not make himself one.

He had spent the day four miles away being told that nothing he did was legally his.

At five o'clock he was in the yard at River Stacks, and Bea Osunde was holding a canvas bag with a zip on it, and inside the bag were a fifty-millimetre compression coupling, a gasket, a roll of PTFE tape, and a length of two-inch galv pipe he had cut himself in the shop on Tuesday and had not told anybody about.

“That is everything in the bag,” she said.

“That is more than everything I said I would bring.”

“You said a coupling and a gasket. I have decided to underwrite the pipe, because I have seen what happens when a man has to go back for a pipe.” She set the bag on the bench. “And you are not taking the roll.”

“I know.”

“You do not, actually, so I am going to say it in a way you cannot misunderstand.” She put one hand flat on the roll. “The roll goes out on a job. A job has a name on it, and the name is on it for a reason, and the reason is so that when you are standing in a corridor at two in the morning with somebody else's mother on a trolley, there is a record of who sent you. You do not have a job out there. You have a favour and a piece of plastic compound you used on Monday night. So the roll stays.”

“All right.”

“And one more thing, because I have been watching you write things down all week.” She turned the bag over. “You have now written four rotas since Tuesday. The gate. My rota. The fourteen-day one you thought I did not find. And now you are going to want a fifth, for a piece of pipe on a street that is not on any of our sheets, because you have looked at it and worked out that the boy who is right about the pipe is not allowed to hold the wrench.”

Jonas did not answer, which was the answer.

“If that is what happens,” Bea said, “then a rota stops meaning *these people, today, and they can put it down* and starts meaning *the person who must not leave*. And you will have written that, and it will be in your handwriting, and in a year somebody will say it was the department’s rule, because the department is where you work.” She picked the bag up and pushed it across the bench to him. “Do not add a name to a rota to solve a Thursday. If a street needs a rota it has to ask for one and then be told no, or be told yes, by the people who are on it.”

“That is not the same as a hire form.”

“No,” said Bea. “It is the same as a hire form, and it is the same as a holder field, and one day you will see that all three of those are the same piece of paper with a different consequence, and by then somebody will have written a name in it who cannot get out.”

He took the bag. The coupling was cold and heavier than it looked.

Pia was at the bottom of the towpath with three bags on the handlebars and no gloves, in a coat that had been somebody else’s, and she looked at the bag and did not comment on it, which was the most generous thing she had done for him.

“Six twenty,” she said. “You are late and I have not waited, I have simply been here.”

“I know what is in it.”

“Good. That means you have not brought a promise.” She got on the bicycle and then got off it again. “One thing before we go up. The county form came. Margo went to the post office on Tuesday and it was in the box, it has been in the box since the middle of the month, it is a form for the *loan of a pump and four tonnes of stone, one tide window, lowland works*, and it has come to a street with no address on it because the form is addressed to *the occupier* and there is one occupier, and the occupier is a woman of sixty-one who is not a parish and cannot be an applicant.”

“Does she want the pump?”

“She has wanted the pump since 1994,” Pia said, “and the pump on that form costs a box to be filled in, and I am not going to tell you which box, because if I tell you which box you will start being useful at it.”

“I would not—”

“You would. You are extremely good at it. That is the whole trouble with you.” She pushed off. “Come on. Bo has been standing in that kitchen since four o’clock pretending not to care.”

The standing pipe came up out of the ground about two metres above the loft, on the wall side, where a length of it ran through a coupling into the branch that fed the stand. It was iron, fifty millimetres, and painted at some point in a decade nobody on the street could remember with a colour that had gone the particular pinkish white that old black paint goes.

Bo had the joint already marked. He had a fine marker and a spirit level and a scrap of paper with a calculation on it that Jonas did not understand and did not ask about, and when Jonas got the coupling out of the bag and put it on the ledge, Bo looked at it for a second and then went and got a second one out of a tin.

“Where did you get that.”

“Trade counter on Marl Street, four pounds ten,” said Jonas.

“Trade counter on Marl Street is six pound eighty and it is a compression fitting with a liner and it will go soft in a year in that frost.” Bo held them both up to the light. “Yours is right. I was hoping you would bring the wrong one so we would have to argue about it and I would get to be right about the pipe without anyone writing it down.”

“You were right about the pipe last Monday. You were right about the four hundred millimetres and you were right about the length above the coupling. Nobody has written that down either.”

Bo put both fittings down and picked up the marker again. “Do the cut-back. I will hold the vice.”

Jonas cut it with his left hand and a pipe cutter he had to reset twice, because the cutter has a screw in it that has to be turned with the heel of the hand and the heel of his right hand could not feel whether it was biting. He could have asked for a hand. He did not, and by the fourth cut he had worked out a way to do it with the left hand braced on the pipe so that the tool did not need to be felt, only watched, and the numbness in the right hand was a dull column of weather running from his wrist to below the elbow that he had stopped thinking about in the way you stop thinking about a cold nose.

Margo held the vice and did not speak for nineteen minutes, which was the longest anybody on the Weir had ever gone.

“Four hundred,” Bo said. “Not three-eighty. Four hundred and ten, because the wrench has to clear the bracket.”

“That is your number from Monday.”

“That is my number from Monday,” said Bo, “and there is a difference between a number I said and a number that is on paper, and I would like it on paper before I am not allowed to hold the wrench.”

Wesley Kpomah came in off a terminal shift at ten past seven with the particular grey of a man who has been inside a container yard for eleven hours. He was forty-four and he was one of three names on the tool rota, and he was the only one of the three who was on the rota and not on something else at the same time, and when Bo handed him the spanner he took it without being asked and stood with his back to the wall and tightened the joint in three pulls, because the wrench belongs to the loft and the loft’s tool is the rota’s, and that is not a rule anybody had to enforce; it had simply never been in doubt in twenty years.

“Hold it while it takes up,” Bo said.

“I am holding it.”

“You are leaning on it.”

“I am leaning on it and you are holding it. That is what a rota is.”

It took up, and then it stopped taking up, and the joint did not weep at the gland. Bo opened the stop valve and let it bleed and closed it and put his hand on the pipe and waited the way a man waits who has been wrong before.

They left it twenty minutes. Margo ran a cold tap in the kitchen and a hot one in the room at the back and, at the end of it, put her hand flat on the pipe and looked at Jonas.

“No water on the coupling,” she said.

“Bo said it would hold for two years if the length above it were right.”

“The length above it was already right,” said Bo. “That is what I said on Monday. It was not right. Somebody put a hundred and forty millimetres of extra in there in the spring when the branch was moved, and every time it leaked, somebody blamed the coupling, and the coupling went in again, and the hundred and forty stayed.”

He said it looking at the wall.

Aurrie came out of the side room with a receipt book in her hand and tore a page off and put it on the ledge above the pipe.

“Coupling fitted to specification of B. Trimble, four hundred and ten millimetres cut back, holds, thirteenth,” she said. “Signed and dated by me because the person who did the work is not on the tool rota, and if I do not sign it then in three months somebody will do it wrong and say the specification was not written down. That is the whole of my paperwork for this year and I am not complaining.”

Then she folded her arms and looked at him, and the look was the one from Wednesday.

“Now. About the rota.”

“No.”

“You have not said anything.”

“You have got your arms folded and you have said no to me before I opened my mouth, twice now, and I am trying to save you the trouble.” He put the bag down on the ledge. “Bo has fitted the most important joint on this street. He did it to a written specification and it held. He is not on the tool rota because he is seventeen, and everything he is allowed to do he has to do with somebody else’s hands on the wrench, and that is a way of saying *he is not allowed to be responsible for anything*. If I write a letter and say that the tool rota ought to include him, will you sign it?”

“No,” said Aurrie.

“Will you tell me why, properly, once?”

“Once. It is the only protection he has and I am not spending it on a recommendation from a man with a bag.” She did not soften it, and she was not being cruel, and both of those things were true at the same time. “When he is eighteen he can put his own name on a rota and refuse it. That is the entire point of a rota. If I put him on it now I have made a rota out of a boy, and in four years he will be twenty-one and unable to say no to it, because saying no to a thing you were put on as a child is a different sentence from saying no to a thing you chose. He has chosen the water rota. He has been on it since he was eleven. I did not put him there. He put himself there because he wanted the job and he wanted the money for it.”

“I am not trying to take his choice away.”

“You are trying to make a signature out of it,” Aurrie said, “and one day somebody will use that signature to tell him he agreed to it.”

Wesley Kpomah, who had been standing at the wall with the spanner still in his hand and had listened to the whole of it, said one sentence and then went to wash his hands.

“The next name on that rota is not going to be anybody who can say no,” he said. “You have three names. Two of us are on a shift pattern and one of us is on a six-week leave that the hospital keeps extending. If I go on a terminal rotation in February you are going to be standing on this wall asking a seventy-year-old man to hold a wrench over a bank at eleven at night.” He handed the spanner to Bo. “Put that back in the shed for me.”

Bo put it back in the shed for him.

It was nearly nine by the time Margo put the county form on the kitchen table, face up, with a mug on it to hold it against the damp.

It was two sides of cheap pale green card, the kind that comes in a hundred from a county office that has had them printed since the year the country decided to look modern, and it had been through a post box and a sorting office and a flooded hall and it had a coffee ring on it.

**BOROUGH OF SALTMOUTH — COUNTY HIGHWAYS AND DRAINAGE.**
**LOAN OF PUMP AND STONE, LOWLAND WORKS (TEMPORARY).**
**Item 1: pump, 4-inch, diesel, with operator, for the period of one tide window (four days).**
**Item 2: stone, 4 tonnes, delivered to the nearest made-up road.**
**Item 3: The applicant shall give the location of the works.**
**Item 4: The applicant shall name the person responsible for the plant and for its safe keeping between tides.**
**Item 5: The applicant shall give an address for service of notices.**

“Delivered to the nearest made-up road,” Aurrie said. “Which is the top of this street, and the top of this street is made up, and it is called nothing. The van will come up there and it will turn round, because there is nowhere to turn round, and I have watched a lorry put a hedge through somebody’s front garden on the far side.”

“It is item three and item four,” Jonas said. “The location, and the name of the person responsible for the plant and its safe keeping between tides.”

“I can write the location,” said Margo. “I have been able to write the location for thirty years. It is the fourth box and it is the only one I can fill in, and if I put a name in it, then in nine years the county can turn up at eleven at night and serve a notice on a person about a pump that has a seal on it, and that person will be responsible for a machine on a bank in the dark, and no matter what they do they will be the one who did not come.”

“And if the box stays blank, no pump.”

“If the box stays blank, no pump, and we keep the four tonnes of stone problem, which is that a lorry with four tonnes of stone on the end of it has to put the stone down somewhere, and the somewhere will be a made-up road with no name on a street with no district, and that is how a heap of stone becomes a wall of stone and a wall of stone becomes a reason.” She squared the form with both hands. “So the question is not whether we want the pump. We have wanted the pump since before some of us came here. The question is whose name goes in box four, and that is a question about a person and not about a machine, and no amount of good work on a joint is going to answer it.”

Nobody put anything in box four for a while.

Then Ottie Beck came in, seventy-one years old, in a cardigan and a coat over it, carrying a plastic bag of apples, which she put on the table next to the form because there was nowhere else, and she read the form from the top with her hand on the top of it, the way she read everything.

“Item four,” she said. “Responsible for the plant and for its safe keeping between tides.” She looked up. “Do you know what that means in words, on a night, on this street? It means somebody has to be *awake*, four times a day, for four days, at a tide, and get out to a chamber at the bottom of that dip in the dark with a lamp, and know whether the water is coming, and know what to do if it is.”

“Can it be asleep?” Hector Mbatha said from the doorway. He was seventy. He had his coat on. “Can a man be asleep and be responsible?”

“No,” said Otie. “That is the whole of what I came to say. I have been a nurse for thirty-one years in this lowland and the only thing I know that is worth knowing is that there are two kinds of person at three in the morning. There is the person who is awake, and there is the person who signed something. And the second one is a worse thing to have on a bank, and I have watched the second one be on a bank, and I have then had to clean up after the second one, and there is not one of us here who does not know which one of those I mean.”

Nobody looked at Margo, and Margo noticed, and Jonas noticed her noticing.

“I am not going to fill it in,” Margo said, to the table. “Not tonight, and not because I am frightened, which I am, a bit, and because somebody is going to have to be responsible for a machine in the dark four times a day for four days, and I already keep a rota with two-thirds of the slots crossed out, and I am not putting my name under a thing at three in the morning that I would be waking up frightened about, and if I did, I would be doing it wrong, and you would all have to live with that.”

“You are the holder of the stand,” Aurrie said.

“I am the keeper of a board with crosses on it,” said Margo. “That is not the same and I will thank you not to write it down as the same.”

They broke up at twenty past ten without a decision, which Jonas had learned by then was what a decision sounded like when the room was honest, and Pia walked him down to the towpath and did not say anything at all until the bicycle was against the railing.

“You will go back up there on Monday,” she said.

“If they will have me.”

“They will have you because you are useful and they have run out of things that are useful, and both of those are true, and I am not going to pretend the second one is a compliment to you.” She pulled her gloves on. “But you have been up there three times and each time something in that kitchen got a little more certain. I have watched it three times. I do not know whether that is you being careful or you being patient, and the difference matters, and I have not decided which it is, and I am telling you that instead of deciding it, because I am not the person who decides things here.”

“I would not want you to.”

“No,” said Pia. “You would not. That is why I keep telling you what I am not doing.”

His handset went at ten to eleven, on the towpath, with the water working under the bank on his left.

Nina Calder, and she was reading.

“I have got something for you and I have been sitting on it for two hours deciding whether to ring you at home.”

“Go on.”

“The barrier is in the chain.” He had not said that out loud to anybody, and he heard himself not saying it now. “The flap at the bottom of that dip. The plate on the chamber wall is a series 6 plate, and the plate is in the chain of the plate you issued, and a fault on a node in a chain of a plate you issued is a departmental asset line the moment anybody writes it down, and somebody wrote it down.” He turned the page. “So there is now an open municipal asset defect on the series 6 chain, priority unassigned, and an estimated cost of repair, and the estimate is four thousand two hundred to six thousand eight hundred, and the field for *asset owner* is blank, and the field for *reporting officer* has your name in it, because you are the issuer, and because you asked Nina Calder in a records room for the lowland chain and got a number you did not have before, and Nina Calder wrote it down when you asked.”

“Who signed the estimate.”

“Bo Trimble did, on a piece of paper, in a kitchen, and you brought it back in a bag.” A pause. “That is not how it works. I have had the paperwork corrected and I have entered it as *estimated by local holder of record, cost unverified, no municipal figure available*, and it is on your file, and your appointment review is on the tenth day, and the reviewer is entitled to read anything on your file including a hole where a number should be.”

“So there is a number on my file now.”

“There is a number on your file now that says a barrier on a street that is not in the index needs four to seven thousand pounds, and the reporting officer is you, and the asset owner is nobody, and I have written *no municipal figure available* under it in capitals.” He heard the flatness again. “And the registrar’s office wants the answer by noon tomorrow, and I am not ringing you at midnight about that, because I have decided that Nina Calder is not going to ring you at midnight about anything ever again, and you may have that as a promise and not a treat.”

The bank beside the towpath made the sound it makes when the tide is under it.

“I cannot fix that number,” he said.

“No,” said Nina. “You cannot. You can turn up on Monday with a wrench, and everybody there already knows that, and it is not the number that is the problem. The problem is that there is a piece of paper with your name on it that says somebody has to pay for a bank, and the payer has not been decided, and you are the nearest responsible person, and the department has started writing that down without being asked to.”

He stood on the towpath with the canvas bag at his feet and the dark water under the embankment and three hundred meters of a street above him where a form sat on a kitchen table with four boxes and one name left to fill in.

“Tomorrow,” he said. “Noon.”

“Tomorrow,” said Nina. “In a room. Bring the bond.”
