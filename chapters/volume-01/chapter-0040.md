# Chapter 0040 — Nine Names, Not Yet

On Sunday morning the shop was empty and the rain had come back and Jonas Mercer took a job card out of the drawer and ruled it himself, in pencil, in the workshop, with the straight edge because his hand was not steady, and drew nine lines across it and wrote the nine things down the left-hand side where the work would go.

He had a list of seven names and two blank lines and a card was not a list, and Bea Osunde had told him in a yard on Wednesday that a card with a name and a day and a time on it was fine and that a card with a subject on it was a list, and there were subjects on this one, and he had ruled it himself the evening before, standing up, in a yard, because it seemed like a way of not writing it down.

He wrote the services first, because he had learned that he could not be trusted with the names.

*1. A crossing. — N. BELL. TUE 14 APRIL, 15:30, BARRIER END. BRING OWN PAPER.*
*2. A door. — M. OKAFOR. CONDITIONS IN HER HAND. WITHDRAWN ON THE NINTH IF THERE IS NO CHEQUE.*
*3. A market. — F. ADEYEMI. NO. Wednesday, in the queue. “You do not need a cook. You need somebody nobody can dismiss.”*
*4. A pump. — E. RUSK. Yes. Asked in the yard of a lock-up on Sunday at ten past nine. Read the five lines back twice and said he would rather be named than be the man who is not there.*
*5. A machine. — T. VLAH. Yes, machines only, and he said the word only out loud before he said yes, and made Jonas write it in the line.*
*6. A school. — R. IDRIS. Yes, and not for anything a child is inside, and she said that before Jonas had finished the sentence.*
*7. A street.*
*8. A platform. — K. SARR. Yes, on the condition that he is a witness to a platform and not to a person, and he did not explain it and Jonas did not ask.*
*9. A hospital.*

Seven lines with a name against them. One of them a refusal, written down in full, because he had decided at about four o’clock on Wednesday afternoon that a roster which showed only consent was a roster that had been cleaned.

Lines seven and nine were empty, and he had been in the shop for two hours on Sunday morning and had not been able to write anything on either of them, and it was not because he did not have two names, because he had two names and one of them had told him on a towpath that she was not going to be a spokesperson in about a week, and the other one had told a room on the twenty-ninth of March that she did not want to be the person who decides.

Both of them had already said the answer out loud to somebody else. He had simply not asked them.

That was the decision and it took him four days and cost him two lines, and he could not tell anybody about it, and the reason he could not tell anybody was that the moment he said the sentence *I have decided not to ask her* out loud, a man in a room would ask him why, and the answer was that a woman of nineteen who lives on a street that this city classified as plant four days ago would be the only person on a list of nine that could be produced in a hearing, and that nobody could protect her, and that he had known since the fourteenth of March what happened to a list.

Tomas Renn came in at half past ten on a Sunday because it was a Sunday and because he had been in the yard on Saturday evening and heard about the ramp and had come to look at the handrail, and he stood at the bench with the card in his hand for a long time without saying anything, upside down, the way Bea read things.

“Seven lines,” he said. “You have got seven, and you have written one of them in the wrong direction, and the wrong one is the one you are proudest of.”

“She wrote it. I wrote it down so that it was not cleaned off.”

“That is the correct reason to write it down and it is not the reason you wrote it down.” He put the card back. “Five years I have watched men in this building build a list, and it is always the same and it always starts as the day somebody writes a name on a card because he did not want to forget it. And then it is a column, and then it is a sheet, and then somebody in an office asks who the witnesses are, and the answer is a list, and everybody on it is a person who can now be served with a hearing, and four of them will not know it is happening for two years.” He looked at the eight blank half-lines. “You have got until the flood and you have not got a method, and a method is the only thing you have ever been short of, and I am telling you that as a man who is sixty per cent of the problem because a shop steward who is the only person asking has made it his own instead of the roster’s, and I have still not solved that, and I have been standing in this yard for three weeks waiting for you to notice.”

“I have noticed,” said Jonas.

“Good,” said Tomas Renn, and went out, and did not say one word about the handrail.

He wrote one line in the margin, in pencil, small, and then he put the card in his pocket and it was the ninth time that month he had written something down and put it somewhere that was not a file, and the count of those was eleven.

The two refusals that week were not a drama and the thing that came out of the weekend was not a coalition.

On Wednesday morning Teo Vlah had a lock-up on Rook Street with a roller door half up and a hydraulic jack on a bench and no radio, and he had told Jonas in the first ninety seconds that he would be a witness to a machine and not to a door, and he had made Jonas write *machines only* in the line before he said yes, and when Jonas asked him why, Vlah had said, “Because a door has a person in it and a machine does not, and the day somebody wants me to say a machine is safe at three in the morning I will want to be able to point at the line where I said what I would speak for, and you are the man who taught me that, on the seventeenth of March, when you wrote a rota instead of a name and made me sit in a car for ninety minutes about it.”

Emeka Rusk said yes in a yard at ten past nine on Wednesday with a diesel pump behind him and a strip of old hose over his shoulder, and asked for the five lines, and read them back twice, and then said the sentence that Jonas wrote on the card afterwards: *I would rather be named than be the man who is not there.* He has run pumps on the docks for twenty-six years and has been the only person in three buildings who knew where the stopcock was.

Roz Idris teaches year five at Gantry Fields and made him say the whole of the offer twice before she answered it, and her condition was not about children at all, which he had expected, and was about *not knowing in advance*: she will witness a service she has been told is failing, and she will not witness a service that has been *described* to her as failing by the person who wants it fixed.

“I have taught for twenty years,” she said, “and I know exactly what it looks like when somebody comes into a school with a story about a gate and a piece of paper with a form on it, and about half the time they are right and the other half of the time the form is how you keep a question from being asked.”

Kofi Sarr took his condition in about four seconds and would not elaborate on it, and Jonas did not ask, and it turned out on the fourteenth to be that a witness may be asked to say what a platform *is* and never what a passenger *is*, and that Kofi Sarr has been on the Saint Orra platforms for twenty-one years and has been asked the second question twice by men in good coats.

So: six yes, one no, in writing, in her own words, which is one more than he had on Wednesday night and two fewer than he needs, and the two he had not asked were the only two on the list he could not have got by going and standing in a doorway, and one of them was the only one in the city who could have opened the corridor on a Saturday night, and he had not asked her, and the reason he had not asked her was a sentence she had said to him in a room on the twenty-ninth of March about not wanting to be the person who decides, and he had written it down in a margin on a card in his pocket, and it was the third time that month he had used somebody else’s sentence as a reason not to do a thing.

---

The corridor at Saint Orra west on Sunday night at ten o’clock had Ruth Nwosu on her third night back, and Ezra Mbeki, and Mina Okafor at the card table, and four people because Selah Mora had sent a card with a *return-of-care* handoff written out on it in her own hand and no telephone number on it, which was her way of refusing in writing and working at the same time.

Ruth read the five lines. Ezra read them back. Ruth signed the box and said, “The eighth,” meaning six in the morning on the eighth, meaning the expiry, and then she said, out loud, in the corridor, to Jonas, “I am not doing the fifteenth. I have told Mina and Mina will write it down and I am telling you so that it is on your side of the table as well, because I have decided I would rather be the woman who said the date out loud than the woman who said nothing and then did it.”

Jonas read the five lines again afterwards in the middle of the corridor, and nobody made him, and Ruth did not tell him to, and when he had finished she said, “You are not on this rota and you have not been on this rota since the seventeenth of March and you read them again tonight because you are frightened of Wednesday, and I am not going to say anything about it.”

He went up the towpath at half past nine on Sunday night with a bicycle lamp he had taken off his own bike, and Pia Santos was at the bottom of it, and she came up with him to the stand, and he was asked to wait at the bottom of the slipway, and he waited at the bottom of the slipway.

From there he could see the green pillar and the bucket on the chain and the board, and he could hear a voice.

Delphine Ofori, who is seventy-eight, was reading the five lines of a term she could not see, off the fence post, out loud, at twenty to ten on a Sunday night, to a boy of seventeen. He could hear the sound of a plate being cleaned with a rag, and then a gap, and then Delphine Ofori reading the renewal clause, which is the only part that does any work and which says: **RENEWED ONLY BY THE HOLDER ON THE WEIR STAND ROTA, READING IT ALOUD TO THE NEXT HOLDER ON THE WEIR STAND ROTA.**

She read it. And then she stopped, and Jonas heard Margo Doré say something about the slot after Bo, and then nothing for about eleven seconds, and then the sound of a board and a pencil, and then Margo Doré’s voice saying a number and a time, and the sound of a cross being put in a box.

Somebody refused a slot. Not Bo, who has not refused a slot in his life. Somebody further down, and the refusal was written on the board, and the ten minutes were not made up, and it has been the same on the Weir since the fifteenth of March and it is the price of the instrument and it is the only price anybody has ever been asked to pay for it.

Then Delphine Oferi handed the plate and Bo Trimble put his hand on it, and Jonas felt it happen about ninety metres up a path in the dark without being anywhere near it: a seam in his arm that had nothing to do with the one in his wrist, an ordinary civic sensation, the specific feeling of a promise being made by somebody else to somebody else that he had once had to write down and could not renew.

Bo Trimble, who is seventeen and has been on that water rota since he was eleven, has a name in the holder chain of a lowland water point on a street with no address. That is what it cost. He knew what it was. He was told what it was, at the fence, out loud, in front of a woman of seventy-eight, and he took it anyway, and he will be findable now, and findable is the thing that thirty-two years of refusal on that street has been about, and he is seventeen and did it on a Sunday because there was nobody else.

The term runs fourteen days from that reading, because the barrier work is finished and the sooner trigger has already fired once. It runs to the nineteenth of April.

Pia came back down the path at twenty past ten and stood next to him on the slipway in the rain.

“Bo took it,” she said. “Delphine read it to him and he said no to being told what it was and then he took it, which is the whole of him, and the slot after his was refused in writing by a man of forty-four who works nights at a container yard, and Margo wrote the time on the board and nobody made it up. It runs to the nineteenth. And there is a flood forecast for the seventeenth.”

“Three days before it runs out.”

“Two days before it runs out.” She was holding her bicycle by the saddle. “And the trap is packed and it has been packed for two weeks and the boil is back on the inner face, and I am not telling you the water is brown because you know that, and I am telling you because you are going to write a card on Monday morning and put a name on the ninth line of it and go to a room, and I want you to have heard a number before you do that.

“The tarp came off on Wednesday and it is lying over the intake grille, and the water in the bucket is the colour of weak tea, and it will be that on Monday and Tuesday and Wednesday unless somebody clears that trap, and we cannot pay anybody, and if we pay somebody out of the tin then there is nothing in the tin for the stone, and the stone is the bank.”

“How much to clear a trap.”

“Two of us, one morning, and the low is at five past five.” She got on the bicycle. “That is what it costs. Two people, on a Wednesday, at five past five in the morning, unpaid, and then it is clear for about six weeks. And that is not a civic term and nobody can put a witness to it and it is going to happen or it is not going to happen.”

She got off the bike again.

“The ninth is in four days. Aurrie has read the whole of what the man from Saint Orra asked for, and the loft has decided that the paper goes up as it is, unedited, no additions, and Aurrie is going to read it, and she has said she is not going to stand up to read it, and when I asked her why she said that a woman of sixty-one standing up in a hospital boardroom is a petition, and a petition gets filed.” Pia looked up at him. “She is right and I have been thinking about it since Friday and it is the most frightening thing anybody has said to me this month, and here is why, and then I am going to go home.

“For eleven days you have been learning that the only power that street has is that there is nothing on any list. And on Thursday, in a room, one of the most powerful things any of us could possibly do is stand up and be a person with a name and an address. Both of those are true. That is what I am not going to be able to hold for very long, and I am nineteen and I am not a lawyer, and I have got a cold, and I am going to bed.”

Jonas Mercer stood on the slipway in the rain at half past ten on Sunday night and then walked up the path, because there was nothing on the path but fifty-nine doors and a bucket, and at the stand he put the bucket under the tap and turned it, and the water came out the colour of weak tea, and the plate on the pillar asked the rota a question for the first time in fifteen days, and the only answer it got was a board with forty slots on it and a pencil in a strap round Margo Doré’s neck and a woman of sixty-three standing in the dark reading the board, and a trap packed on the second, and four tonnes of stone still owed to a bank that the city has now certified is plant.
