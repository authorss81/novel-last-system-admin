# Chapter 0024 — What the Count Is For

Sunday on the Weir was a day when nobody expected anything of anybody, and Jonas came down the towpath at ten past ten in the rain without a bag, and was let in through the side door by a girl of about five who had decided that opening the door was now her job, and who was therefore the most senior person in the building.

Aurrie was in the big room with nine children and a rug and a kettle. Margo was on the telephone in the kitchen to somebody at the church hall about a boiler. Ottie was in the clinic room with the door shut and a queue of four, because it was a Wednesday clinic but Ottie had moved it to Sunday, once, for no reason she gave, which Jonas decided not to ask about. Hector Mbatha was sitting at the kitchen table with a tea towel over his shoulder and a hard-backed book on the table in front of him that he had clearly been holding for some time and had clearly been waiting for an audience, and Jonas understood within about four seconds that he was going to be handed something.

“You cannot have it,” Hector said.

“I have not asked for anything.”

“You have a face like the man from the van in a coat, and that man asked me for a cigarette outside a hardware shop in 2019.” Hector put his hand flat on the hard-backed book. “This is not a favour. This is a thing I have carried for thirty-two years and I have never once had a reason to put on a table, and I have got one, and you are the reason, so you are getting it before anybody who will make a better job of it.”

The book was a pocket diary with a rubber band round it, and the rubber band had perished and been replaced with a length of green twine, and when Hector took it off he did not open it, he turned it round and round on the table so that the cover faced Jonas, so that the decision to open it stayed his.

“April 1994,” Hector said. “Aurrie had been saying for a year that we should find out what we were, because you cannot ask for a service for a thing that does not have a size. And I went out at six in the morning with a pencil and a piece of paper and I counted the doors on this street. Not the flats. The doors. Blue doors, brown doors, the ones that are a gate, the ones that are a hole with a curtain in it, and the two boat sheds that are not homes but that somebody lives behind, because I counted what I walked past and I did not stop to ask anybody, because if I stop to ask then it is a survey and a survey has a form.”

“How many.”

“Sixty-one.” He said it without any weight at all, which was how you said a number you had said to yourself several thousand times. “Sixty-one doors in April 1994. And I have counted them every year since, because the first count was a count and the second count was a habit, and now it is thirty-two years and I do the whole street in eleven minutes and I have a table.” He tapped the book. “Sixty-one. Fifty-eight. Fifty-five. Fifty-nine. Fifty-five. Fifty-two. Fifty-two. Fifty-two. Fifty-two. Fifty-two. Fifty-one. Fifty-one. Fifty. Forty-nine. Forty-nine. Forty-seven. Forty-seven. Forty-seven. Forty-seven. Forty-nine. Forty-nine. Fifty-one. Fifty-three. Fifty-five. Fifty-seven. Fifty-nine. Fifty-nine. Fifty-nine. Fifty-nine. Fifty-nine. Fifty-nine. Fifty-nine. Fifty-nine. Fifty-nine.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

“You have counted this street every year for thirty-two years,” Margo said, from the doorway with the telephone still in her hand.

“Every year in April,” said Hector. “Because that is when Aurrie started, and because a number is only a number if somebody says it twice.”

“I did not know you were doing it.”

“That is not the point of it, Margo. The point of it is that I have never once been asked to stop, and I have never once been asked what it was for, and if I had been asked what it was for I would not have been able to tell you, and that is the whole trouble with me and I am aware of it at eight in the morning every April.”

Aurrie came in and stood behind him with a cloth in her hand, and did not sit down, which she did perhaps twice a year.

“Hector,” she said.

“Aurrie.”

“You have known for thirty-two years that we do not have a district, and you have been out here counting our doors, and I have been standing in this kitchen saying *we should ask*, and it turns out that I have been saying it to a man who was already answering a different question and never told me.” Her voice did not go up, which on her was worse. “Do you know how long I have wanted a number. I have wanted it since 1994. It is the oldest thing I have wanted and I have never once been embarrassed about it. And you have had one in your pocket for the same thirty-two years, and you have never mentioned it, and I have spent every one of those years being the person who wants things, in front of everybody, including people who did not want to hear it.”

“You would have used it.”

“Yes,” said Aurrie. “Of course I would have used it. That is why I am angry and it is not your fault and it is completely your fault.”

“Where would you have used it,” said Margo, and she had come fully into the kitchen and had not put the telephone down, “and on what, and to whom.”

Aurrie stopped.

“That,” she said, after a moment, “is the question, and it is the question I have been not asking myself for thirty-two years, and I would like it noticed that it has taken a man with a head wound from a municipal plate at the bottom of a dip to make me notice it.”

She put the cloth down.

“All right. The number is on the table. Fifty-nine doors in April of last year, and every one of them has a person in it, and the number is a number of doors and not a number of people, and I want that said first, out loud, in this kitchen, before anybody writes it anywhere. Hector. Say it.”

“Say what.”

“Say that it is doors and not people. Because I have watched what a number does to a person who is asked to produce one, and I am not going to be that person, and I have not got the moral authority to stop it, because I am the one who has wanted it longest.”

Hector looked at the book on the table for a long moment.

“Fifty-nine last April,” he said. “It is fifty-nine and it is doors and it is not people and one of them is a boat shed and one of them is a door that has been bricked up since the flood year and should not be counted and I count it every year because leaving it out is a decision and I am not making decisions.”

The door opened and Pia Santos came in out of the rain with her bag over her shoulder, and she took one look at the table and stopped inside the door.

“No,” she said.

Nobody had asked her anything yet.

“No,” she said again, and put her bag down against the wall, “whatever this is. Whatever it is about. I have been up this street since I was six and I have never once been in a room where a number of houses was on a table, and I am telling you now that I am not sitting down, so if the meeting needs me I will be standing up for it.”

“Your mother’s door is number thirty-one,” said Margo, which was a thing she had evidently said out loud on a rota board two hundred times and had never once said to a room.

“Then somebody else can sit in her chair,” said Pia.

Hector, unexpectedly, laughed.

“Absolutely not,” he said. “Sit down, sit down. I have carried this book for thirty-two years and I have never once had a room to say it in, and now there is one, and you are the only person in it who is going to be asked a question about it that I have not already answered for thirty-two years, and that is not an insult, that is a compliment, and you may take it as one or leave it on the table.”

Pia did not sit down.

“Say the question then,” she said.

“The question is what the number is for,” said Margo. “And I have got an answer, and it is not the answer anybody expects, so I am going to say it before somebody else spends an hour on a good argument.”

She went and got the clipboard and took the cardigan off the corner of it and put the clipboard on the table next to the pocket diary, and the two objects looked, side by side, like the two halves of a sentence neither of them had ever heard the other half of.

“Go and look at the board on the fence,” she said.

“Why.”

“Because you have not looked at it properly, and neither has the man, and he has been in this building four times. Look at it. Go on. It is at the top of the street and it has been there for thirty years and you have all read it.”

Jonas walked up in the rain and stood in front of a green fence with a rota board nailed to it, and read it, and came back down again.

**WEIR STAND — LOWLAND DRAW — DAILY.**
**SLOTS: 40**
**CROSSED TODAY: 11**
**HOLDER: SEE ROTA**

**SLOTS: 40.**

“Forty,” Jonas said.

“Forty minutes,” said Margo. “Forty minutes a day. Two ten-minute turns, or four, or one long one if the trap packs, and it is the water we drink and the water we wash in and it is not twenty-two litres, it is twenty-two minutes of a tap running, and when the trap packs on the far side of the lowland there is nothing to draw and the whole board is a lie until four of us get down there with a shovel.”

“Somebody has read that wrong,” said Jonas.

“Somebody read that wrong nine years ago,” said Margo, “and put it on a form.”

She put her finger on the clipboard, where a form was, and Jonas saw that it was a photocopy of a county document with a box on it and a figure in the box.

“A man from the water authority came round in the year the stand was re-painted,” she said. “He was very pleasant. He was asking about lowland households for a report and he wrote down forty and he put it in *households served* and he did not look at the heading, and I did not stop him, because I was fifty-one and I had been doing it for nine years and I told myself he would not use it, and he used it, and it is in a report in a library in Saltmouth, and there are forty households on this street in a piece of paper in a library in Saltmouth, and there are fifty-nine doors, and there are nineteen people who are on this rota who are not in forty households because they are not households, they are two people in a house and a house is not the unit, a *draw* is the unit, and it is the only unit I have ever had.”

She squared the form with both hands.

“That is your new local fact,” she said. “Not a reason. Not a politics. It is a number in the wrong unit that has been lying about us in a public document for nine years, and I have known about it every single day, and I did not correct it because correcting it means putting a figure on a form, and I have watched what happens on this street when a figure goes on a form, which is the four hundred and ten millimetres and the ten years of it, and the man from the van, and the water that was off for six weeks.”

Nobody in the kitchen had said anything for some time.

“That is the first honest number anybody has put in front of me in a month,” said Otie Beck, from the doorway, with a file under her arm and her clinic finished and no coat on, “and it is a number about a *tap* and not about a *street*, and I would like it observed that thirty-one years of lowland nursing has taught me exactly one useful thing about forms, and it is this. A form does not know what it is asking. It knows what shape the answer has to be. A number of doors and a number of minutes and a number of households and a number of people are four different shapes and the form does not know the difference, and the man filling it in does not know the difference, and nobody who has ever handed a form to a man in a van has ever known the difference, and that is how you end up with a street of forty households in a report in Saltmouth and fifty-nine doors and nineteen rota names and a number of people who would be appalled at all three.”

She put the file down.

“So if you want my view, and I am the only person in this room who has filled in a district form in my life, then here is my view. You do not get refused a number. You never get refused a number. What you get refused is the *name under the number*. The number is harmless. The name is the leash, because the number is a size and the size is how they decide what to send you, and then the size is how they decide who has to pay for it, and then the name is how they find you at eleven at night when the thing they sent you has not worked. The day I got a number in this lowland, the day before I got a number, and the day after, I do not remember. I remember the paperwork. I remember four forms in a year where before there had been none, and I remember that the forms were on a clipboard and the clipboard went to a building in Saint Orra and did not come back.”

“Fifty-nine doors,” said Hector, to the table.

“Fifty-nine doors,” said Aurrie Nkemdirim. “And the number goes in a box in a form in an office, and the office is a man in a county, and the man does not know the unit, and after the man it is a system that does not know the unit either, and it is all perfectly honest at every single step, and at the end of it a piece of paper in a report says that this street has forty households and is a place of *low priority*, because forty is smaller than the number over the border, and the number over the border is a number of people and we do not know what the number over the border is either, and I am not going to be *low priority* with a school.”

“You are asking the city to put a number on us,” said Otie, “and to call us high. That is what you are asking. Say it out loud once so that a man can hear it and so that I can hear it.”

Aurrie stood very still at the side of the table with the cloth in her hand.

“Yes,” she said. “I am asking the city to put a number on us and to call us high, and I have wanted that since 1994, and I am not going to pretend today that wanting it is the same as being able to have it.”

And Hector Mbatha, seventy years old, with a rubber band and a piece of green twine and thirty-two years of eleven-minute walks in April, said the thing that had been sitting under the whole morning since the first count, and said it to the table and not to her.

“No,” he said. “You are asking them to put a number on us so that you can stop being the only one who wants things, and that is a reason, and it is not a bad one, and it is thirty-two years of me counting doors for a woman who could not bring herself to ask me for the number, and I have finally got somebody to hand it to and I find I would rather it was still mine.”

“Which of the two is it,” said Margo, “because the kitchen has been standing on that for ten minutes.”

“It is both,” said Hector. “I want the number. I have wanted the number longer than she has. I have wanted it since I watched her say *we should ask* in 1994 and I thought, the man does not know either, and I went and got a pencil because I could do the one thing and she could not.” He turned the diary round on the table. “And I do not want it to be used. And I know that those are the same wanting, and I have known for a long time, and I have never told anybody, and I would like somebody to be angry with me about it and then to leave me alone.”

“I am not going to be angry with you about it,” said Aurrie, “because I have wanted a number for thirty-two years and I have never once put my hand on a pencil.”

“You did the hard one,” said Hector.

Aurrie Nkemdirim, who had run the net loft exchange on that street for nineteen years and had wanted a district for thirty-two, looked at a table with a pocket diary and a photocopy and a boy's handwriting and a run of pencil crosses, and said the sentence that turned an argument into a position.

“Right,” she said. “Then we do it properly and we do it in ink, and we stop having this in a kitchen at two in the morning in the rain. Three things on that wall and a name against each one, and if it is a name then it is a name and nobody hides behind the exchange.”

She wrote on the back of the paper Bo had pinned up on Saturday, in the middle, with room round it.

**FOR THE APPLICATION — Aurrie Nkemdirim.**
**FOR THE COUNTY PUMP — Margo Doré.**
**FOR CARRYING ON UNNUMBERED — Ottoline Beck.**
**THE BANK — anyone who will take it, and there is no name on that line because there is no name yet.**

She pinned it up over the shelf of cleaned cups, under the plate with half its face scraped off, so that anybody coming in out of the rain read it at eye level before they read anything else.

“That is not democracy,” said Pia from the door, where she had not moved. “That is three people you have put on a wall. Hector is on no line. Wesley is on no line. The bank has no name on it. And the day somebody needs a name on that bank you are going to have three names on a wall in a kitchen and eleven people who were not in this room.”

“Yes,” said Aurrie, without turning round. “And you are the one who is going to say the next thing and I want to hear it.”

“The next thing is that you have just made me a spokesperson,” Pia said, “by not putting me on it. You have stood a boy of seventeen in front of a room and written *the bank* underneath, and he is the best pipe fitter on this street, and now every man in this city who comes down here is going to ask the boy. And I am not carrying that. I am nineteen and I carry bags. I am not on a wall and I am not a signature and I am not going to say *the street agrees* to a man with a form, and if you want the street to say something then the street has to say it, out loud, in a room, with all of it, and if it says no then it is no and you have to take the no to your department and not come back here with a face on you about it.”

“I have not asked you for anything,” Jonas said.

“You have been sitting in this room for two hours looking at a thing I carry,” said Pia, “and I can see you doing it, and I want you to hear yourself do it, because the number on the wall this morning is going to be the door through which everything comes, and it is not the door through which anything good comes, and I have watched six men go through doors like it and every one of them came out with a story about us.”

She picked up her bag.

“Which of the two,” she said, to Jonas, “is it, then. The location for your inspection. Because you have not asked and you have been not asking for an hour, and I can see you, and you have to say it out loud in front of these people or you are doing it in your head and that is the same as doing it.”

“I am not asking you for it,” Jonas said. “I am asking the room, and I am going to put in the form exactly what this room agrees to put in the form, and if the room says no, the form goes back, and I will write *the department could not establish a location* and that will be the answer and it will be my fault and not theirs.”

He took the tide table off the wall, turned it over, and wrote on the back of it in his own bad left-hand writing.

“LOWLAND SERVICE NODE 6-L/14. CONCRETE CHAMBER AND HINGED FLAP GATE AT THE BOTTOM OF THE LOWLAND DIP, WEST OF THE CANAL PATH. ACCESS BY FOOTPATH ONLY, FROM THE EASTERN EMBANKMENT, ON FOOT. NO MADE-UP ROAD. NO ADDRESS. NO HOUSE NUMBER. NO INSTITUTION.”

He read it out loud to the room.

“Departmental officer attending, per the response of the fourteenth. The department will send somebody here on a date between now and the twenty-eighth, and they will need somebody with them, and the only reason they will find this at all is that I am standing next to them and pointing, and I know that, and I know that it is me using your not-being-found as a convenience of my own job, and I am telling you that in a room with your people in it instead of in a car park afterwards.”

Aurrie Nkemdirim took the paper off him and read it twice.

“Take the word *no* out of it,” she said. “The whole rest of it is true and it is the only true thing about this street that has ever been written down for the city, and I am not having *no address* printed in a department file, because the day that line is in a file somewhere it is a line that somebody in a county office reads and adds a word to. It will be *address to be determined*. It will be *unresolved*. It will be *area of outstanding survey*, and then it is a place that is waiting for a survey, and a survey means somebody comes with a clipboard.” She handed it back. “Write what is there. The chamber. The dip. The footpath. Let the absence be silent and let it be somebody else’s job to say the word.”

“That is not honest.”

“That is the truth with the lying taken out,” said Aurrie, “and I have been on this street thirty-two years and it is the only version of the truth that has ever got anybody anything.”

Jonas looked at the paper for a moment, and then struck the three words out with his left thumb, which smeared the graphite, and wrote instead:

**NO MADE-UP ROAD. ACCESS BY FOOTPATH ONLY, ON FOOT.**

“All right,” he said. “And one more thing, and then I will go and telephone a clerk who is going to ask me for a postcode. Whatever I say, this is a place with no address, and the reason is not that it is secret, it is that there is no name for it anywhere in this city, and I have spent four afternoons proving that, and the department has written it down in an answer I signed the price of, and that is where we are. I am not on your side. I have not been on your side since Wednesday. I am a man standing in a kitchen with a form, and the reason the form exists at all is that I opened a drawer.”

“Which is the first true thing anybody has said in this room since Saturday,” said Pia.

She went out into the rain.

On the towpath, halfway between the loft and the boat sheds, Jonas rang the clinic and got Mina Okafor, and she was pleased to hear from him in the flat particular way of somebody who is pleased and is not going to say so.

“The day porter’s box is empty,” she said, before he had got a word in. “Sunday. It is the third Sunday. Ruth’s says the same. I have said it three times on the board and nobody has read them because on a Sunday morning there is no non-urgent dressing and no transfer and nobody comes, and I sat there from six to eleven on a corridor for nothing twice, and I am telling you it for the record, because when somebody eventually does come on a Sunday at ten past six I want it to say that we were here and not him.”

“You sat there for nothing.”

“I sat there for nothing,” said Mina, “and I would sit there again, and that is the sentence, and you may put it on your file or you may not, and I am going to hang up now because I have a bus to catch and it is my day off.”
