# Chapter 0007 — No One Signed the Map

The old pump house sat below the canal embankment in a concrete trench that smelled of iron and wet soil. A culvert ran beneath the access road, carrying runoff toward the tidal gates. The pump’s painted casing was the same dull green as the shelter door, though the water had worn the paint thinner.

At 11:36, Public Works inspector Hester Vann checked the pressure gauge twice and declared the line unsafe before Jonas had taken his tools out.

“That is quick,” Jonas said.

“The gauge is telling you what you were afraid of,” Vann replied. “It is already at the red mark.”

The gauge needle trembled above the safe band. A slow knock came from the pipe whenever the pump engaged, and the concrete around the base had a dark stain shaped like a thumbprint.

Oren Pell stood at the top of the trench with the closure order in a clear sleeve. He had arrived with a municipal recorder, a Bellow Market shelter steward, and a woman named Emeka Rusk, who had worked the pump for eleven years. Selah Mora stood behind Emeka with a folder of maintenance slips. Neither woman had brought a resident list.

“You said the shelter residents were relocated,” Vann said to Oren.

“They were.”

“And the people who maintain this line?”

“Working,” Emeka said. “Which is different.”

Jonas stepped into the trench. The pump plate gave him a dull flash, and the Margin appeared as a silver line between the mechanical strain and a term that had no name. The line split around two empty spaces, one beside the pump’s right to draw water and one beside the duty of maintaining the shared line. The physical leak was easy to see. The missing record was not a broken part. It was a gap between the service and the people who kept it alive.

A notice surfaced across his maintenance lens, the same ceramic white as the clinic plate, and it was no more helpful than that one had been.

> **Water available. Maintenance term: —**
> **Duty present. Record absent.**

Jonas touched the pump casing and looked away before the line could spread into the whole trench. Metal filled his mouth. The pulse of the pump beat behind his eyes, and the sounds above the wall arrived in fragments: fear, impatience, the scrape of a pen, and Emeka’s hand answering the casing with eleven years of practiced certainty.

He set both hands on the wrench and the casing.

“Emeka,” he said, “tell me the first safe pressure.”

“Forty-two,” she said. “Not red. Not blue. The patch weeps near forty-four.”

“Vann, can you test the physical line while I stay on the casing?”

“Can you stay there?” Vann asked.

“I can stay here.”

He did not tell her why. The trusted person had to be someone who could pull his attention back, and Emeka had spent eleven years doing exactly that with a machine.

Vann opened the inspection cover. The coupling at the pump base had a bright crescent of corrosion. A length of the line beyond it was patched with black resin and ordinary wire. The patch was holding better than the surrounding metal.

“Who authorized that repair?” Vann asked.

Emeka produced a slip from Selah’s folder. “We did. The line was leaking. We closed the branch, drained the low section, and replaced the coupling. We have a witness, a time, and a return of service.”

“The route has no current maintenance term,” Oren said.

Emeka’s mouth tightened. “The route has a person turning the wrench.”

“That is not the same as a record.”

“It is the only one that got wet.”

The answer landed in the trench and stayed there. Jonas felt the difference between them as a physical heat under his skin. The rule needed a term. The term needed someone to be accountable when the line failed. The person had been accountable. The record had left.

Jonas opened the ordinary maintenance schematic. It showed the pump, the culvert, and a maintenance duty connected to a district code that ended in a cut-off mark. The code did not tell him when the term had been removed, who had removed it, or why. He could see the absence in the Margin, but absence was not a confession.

The municipal recorder asked for a list of everyone who used the water.

“For the pressure test?” Jonas asked.

“For the closure record.”

“The line is unsafe. The test is unsafe. The number of people does not change either result.”

“The number determines the temporary tank size,” Oren said.

“That belongs to the receiving shelter and clinic, not to a headcount of unlisted residents.”

Oren nodded, not in agreement but in acknowledgment. “Then record the service load as unknown and let the supply decision follow the hazard.”

Emeka passed Jonas the pressure wrench. Her hand was steady. Jonas loosened the coupling in quarter-turns while she watched the gauge. At forty-two, the line began to sing. At forty-four, the patch wept a dark thread of water.

“Stop,” she said.

He stopped.

Vann recorded the pressure. “The pump cannot reopen. Not today, not until the coupling is replaced, the line is pressure-tested, and a maintenance term is recognized.”

“The residents are staying in Bellow Market,” Selah said. “They need water before the next care shift.”

“They will receive bottled water and a clinic-supplied tank,” Vann replied. “This is an emergency arrangement, not a right to reopen the line.”

“The load is recorded as unknown,” Jonas said. “Then how does anybody size a tank against an unknown?”

Vann kept one hand flat on the inspection cover. “By the meter, not the roll call. The clinic’s own recorded draw for observation four, and the ration the receiving shelter has actually drawn on its bottled supply this month. That is a measured figure. A tank does not need to know how many people it is for.”

“That arrangement ends when the next shift comes,” Jonas said.

“Then the next shift receives the next arrangement,” Vann said. “I am not turning a failed pipe into a promise I cannot keep.”

Jonas looked at the silver line. It narrowed as the pump stopped, but it did not disappear. The local duty remained in the same room as the missing term. He could not pull one into the other with a report. He could not make a record true by writing it, and he could not make a live practice disappear by refusing to name its people.

For a moment, the thought of the old service code opened a path toward Evan. Lowland service codes were what his father’s last file had been full of — the copy Nadia kept in a kitchen drawer because she could not bring herself to throw it away and could not bear to put it in an official archive. A single code might lead to a date, a station, a name. If Jonas asked for the old maintenance record now, the city might give him a fragment.

He could not ask for the residents’ roster in exchange.

Emeka wiped water from the coupling. “Write that the line sings at forty-four,” she said. “Write that the patch held until inspection pressure. Write that we closed it ourselves when the pump would not stop.”

“I will.”

“And write that the missing term is not the same as a missing duty.”

Jonas looked at her. “I will write that too.”

At 12:18, Vann signed the inspection. The pump stayed closed. Bellow Market arranged bottled water for the residents and asked Saint Orra west clinic to send a sealed tank for the next care shift. Mina Okafor confirmed delivery, keeping the clinical supply separate from the pump’s service record. Oren added a closure note: **REOPENING DENIED—PHYSICAL LINE UNSAFE; MAINTENANCE TERM UNRESOLVED**.

Jonas had hoped the inspection would produce a clean separation between the broken machine and the missing rule. Instead it gave him two failures: the pipe needed repair, and the rule needed a holder whom the city had not recorded. The residents’ immediate claim outranked his private need for a piece of his father’s history.

The municipal recorder closed the folder. “No headcount.”

“No headcount,” Oren said.

They left the residents’ names in Selah’s custody and the water arrangement in the receiving shelter’s hands. Jonas carried only the pump’s redacted service code to the NDC records room.

His department handset vibrated before he reached the van.

**Management review: close the Saint Orra incident as hardware failure before 16:00. Probation file requires a clean classification.**

He looked at the message, then at the locked pump house. The old code on his pad was a route without a destination. The residents had water for the next shift only because he had stopped pretending that a closed gate and a cared-for person were the same thing.

He typed **unresolved permission conflict** into the reply, waited for the rejection that followed, and then walked toward the records room.
