# Chapter 0029 — The Second Map

On the Monday morning Bea Osunde took the keys to the wall cabinet off her belt and put them in her apron pocket and looked at him over the bench for a moment before she said it.

“No.”

“Consequence two came out on the fourteenth.”

“Consequence two came out on the fourteenth and I read it on the fourteenth, and I have read it every working day since, and it says that the individual is removed from all gate-initiation duties at six-four-one and from cabinet six-R-fourteen until a review of the review is concluded.” She took the sanding block out of his hand, which he had not known she could do without touching him. “And there is no review of the review on any board in this building, because there is no such thing yet, and until there is, you are a man who may not open that cabinet, and I am not going to be the person who finds a reason.”

“It is due on the twenty-eighth.”

“Then it is due on the twenty-eighth without the cards in it,” said Bea, “and I am going to say the rest of this now, because you have got a face and I have got four days to say it in. You are going to write that response without those cards, and you are going to write it badly and honestly, and it is going to be the best thing in that file, and every single person who reads it is going to think *that man did not have the box*.”

“I know.”

“You do not know, because you have never once been the person who did not have the box, and it is the only genuinely useful thing that has happened to you this month.” She put the sanding block down on the bench with its flat side up, the way she always did. “You have made four things this month with your name at the bottom of them. The answer to the registrar, the classification of two nodes, a piece of work on a bank, and a term on a tap — no. Take that one back, that one is not yours, you did that one at somebody else’s instruction with my hand on the bench.” She held up a finger. “So three. Three things with your name at the bottom. And on that street there are now eleven things with somebody else’s name at the bottom of them, and one of them is a paper with a ring drawn round a blank space, and the ring is there on purpose.”

She picked the block up again.

“You have stopped being the answer on that street and you have not noticed, and the reason you have not noticed is that it was never the thing you wanted,” said Bea Osunde. “You wanted it to be enough. It is not ever enough. Go and do the twenty-eighth.”

The Old Ward board had a new map on it.

Jonas had walked past that board four times in twelve days and had never once looked at the map, because the map had always been a map, and because the thing on the board that mattered to him was a line of text. But on the Monday morning there was a printed service sheet pinned over the lower half of the map, and it had a diagram on it, and the diagram was the city, and in the diagram there was a mark.

The mark was a small filled circle, the same size and the same colour as the circles for the Saint Orra bridge and the River Stacks cabinet, and it was down in the bottom left-hand corner of the diagram in a place where the printed coastline has a shape that Jonas recognised, because he had looked at that shape on a water authority standpipe map and on four thin sheets in a reference library, and had never once managed to put a name to it.

There were two marks and they were four millimetres apart. One was a filled circle. The other was an open square, in a lighter ink, and the key at the foot of the diagram called the open squares *unregistered plate*.

**6-L/19 — LOCAL TERM. HOLDER: ROTA, WEIR STAND. EXPIRES 06:00.**

He stood and looked at it for a long time.

He knew exactly what it was. It was a mended field at a plate he had worked, on the seventeenth of March, at a stand at the top of a street, with a woman of sixty-three issuing it and a line technician from River Stacks on the probe, and a term that ran fourteen days and had a claim on it and could not be renewed by him.

It was the first thing he had ever made that had put a mark on a map of this city, and it had put it in a place with no name beside it, four millimetres from an open square that said *unregistered plate*, and the mark was a term about a tap.

And pinned to the left of the board, on the frame rather than the glass, with a paperclip bent round the edge of the sheet, was the other map.

It was a sheet of the net loft's paper, ruled faintly, about the size of a large postcard, and it had been drawn in pencil by somebody who could not draw. It was a plan of the lowland street from end to end: the line of the canal embankment along the top, the line of the boat sheds along the bottom, the gap between them where the road is. There were door marks. Jonas counted them without meaning to and there were fifty-nine, in five clusters, and beside five of them were the letters A, B, C, D, E in a hand that started large and got smaller. There was a green circle at the top of the street with three lines coming off it and a small rectangle beside it. There was a filled-in rectangle in the middle with a heavy black line across it, and a wavy line running away from it to the right and down, and beside that a date and an initial.

At the bottom, in capitals:

**THE WEIR. 59 DOORS. DRAWN BY H. MBATHA, 24 MARCH. STAND, CHAMBER, BANK, AND THE TREE BY NUMBER 12.**

Two maps of the same place, forty centimetres apart on the same board, and between them a difference that Jonas could not get out from under all day: the city's map had one filled circle in it and no name; the street's map had fifty-nine doors and one black rectangle and no filled circle at all.

Nobody in the Old Ward had put either of them there. The city map was the department's own. The pencil map had been clipped on by somebody with a paperclip at about eight o'clock that morning, and there was no docket on it and no name on the docket, and the man at the board's office said he had not seen who.

Jonas took out his pad and wrote down the date and the two numbers, 6-L/14 and 6-L/19, and the four millimetres, and the fifty-nine doors, and then he stopped and looked at what he had written, and put the pad away, because a pad is a pad and Bea Osunde had told him a fortnight ago not to keep things in a pad that turn into facts.

At ten past twelve on the same Monday, a stretcher came up the ramp at Saint Orra west.

Mina Okafor put the claim on the plate and the plate said, in the four words it had been saying for three weeks, from the doorway where anybody could read it:

**HOLDER NOT NAMED. ROUTE CLOSED.**

It was a woman of eighty-four from the day assessment list, on a trolley, with a daughter at the bottom of the ramp in the rain, and it was not an emergency, and it was not a non-urgent dressing claim, and the eleven o'clock handover had happened on time with a signature and no successor, because the route is closed between Bram's shift and Ezra's, and the outgoing holder reads the term aloud to the clerk and signs the box, and there was no incoming holder to read it to.

Mina telephoned the on-call clinician at two minutes past twelve.

Nothing happened for nine minutes.

Jonas was standing at the foot of the ramp in the rain with his left hand in his pocket because the right one was in his coat, and he could not have opened that door for anything in the world. He was not the bearer. He had never been the bearer. The fallback that had once followed his personal availability had been taken off the door in a room full of witnesses an afternoon a fortnight ago, in front of a woman of ninety-one who had come in her coat, and what had replaced it was four lines that said the route is closed unless the named on-call clinician declares an emergency in writing, and Dr. Sanaa Iqbal was at that moment in the resuscitation room of the west site with both hands full of somebody else's family, which is the day she named in a room above a laundry, in a coat, before she said yes.

At eleven minutes past twelve the telephone rang back.

“Open it,” said Sanaa Iqbal.

Mina put the declaration on the plate and the door opened and two orderlies came down and took the trolley up, and the daughter went up behind it, and the door closed, and the whole thing took nineteen minutes including the walk.

The entry in the log read: *12:02 declaration sought, on-call clinician. 12:11 declaration given. Opened 12:12. Returned 12:31. Nine minutes unanswered.*

Jonas copied it onto the board in the box because that was what the box was for, and the box was full, and his own handwriting in that box had not changed since the Wednesday he had started it.

Sanaa came down the ramp at half past one with a coat on and a chart, and she read the box, and she read the line about the nine minutes, and she did not say anything about it for about a minute and a half.

“I want three things written down,” she said, “and none of them are about you, and I want them in the record, and I am going to say them to you and then I am going to go and do a ward round.

“The first: I said yes in the room above the laundry on the afternoon the plate printed it, with my coat still on, and I said *I am saying yes knowing that, and I want it in the record that I said yes knowing that, and I want the number on that board to be a person’s number and not a desk.* That is a record. It is in the minutes and it is on the wall in a plastic sleeve at eye height. I have not hidden from it for one second.

“The second: the clause works. It is a good clause. It is the best thing anybody in this building has written, and it is better than the thing it replaced, and I want that said in a public document, because in four months there will be a version of this story in which a man broke a rule to get a patient through a door and a woman in a resus room did not answer, and that version has both of us in it and neither of us in the right place. On the third day a technician acknowledged a shift he had no right to acknowledge, and I know what that was and I know what it cost and I have said so. On the afternoon the plate printed it he took himself off the door, and he did it because Marta Sowle stood up in a room and said *take it off*, and nobody asked him to, least of all me. And that is why there was a design in this corridor this morning instead of a man in it.

“The third.” She looked at the box. “Nine minutes. On the twenty-fourth of March, at twelve minutes past twelve, an eighty-four-year-old woman waited nine minutes on a ramp in the rain because a doctor was doing her job. That is not a fault. That is a Tuesday. And the number that will be on that board in five years is not going to be nine, it is going to be nine out of a total, and the total is going to be something like four hundred and eleven, and somebody in a review will do the division and get a percentage and the percentage will be the number, and the percentage will be *fine*.”

“Then what do you want me to do with it.”

“I want you to leave it on the board,” said Sanaa, “in the box, with the other handwriting in it, and I want you to notice, once, that the man who put that line there could not have made it better by being there. That is all. That is the whole of what I have come down the ramp for. Do not read anything into it, because I have got four hundred patients and no gift.”

She went back up the ramp.

Mina Okafor caught him at the card table before he got to Halvorsen's corridor, with a board under one arm, and she had the face of somebody who has been let off something and does not think she has.

“Two things and then I have to go and do the four o'clock round,” she said. “One: Bram has refused Thursday. He has read the term aloud to me at five to eleven and signed the box and gone home and he is not coming back in on Thursday, and the reason is his granddaughter’s birthday, and he told me the reason, which he has never done, and he told me because he said that if he did not tell me then the day would go down on the board as a refusal and not as a birthday.

“And the board now says *06:00. NO HOLDER* for Thursday morning, and there is nothing under it, because there is nothing under it, because the roster does not say who covers a refusal, it says *a refusal of a whole shift ends the shift, not the job*, and the job goes on and the door is shut.”

“Is that the first time?”

“It is the first time there has been a box with nothing in it on a weekday morning since the fourteenth,” said Mina, “and I am telling you it on purpose, because you are going to hear about Thursday by Wednesday and I want you to have heard about it from the person who has to sit at the desk while it is empty. That is all. It is not a complaint. It is a piece of information about the roster and it is mine and I am giving it to you freely and I am not going to discuss it again.”

She put the board down and squared it.

“The other thing is that the four of us have to read five lines out loud to each other tonight at ten o’clock, and the term runs to six o’clock on the thirtieth. And before you say anything about that: it is the third reading since the thirteenth. The one before this was on the nineteenth and it runs to six o’clock tomorrow morning, and Ruth read this one tonight anyway, a day early, and when I asked her why she said *because a six-day term is not a formality and I am not going to sit on one I have already decided about.* Six days is somebody standing in this corridor at ten o’clock at night and agreeing to be responsible for the same thing again in six days, and she argues for every one of them as though she is arguing for money, and she is right to, and I am not going to be the one who says *just read them out* to that woman. It is a rota, Mr Mercer. You keep saying it like it is a word you have read.”

The west site administration’s office was a corridor with two desks and a printer, and Gunnar Halvorsen had been in it for eleven days without signing one thing, and he was extremely glad to see Jonas, and said so, and then made him wait nineteen minutes while he dealt with something else, which Jonas understood to be a man making sure he was not going to be thanked for anything.

Two things were on his desk.

The first was a rostering exception.

“The answer you filed on the fourteenth is public and it names the six-four-one term and it names the bearers, and the bearers’ hours are on it, and I have had a clerk from my own administration ring the ward twice to check them, because the answer says the department finds the acts were made without a named bearer initiating a route, and if the record says her hours are twenty-three hundred to six hundred and she was actually there from twenty-two hundred to eleven hundred, then somebody in my building has been wrong on a schedule for nineteen years and it is my building.” He turned a sheet round. “This is a rostering exception. It corrects the record of record to the declared hours, twenty-two hundred to eleven hundred, effective from the fifteenth of March, and it is signed by me, and it is the only thing on this desk I have been able to sign in eleven days.”

Jonas read it twice.

“What does it do about the other nineteen years?”

“Nothing,” said Halvorsen. “Nothing whatsoever, and I am not going to pretend otherwise to make this feel better. It corrects the record going forward. The arrears are not settled by correcting a record, and the arrears are not even computed, and if they are ever computed they are a claim and a claim of that shape goes to a committee and a committee will take two years.” He put his hand flat on the paper. “And I will tell you what I have learned in eleven days, which is that I can fix a schedule in four minutes and I cannot fix an institution, and there is no instrument in this city for the second thing, and the reason there is no instrument is that the second thing does not exist on anybody’s spreadsheet, and it exists only in the fact that a woman of some years has been getting up at ten at night for nineteen years and arriving at a corridor nobody was at.”

Ruth Nwosu was at the end of that corridor at two o'clock, and she had been at the end of it since eleven, and she had been told about the exception by Mina at eleven and had gone back to work.

“You have not asked me properly,” she said, before Jonas got to the door of the card table.

“I know.”

“Then do it, and do it now, and I will answer it and then I will go back to work, because I have been asked twice and both times you came with a piece of paper.”

“I am not asking you to hold the door.”

“No,” said Ruth, “you are not, and that is the first time anybody has come down here in nineteen years and asked me a question that was not about the door, so go on.”

“I am asking you what it costs to have your hours corrected, and whether you want it corrected, and whether there is anything about it that you would rather I did not put in a document.”

Ruth Nwosu put the black key on the card table.

“Nineteen years,” she said. “Two and a half hours a night, most nights, for nineteen years, and nobody has ever counted it, and I have counted it in my head every single night of my life, which is a stupid thing to have done to myself, and I am not going to give you the number and you are not going to have it.

“And it will not be corrected. That is what I want said. Corrected means from the fifteenth of March, which means from the fifteenth of March I exist, and before the fifteenth of March I am a woman who was there, and I want that sentence kept exactly as it is. You put it in a document, Mr Mercer, and you will put the number in it, because you are the sort of man who cannot leave a thing uncounted, and if the number goes into a document it will be a hundred and eighteen thousand pounds, and the department will spend two years on it, and the two years will be about the hundred and eighteen thousand pounds and not about me, and at the end of it there will be a payment and a form and a letter saying the matter is closed.

“You asked me properly and I have answered you properly and I have also said no, and the no is to the money and not to the correction, and I would like both of those on one line.” She picked the key up. “The hours are right from the fifteenth. Say so on the board. Do not say anything else.”

“Nobody is going to—”

“Nineteen years, six months, and I have it to the day because I have a diary,” said Ruth Nwosu. “It is a red diary from a newsagent and it is in my bag. That is the end of the conversation and you may not have it and I am telling you that it exists so that you understand that I have not been not saying it. I have been saying it every night into a book, which is what a person does who cannot get anybody to hear her, and it is not a good system, and I would like it to have been a good system, and it was nobody’s fault but mine, and I would like the board to say the hours are right from the fifteenth and I would like the other thing left alone, and now go and see Gunnar, because he has had a thing on that desk for eleven days and it is not the rostering.”

Gunnar Halvorsen had the retainer on the other side of the desk, face down, which Jonas understood to be a thing he had been doing with it for eleven days.

“Selah Mora rang me,” he said, “this morning, at nine, and she read me the line on your board, word for word, and then she said *read it back*, and I read it back, and she said *and now say the date you will take it to a board*, and I said the ninth of next month, and she said *write it down*, and I have written it down, and here it is.”

It was a single line in a diary, in Halvorsen’s own hand.

> **NINTH OF NEXT MONTH. SAINT ORRA WEST SITE BOARD. RETROSPECTIVE RETAINER, RETURN-OF-CARE LIMB, 22:00–06:00. CARRIED UNPAID SINCE THE FIFTEENTH OF MARCH. (G.H.)**

“That is a promise,” Jonas said.

“It is a diary entry, and a diary entry is not a cheque, and the cheque comes out of a budget that closed on the thirty-first, and a retrospective payment for hours worked in a financial year that has already closed is a board minute, and a board minute is nine hundred and sixty pages of agenda and there is a standing item on the ninth, which is *matters arising*, and it has forty minutes.” He put his hand on the page. “And I am not going to promise you the cheque in a corridor, because the last service manager who promised a porter a cheque in a corridor is why I have a job.”

“And if it is not on the agenda in forty minutes?”

“Then it is not on the agenda, and I will ring her on the tenth and tell her so myself, and she will tell me again on the eleventh, and she has told me she will do that and I believe her, and I would like it recorded that I have said that sentence out loud to a man who is her friend and that I did not enjoy it.” He picked the page up. “And the words stay on the board. I have not asked for them to come off and I am not going to. If the cheque is written on the ninth then they come off on the ninth, in this office, in front of her, and not before, and not by me at a quarter to six on a Friday because I feel awkward.”

Jonas stood in that office for a moment longer than he needed to.

“Mr Halvorsen, in eleven days I filed an answer that said a department would send an officer to look at two nodes on a street that has no address, and the reason it says *could not be produced* is that the street has no address, and the reason the street has no address is in a filing system in this building, and you are the service manager of the one building on the chain that has ever done anything about any of it.” He said it as a fact and not as an accusation, and Gunnar Halvorsen, who was a pressed and competent man with a gap on his own rota and no answer in his own institution, took it as both.

“I know what is in the filing system,” he said. “So does everybody who works here, and none of us can say it out loud in a room with a minute being taken, and that is not cowardice, that is what a filing system does to a person over eleven years. If you want it said, it will have to be said by somebody who does not work here.”

He was at the shop at half past five, and Tomas Renn was at the bench with the bond's yellow board behind him and a cup of tea going cold, and had clearly been there for some time and had clearly been waiting.

“Sit down,” Tomas said. “No, stand up. You look like a man who needs to sit down and I have seen men like that use sitting down to get out of a conversation.”

Jonas stood up.

“I have asked you one question in this building that you have not answered and I have not asked it twice, and you have not noticed that I have not asked it twice, and I have decided that I am going to ask it a second time today, in person, once, and that after today I am going to stop asking it and that stopping is not an answer and both of us will know it.” Tomas put his cup down. “Chapters are for books. Here it is. When two people on the same rota both refuse — and on your roster, on a bad week, with a bad Saturday, that is not a hypothetical, that is Bram refusing Thursday because it is his granddaughter’s birthday and the evening man refusing because his mother is in the ward, and it is not a matter of if — somebody is the least-bad option at four in the morning, and there is no rule, and Ruth will not be there because it is Ruth’s day off, and you are not going to be there because you are suspended and because I would not let you and because if you were there it would be your fault that the door was shut in a way nobody can explain to a family in a corridor.”

He waited.

“I do not have an answer,” said Jonas.

“I know you do not have an answer. I have not got an answer. I have got a stop-work clause and a refusal clause and neither of them says who holds the door, so I have built a rota with a hole in it, and I have signed it, and I have watched a man with a seam in his arm put a name on a plate on a street with no address so that somebody else’s name is on it, and I have thought about that every day since and I have not been able to work out whether it is a solution or a way of making the hole look like a wall.”

He picked the cup up and did not drink from it.

“Do not answer me,” said Tomas Renn. “That is the whole of it and I am going now. Ask somebody who is not me, and when you find the person, bring them here, because it will not be me and it will not be you, and I have been the only person saying it out loud for four weeks and I am sixty per cent of the problem, because a shop steward who asks the question and is the only person asking it has made it his own instead of the roster’s, and I did that in 2011 and it cost a man nine days and a scaffold.”

He put the cup down and went out into the yard, and Bea Osunde, at the bench, with a plate in one hand and a screwdriver in the other, waited until he was round the corner before she said anything at all.

“He has been waiting two days to say that,” she said. “You did not do anything to bring it on. He wrote it in the diary last night. I saw the page when I was looking for the other one.”

“Which other one?”

“The one where you put your name at the bottom of three things and somebody else’s name at the bottom of eleven,” said Bea, “and did not notice, and I have now told you twice, and I am not going to tell you a third time, and the third time has to come from you or it does not count.”
