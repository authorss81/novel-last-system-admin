# Chapter 0005 — The Price of a Door

The old-canal shelter had been a sailmaker’s warehouse before it had been a clinic, a hostel, or whatever mixture of both it served now. Its front door was green metal with salt scars along the lower edge, and the access plate beside it had been wired over so many times that the ceramic showed through in pale chips.

Jonas stood on the canal side with Alma Reyes on a transfer stretcher, Mina Okafor beside him, and the patient’s son Rafi holding the medication bag. The shelter door stayed shut.

The speaker above the latch clicked.

“State the return after arrival,” a woman said.

Mina looked at Jonas. “That is the service term, not the patient.”

Jonas opened his field reader. The plate accepted the clinic’s care handoff and refused the return because no current term connected the shelter to the person waiting inside. The fault was clear enough to name. The missing part was not a wire or a loose screw. It was the duty that would continue after the stretcher crossed the threshold.

The woman on the other side spoke again. “Selah Mora, watch steward. I will not open for a technician’s promise.”

Jonas recognized Pia’s description before he had put the name to her. “Selah, my name is Jonas Mercer. I can put my maintenance credential against the route for the handoff.”

There was a pause. “You can?”

“Yes. It will make me the responsible technician on the record.”

“Until when?”

“The end of the return.”

“Which end do you mean? The end of the stretcher’s passage, or the end of her care?”

Jonas watched the green light pulse. He had a tool for the ambiguity. He could choose a limit that sounded exact enough to pass the plate and then discover that the care had no owner after the door closed. His name would be on the first part. Someone else’s life would be on the rest.

“Until the handoff is complete,” he said. “The patient enters the shelter, her next care shift is named, and the route closes.”

“That is a description,” Selah said. “Not a promise.”

“That is the most the clinic can give us today.”

Mina turned to the plate. “The patient is stable. Her follow-up is at ten-thirty tomorrow. I have the medication list. I can remain responsible for the transfer, not for the shelter’s care.”

A man’s voice came from deeper inside the shelter. “Raf?”

“It’s me, Ma.”

Selah’s voice softened, but the door did not open. “Raf, you do not have to carry her alone. That is the point of a return route.”

“I know,” he said. “Let me carry the bag.”

The speaker went silent. Jonas could hear people moving inside, shoes on a floor, a kettle beginning to whistle, someone telling another person to keep the side corridor clear. The shelter was not empty. It was simply not represented by the plate in the way the city expected a shelter to be represented.

“Can you ask the residents to witness the handoff?” Jonas said.

“No,” Selah answered. “I cannot turn frightened people into a signature because a technician needs one.”

“I’m not asking for signatures.”

“You are asking for a witness.”

“I am asking for the person who keeps the door.”

Selah’s silence lasted long enough for Jonas to understand how badly he had worded it.

Pia had told him the shelter’s watch steward kept the return-of-care log. It had not occurred to him that the watch steward might be the person most careful of what that log exposed. He was used to seeing a missing field as an invitation to fill it. Selah saw an empty municipal record as a place where the city could put its hands on whoever had kept working after the record was emptied.

The kettle stopped whistling.

“Here is what we can state,” Selah said. “Raf will escort Alma to the room assigned by this watch. Mina will verify the medication and name the follow-up. The escort route ends when the room and follow-up handoff are complete. My watch and Mina’s clinical list continue until their named endpoints. If any one of us cannot do the duty, the person who remains calls the clinic.”

“And the shelter’s residents?”

“Stay out of the record.”

Mina stepped forward and spoke toward the speaker. “I can witness the medication transfer and the follow-up time. I cannot certify the building or the residents’ status.”

Raf looked at Jonas. “I can get her to the room. If I promise to keep her safe forever, that is a lie.”

“So don’t,” Jonas said.

He put his field reader on the ground, outside the door’s threshold, and took out the paper maintenance form. He drew a short line from the clinic care claim to the shelter entrance, then added three boxes: escort, room, next shift. He did not fill the patient’s name in any box visible to the public panel.

Selah listened through the speaker. “That is smaller than you wanted.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

The access plate accepted the three duties when Raf, Mina, and Selah each stated the part they could perform. Jonas’s maintenance credential served only as the physical route for the handoff. The door opened twelve centimeters, then enough for the stretcher.

He did not enter.

Mina and Raf took Alma inside. Jonas remained on the canal side, where the air smelled of wet brick and somebody’s onions frying in a pan. He could see the entrance lamp and hear wheels crossing the floor beyond it. The silver Margin remained pinned to the plate, a thin line around the three duties and the local person who held them. It did not open into the shelter. It did not tell him whether the people inside were hidden, safe, or simply accustomed to doors that asked questions before letting anyone pass.

At 9:26, Mina’s voice came through the speaker. “Medication list checked. Alma is in room nine. Next care shift is two o’clock tomorrow, with me responsible for the follow-up record until then.”

Selah answered, “Room and follow-up received. Escort closed. My watch and the clinic record remain until two.”

Jonas wrote the time. The temporary return route closed with a click. The green lamp beside the shelter door went dark.

He packed his field reader.

From inside, Raf asked whether he could bring the patient’s blanket outside. Selah told him to wait. A minute later, the door opened again under the local watch, and Raf emerged carrying a folded wool blanket and a paper slip.

“Thank you,” Raf said. “For not taking the whole thing.”

“She took the whole thing,” Jonas said. “I held the door for the part she named.”

Raf nodded. It was not agreement, exactly. It was the kind of recognition people gave when they needed to keep working and did not have time to name what had changed.

Selah opened the hatch only wide enough to take the blank route sheet. “No copy?”

“Only the custody reference,” Jonas said. “The route stays with your watch.”

“Good.” She looked at him through the hatch. “You can write that on your city form.”

“I will.”

“Then you can leave.”

Jonas looked at the shelter. He had fixed a relay, opened a clinic door, and held a short return. Each time, the next door had appeared in a different record, belonging to someone who had kept working while the city called the work missing. He wanted to put his name where the next failure would find it. The urge felt like responsibility because it was easier than asking who else had to answer.

Selah waited until he moved away from the hatch before closing it.

By 9:41, the stretcher had returned to the clinic corridor, the patient’s follow-up was recorded, and the old-canal shelter had a closed door with a living watch behind it.

A city inspection notice arrived ten minutes later.

Deputy Registrar Oren Pell had ordered the shelter closed at dawn under a public-safety closure. The water line was unstable. The south wall had taken enough moisture to make the front rooms unsafe overnight. The order named the building, the permitted removal window, and the public shelter that residents could request.

Jonas read it under the green lamp. The seal was valid. The building looked old enough to deserve the caution. The residents inside looked like people who had already learned not to trust a door that could be closed by a signature.

Selah opened the hatch again.

“Does the order let us ask for another place?” she asked.

Jonas read the removal clause. “It says you can request a recognized shelter. It does not say the city has to recognize the people who need it.”

“Then help us find the address before dawn.”

Jonas took out his work pad. He had a night ahead, a pending appointment, and a valid closure he could not repair with a screwdriver. For the first time, competence felt like a tool that could only open one door before leaving him responsible for the weather.
