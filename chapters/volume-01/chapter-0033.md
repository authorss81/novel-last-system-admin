# Chapter 0033 — The Waiver, Refused

The room was the department’s second-floor briefing room at half past nine on Saturday morning, and there were six people in it and a table and a document tray, and the document tray had one item in it.

Royden Achebe had it out before Jonas had the door shut behind him.

“I will read the operative sentence and then I will get out of the way,” said Achebe, “because you have read it and I have watched you read it.”

Jonas had read it. It was one page, and it was better drafted than the department’s own version of the same idea, and the operative sentence ran: *...no local term, holder, refusal, witness arrangement, or standing practice within the contracted area shall be held incompatible with continuity maintenance; and where a local term and a continuity maintenance requirement conflict, the requirement is served and the local term is suspended for the duration of the requirement...*

Clause five was the one he could not get past.

*...the refusal of any person, rota, or institution shall not suspend, delay, or condition continuity maintenance...*

“Nobody has to be told no,” he said. “That is what it means.”

“That is what it means,” said Ceri Vaughan.

“No,” said Royden Achebe. “That is not what it means, and I would like to put the department’s position before you do, because you will otherwise spend the next two years telling people I offered you a blank cheque, and I did not. I offered you a signature that says continuity outranks a local practice for the length of a repair, and then we all go home, and the west clinic keeps its rota and Ruth Nwosu keeps her key and Selah Mora keeps her eight hours and there is a crew in that building in ninety minutes instead of nineteen days. That is a real number. You have been on nineteen days. I sat in your shop on Friday and heard it off a man who is not on my payroll.”

“You have heard it off a man who is on somebody’s,” said Jonas, “and the somebody is the one the clause is for.”

Bea Osunde said nothing. She had come because the bond says a named shop steward may stop work and she had decided to be in the room, and she had said on the stairs that she was there to watch and not to speak unless she was asked, and Tomas Renn had come with her and had not sat down.

Oren Pell was at the end of the table with his coat still buttoned and his hands flat on the table in front of him, which is what he does when he is here to be a record of something rather than to argue about it. He had not brought the file. He had not brought anything.

“I am going to say the thing that everybody in this room is thinking and that Mr Mercer has not said yet,” said Royden Achebe, “and then you can refuse it properly instead of half of it. It is not true that anybody is erased.

“A category cannot say no. Fine. A *person* on a rota can say no, and I have read the twenty-fourth, and it is right, and the woman who reads those five lines out loud every week at ten o'clock is not a category and neither is the boy who reads them back. Your objection is about the door and about the refusal, and on the twenty-fourth of March that woman read a term aloud to a man of twenty-six and renewed it because she wanted to, and the two of them did that in a corridor for nothing. No clause in this document stops a rota doing that on Tuesday.” He put two fingers on the page. “What this document does is say that when a *continuity maintenance requirement* exists, it is served. A flooded lowland. A pump house with a burst main. A clinic door with a patient behind it and no one at it. It does not say anything about a rota on a good day and I have had it re-drafted twice to make sure of it.

“So tell me the sentence in that document that stops a woman keeping her own door, because I have not been able to find it and I would like to go back to a drafting meeting with something.”

There was a silence of a kind Jonas had not been in a room for in a month.

“There is not one,” he said. “I have been trying to find it since eleven o’clock last night and that is the whole of what I have done since eleven o’clock last night. I have got four reasons and every one of them is about me.”

Bea Osunde was asked, because Tomas Renn said her name and then said, “You have been sitting there for forty minutes not saying anything, and it is not like you.”

“I am not going to help him,” said Bea.

“He did not ask you to help him.”

“He asked me to be in the room, and there is a difference, and I know the difference, and I am in the room.” She did not look at Jonas. “I will say one thing, because I owe it to him and it is not on his side. If he signs that page, then for the next five years every time anybody in this city wants a thing done to a person who has not consented to it, the answer is *well, the technician signed it*. And that is not because he is a villain. That is because he is the only man in this city anybody can point at and say *you were here when it started*. I have watched four men do that in nineteen years and I have watched two of them lose the right to be asked anything at all. He is thirty-two. In five years of this he will be thirty-seven and he will not be able to walk into a room without somebody in it deciding what he is for.”

Nina Calder wrote all of it down, including that.

Jonas looked round the table for the person he wanted in it and she was not in it, and then he telephoned her, in the room, in front of all of them, which Oren Pell observed and said nothing about.

She took it on the fourth ring.

“I am in a corridor with four hundred patients,” she said. “Say it in one line.”

“There is a form you have read. It says a refusal does not stop a continuity repair.” He put it in the plainest words he had. “It would mean that at any hour, on any of those doors, anybody with a continuity claim could come and open it, and the rota could not stop them, and the named on-call clinician would not be the person who decided.”

There was a pause on the line of about three seconds.

“I want to be very careful with the next thing I say,” said Sanaa Iqbal, “because I am going to say something to you that is not advice, and you are going to use it, and I am telling you now that I know that and I am saying it anyway because the alternative is saying nothing and letting you decide on your own.”

“Say it.”

“It is the same clause that would have stopped me being nine minutes late.” Her voice did not change at all. “On the twenty-fourth, at two minutes past twelve, the only reason that door opened at twelve past twelve instead of not at all is that the clause requires a named clinician in writing. Remove the refusal and the rota and you have a continuity claim with a crew on it, and the door opens, and nobody is standing in a resuscitation room missing it, and nobody is standing in a corridor afterwards being told that the design worked. That is true, and it is the best argument for that piece of paper anybody will make in the next ten years, and I have made it to myself at about four in the morning on three separate occasions since the fourteenth.

“And I do not want it,” she said. “Not because of the rota, which is your argument and a good one. Because the day that door opens for anybody who claims it, I stop being the person who decides, and I am not certain I would want to keep being that person if I could. And I would like somebody to write down that the woman who said no to it said no for that reason and not out of loyalty to a man she has not certified.”

“That is not in your favour.”

“No,” she said. “It is in nobody’s favour. Go on then.” And she hung up, and Jonas stood there with a dead telephone in his hand in a room with five people in it.

Nina Calder had her pen over the minute book.

“Mr Mercer,” she said, “before you say anything. You have refused three instruments in a month and every one of those refusals is on a page in this building, and if you are about to refuse a fourth I have to write down whether you have got a reason or whether you have got a mood. Do you have a reason.”

“Yes.”

“Give it to the room, not to me.”

Jonas put the page face down on the table, which he had never done to a document in his life, and then turned it back again because it was not his to put anywhere.

“I have two, and I am going to give you the one you expect first so that the other one is not doing the work of the one you expect.

“The first is that a category cannot be told no. That is true and it is not why, and I can hear how it sounds, coming from a man who has refused three things in a month and would like it minuted that he has. Put it down and cross it out if you like, but it is true.

“The second is this. I cannot tell you whether I am signing that page as a man who believes it or as a man who needs the cabinet I am not allowed to open, and the appointment that is not mine, and the arrears of a woman with a red diary in her bag, and eight hours a night for a woman I have never met. There is no box on that page for that. There is no box anywhere in this building for *I am signing this because I am frightened of what happens to me*, and the reason there is not is that nobody here thinks a technician can be frightened of what happens to him, and one of you is wrong about that, and it is not me.

“And there is a third thing, and Royden, this part is for you.” He turned the page round and put his thumb on the middle of it. “You are not buying trucks. This page works by finding whoever is closest and putting their name in, and that is the only mechanism on it. The nearest responsible person on the lowland chain is me, because I issued a plate, and the department has been writing that phrase in a file about those nodes all month and I have not corrected it once, because it is true.

“So this is a form that says a man is a good place to put a city.” He took his thumb off the page. “I have spent eleven days watching a street say no to that, and I do not get to sign it for them.”

Nobody said anything for a while. Somewhere below the window a truck went past with a load on it.

“That is a reason,” Nina Calder said, and wrote for about a minute, and then looked up. “It goes in as written, all three parts, and I am going to read it back and you are going to listen to it, and then it goes on the public board on Monday morning with the time on it, because that is what happened to your registrar’s answer and I am not doing different for this one.”

“Read it.”

She read it back. It was accurate and it was uglier than his version and it kept all three parts in the order he had said them.

“Then I will take the department’s answer,” said Jonas. “I decline the blanket continuity waiver. My reasons are in the minute. I am not saying the contract is a bad thing, I am saying I am not the instrument that makes it lawful, and I would like that distinction kept in the minute as well, because in three months somebody is going to need a name to put on the refusal and it is not going to be mine.”

Royden Achebe stood up. He did the thing he did, which was to be fair about a thing he had wanted, and he did it without a scrap of theatre.

“Then here it is, and I would rather you had it in this room than read it on Monday. Your review of the review was concluded at ten past nine this morning. Your civic practice is sound. The failures to produce were not yours. Everything I have to say after that is bad, so I am going to say it quickly. Grade three, probationary, no appointment, reviewable at the end of the season. The department is not holding it against you. The department is holding it against you because the post the contract needs is the post the waiver makes, and I am not going to let a thirty-two-year-old carry a contract in his first year of service.”

“Say the rest.”

“The claim on that gate is yours now, in writing, in the file, by the default, because there is nobody nearer.” Achebe put his hands flat on the table. “I have read it back three times to make sure it says what I think it says, and it does, and I am not able to make it say anything else, and I am sorry about that more than I am about the appointment, because the appointment you can get back in a year and that one follows you about for as long as the plate stands.”

“And the money.”

“CiviCore’s participation fund withdrew at ten past ten this morning,” said Vaughan. “Two crews come off the Saint Orra queue on Monday, which puts the Tarn Street pump and eleven other city jobs back to a median of nineteen days, and Mr Mercer, I am not telling you that as a threat, I am telling you because you were in the room when the number was read out and it would be dishonest of me to leave it out of my own part.” She closed the folder. “The retainer and the arrears are not ours to pay and will not be offered by us again. I am sorry. I did think I could do that one.”

At ten past ten they let him out.

He went down to the ground floor and telephoned Margo Doré from the step.

“Saturday,” she said. “What do you want.”

“The trap has packed again,” she said, before he had said anything. “It packed on Thursday night while it was shut, and the lowland main has been filling all week because nobody has been down there drawing off it, and the boil is back on the inner face and I have been up and down that path with a spanner and a piece of slate since Friday. Four more tonnes of stone and I have got about ninety pounds, and I am telling you because you wrote the sentence and because there is nobody else to tell.”

“I am sorry.”

“Do not be sorry at me,” said Margo. “Be sorry on Friday the twenty-eighth, when the term went, and on the twenty-first, when it finished early, and on the fourteenth, when you wrote six words.” She put the telephone down on her end, which she had never done to him before, and then picked it up again. “The rota is not going to read anything out loud because you asked it to. Do you understand what I have just told you? Not because it is refusing you. Because there is nothing to read. Nobody is a holder of anything. Delphine wrote a time on the board in pencil on Thursday and that is the entire civic apparatus on that street at present, and it is a pencil.”

“Yes,” said Jonas.

“You have come back from a room and you have said no to something,” said Margo, “and I have no idea what and I am not asking, and I want you to hear the sentence I am about to say, because I am going to say it once and I am going to go and buy four tonnes of stone.

“Pia has been at my elbow all week asking whether the city was going to do anything, and on Saturday morning she told me that if anybody comes up that path with a good offer for the water, she will not be the one who tells them there is a rota. She said: *the moment anybody does anything for us, we are a client, and I have asked a lawyer what a client is and she says it is somebody who can be served.*”

At ten to ten that night, in the middle of the Saint Orra west corridor, in front of a boy of twenty-six and a woman of some years with a board, Jonas Mercer read five lines out loud in the middle of the corridor, where the person on the trolley could hear him, and then stepped back.

Ruth Nwosu read them back to him word for word, and then said, “New expiry. Six o’clock on the fourth. That is five days, not six, because you read it a day early and I am not going to pretend I did not.” She signed the box. Then she did not move away from the card table.

“I heard you refused something on the radio,” she said. “Tomas’s boy had it on in the workshop and he turned it up, and he did not have to.”

“It goes on the board on Monday.”

“Then the board will say it in the same flat print it says everything else,” said Ruth Nwosu, “and there will be no name on the water, and I will read this to Ezra on Wednesday night, and on Wednesday night somebody in this building is going to ask me why I read the same five lines to a man every week, and I am going to have to give an answer that is about a door.” She put the black key in her pocket. “I do not know what the answer is. I have told you that before and I have not stopped telling you, and I would like it recorded that I am still telling you, at sixty-eight, in a corridor, at ten at night, to a man who has just refused something for reasons he could not write on a form.”
