# Chapter 0006 — The Registrar’s Rule

The sun had not cleared the canal warehouses, and the removal window would not open for another thirteen minutes, which made the order look less like a precaution and more like a countdown already running.

Jonas arrived at the old-canal warehouse at 5:17 with a department van and two folding carts. Rain had left the loading yard glossy. The shelter’s south wall bulged outward by enough to make the top course of brick sit unevenly, and a damp line climbed the inside wall beneath the loading doors. A municipal inspector had marked the wall with three red bands. No one needed the Lattice to see that the building was unsafe.

Oren stood beside the closure notice with a box of numbered property seals. “You are early for a technician who has no authority over this inspection.”

“I am not taking the inspection.”

“You were circling the building last night.”

“Reading a notice is not accepting a duty.”

“Most people find a way to turn it into one.”

He handed Jonas the removal sheet. The public-safety clause allowed residents to leave between five-thirty and eight. It named the building, the hazard, the receiving shelter, and the cost of a second night in temporary accommodation. It did not require the city to provide a vehicle for every possession, and it did not say what happened to a resident whose ordinary address had been absent from the register.

Selah opened the staff entrance at 5:24. She had a canvas satchel over one shoulder and a ring of keys in her hand. Behind her, people moved through the shelter in the careful quiet of a room where every object had an owner and no one could prove it to the city.

“Bellow Market shelter is accepting us,” she said. “Not because the order is kind. Because its steward found room after I called.”

“Named custodian?”

“Mara Holt, night steward. I have her direct line.”

Jonas wrote it down. “Tell residents they can take what they can carry. We can move the medical cart and the sealed care boxes first.”

“We are not your equipment.”

“No. You are the people deciding what moves.”

That answer earned a brief look from Selah, not trust, but enough room for the next instruction.

The removal took nearly two hours. The first residents left with plastic bags, blanket rolls, and the kinds of small necessities that accumulated around a bed: a comb, a photograph, a charger, a child’s shoe. Others carried cardboard boxes tied with kitchen string. One man insisted on taking a broken radio that had not worked for six years. A woman carried a ladder too long for the van until Jonas folded the rear seats and they wedged it diagonally across the luggage.

He learned names as people introduced themselves, then stopped asking. Names belonged to residents, not to his work sheet. When a dispatch clerk asked for a headcount, he answered with the number of seats in the vans and the receiving shelter’s confirmation, not with a list of who was inside.

Oren walked the yard with a clipboard and checked each seal. He did not hurry anyone. He also did not soften the order. At 6:40, a section of the loading canopy gave way with a crack that made everyone look up. A sheet of corrugated roof landed in the rain. No one was beneath it, but the sound changed the pace of the room.

A boy near the carts started crying. Selah crouched beside him, but the mother was already moving toward the shelter. The boy’s father wanted to carry the broken roof piece away as evidence. Oren told him the piece belonged with the building and would be recorded in the inspection.

“Record it,” the father said. “It almost came down on us.”

“It will be in the report,” Oren said.

The words were true, which did not make them comforting. Jonas saw Oren glance toward the receiving van, then at the order on his clipboard. The man was frightened in a practical way, surrounded by people who had no address and a building that might have injured them. His fear had produced a rule. The rule had consequences he could not carry alone.

Jonas had a maintenance credential that could let him inspect the door plate, and for one bright instant he imagined taking responsibility for the relocation himself. He could write his name on the form, promise the building would be checked, carry every question the residents had. The temptation was not a civic power. It was the old instinct to become the only person who could be blamed.

Selah appeared at his elbow. “If you are going to help, help with the cart.”

“I can inspect the wall.”

“Someone with the proper appointment is inspecting the wall.”

“I know.”

“Then move the cart.”

At 7:12, the final resident crossed the threshold. Rafi was helping Alma Reyes into the Bellow Market shelter’s intake room, the medication bag held against his chest. Mina Okafor had come with the clinic transfer team. She checked the room assignment, the medication list, and the next care shift before Selah signed the handoff.

Mara Holt, the Bellow Market night steward, was a broad-shouldered woman with a ring of colored keys and a voice that carried over the shelter noise. Selah would keep the old-canal residents’ first-night handoff log under Mara’s ordinary watch. Mara counted boxes by category, not by resident name.

“Linens,” she said. “Two cots. One broken radio. Four personal bags. One ladder that does not belong in this room.”

“It belongs to the resident who carried it,” Selah said.

“Then it is one ladder, not a municipal fixture.”

The receiving shelter logged each item with its source and promised a return-of-possession route when the old building reopened. Selah kept the old keys. She placed them in a labeled envelope, signed the receipt, and gave the receiving steward one copy. She did not ask the city to hold the keys for her.

Jonas helped load the last cart and then stood apart while Oren sealed the door. The access plate went dark. The closure route remained valid for the building, but it did not take anyone’s claim with it.

“You could have kept the old service open,” Oren said.

“No. I can’t make an unsafe building safe by wanting it to be.”

“Then why are you still here?”

“Because the people who left are still waiting for the city to tell them where they live.”

Oren looked at the red band on the wall. “The city told them they have a receiving shelter for tonight.”

“They have a receiving shelter. They do not have a place in the morning.”

“That is a different order.”

“It is a real cost.”

Oren’s mouth tightened. He wanted to argue that the cost was not his fault, and Jonas could see the wish for absolution move through him. Instead the registrar pulled a second sheet from beneath the closure notice.

“Water inspection at noon. The old pump house and culvert are connected to the building’s supply. Public Works will test pressure and the physical line. The pump stays closed until both are documented.”

“I am not the safety inspector.”

“I know. I will be there as registrar.”

“Do you want me to inspect the plate?”

“Yes.”

Jonas hesitated. The request was narrow. He could examine the access plate without taking responsibility for the building’s load, the water pressure, or the order. It would give him a reason to be present and a chance to understand why the old shelter’s service chain had been kept out of the index.

“Attended inspection only,” he said. “I document the plate. I do not clear the water.”

“Agreed.”

Oren extended his hand. Jonas looked at it, then took it. The agreement was a tiny professional contract, and he wrote it down before they reached the vans.

Tomas Renn arrived with the union’s covered transport as the last vehicle pulled away. His shop coat was buttoned to the throat despite the rain, and he carried a clipboard of his own.

“You called for a van,” Tomas said.

“It needed one.”

“You also promised a person the city would be ready with a replacement.”

“I asked for a receiving shelter.”

“You are the city.”

Jonas looked at the residents entering the Bellow Market building. A woman was explaining to a child how to find the washroom. A man carried the broken radio under one arm. The old keys were in Selah’s envelope, not in a municipal record.

“I can’t promise readiness,” Jonas said. “I can keep the handoff honest.”

Tomas held out the union form. “Then write this. Any continuation of shelter work, medical transfer, or access repair is carried by a named institution or witness. No technician signs a blank guarantee because he feels responsible. No undocumented route goes live without a person who can say what happens when it fails.”

Jonas read the form. It was longer than the promise he had wanted to make and less satisfying than a personal oath.

“Does this come from the department?”

“It comes from people who have watched technicians become convenient.”

Jonas signed the transport record, not the blank guarantee. Selah’s receiving shelter, Mina’s clinical duty, and Mara’s night watch were named. The route could continue only while those people chose to continue it.

At 8:03, Oren closed the old-canal shelter. He placed a strip across the door and wrote the time on it. The closure was valid. The building was dangerous. The residents were elsewhere. None of those facts canceled the others.

Jonas kept the noon inspection appointment in his pad and the unresolved service conflict in his department report. As he walked away, the old plate stayed dark, but the silver residue around its lower screw remained visible under the rain.

The pump was the next door.
