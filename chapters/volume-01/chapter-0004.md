# Chapter 0004 — The Unlisted Stop

The shuttle was empty when it reached Saint Orra platform, except for the people who had hidden from the camera behind its emergency curtain.

A small hand appeared at the rear window. It held a brass route token marked with three lines of blue tape.

“That is not a transit token,” Kofi Sarr, the platform dispatcher, said. He had one hand on the vehicle impound switch and the other raised toward the driver. “That is somebody’s lunch card.”

The shuttle stopped half a meter short of the platform curb. Its destination panel remained dark. There was no route number on the side and no municipal plate.

Jonas stood well away from the lane. “Before you impound it, separate the vehicle from the platform.”

“We do not separate unverified vehicles from platforms.”

“That is an excellent principle until there is a sick person behind your curtain.”

Kofi looked at him. “You are the bridge technician.”

“Which is why I know it did not use any route we just worked on. Check your arrival feed. This vehicle came from Saint Orra service lane seven, not the pedestrian bridge. It is a separate problem.”

Kofi’s radio clicked before he answered. A route clerk confirmed that no vehicle matching the green shuttle had been assigned to the stop. Its lane seven feed showed a scheduled medical transfer with a local service code, but the code had no address and no operator record in the transit authority’s ordinary index.

Jonas felt the first hint of the silver Margin along the shuttle’s rear access plate. It was not a fault like the bridge. Several dim terms overlapped around the vehicle: the driver, the clinic, a child’s hand-marked token carrying a local duty, and a route that held many small promises without offering one obvious missing place. The view was incomplete in a different way. It showed relationships, not who had made them or why.

He looked away before the details could swim.

Kofi opened the emergency curtain. “Route contact, declare the number of passengers and the destination.”

A young woman leaned into the opening. Her courier bag was slung across one shoulder, and rain darkened the hem of her jacket. She was nineteen or twenty, with cycling gloves tucked through a belt loop and a paper route sheet folded under one arm.

“Three passengers,” she said. “One child, one courier, one patient returning from Saint Orra. We are not carrying freight and we do not have your route number.”

“Name.”

“I am not giving you a passenger list for a vehicle that is safe and on time.”

Kofi’s hand returned to the impound switch. “You are at a public platform.”

“I am at a public platform that is not on our map,” Pia Santos said. “That is not the same as being nowhere.”

The driver was an older man in a soaked work vest. He leaned forward, one hand braced on the seat rail. The child was a girl, perhaps seven, with a black wool hat and a brass route token around her neck. She had drawn the three blue lines over so often that the brass had worn pale. When Kofi pointed at the shuttle, she put the token inside her coat.

A stretcher lay across the rear seat. Its monitor made a soft, fast clicking sound.

Jonas’s irritation with the argument vanished. “Kofi, stop reaching for the switch.”

“The vehicle has no operator record.”

“The person is short of breath.”

“Patient’s care route is local and incomplete.”

“Which means the doctor decides whether the patient goes through a door while you decide what kind of vehicle it is. Those are different jobs.”

Pia looked at him for the first time. Her gaze moved over his municipal jacket, his tool roll, and the bridge seal still stuck to his sleeve. “You are the one who opened the street for the ambulance.”

“Jonas Mercer. NDC.”

“I know what an NDC technician is. I did not say I trusted you.”

“Good. I do not trust an unregistered vehicle either.”

“Can you trust the person in it?”

Jonas looked at the child, the older driver, and the monitor. “I can work with the answer you give me. That is a better beginning.”

Pia held up the folded route sheet but did not offer it. “Alma Reyes has a return-of-care appointment at the old-canal shelter. Her clinic record is valid. Her return route is not in your index because the shelter is not in your index. That is not a vehicle offense.”

The monitor clicked faster. The older woman beneath the oxygen blanket stirred and tried to sit up. Pia placed a hand on her shoulder.

“Stay down,” she said. “You promised me you would not make the doctors angry by pretending to be fine.”

“I am fine,” the woman rasped.

“The machine disagrees,” Kofi said. He had already lifted the clinic channel on his radio. “Saint Orra west clinic, I have an unregistered medical shuttle at platform four. Patient is deteriorating.”

Mina Okafor answered. The shuttle was outside the clinic’s official pickup zone, but its service chain touched the same plate that had accepted Alma Reyes that morning. The clinic could receive her, but the vehicle needed an attended escort to the west entrance. Its route could not be opened as a public transit permission.

Jonas checked the platform clock. The bridge test had closed, and the south curb was filling with people waiting for buses. The shuttle had already been stationary for too long.

“Pia,” he said, “can your driver hold it here while the clinic takes the patient?”

“Yes.”

“Will the driver release the vehicle to your custody after the patient leaves?”

“Yes, if you do not hold the vehicle.”

“Kofi,” Jonas said, “mark this an attended medical escort, not an impound. Keep the public side of the lane clear. Do not take a passenger list.”

“I am required to take a passenger list for an unregistered service vehicle.”

“Then make a list of the vehicle’s failure to have a route, and let the clinic clerk witness the medical handoff. Send the rest to the local service steward who owns the record.”

Pia’s eyes narrowed. “You do not know who that is.”

“Exactly. So you name the custodian and I do not make one up.”

For a moment, she considered him. Then she opened the route sheet. It showed stops, times, and a narrow route through old service lanes. No civic district, no population count, no list of residents. At the bottom, a hand-drawn line ended at an old canal shelter.

“We have a local steward,” Pia said. “Selah Mora. She keeps the return-of-care log. She will not let you take it.”

“Fine.”

“She will not let anyone in the city take it.”

“Also fine.”

Pia wrote **Selah Mora—local custody** on the back of the sheet and gave it to the older driver. He tucked it into the dashboard without handing over the sheet.

Jonas checked the municipal order in his jacket. It still suspended his independent gate use, and the wording did not tell him whether an attended escort counted. He would not make the request himself. He could inspect the physical path, but the clinic’s care claim and attendant procedure would have to open the route.

Jonas kept his eyes on the physical access plate. The silver line tightened around the route, but he could not tell whether the missing record was a deliberate protection, an old failure, or both. He put two fingers on the shuttle’s hinge and asked Kofi to bring the clinic’s attendant procedure to the vehicle.

The attendant procedure did not open the shuttle to the crowd. It opened a narrow path from the vehicle to the west clinic entrance, with the driver, Pia, Mina, and the patient’s care claim as witnesses. Jonas walked the path, testing the platform lip and the service threshold. The child left the vehicle first and stood inside the clinic’s marked family area while Pia stayed with the driver and the patient. A woman arrived from the shelter, breathless and apologetic, and took the child without giving her name to the dispatcher. The child held out the brass token.

“This is mine,” the woman said.

“Course it is,” the child replied.

The older patient came through last. The route closed behind the stretcher, and the shuttle stayed where it was.

Mina signed the disposition: **Alma Reyes—admitted to west clinic for reassessment; local return-of-care obligation pending; no public-route record created.**

Kofi watched her stamp the form. “You are giving a medical permission to a service operator with no address.”

“I am recording a person who came through my door,” Mina said. “Her return route is their problem until she is well enough to ask for it.”

Jonas inspected the shuttle and its driver’s emergency equipment. The vehicle itself was sound, although the rear step was worn and the destination panel had failed. He signed a safety check, not a route approval.

The driver took the custody note from the dashboard. “If Selah says no, we stop.”

“If Selah says no, the route stops,” Jonas said. “I will not use an ambulance’s permission to make her answer yes.”

The driver nodded. Pia watched him sign the check, not because his signature made the shuttle official, but because he had not tried to turn a safe return into ownership of the route.

At 8:41, the clinic confirmed that Alma Reyes was stable enough for a return of care. Mina arranged an escort to the old-canal shelter. The shelter’s service plate accepted the care handoff, but the door did not open. Instead, a small green light appeared beside the latch, and a voice came from the speaker asking who would be responsible after the patient crossed the threshold.

Jonas saw no name in the request. He saw the person waiting on the other side of the door.
