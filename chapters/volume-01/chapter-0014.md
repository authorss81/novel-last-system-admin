# Chapter 0014 — The Useful Mistake

By half past eleven on the second day the door had become a fact of the building’s morning, and by one o’clock Jonas Mercer had begun to enjoy it in a way he would later find difficult to defend.

The laundry service started using the public entrance again. The supply run came through at ten past nine and again at quarter to ten, and the porter who brought it signed for the cage and said, without looking up, “You fixed the door.” Four people said some version of that to him before lunch: a crossing guard, a driver, a man from the hospital stores office with a clipboard, and a cleaner who wanted to know, quite reasonably, whether she could now use the service lift at any hour.

He said yes to the lift because the schedule did not say no. He checked, twice, and wrote the answer in the day book where the next person would find it.

Sanaa came down the ramp at half past twelve with a folder under her arm and stopped at the bottom step, and looked at the door, and then at him, and did not come up.

“You have done something very clever,” she said. “You have made a gate that no longer needs anybody. That is what all of this has been, isn’t it. Not the term. The fact that you no longer have to ask Ruth to be there.”

“I have made a gate that Ruth can open.”

“You have made yourself the answer to every hour that Ruth is not in the building, and you have not asked her, and she has not noticed yet because she is too busy being grateful.” Sanaa shifted the folder. “I am not going to argue about it in a corridor. I am going to say one sentence and then I am going back in. You have made yourself the last available answer again, and this time you have written it into a plate, and plates do not get tired.”

Then she went back up the ramp, and Jonas stood there for a while with a screwdriver in his left hand and no answer.

By one o’clock he was at the bench in River Stacks with the schedule out of his coat pocket and the right hand in a sling that he had stopped pretending was temporary, and Bea Osunde was coiling cable two meters away and watching him not read.

“You have had that page in front of you for nine minutes,” Bea said.

“Reading it.”

“You have read it four times. I can hear the difference.” She put the coil down. “What is on it that is worse than what you wrote?”

Jonas turned the sheet so she could see it. He had been hoping she would take it out of his hands, and she would not, and that was the correct behavior.

> **11:00–07:00 — HOLDER NOT NAMED**
> **07:00–23:00 — HOLDER NOT NAMED**
> **IF NO HOLDER IS ON DUTY: the route is held by the issuer.**
> **ISSUER FALLBACK — MERCER, J.**
> **AVAILABILITY: acknowledged per shift.**

Bea read it twice as well, which was more than he had expected and less than he had hoped for.

“Say the fourth line in the words you would use at four in the morning,” she said, and he did, and she nodded. “So the door is held by you.”

“Yes.”

“At eleven o’clock this morning, who is holding the door?”

“Nobody, according to that page. Mina is on the corridor, but the term names a watch or a care worker as holder and the day has no name in it.”

“So a porter pushes a bed up that ramp at four in the afternoon, and the plate looks at your signature and lets him through, and the record says a person was on the corridor. Except there isn’t one.” Bea picked her coil back up. “That is not a repair, Jonas. That is a rumor that works.”

He put both hands on the bench. His right hand was still dead to the second knuckle, and he had noticed that the sensation had stopped being frightening somewhere around the second hour, which frightened him more.

“I can cover it,” he said.

“You can cover it for how long?”

“Seventy-two hours. I can acknowledge every shift for three days and I can be here for the ones nobody else takes, and by Friday somebody at the shop will say that this cannot go on, and by Friday it will not have gone on, and nobody will be worse off than they are now.”

Bea stopped coiling. “That is not coverage. That is a man saying that for three more days the answer to a staffing problem is himself.” She looked at him steadily. “And then what?”

“And then somebody takes it.”

“Nobody has offered to take it. You have not asked anybody. You have been awake for over thirty hours and you have decided to stand there instead of asking, because asking is slower and standing there is the version where you are the answer.” She turned her coil over. “I have watched four technicians do that. Two of them are still doing it. One of them left the department. The other one got a very large award and a crew that could not run anything without him and a nervous breakdown in a hotel conference room in Gantry Ward. That is the whole career, and it always looks like dedication for about eighteen months.”

Tomas came in through the front door at ten past two with a folder under his arm and the expression he had been improving on for twenty years.

“The department has offered a solution,” he said, and put the folder on the bench, and it did not appear to please him. “Not a good one. But a solution.”

Nina Calder had sent it up from records. It was a single sheet headed **SERVICE CONTINUITY RIDER — TEMPORARY MAINTENANCE CLASSIFICATION**, and it would have permitted the department to reclassify the west clinic care entrance as equipment maintenance under an existing service, on the grounds that the door had failed, and would have paid for a standby technician out of the regional continuity budget.

“The effect,” Tomas said, “is that this afternoon’s problem becomes an equipment problem, and an equipment problem gets a technician, and a technician is a thing you can buy. Your term and your name come off it.”

“Nobody has to say a technician is me.”

“Nobody has to say a technician is you. That is what makes it good.” Tomas laid his hand flat on the folder. “Nobody will be responsible, and therefore nobody can be found out, and therefore a twenty-six-year-old porter will not be standing on an empty corridor at four in the morning explaining why a man she has never met is the door.”

“If I sign that, the record says this happened for equipment reasons.”

“The record says the record says.” Tomas pulled his hand back. “I am a shop steward, not a lawyer, and I will tell you what I know about this kind of form because I have watched it work. It does not delete anything. It relocates the blame to a category, and then everybody below the category gets to sleep. The department will pay for a technician tomorrow. The technician will be somebody young and temporary and they will hold a door for a term they did not write for an issuer they will never meet, and in eight months when that term expires, the record will say *equipment*. Not a name. Not a claim. Equipment.”

Jonas read the rider twice. It was a beautiful piece of paperwork. It was unsigned, and it was not a lie, exactly; it was a description of the world in which nobody was responsible.

“I am not signing it,” he said.

“You are not signing it,” Tomas agreed. “But I want it in the file that you were offered it and that you understood the price, because in nine days somebody in an office is going to tell you that the only alternative to a real answer was a bad form, and I would like your word on paper before that conversation happens.”

“Nina can record it.”

“I will copy her.” Tomas picked the folder back up. “Now the other thing. I spoke to six people today about the day window, and I am going to tell you exactly what I said and exactly what they said, because I think the problem is not that nobody will help you. The problem is that nobody can help you and still be honest.”

He listed them on his fingers. A west-side porter who did two jobs already and had not been trained on a civic term. Two care assistants who said the same thing in different words, which was that they would not hold a permission they could not describe, and could not describe one they had not read, and would not read one on a shift. Mina Okafor, who said she would hold the clerk’s window if somebody with authority named her and the term said what she was allowed to refuse, and who wanted that naming to come from the clinic and not from the department. A man from the hospital stores office who asked, quite reasonably, whether a stores delivery counted as urgent care. And Ruth Nwosu, who was not in the list, because Ruth had said no before he finished the sentence, and had added, in a voice he did not care to repeat in the shop, that if the day window were a real job then it should be on a payroll with a name on it, and not a favor extracted from a person who was already on a corridor.

“So the answer is no,” Jonas said.

“The answer is: not by asking, not this month.” Tomas closed the folder. “You want a term that lets four people hold one door honestly. That requires training, a payroll line, and a person with authority to name a successor. You do not have any of the three, and I cannot conjure a payroll line out of a shop steward’s pocket, and if I could, it would be a favor, and favors are the thing we are trying to get rid of.”

At twenty past three his handset went.

It was Nina, and she was reading, which she did not do and which meant she was copying something verbatim.

“The Bellow Market clinic slot,” she said. “Twenty past four. Their own run, the one that shares the dispatch block with the shelter vehicle.”

“Read me the route.”

“City side, south span.” She let that sit for exactly one second, which was Nina Calder being generous. “The same run as the shelter line on the test day. It is not on the taxi line, because Selah’s claim has never covered the taxi line and it is not going to start now because you are in a mood.”

Jonas went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

He had the department’s transport line on the bench in front of him. It was a clinic vehicle, Saint Orra to Bellow Market and back, and it existed because Saint Orra owned it and Saint Orra used it. Moving it did not need a clearance; it needed a dispatcher, and it needed a risk note with a technician’s name at the bottom of it, because every route change on that desk was recorded against the person who asked for it. That was the one thing a grade III could always sign. It would cost the department a logged amount of money and about thirty-eight minutes of vehicle time.

“If I move it,” he said, “the cost lands on the department.”

“Yes.”

“And the alternative is that it crosses the south span again and gets paid for by people who have no water.”

“Yes.”

“The side service route has been isolated since the test under the registrar’s order,” Nina said. “It is still isolated. A vehicle on that line stops at the gate and then costs thirty-eight minutes, and the order has not been lifted, and I am not the office that lifts it.”

He put the order in. He booked the clinic vehicle onto the alternate street grid and wrote the reason into the field himself rather than letting the terminal pick a category, and the reason was the words *span isolated under safety order*, and the cost came back at forty-one minutes and one logged vehicle hour.

“Nina,” he said, “put that on the conflict record, with the number, in the same place as the shelter line from the test day. Not in a separate file. In the same place.”

“I am putting it in the same place,” Nina said. “It will read as an unnecessary expense.”

“It was not unnecessary. It was the same decision with a different budget.”

He went back up the ramp at a quarter to nine that evening because Ruth Nwosu had sent him one line on the back of a handover slip, which said: *come and look at this before you do anything clever.*

She was standing at the top of the ramp with the black key in her hand and the printed strip in the other one, and she had been there long enough for the corridor lights to come on.

“My hours are wrong,” she said. “Twenty-three hundred to six hundred, on a plate, in my name, since somebody wrote it on there with a pencil before I came here. Six hours of my night, the record says I am not here. Six hours. And every one of those six hours the sixth line says the door is held by you.”

“I know.”

“You do not know it, because it is not a problem until it is a problem.” She held the strip up. “Here is the problem. In a quarter of an hour my shift starts, and the record will say nobody is here. And at six this morning it will start saying I am here, and it will go on saying it until eleven, and I will be standing on this corridor for all five of those hours on the strength of a pencil somebody scratched into a plate. That is the whole of the difference between me and you, and it is written on a piece of paper in my pocket, and I am telling you about it because in nineteen years nobody has ever once asked me to.”

He stood on the ramp and listened to it, and understood that she was not asking him to fix it and would not thank him for trying.

“What do you want me to do?” he said.

“Nothing,” Ruth said. “That is the second time in two days somebody has asked me that in a tone like that. Ask me what I want and then do the thing you have already decided.”

That night, at nine minutes past nine, he acknowledged the night shift on the clinic plate, sitting at his kitchen table in River Stacks with his right hand useless and his left one on the terminal, and it took ninety seconds, and it required nothing of anybody at all. He had done the arithmetic first. Ruth’s window on the record ran from eleven at night to six in the morning, and she was actually on from ten and actually there until eleven in the morning, which left one hour on either end that the plate believed belonged to nobody. He put his name against both of them and the plate wrote **AVAILABILITY: ACKNOWLEDGED** and he sat back and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like rest, that he had in ninety seconds made a person into a rota.

He worked until two. He went back to the ramp at six the next morning and watched the day window open with nobody in it, and the plate let a supply run through at seven because it had decided overnight that somebody was on the premises, and nobody was.

At twenty to three that morning Ezra Mbeki brought a fall-risk patient back from a scan on a chair, alone, because the plate said the door was open and the man on the chair had a call bell and nobody else appeared to be needed.

The patient was Marta Sowle, and she was ninety-one. She was found on the corridor floor at seven minutes to three by a cleaner, and she had been on the floor for eleven minutes, and she had not broken anything and she was extremely angry about it, and her daughter arrived at half past three and made a statement that the ward then had to type.

The safeguarding form went into the record with the words *transported without a second person present* in the box marked *contributing factor*, and in the second box, where the ward wrote what the record itself had contributed, somebody typed: **route held on plate record; holder of record not on site. Holder of record contacted at 03:05 and did not answer until 03:31.**

Ezra stood in the corridor at four in the morning while Sanaa explained what had happened, and then he found Jonas on the ramp and said the only thing anybody said all day that Jonas could not argue with.

“The plate said the night watch was on duty,” Ezra said. “I asked it twice. It said Nwosu, R. I rang the ward house and there was nobody, and she was not answering, and I knew that before I moved him and I moved him anyway, and I am going to have to write that down as well.”

“Write it down,” Jonas said. “All of it. And put my name on the line that says the route was open at twenty to three.”

“You put your name on a shift last night,” Ezra said. “In your kitchen. At nine minutes past nine. And I want to say this next part carefully, because you have been decent to me all week. It did not help me at twenty to three. At twenty to three the plate was not asking anybody. At twenty to three the plate thought it had the night watch, and it was right to think that, and she was in her own bed. You put your name on the two hours at the ends of her night. The middle was never the problem.”

Jonas stood very still on the ramp.

“Write that one down as well,” he said. “And write that I did it from a kitchen two kilometres away on a gate I am suspended from initiating, and put my name against that sentence too.”

By one o’clock on the third day he could not hold a screwdriver in his right hand and could barely hold anything in his left. He had been awake for thirty-two of the previous thirty-four, and the grey line on the inside of his wrist had gone from a mark to a thing he was aware of in his sleep. At twenty past one he was at the bench in River Stacks trying to write a second term, not a civic one — he was going to reseat a ward pressure plate in the east wing, because it was the same family of fault and it would take four minutes and it was the kind of competence that made him feel like he was fixing things instead of postponing them.

The screwdriver went through his hand and across the bench and hit the floor.

He sat down on the paint bucket. The shop went on around him. Bea asked him if he wanted water. He said he wanted a minute.

Tomas took one look at him from the dispatch office doorway and said, “Twenty minutes. Then you go home. I am not asking.”

“Nothing needs me until five.”

“Everything needs you to be able to hold a tool. That is not a request, that is a fact about a person.” Tomas came and stood over him. “You are not permitted to be brave in my shop this afternoon. Sit down, drink the water, go to bed, and acknowledge your shifts from the bed, because that is what the plate wants, and I have watched you realize that it is what the plate wants, and that is exactly when men do permanent damage to themselves for a term with a six-day expiry.”

He was asleep at a quarter past three with his coat on and his handset on the counter and his boots still laced.

At twenty-two minutes past three, on the other side of the river, a hospital transfer crew came up the Saint Orra service lane with a woman of sixty-one in a chair under a blanket, and stopped at a closed door, and pushed, and the door did not open, and the plate on the wall of the ramp said four words in the department’s own language:

**HOLDER NOT NAMED. GATE CLOSED.**

His handset lit up on the counter and went on lighting up, and Jonas Mercer slept through it twice.
