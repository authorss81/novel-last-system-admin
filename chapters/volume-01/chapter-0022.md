# Chapter 0022 — The Registrar's Answer

The records room over the River Stacks shop had a table, six chairs, a wall clock, and a certificate about fire exits that had been correct in 1998, and at five to ten on Friday morning six people were in it and one of them had brought a box file the size of a paving slab.

Royden Achebe was forty-nine and had come up from field services with a folder of his own, and he put it on the table before he sat down, which told Jonas that he had come expecting to win something and had brought the paperwork for it.

Oren Pell had come from the Assembly offices in a coat that was too good for the room and had not been in the room in four days.

Tomas Renn had come off a night and had not sat down.

Nina Calder had the minute book open in front of her and her pen out and had not written anything yet, which Jonas had learned to read as a warning.

Bea Osunde was there because the consequence about cabinet 6-R-14 was going to need a grade II signature and Jonas was grade III and would not be permitted to sign away his own access.

And Jonas Mercer was standing by the door with a stapled, initialled, posted, publicly visible responsibility bond in a plastic sleeve, because Nina Calder had told him to bring it and he had not understood why until he was in the room and saw it lying face up in the middle of the table with the seventh clause on top.

“I am not going to begin,” Royden Achebe said, “by letting the room guess. The registrar’s office asked the department one question. It asked whether two acknowledgements made by an issuer on a suspended gate were continued attended maintenance, or breaches of the review order. The department has an answer. I have three drafts, because I have been in this job nineteen years and I have learned that if you do not write down all three you only get to be wrong once.”

He squared the folder.

“Draft one. Both acts were made in good faith in the interest of patient care. The department accepts responsibility. No individual action was required.”

Nobody said anything.

“Draft two. Both were breaches of the review order. The individual is suspended from plate work for twelve months and the department considers the term itself unsafe.”

“Draft three,” Achebe said, “is the split. The 21:09 was a breach. The 15:58 was an emergency opening under a standing reduction and is ratified. The department accepts the claim for that delay. And there are consequences.”

“Draft one costs the city nothing,” Nina said.

“Draft one costs the city nothing,” Achebe agreed, quite pleasantly, “which is the entire reason I wrote it, and I am not ashamed of that, and I would like it minuted that I said so.”

Nina wrote it down. She had not written anything in the first four minutes and now she had written a sentence with a name in it, and Jonas understood that this was the entire difference between a record and a piece of paper, and that she had made it in the first four minutes on purpose.

“Why draft one is unavailable,” said Oren Pell, who had not spoken until then. “If the department accepts responsibility and no individual action was required, then the gate at Saint Orra west has an issuer in the record, and the issuer carries the claim, and the department has accepted the claim for one night’s delay. There is no such thing. It is not available to us, because the day the department is the answerer for a plate term, the plate has no holder and no issuer and no claim, and it becomes a building with a light on it.” He turned his pen over. “I do not want that. If that gate fails in February, I am going to be asked who owns it, and the answer on the file will be a department, and a department cannot be disciplined and cannot be called at three in the morning.”

“You would rather it said Mercer.”

“I would rather it said a person with a name, a claim, and an expiry,” Oren said. “I have read the old-canal file this week, because I have to, and I closed that building in 1998 — no, in the year before, and I was right, and I have never once thought about it otherwise. There was a woman in that building who told a department man to go away with a tone I still hear. And she was right too, and both of those were true, and the reason I have to answer the question about February is that on a street with no address and no asset owner, the answer is going to have to be a person, and I am telling you now that I intend to make it one, and that I would rather it were one on Thursday than in March.”

Nina Calder’s pen had not stopped.

“Minuted,” she said. “Verbatim, at 10:06, including *I would rather it were one on Thursday than in March*, which I have never written in a minute book before and which I expect somebody upstairs to object to.”

“Object all you like,” said Oren.

Royden Achebe looked at the folder for a moment, and then closed it.

“Then draft one is out,” he said. “I withdraw it.”

“Name it,” Nina said.

“I withdraw it.”

“Name who withdraws it, Mr Achebe, and the time, because in about four months somebody is going to want to know whether the department considered accepting the claim and decided not to, and the difference between that and never considering it is the whole of this job.”

Royden Achebe looked at her for slightly too long.

“Royden Achebe withdraws draft one at 10:09,” he said, “because a category cannot be the holder of a plate, and because if the department is the answerer then the register has an owner and a name, and a name is the only thing in the city that can be told no.”

Nina wrote it down, and then she put her pen flat on the page.

“Now I am going to say my part, and then nobody ask me to say it again,” she said. “I will minute. I will file. I will read any version aloud to the room before it goes on the file, and if I am handed a draft I will read it aloud anyway, because a refusal that is not read into a record is a preference. I have been handed this draft,” she said, without looking up, “by the field services manager at 08:40 this morning, by hand, and I have read it out, and it is in the minute, and I am not the person who decides which answer it is. I am the person who signs that this was the answer. Those are two different jobs and I have watched people in this building confuse them for nineteen years, and it is how the shop ends up managing a technician instead of a file.”

Nobody contradicted her, and Jonas understood that she had done it in front of the union shop steward on purpose.

Tomas Renn had said nothing at all for nineteen minutes, which for him was a performance.

“Public,” he said now. “The answer goes on the public line and it goes on the Old Ward board today, in full, with the findings and the consequences, and the clinical record goes with it, and if any of it is not going on the board I want it written down which part and why, in the minute, with a name.”

“Everything goes on the board,” said Oren.

“Then the consequence about the man’s hands comes off a private letter and goes on a wall.”

“There is no private letter,” Oren said. “There is a file. The file is the department’s and the board is the public’s, and on this one they are the same document, which is the only reason I am here instead of writing you a letter, because I have read that board every morning for a week and there is a released cardiac slot on it and a door nobody was standing behind, and I am the man who closed a building on this city, and I would like the city to be able to read what happened to a man who opened one.”

That landed, and Jonas felt it land, and did not enjoy it.

Sanaa Iqbal came in at twenty past eleven with a folder and did not say good morning.

She put the folder on the table in front of Nina Calder and did not take her coat off.

“Four things,” she said. “One: the safeguarding form for the 02:40 event, including the ward’s own contributing factor, which is the sentence *route held on plate record, holder of record not on site*. Two: the gate log, complete. Three: the conflict record with the released cardiac slot and the next available date. Four: the entry from 12:05 and 12:50 in the published gap, which is the refusal of a non-urgent dressing claim and the admission under a written clinical declaration, and both lines, and the forty-five minutes, because that is the price of the gap and the gap is on the board and nobody has ever put the price next to it.”

“Thank you,” Nina said, and meant it.

“I am not doing it as a favor.” Sanaa looked at Jonas, and it was not an accidental look. “I am putting my floor in your file because it is where my floor belongs. The day it stops being in your file is the day somebody has started managing me, and I have watched it happen twice in eleven years and I am not doing it a third time.”

“And will you sign the answer,” said Nina.

“I will sign the annex list,” Sanaa said, “because I wrote it and I know what is in it. I will not sign a statement that the gate is safe, I have already refused that once and it is on the wall where I asked for it to be. And I will not sign anything that says the record is complete, because it is not complete; it has two things in it that nobody has yet put in writing and I have been told about both of them this week and neither of them is mine to publish.”

“Which two?” said Oren.

“The board line the issuer wrote about me on the wall, which is a promise of availability, and the other thing is a document I have been told exists and have not read.” She said it flatly and she did not elaborate, and Jonas understood that she meant Bea’s fourteen-day rota, and that she had understood within a day of it being written, and that she had not gone to the shop about it, and that she had told Nina Calder instead, which was a thing he would have to think about later and did not think about now.

Nina Calder read the whole answer aloud, in a flat voice, in a room with a bad clock, and the room listened to it properly, which Jonas had not expected and which was the first thing since Tuesday that had gone the way it was supposed to.

> **RESPONSE OF THE NDC TO THE REGISTRAR’S OFFICE — SERIES 6-41, SAINT ORRA WEST CLINIC CARE ENTRANCE**
> **Question: were the two solo shift acknowledgements of the sixth and seventh days continued attended maintenance, or breaches of the review order of the second day?**
> **Finding 1.** The acknowledgement timed 21:09 was a breach of the review order. It was made without a named bearer initiating a route. It served no patient and it opened nothing.
> **Finding 2.** The acknowledgement timed 15:58 was an initiation of gate use by a suspended technician. The department finds that it was an emergency opening made under the standing reduction, and ratifies it. The department, and not the individual, accepts the claim arising from the delay to that patient.
> **Consequence 1.** The individual remains issuer of the 6-41 term and remains on the issuer line. The maintenance claim attaching to 6-41 is not discharged and is not transferred, because a department cannot be a holder of a plate term and because the bearers of 6-41 are hospital staff.
> **Consequence 2.** The individual is removed from all gate-initiation duties at 6-41 and from cabinet 6-R-14, effective on the filing of this response, until a review of the review is concluded.
> **Consequence 3.** The maintenance history of nodes 6-L/14 and 6-L/19 in the series 6 chain cannot be produced. A physical inspection of both nodes before the review date is proposed, at the issuer’s cost of attendance.
> **Consequence 4.** The individual’s permanent appointment review is deferred to the review of the review and shall not be concluded before it.
> **Annexes, public:** the gate log; the safeguarding form of the 02:40 event; the conflict record; the released cardiac slot and next available date; the 12:05 refusal and 12:50 declaration in the published gap.

Royden Achebe signed at eleven forty-one. Nina Calder filed it at eleven forty-four and, as she promised, read the filing note aloud first, and the filing note said that a version accepting the claim had been withdrawn, and by whom, and at what time, and Nina read those four words out loud twice.

Then it stopped being a room with people in it and became a public document, and Jonas went down the stairs and out into the yard with the rain coming in off the parapet, and Royden Achebe came after him with his coat over his arm.

“For what it is worth,” Achebe said, “I wrote draft one because it was the answer that would have let me sleep, and I withdrew it because a registrar who does not get an answer will get one from somebody, and I would rather it came from us.”

“You could have said that at ten o’clock.”

“I could,” said Achebe, “and you have a habit of waiting to be asked which is either very good manners or a very expensive one, and I have not worked out which, and I am about to find out, because consequence two means you cannot open the cabinet and consequence three means somebody has to go and look at those two nodes, and there is only one of us who knows where they are.”

Oren Pell was in the doorway with his coat on and his hat in his hand.

“Mr Mercer,” he said. “There is a third thing in consequence two that the answer does not say out loud and I am going to say out loud, because I would rather you heard it from me than worked it out on Tuesday when it matters.”

“Go on.”

“You have been going down that lowland street four times as a man with nothing to declare, and that was genuine, and I have no difficulty with it.” Oren turned the hat over. “Consequence three is a departmental inspection of two nodes in a chain, at the issuer’s cost of attendance, carried out by an officer of this office before a review date. An officer of this office requires a location. And a location, in a departmental record, becomes an address, because a form has a box and the box says *location of works* and the officer will have to put something in it, and whatever goes in it will be the only line in this city’s records that says where that street is.”

He put the hat on.

“I know what that sounds like coming from me. I have thought about it. I have thought about it for four days, which is longer than it took me to close the old canal.” He nodded once, at Jonas, and it was not unkind. “You may tell them, or I shall, and it is better from you, and if you tell them badly I will correct you in writing, and the correction will be on the board as well.”

He went down the stairs.

Jonas went back up them instead, because consequence three had a box on it.

The form was on Nina Calder’s desk and it had been there since about a quarter past twelve, face down, with a Post-it on it in her writing: **SERIES 6, 6-L/14 AND 6-L/19. STATE CIVIC FUNCTION OF EACH NODE IN CHAIN. ONE LINE EACH. IN SCOPE BY 1 APRIL OR OUT.**

He stood in the doorway with it for about a minute and then went in and sat down, and Nina did not look up, and did not leave, and let him have it.

The box wanted six words. He had four days’ notice of a review that thirty-one people would attend and he had one sheet of paper in front of him and a line to fill in, and the line did not ask him a question so much as ask him for a shape, and the shapes available were *civic* and *not civic*, and he was not able to write a sentence about a function he had never been told about because nobody on that street would tell him and he had not earned it and he knew that on Saturday.

So he wrote the true one.

> **6-L/14 — NON-CIVIC PLANT. 6-L/19 — NON-CIVIC PLANT.**

Eleven minutes, including a coffee he did not drink, and he read it four times, and it was true. A flap gate is a piece of iron in a chamber. A standpipe is a standpipe. Neither has a holder, a term, a scope, a claim, a witness, or a person whose name is on any of it, and there is no office in this city that could be answerable for either, and the review is a review of civic permissions and there is nothing at either node to review.

“Nina.”

She turned her chair round.

“If I leave that blank, what happens?”

“The box stays blank and the review has nothing to work on and both nodes stay in scope and somebody has to go and find out what they are, and that somebody is a man with a clipboard, in about three weeks, on a street that has no address, and you will be standing next to him because you are the issuer and you are the only person who knows where it is.” She said it evenly. “That is the real answer. The rest of it is that a blank in a box is not an answer to a question, it is a hole with a line round it, and a line round a hole in a departmental form is the single most dangerous object in this building, because it will sit there until somebody senior asks what the hole is, and then it will be my hole, because I am the records supervisor and I signed the box.”

“So it is a choice between a hole and six words.”

“It is a choice between a hole that will be traced back to me in four months and six words that will be in the file on Tuesday morning.” Nina put her pen down. “I am not going to tell you which to write. You are the reporting officer and I have said the whole of what I know about it, which is more than I should have and considerably less than you are going to want on Friday.”

“And if it is wrong?”

“Then it is wrong, and there will be a record saying you wrote it and a record saying the registrar’s office asked the department to state things and got a document back, and the two records together will make a very good paragraph in something in about two years.” She looked at him, and it was not unkind, and it was not soft. “I will also say this once. In eleven days you have written four documents and three of them have been about a door, and every one of the three has been true, and the fourth one about the two nodes is also true, and I have watched you go from one to the other and I do not think you have noticed that they are the same act. A blank is a form of answer and you have never yet been the man who is asked to give one.”

He signed the line and initialled the time, 12:41, and put the form on her tray, and she put it face down again without reading it, because she had read it over his shoulder and because it was not hers.

Jonas stood in the yard afterwards for a while. Consequence one kept his name where it was. Consequence two took the cabinet. Consequence three was the Weir, and at twenty to one that afternoon he had written six words about the Weir without asking one person on it, in a room two floors above a shop, and the six words were true, and he was going to find out on Saturday morning what true does when it reaches a public board.

Consequence four was the appointment, and the appointment was the thing he had wanted since he was twenty-three, and the answer had deferred it, publicly, on a board between the ferry office and the Assembly doors, with a registrar’s question answered in the flat register he had taught himself to read on that same board six days ago.

He walked down to the board at two in the afternoon, in the rain, and read it standing back at arm’s length so that a woman getting into a taxi could read it over his shoulder, which was a thing Nina Calder had told him to do once and he had thought was about legibility.

**DEPARTMENT RESPONSE, SERIES 6-41 — FILED 11:44. PUBLIC.**
**FINDING 1: BREACH. FINDING 2: RATIFIED — CLAIM ACCEPTED BY THE DEPARTMENT.**
**MAINTENANCE CLAIM: NOT DISCHARGED. NOT TRANSFERRED.**
**6-L/14 AND 6-L/19: MAINTENANCE HISTORY CANNOT BE PRODUCED. PHYSICAL INSPECTION PROPOSED.**
**APPOINTMENT REVIEW, ISSUER 6-41: DEFERRED.**

Eleven inches below it, on the same enamel, in the same flat register, the gate’s own log was still running.

**22:00. HANDOVER: E. MBEKI / R. NWOSU. BOTH HOLDERS READ TERM ALOUD. TICK IN BOTH BOXES.**
**RENEWED 22:00, THURSDAY 13 MARCH. SIX DAYS. NO LINE OF THE TERM CHANGED.**
**AND UNDER IT, IN THE BOX, IN A LARGE HARD HAND: *MY HOURS ON THIS RECORD SAY 23:00 AND I HAVE READ THEM BACK TWICE AND THEY STILL SAY 23:00. I HAVE RENEWED THIS TERM WITH THAT WRITTEN DOWN HERE. — R. NWOSU, 22:14.***

He read that three times, standing at arm’s length so a woman getting into a taxi could read it over his shoulder, and the part he could not get past was that the two bearers had got to the end of a six-day term, in a corridor, at ten o’clock at night, and had said yes to the same six days again — and that the first thing the woman who said yes had done with the paper was write down, in public, in her own capitals, the reason it ought to have been no.

He stood there until the rain had got into the collar of his coat and then went to find a telephone.
