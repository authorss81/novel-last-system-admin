# Chapter 0034 — A Separate Closure

The plate was in the basement of Brine Wharf, on the back of the door of the refuge store, at the bottom of nine steps, under a forty-watt bulb, and Jonas read it standing up because there was nowhere to sit.

**LOWLAND REFUGE STORE 1 — SERIES 6, PLATE 6-L/21. CONTENTS: FOAM ROLLS 60, SANDBAGS 2,400, PUMP PORTABLE 1, GENERATOR 1, BLANKETS 140.**
**HOLDER: —**
**IF THIS HOLDER IS EMPTY, THIS STORE IS NOT A STORE.**

That was all of it. There was no precedence line, because there had never been a dispute. There was no term, because the store had been in the same basement since before the district had a name. There was a witness field with three initials in it that had been painted on in 1988, and under the holder, eleven centimetres of bare plate where a line of lettering had been taken off with a chisel and not filled.

Jonas got his hand off the door and stepped back so that Oren Pell could get to it.

“I am not going to mend it,” he said. “For the record, before you ask, and so that you can put it in your minute. I am not going to mend it, I could not mend it with a room full of witnesses, and I would not do it with the building under your order.”

“You could not,” said Oren Pell, “because the order is made. That is the answer and it took you eleven minutes and a basement to arrive at, and I want it noted that you arrived at it before I asked.” He was in a coat and had a clipboard and he had not sat down either. “Mr Mercer, I did not bring you here for your opinion. I brought you here because I am going to close this building at six o’clock this evening and everything in that store is going to be taken out of it, and when the store is emptied the plate above is going to find that it holds nothing, and the claim on the contents of that store — which is real, and which is thirty-one years old, and which is the only claim in the lowland that anybody has ever honoured — is going to have nobody.”

“That is not my problem to hold.”

“No,” said Oren Pell. “That is exactly why I have come to tell you about it in a basement instead of telephoning, and I will now say the part that is my problem and your problem at the same time, which is the part I have been putting off since Tuesday.

“The claim follows the plate. The plate is series six and so is your problem. Six-four-one is the west clinic, and the lowland chain hangs off that, and your name is in the issuer line at the top of it. So when that claim has no counterparty, the maintenance route it lands on is the nearest one on the chain, and I have pulled the chain, and there are two names on it, and one of them is a hospital and one of them is you. That is not a threat and it is not a favour and it is the third one of these in a month.” He looked up. “I am not going to do that to you over the telephone. That is why we are standing in a basement at eleven in the morning on a Sunday.”

“Thank you,” said Jonas, and meant it, and was immediately angry about how much he meant it.

Yasmin Rohan came down the stairs behind them carrying a ledger the size of a paving slab.

She was fifty-three and she had a key ring with eleven keys on it and she had been the steward of the Brine Wharf residents’ association for eleven years and the holder of the refuge store register since her uncle had it, and she had come down to watch two men read a plate and she did not sit down either.

“Mister Pell,” she said. “The order went up on the noticeboard in the entrance on the twenty-second with the consultation dates on it, and there is a line on it that says *the association may make written representations before the order is made*, and my association made written representations on the twenty-sixth, and the letter is in the file, and you are welcome to summarise it.”

“I have read it,” said Oren Pell.

“Then I will not summarise it,” said Yasmin Rohan, “and I will not stand here while two men talk about my store, and I will be in the flat if you want me.”

The door at the top of the stairs opened again, and a woman of about thirty-four came down in a coat over a night shirt, and Jonas understood from the way Oren Pell turned round that this was Ferdy Oduya, who held the second key and who worked nights at the grain terminal and who had been asleep, and who was not apologising for it.

“Whose store,” Ferdy Oduya said.

“Yours and mine,” said Yasmin Rohan. “Eleven flats and a basement, and there are two keys, and one of them is on my belt at eleven in the morning and the other one is on his at three in the morning, and neither of us is paid, and if you want to know how long that arrangement has existed, it is longer than the department has had a form for it.”

“It has not existed for thirty-one years,” said Jonas.

“It has existed for thirty-one years,” said Ferdy Oduya. “You have a plate with a number in it. I have a key and a torch and a mobile telephone with four per cent battery in it.”

---

The defect was not in dispute and never had been.

Oren Pell put the January file on the top of a sandbag stack and opened it. Substructure survey, twelfth of January. North corner of the ground-floor slab had dropped thirty-four millimetres since the 2019 survey. The store is the lowland’s only registered refuge capacity for the Tideglass and Gantry bank, and its north escape door discharges directly into the yard, and the threshold of that door is a hundred and eighty millimetres below the design flood level for the two-in-hundred-year event that the whole scheme of that street was built around.

“It was a remedial notice on the twelfth of January,” said Oren Pell. “North threshold, escape route, a hundred and eighty millimetres of temporary works to be installed and a monitoring pin. That is what I signed. It is not a closure and it does not close anything and it went to the freeholder and to the association. The freeholder’s agent wrote back on the ninth of February to say the works were not a landlord obligation and that the association had been advised. I have that letter. I have it in the file and I have read it and it is a well-written letter and it is correct, and I am not going to pretend in front of you that a freeholder is a monster about a threshold.” He turned a page. “The monitoring pin is gone. There are two holes in the slab where the pin was and the holes are dry and nobody has measured anything since February, and the figure is fifty-one now, against thirty-four in January, and in the eight days since the twenty-second it has moved four, which is a fortnight’s rate, where the two months before that moved less than one. That is not a building settling, Mr Mercer. That is a raft on made ground with a canal on three sides of it.”

“So the order is right.”

“The order is right,” said Oren Pell. “I have been right about a building in this city twice now and I have got a great deal of practice at being right about a building. In March the twenty-second I closed a shelter on the canal and it was the right thing to do about a wall and it took an address away from a set of people who still have it. This is the same instrument in a different place with a different set of people in it, and the reason I do not get tired of it is that there is no version of the job in which I get to be the one who is wrong about the wall. The order goes at six o’clock.”

“I am going to measure it.”

Oren Pell looked at him for a second. “I know you are. That is why I have not asked you to leave.”

It took him forty minutes with a steel tape, a lump hammer, a chisel, a datum block and a spirit level, and Ferdy Oduya had to hold the datum block because there was nobody else to hold it, and Yasmin Rohan watched from the doorway with her arms folded, and nobody spoke for most of it.

He cleaned the two holes out, and he found the pin’s grout collar still in the slab on the north corner, and he chipped it back level with a lump hammer and a chisel and laid the datum block over it, and he measured from the block to the finished floor at four points around the room, and at the last point he stopped for about a minute and then went round and did it again, because the first time he thought he had made a mistake.

Thirty-four millimetres in January. Fifty-four now, at the north-west corner, which is three millimetres worse than the registrar’s own figure and in the corner nearest the canal and nearest the escape door.

“Twenty,” he said. “You have under-written it by three and you have put it in the wrong corner. The corner that matters is the one the escape route discharges into and it is the one that has moved.”

“I know which corner it is,” said Oren Pell, without any satisfaction at all. “I wrote the order at five o’clock using fifty-one. It stays at fifty-one, because the order is served on the number that was measured on the twenty-second and a person may not be moved out of a building on a measurement taken on the day the order is made. And I have written fifty-four in the file as an engineer’s observation made after service of the order, and it is in the file because it is true, and it is in the file because you were here and you measured it and I am not going to have you measure it and then say nothing about it.”

Yasmin Rohan said, from the doorway, “You have just made it worse for us.”

“I have just made it true,” said Jonas.

“I know the difference,” said Yasmin Rohan, “and I have got four hours to think about it and I am not going to spend them on that.”

He cleaned his tools off and put them in the roll and stood for a moment in the middle of a basement in a building that was going to be shut in three hours, with a number in his head that he was not allowed to use for anything except the truth.

“Then let me say the other thing once, and then I will stop,” said Jonas, “because I have been on the other end of one of these and I am not going to pretend I did not learn something.

“Your order closes a building. The building is the only registered refuge capacity for that bank, and the contents are the only foam and the only sandbags between here and Gantry, and the department is going to put the contents in a lock-up on Marsh Lane with a contracted key, and the key is on a clock from seven in the morning to seven at night, and in a flood at two in the morning the lowland has a registered refuge store it cannot open.”

“Yes,” said Oren Pell. “All of that is true and all of it follows from the order, and I have spent eleven days with it, and I am going to tell you what I did about it, and then I am going to close the building.

“I wrote to three bodies. The borough drainage put it in a quarterly review for June. The borough’s own emergency stockholder said the store is not a borough asset and the borough will not be the freeholder of a private basement by contract. The Gantry Ward depot said the sandbags are out of date by two years and would have to be re-tested before they went back on any register at all.” He shut the file. “Those are the answers. Those are the actual answers of three institutions in this city, in writing, and I have them in the same folder as the freeholder’s letter, and I have read the whole folder about nine times this month, and none of them is anybody being wicked, and the folder is a hundred and forty pages long and it is the best possible outcome any of those four bodies could manage inside a fortnight, and in a fortnight the building has to be shut.”

---

At four o’clock the association met in the laundry room, which is what the laundry room was for, and eleven people came, and two of the eleven were over seventy-five and one of them was on the stairs and had to be helped up them, and Jonas was asked to leave and did.

He waited in the yard with Pia Santos, who had cycled over from Bellow Market on hearing about it from Selah Mora and who had not been asked either.

“You are not going in there,” said Pia.

“No.”

“Right, and I am going to tell you something and then I am going to go up that path, because I have been up it twice this week already and I have to wash my kit. Yasmin Rohan has been at me since Tuesday asking whether the Weir would lend the loft’s Wednesday clinic space, and I have said no twice, and I would like you to know I said no before I knew you were going to be standing in this yard.

“Do not put the two streets in the same place. Not for the paper, not for the appeal, not for anything. That is fifty-nine doors and a stand and a woman with a board on a fence, and this is eleven flats and a basement and a key on a belt, and we did not come to you, and if we are next to each other on a document then the next thing that happens is that we are counted together, and the count will be done by somebody who is not either of us, and it will be wrong in the way that counts are always wrong.” She got on the bicycle. “That is her argument and it is the same argument as ours and she arrived at it without ever having met any of us, and I have decided that this is the most frightening thing I have learned this month and I am not going to think about it tonight.”

Jonas stayed in the yard until about half past four, and then went and stood on the wall at the head of the buried canal, in the wind, and looked at the water, which was out.

The order was made at seventeen forty and served at seventeen fifty-two and it took effect at eighteen hundred and eleven people and four hundred and six of their possessions came out of a five-storey building into two borough vans and a hired coach, and the coach went to the Anchor House hostel in a street off Salt Row, which the borough had bought out for fourteen nights at a rate per room per night that Jonas did not think about and would think about for a week.

The last person out was a man of eighty-one called Joseph Anum, who had lived in flat nine since 1991 and who wanted his own sheets, and who was helped down the stairs by two orderlies and one of Ferdy Oduya’s neighbours, and who sat on a bench in the yard with a pillowcase over his knees and asked somebody, twice, whether he could come back in the morning to get his reading glasses.

Jonas was asked to leave the yard at a quarter past six by a woman from the borough’s contract office who had a clipboard of her own and did not know who he was, and he went, and stood in Cooper’s Reach’s mouth, and watched from there.

And at twenty past six, four men from a contracted haulier started taking sandbags out of a basement, two thousand four hundred of them, in pallets, forty-eight to a pallet, and stacking them on the pavement in the dark, and the generator went on a trolley, and the pump went on a trolley, and sixty lengths of foam went out flat against the wall in bundles, and the blankets went out last in four blue crates because somebody thought of the blankets first.

Nobody had a place to put any of it. The borough had declined to be the freeholder. The depot had said the sandbags were out of date. The van was going to Marsh Lane, where there is a lock-up with a keyholder on a clock from seven in the morning to seven at night, and the keyholder is not a name on any plate in this city, he is a name on a rota at a security firm, and the rota does not know what a refuge store is.

At seven o’clock Jonas Mercer stood on a pavement in Cooper’s Reach and watched a man stack the last pallet, and the plate in the basement behind him, with its eleven centimetres of chiselled-out lettering and its three painted initials from 1988, was asking a question that could not be read any more, and could not be written on, because the door was locked and the building was shut and the two people who had keys to it were on a coach in a street off Salt Row with fourteen nights left of somebody else’s money.

He got out his pad, because he was a technician and it was a hinge, and then he put the pad away again without writing anything in it, and stood there, and understood, for the second time that month, that the nearest responsible maintenance route was him, and that he was not permitted to carry it, and that there was nobody else standing on the pavement.
