# Chapter 0048 — Permission: Find the Blank Bearer

At ten past six on the morning of Saturday the nineteenth of April the plate on the Weir stand had gone quiet.

That is not a fault. That is what a plate does when the term it is carrying has ended, and it did not print anything dramatic and it did not change colour, and there is no sentence in any manual in the city that tells you what to do at ten past six on a Saturday when the water point you have been keeping for thirty-two years has stopped asking.

The water was the colour of weak tea. The trap had eighteen inches of silt out of it and a foot and ten of it in, and the stand was three hundred metres from a block of fourteen households whose back doors were open to the weather and whose floors were drying on a chapel-hall floor three miles away.

Bo Trimble was seventeen and he had been awake since five, and he read the five lines out loud on the step to Margo Doré, who is sixty-three and keeps the board, and he read them out loud because that is the only way a term on that pillar has ever been renewed on that street and it has been the way since the seventeenth of March, and there is no other way and there will not be one.

“The term runs fourteen days from this reading,” said Margo Doré, “or until the trap is clear, whichever is sooner, and there is a claim on the bottom of it and I am going to read you the claim.”

She read it out on the step, in daylight, to a boy of seventeen, and the claim says that the holder keeps this point true until the silt trap is clear, unpaid.

“That is not true,” said Bo Trimble. “The trap is not clear. It has a foot and ten of silt in it and I have been down there and I have put eighteen inches out with a bucket and there is a foot and ten left and it needs a shovel and two people. So I am reading out a term that says somebody is keeping a thing true, and the thing is not true, and I am seventeen and I am doing it at six in the morning because the water is off and there is a block of fourteen households with their back doors open.”

Margo Doré stood on the step and looked at the water and did not say anything for about half a minute, and Jonas Mercer, who was nine feet away with a lamp he had not needed, understood that he was about to be asked to leave, and that he was going to be asked to leave by a woman of sixty-three, and that he was going to go.

“Yes,” she said. “It is not true, and I am not going to pretend to him that it is, because a plate does not care whether a claim is true and a person does, and the difference between the two is the entire argument of this street and I have been making it since before he was born.

“Not fourteen days, though. One window. Six o’clock this morning to six o’clock tomorrow morning, and then it is dead again and there is no name on it, and I am the holder for that window, and after six o’clock tomorrow there is nobody, and we will do it again next week with a different person, and it will be another day and another name, and none of them will be on any list, and that is how it has worked on this street for thirty-two years and it is going to keep working like that until one of us dies, and I am not having it written down as a plan.”

She said the words into the term, out loud, on the step, and Bo Trimble took them down, and the plate on the pillar went back to asking the rota its question.

And the part that cost, and it cost it publicly, in daylight, is this: that the stand takes water only from the person named on the rota for that hour, and a block of fourteen households with no roofs on the back of it is not one person on a rota, and fourteen names on a piece of paper is the one thing that a tree by number twelve and a man with a rubber band round a pocket diary have spent thirty-two years not doing, and everybody on that step understood the position and nobody resolved it.

So the rota for the twenty-four hours was one name for the window and no names under it, and every draw got entered on the board as it happened, with a time, and not with whose, and the consequence, which Margo Doré stated out loud before anybody had thought of it, was that not one person on that street would be able to prove in June that they had water.

“Right,” she said. “Then that is the price of not having a list, and I have known that price since 1994 and I am not going to be asked about it by a man with a lamp, and you may as well take the lamp home.”

He took the lamp home.

The tool roll in the corner of the shop is a canvas roll nine feet long and it weighs about sixty pounds with the contents, and Jonas Mercer packed it on the Saturday morning on the bench with the yellow board behind him and the bond on the board, and he had got the lid of it shut about four times and picked it up twice and it was too heavy for one person, and the reason it was too heavy for one person is that there are two men in this city who have been handing him things for five weeks and have carried a great deal of it themselves.

Dev Singh came in at eleven on the Saturday morning with his coat off, which Jonas had never seen him do.

“Let me take the far end to the door,” he said.

“That is the whole length of the shop.”

“I have got a car and you have got a bag and it is eleven o’clock on a Saturday and I am not going to ask you what is in it,” said Dev Singh, and took the far end, and carried it eleven feet, and put it down in the doorway, and said, “The west lane is clear and I have got the two handovers in the book and I have put the fifty-eight in the box with a line under it,” and went out to his car.

Bea Osunde read the register of attachment at the bench at about half past eleven that morning, standing up, with her tea going cold on the bench beside her, and read all of it, twice, and put it face down, and did not say anything for about forty seconds.

“What are you not going to say,” said Jonas.

“I am not going to say *never mind*,” said Bea Osunde, “because never mind is what you say to a woman whose dog has died. And I am not going to say I am sorry, because you did the things that got you there and I told you about three of them in a yard. And I am not going to say *we will manage*, because the last person who said that to me about a shop was my supervisor in 2016 and in the March of 2017 there were two of us left.”

She picked the tea up and looked at it and put it down again without drinking it.

“It is a register of one man,” said Bea Osunde, “and the man is a probationary grade three, and in about four months there will be a piece of paper in this city with the words last working system administrator on it, and it will be true on the eighteenth of April and wrong by the twenty-second. And the line about being released by no person I have read four times since Friday and I have still not worked out whether it is a wall or a floor, and I am not going to work it out for you, because if I work it out for you then I am the thing you keep putting in the box, and we have had that conversation and it was on a Saturday in a yard.”

“No,” said Jonas. “You are not going to know.”

“No,” said Bea Osunde. “And I am not going to be the person who carries any of it either, and I have not been since the twenty-ninth of March, and I am not going to be this week, and if you put that roll down that yard wall outside and come and tell me about it I will read the card, because I read cards. That is the whole of what I have.”

She picked the register of attachment up off the bench and squared it to the edge of the bench, which she does with everything, and then she said, without any particular weight in it at all: “That is the ninth time in five weeks that somebody has put you in a box and gone away. You have noticed it. I have watched you notice it four times this month and do nothing about it, and I am not going to be the fifth thing that happens to you. Go and do your round.”

He did the round on foot on the Sunday morning of the twentieth, the way he has done a round on foot on footpaths and pavements and in car parks since the second of March, with a bag and a slate and a length of flat bar and a pen he could not hold.

He started at the Bellow Market loading hatch at seven in the morning, where the hatch opened and stayed open for a woman with a chilled van, and then opened and stayed open for a man with a pallet of ice who had been locked out of his own stock for a day in April, and where the one line on the plate said the collectors' rule eleven and the rule said the market's working chef, and the market's working chef was forty feet away behind a partition with her back to him and did not turn round, because she had heard the hatch and had decided that a hatch which opens and stays open does not need anybody standing next to it, and she was right, and it is everything she said in a yard in March.

At the Saint Orra platform at twenty past eight, Kofi Sarr was doing a handover and had a form in his hand, and he said, without stopping, “Somebody has asked me for a list of who got off at bay four on Thursday night and I said no, and I am telling you because I told you on the Monday I would, and there is a man from the council’s flood forum coming on the second of May to take me to dinner, and I have said no to the dinner, and I am telling you about the dinner as well because you will hear about the dinner later and I would rather you heard it in the right order.”

At Tarn Street Primary at half past nine, Ibrahim Sesay was in a shed with a key on a nail and a book on the gate, and there was a new line in the book in a hand that was not his and not Roz Idris’s, because Roz Idris had been asked and had said that she would read it in a fortnight and not before.

I have not put a line in for that and I would like the nine people on your card to know that there is no line for it, because you all read five lines to each other and I have got one and it is about a key.”

At the lowland outfall at half past ten, Emeka Rusk was standing on the gantry above a hand wheel with a strip of old hose over his shoulder, on his own, at half past ten on a Sunday, in the rain, doing nothing in particular, which Jonas Mercer understood to be a form of statement.

“The report came in Friday afternoon,” said Emeka Rusk. “It says *surge current associated with the storm overflow gate operation*. It has got a box on it for *operator* and the box is blank and I have telephoned them twice and both times the woman has said there is nobody to fill it in. I have decided that is the correct outcome and I am not going to chase it.”

“You do not have to.”

“I know I do not have to,” said Emeka Rusk, “and I want you to hear me say that while there is a wheel in the frame, because in about six weeks one of those men is going to come and tell me the wrong operator put that gate over and moved a hundred and forty millimetres of water into a lowland channel, and I have got nothing to say to him except that the box is blank, and that is what I have got, and I have been running pumps for twenty-six years and it is the first time anybody has let me have the blank.”

At Saint Orra west, in the corridor, at two o'clock on the Sunday afternoon, there was nothing happening at all, because it is a Sunday and the day holder works Monday to Saturday, and the plate on the doorframe said four words in a flat register with a full stop after each one of them, and they were true.

And on the way back to the shop he went past the old pump house, which is on none of the four sheets and is not a node and is the one thing in this city that the department has separately told him not to touch. It is still barricaded. The tape across it is the fault line's own and it has sun-bleached out of white, and Jonas Mercer stood on the wrong side of it for a while and did not lift a corner of it, because the line behind it came back unsafe on a pressure inspection in the second week of March and the shared-maintenance term that would let anybody open that door has never existed, and everything he could have done about it with a bar and his own hands would have taken a week and put a hole in a line that a fault line has already written *do not close* about. So he stood there, and then he went and did his round, and the pump is closed.

At ten o'clock that night Ezra Mbeki read the five lines out loud to Ruth Nwosu in the middle of the corridor and Ruth Nwosu read them back and Mina Okafor read them back a second time, because the roster requires two named people at every handover who have read the term and can say when it ends, and the ninth line on a job card in a coat — a man with a laptop bag who had said *I am not doing it because it is a good idea* in a yard in the rain — was not in a corridor at ten o'clock on a Sunday night, and the person who did it instead was a clerk who stopped being a witness on the ninth of April and has not stopped being the person who writes down what happened, and she did it because it is her job and not because anybody asked her to, and she said so, twice, to two different people.

And on the wall above the card table, on a cast plate the size of a sheet of paper, four bolts, and a hinge on the left, was the term that had kept that door open on the night of the seventeenth of April and on the morning of the eighteenth, and Jonas Mercer stood under it and read it, because he reads them, and he had read it standing in this corridor on about forty other evenings since the second week of March, and it was never once the same document twice — the plate holds the shape of the term and the terms are read into it fresh, and that is everything on that wall. The issuer field said:

> **ISSUER: MERCER, J.**

which is the only thing on that plate with his name on it, and which Marta Sowle asked to be taken off the public board in the first week of April and which is not the public board, and which he has asked about twice and has been told twice that a plate carries the name of the man who made it and there is no field on a plate for taking it off, and he has stopped asking.

And the holder field said **HOLDER: THE HOLDER ON DUTY**, which is a rota, which a plate will not take without a name beside it, and there was no name in the field, and the four names were on a board on the wall two feet away in Mina Okafor's hand, with times in it, and the box underneath them said:

And the retainer line on the board, which had read **not yet agreed** since the fifteenth of March, and which is struck through in Selah Mora's own hand, in a corridor, in front of a clerk, on Saturday the eighteenth of April at twenty past three in the afternoon, because the cheque cleared on the Friday morning and Halvorsen telephoned her himself and she went into his office and the words came off the board in front of her, and the words came off the board nine days after the woman who put a date on her consent stopped being a witness anywhere in this city.

“I am not going to ask her,” said Selah Mora, who had been standing four feet away.

And a witness who comes back out of politeness is a witness you can lose twice, and the second time she will not say why, and Mr Halvorsen, in a car park, in November, when a limb is unpaid again and he is busy: *of course, Selah, we will look at it in the new financial year.* And then there is no date, and then there is nothing, and then the door goes without a witness and somebody writes *no cover available* on a piece of paper instead of *offered, refused, twice*, and that is how a hospital ends up with a plate on it that nobody in the building can read out loud.

“It is money that is four days late and it is not going to be five weeks late next time, and it will be five weeks late next time if there is nobody standing in this corridor at four in the afternoon on the day it goes wrong, and the money was never the thing that was going to save it, and I have been unpaid since the fifteenth of March and I am not going to pretend to you that the cheque has changed my life. It has changed a line on a board. That is what it has changed, and it is a good thing, and it is nine days after she stopped, and I am not going to ask her.”

At the bottom of the ramp, on the way out, at about ten to five, the handrail on the east side — flat bar, four bolts, twenty-five minutes on the Saturday morning of the fifth of April, done by hand by a man with no plate and no witness and no permission, the one repair in five weeks that nobody had ever once refused him — was sound, and Jonas Mercer put a hand on it and pushed, and held it, and it did not move, and nobody was there, and he did not write it up.

When he came back into the yard at about six on the Sunday evening there were three people standing at the gate waiting, because it is a shop and the gate is the front of it and anybody in a hundred yards who has a hinge comes and stands at the gate.

A man from the top of Tarn Street with a consumer unit that has been tripping since Friday and a board that has no money on it, and Jonas gave him a forty-amp board out of the drawer and wrote the date on the back of it in pencil and did not put it in the book, because the book is for the department's work and this is not the department's work and has not been since March.

He got the four sheets of the map out on the bench at about a quarter to seven on the Sunday evening because he wanted to look at the middle of it again, and the print had been lying on the crate in the yard all day with the rain on it, and it had come in at the corner in one place, and when he opened it out on the bench there was a name written across the middle of it in biro, in a hand that pressed very hard and had gone over the top of a printed line twice to get the letters big enough to read.

It was written in the margin, over the blank square in the middle of the sheet, which is the middle of the city, in the place where a map has a name.

Jonas Mercer read it twice.

He looked at the two small printed marks first, because they were the only two on the four sheets and he had read them twice on the Friday night without asking about them, and this time he read the line that is under them. It was not a warning and it was not anybody's opinion. Under each one, in a box the size of a thumbnail, in the same smallest type the printer has: **INSPECTED —** and a date, and no inspector, and no location of record, in the shape the drawing layer uses when it has somewhere it is able to point at. Two districts out of nine have been looked at by somebody. Two corners of one print. He turned the sheets until the marked corner was under the lamp and then turned them back, and he did not write it on the card with the two buildings on it, and he thought, standing at a bench with somebody else's name in biro across the middle of a map of his own city, that the last five weeks had been an argument about who is allowed to be looked at, and that the drawing layer had settled it in four millimetres of type in a corner, and had been right to.

He did not know it. He had never seen it. It was not on a rota and it was not on a wall in a net-loft kitchen and it was not one of the fifty-nine doors that a man of seventy has counted every April since 1994, and it was not a name he had been given in five weeks by any person who owed him an answer, and it was written on a printed map of the city by somebody with a biro who had wanted their hand to be able to find it later.

He stood at the bench with a biro name on a piece of paper for about two minutes and he did not ask Bea Osunde, who was at the bench on the other side of the shop with a plate in a vice, and he did not ask Tomas Renn, who was not there, and he did not go up the towpath, because it was Sunday evening and a man with a map in his hand who goes and asks a street whose only power is that there is nothing on any list which name is on his map is a man who has started something he cannot finish, and he knew that, and it is the only thing in this account that he was ever completely right about in advance.

He folded the four sheets along the middle with the margin facing in, so that the name was inside the fold, and put it in the inside pocket of the coat where a job card had been for five weeks, and it went in next to a piece of folded slipway paper that four names and a ring and a pencilled line had been written on by a woman of nineteen in the rain on the twenty-ninth of March, and which he had never once written into a file.

Then he put the tool roll over his shoulder and it was too heavy for one person, and it had been too heavy for one person since the fifteenth of March, and he was going to have to do it in two trips from the shop to the Old Ward board and there was nobody in the shop to do the other end because Bea Osunde was at a plate in a vice and had told him at the bench on Saturday morning that she was not going to be the person who carries any of it.

He made the first trip at about ten past seven and left the roll in the porch of the Old Ward public board under the glass canopy, which is the only dry place in that half of the city, and the board was lit, because the board comes up at eight, and the four sheets of the map were under his arm where they had been since Saturday night, and the board itself had been repainted by the caretaker and the tape gone, and the rain had got into the frame and there was a corner of the Old Ward notice curling.
