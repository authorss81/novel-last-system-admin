# Chapter 0031 — The First Offer

The shop had a CivicCore van in the yard at ten past eight on Thursday morning and it was not there to fetch anything.

Jonas came down the stairs with his coat half on and stopped in the doorway, because a young man in a clean navy jacket was kneeling at the base of the yard wall with a torque wrench, and the wall is where the department’s own men had been stacking seized cable drums for a fortnight because there was nowhere to put them, and the young man had one of the drums open and was counting the fixings out loud into a phone.

“You all right, mate,” he said, without looking up. “Sorry — we’re on the barrier gate, tide today.”

“Barrier gate where?”

“Bridge Street flood barrier, the little electric one behind the bus stop.” He counted four more fixings. “It’s been screaming since Tuesday. Your lot logged it Tuesday. We’ve got it done by eleven.”

Jonas put his bag down on the bench and got close enough to see the tag on the man’s jacket — CIVICCORE CONSORTIUM / FIELD OPERATIONS / G. RAYNOR-HALE — and the little laminated card on the lanyard, which was the size of a library ticket and had a rate on it and a telephone number and a line that said the call-out is charged whether or not the fault is found.

“Forty minutes,” said Jonas. “That’s a small gate.”

“It’s a small gate on a flood defence,” said Raynor-Hale, “which means if it goes, forty minutes becomes a street. That’s not me being clever, that’s on the card as well.” He nodded at the card. “We don’t get paid to find it. We get paid to make it work. If it’s a four-hour job, somebody in an office has to decide to pay for the four hours, and that is the part everybody hates about us and it is the part that means the gate opens.”

Bea Osunde was at the bench with her back to the door, and she had heard all of it.

“Say that again,” she said.

“That the gate opens, Mr—”

“No. The other part. The part about somebody in an office.” Bea turned round on the stool. “Say it slower, because I want it on the record that a CivicCore man said it slower. Go on.”

Raynor-Hale wiped his hands on a rag, and said, patiently, the way you explain something to somebody you have decided is not arguing: “Call-out is charged. If the budget holder declines, the fault waits. That is not a criticism of anybody. It is a design.”

“Right,” said Bea. “Thank you.” She looked at Jonas. “That is your fault on Tuesday, by the way. Tuesday’s job on Bridge Street came in at ten past four and I have had the log here since, and there is nowhere in this building for it to sit, so it has sat in my head, and I have been carrying it for three days, and this morning a man with a card made it in forty minutes and charged the office for the hour, and the office will say yes, because the office has money for CivicCore and not for a Tuesday.”

“It was on the list.”

“It was on the list on Tuesday and the list is nineteen days long.” She put the sanding block down with its flat side up, the way she always did. “Thirty-four technicians, nine hundred and ten hinges, two crews gone to CivicCore-failed nodes on Monday, and you are the only grade III in this shop on a Friday, and your appointment review is a review of a review. So write your series six response and let the man with the card do his gate.”

Jonas wrote the series 6 response that morning on the plank across the two milk crates, because the plank was the only flat dry thing in the building, and Nina Calder had already read him the third version of the first three lines on the telephone at half past seven, twice, in a tone she reserves for people who have not slept.

Then at half past two a woman called Ceri Vaughan rang the shop and asked whether Jonas Mercer had twenty minutes and would not take them on the telephone.

---

The room was over a tool-hire place on Marsh Lane and it had a clean table and a screen on the wall showing a map of the city with two thousand and ninety civic hinges on it, coloured by who held them. It took him a minute to work out that the blue was not a district and the grey was not a fault. It was the department, and CivicCore, and *held by neither*, and the grey was a quarter of the map.

“You have read my file,” said Jonas.

“I have read your six words and your series six response and your registrar’s answer, and I have read the registrar’s answer twice because it is the most interesting document the city has produced this year.” Vaughan sat down across the table and did not put a bag on it. “Mr Mercer, before we start: nothing on this table is a threat and nothing on it is confidential, and if you want it in writing I will put it in writing, and the writing will say the same thing. There are four parts and you can take them away and think about them and no one will chase you.”

“All right.”

“First: appointment. Your probation closes. The review of the review is concluded — it was concluded this morning, in eleven minutes, and the department found that the individual’s civic practice is sound and the delays in production are not attributable to the individual. You are appointed. Grade four equivalent. Permanent. Forty-four thousand, and a pension, and a desk you do not have to earn every March.”

Forty-four thousand. He was on thirty-one four. He did the arithmetic anyway, and it took about a second and a half, and it was the first thing that happened in that room that he did not choose to do.

“Second: notice access. Root level. You would see every notice the root sends to anybody in this city, at the moment it is sent, including the ones nobody has to show you.” She turned the screen round. “That is not information about CivicCore. That is information about Nacre. There are four hundred and six hinge notices a week on that screen and about ninety of them are the kind you would have to be senior to see, and you would see all of them.”

“Third,” she said, “and this is the part you will want most and I am not going to dress it up. Every term you have ever issued comes across to us carried forward. The six-four-one at the west clinic, the precedence you mended on the lowland stand, the flap-control plate at the chamber. Not renewed — continued. No expiry, no six-day reading in a corridor, no term that dies on the word *sooner*. They continue for as long as the service does, and the maintenance sits with a contractor who is on call, and the claim on them is discharged.”

“And the fourth part is what you get for it.”

“The fourth part is a signature.” Vaughan turned a folder round on the table. It was one page. “Blanket continuity waiver. Municipal officer’s form. It says that for the duration of the continuity contract no local term, holder, refusal, witness arrangement, or standing practice in the city is incompatible with continuity maintenance; that where continuity and a local term conflict, continuity is served and the local term is suspended for the duration; and that a refusal of a person, a roster, or an institution does not suspend continuity maintenance.”

“You are asking me to sign away refusal.”

“I am asking you to sign away *incompatibility*. The sentence is drafted so that nobody has to be erased. Your friend on the west clinic keeps her door. Her rota keeps its rights. Selah Mora gets her eight hours.” Vaughan said the name with a small, deliberate courtesy, and Jonas understood that she had been in the building at half past nine on the twenty-fourth and had read the board. “Everything local survives. It just stops being able to say no to a truck arriving.”

“For ninety minutes.”

“For however long the work takes.”

Jonas put both hands flat on the table. His right one would not close all the way and he had stopped apologising for it in public some time in the previous fortnight.

“There is a fourth thing,” he said. “You have not offered it because it is not in the contract, and because it would embarrass both of us, and I am going to ask for it anyway, and then you are going to say no, and I am going to have asked.”

Vaughan folded her hands.

“The arrears,” she said, “and the return-of-care retainer. West site administration’s outstanding, on your own board, in your own building’s writing, and the word on the board is *not yet agreed*. The hours that are wrong on paper. The hours since the fifteenth of March at night rates. The return-of-care limb since the fifteenth. All of it retrospective, one cheque, one signature.

“And it would go into the west site’s own participation fund, administered and countersigned by a service manager, so that by the time it landed it would be the hospital’s money and not ours, and nobody could say a contractor bought it.

“And I am not going to say her number in this room, because she has not written it down anywhere, and I have read everything she has written, and it is not written down. If you want that money for her, you will have to go and ask her for it, and she will tell you no, and you will have to do that twice.”

“That would take the words off the board.”

“It would take the words off the board on the day the cheque cleared, and not one minute before, and I have read her condition and I will honour it.” Vaughan said it without any pressure at all, which was the worst of it. “She has asked for it in writing. She is owed it in fact. And in nine weeks I can make it a cheque, and the only thing standing between that woman and the money is a man with a signature and a council budget that closed on the thirty-first.”

He got the pad out of his coat. He did not mean to. His hand went into the left pocket and came out with the pad and the pencil and he squared it on the table in front of him the way he does on a bench, and Vaughan let him, because she had seen men do it before and knew what it meant.

He wrote two columns. In the first column he wrote four names and he wrote them without hesitating, which is how he knew what he was doing, because he did not have to think about Ruth Nwosu’s face to know what eight hours a night does to a woman of sixty-eight, and he did not have to think about Selah Mora to know that a cheque is not the only thing she has been asking for and might be the only thing she gets.

In the second column he wrote: **9 houses. 90 minutes. 1 ticket.**

Then he stopped, because the second column was not a list of people and it was written in the same hand and the same pencil and it was the only reason the first column existed.

He looked at it for about four seconds and then he closed the pad and put it back in his coat, and Vaughan, who had watched all of it without moving her face at all, said nothing about it, and let him have it.

“You knew I was going to do that,” he said.

“I knew a technician would,” said Vaughan. “I have been in this trade for eleven years and I have watched three of you do it. The one who did not do it was the best of the three, and he is a council employee in Tideglass on a drainage team, and he is bored, and I would have hired him in a second.”

“Let me put the other side of it up,” said Vaughan, “because I have read the department’s position and I do not think it is honest.

“There is a street you have been to four times in a fortnight. I am not going to ask you for its name and you are not going to give it to me, and I have not sent anybody up that footpath and I am not going to, for a commercial reason and not a decent one: I cannot write a contract for a holder who will not sign one, and I have been trying to find an address for that service since the fourteenth of March and there is not one in any system I can buy.

“Under this instrument, when that trap packs, we clear it. Crew of four, ninety minutes, a ticket, and the crew do not need a rota and they do not need to be paid out of a tin and the water does not need anybody’s name on it. And I want you to understand what the *first* consequence of that is, because you are a technician and you will hear the service and not the shape. The first consequence is that the first person who ever does anything for that street is a company. The second is that the company does not have neighbours.” She turned the folder back. “After that it is just maintenance, and maintenance is what it is for, and I do not think you would take it away from them.”

Jonas looked at the screen with the two thousand and ninety hinges on it for a while.

“You have just told me the company that would own them does not have neighbours,” he said.

“I have told you it does not have *a rota*,” said Vaughan. “Which is worse and cheaper.”

---

He did not sign anything, and he did not say no, and he was aware of both of those as decisions rather than as outcomes.

The department’s response came at ten past four in the afternoon on the Friday and would be in the minute and the minute goes public on the Monday. The four parts would be on a laminated card, and the card would have a number on it, and Vaughan gave him the card, and said the offer did not expire before the twentieth of April, and that she had put his name on it as *refused* rather than *deferred*, on the grounds that a city that cannot tell the difference between no and not-yet is how the Weir happened.

He said he would rather it said *deferred*.

“It is written as refused,” said Vaughan, “and I am not going to argue you out of a word on a form in the last ten minutes of a meeting. You did not say no. I wrote what you did not say.”

Then he drove down to the towpath at about half past six in the evening, because he had been not going to do it and then did it, and because he wanted to see one thing.

The tap was off.

The green pillar stood in its fence with the bucket on the chain and the forty-slot board beside it, and the board had a fresh pencil line across it and a time written in the corner in a shaky hand, and the hand was Delphine Ofori’s, and the time was 19:20.

The rota had closed the stand. Of course it had. Nine houses had been drinking weak tea out of a bucket for eight days and a seventy-eight-year-old woman had walked up that path at ten past seven, put her hand on the tap, seen what came out of it, and closed it, and crossed her slot, and written the time down, exactly as she had at one o’clock in the morning on the twenty-first of March when the term had still been alive and had asked her a question.

The plate on the pillar said its five lines, and the five lines asked the rota nothing, and no term in this city could put water back in those nine houses on Friday morning, because the only instrument that could was a reading given by a holder on a rota to a holder on a rota, and there were no holders, because a holder is a person who has agreed to be findable, and not one of the fifty-nine doors had agreed to anything that day.

Nobody was on the rota. The rota was a board with forty slots, thirty-two crosses and an eighth of a page of pencil.

Jonas stood at the bottom of the slipway in the cold with a CivicCore card in his pocket that would put a crew on that path in ninety minutes, four thousand pounds a year that he did not have, and a clause in a folder upstairs that he had neither signed nor refused. There was no way to answer it. There could not be. The only thing that could move was somebody on that street agreeing to be the person who held the question, and every day he spent in rooms like the one on Marsh Lane was a day those nine houses spent with no water at all.

He got back in the van and sat with his hands on the wheel for a while.

Then he got out again and walked the forty feet back under the glass canopy to look at the two lines in the dark, where nobody could see him doing it.

They were four inches apart, in the same flat register, and he had read them twice that day already and had not stopped being able to see them.

He had noticed, standing in the dark, that they had changed in a way nobody had written down. Ten days ago they were a classification waiting for a review and a field with nothing in it, and there was a procedural difference between those two things, and a person could appeal against one of them. As of this afternoon both of them were a sentence with no process in front of it at all, because a standing tap that nobody has to answer is not a lapsed term, it is simply how the water behaves, and there is nothing on that board or on any board to appeal against.

Clipped to the frame on the left of the board, with a bent paperclip, was a sheet of ruled net-loft paper with fifty-nine door marks on it drawn in pencil by somebody who could not draw.

He could have taken it down. It was on his patch, it was curling at the corner, and it was the only unofficial thing in the whole canopy. He could have taken it down and straightened it and written a date on the back of it, and a man with a laminated card in his pocket and a review on Monday would have been doing the department a small favour.

He did not touch it, and he understood, standing there with his hands at his sides, that this was the third time in eleven days that he had wanted to do something to that piece of paper and had not, and that the reason was not integrity. The reason was that he did not have anybody’s permission, and that not having anybody’s permission had started to feel less like a wall and more like a job.

On the seat beside him, face up, was the card. Four parts, a reference, a date, and the twentieth of April. In twenty-four days the nine houses at the top of that street would have had three weeks without water, and there was no version of the arithmetic in which that was a decision anybody had taken, because no decision had been taken at all.

That was the thing he could not say in any of the rooms he had sat in that day. It had not been accepted and it had not been refused. It was a card in a van, and a rota that could not prove it had been asked to hold anything, and nine houses with a bucket on a chain and a sixty-three-year-old woman carrying the water up a path in the dark because a term he had written to expire sooner had expired sooner.

He got back in the van and sat there until the board lights went off.
