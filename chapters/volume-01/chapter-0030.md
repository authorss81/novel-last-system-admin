# Chapter 0030 — The Price of an Unlisted Service

It rained on the Tuesday and Jonas Mercer wrote the series 6 response on the bench in the yard at River Stacks with a plank across two milk crates, because it was the only flat dry thing in the building, and he wrote it with his left hand in block capitals, and it took him until half past two in the afternoon because it was two hundred and forty words and he had started it eleven times.

**SERIES 6 — RESPONSE TO THE RECLAMATION SEASON MAINTENANCE REVIEW.**
**6-38, 6-39, 6-40: history complete and held in the shop cabinet, 6-R-14, which I am not presently cleared to open.**
**6-41: history complete. Term issued by me, witnessed, in force, expiring.**
**6-L/14 and 6-L/19: the maintenance history cannot be produced. The nodes are physically present. Both were inspected on the fifteenth, the seventeenth, the twenty-first and the twenty-second of March by a local fitter and a local holder of a water rota, and one municipal technician, and the work done is recorded on a wall in the net loft and is not in this file because it is not mine.**

He read that out loud to Nina Calder twice on the telephone before he was prepared to send it, and she said one thing about it both times.

“Say who *is* mine,” she said. “You have written four lines that say the work is not yours and not in the file, and a department reading that is going to conclude that the reporting officer considers the lowland nodes somebody else’s business. And they are, as far as you are concerned, which is the problem, and you have just hidden it in a subordinate clause. If the history cannot be produced, say why. You have got until Friday.”

“All right.”

“And do not put a *local holder of a water rota* in a departmental response,” Nina said. “It is not a category anybody recognises, and if you want it recognised you have to name a person, and if you name a person you have put a name on a street with no address into the series 6 file, and I have spent nine years keeping that file clean and I am not going to be the person who opens it.”

He read the second version out loud to Ruth Nwosu at the end of the evening shift on the Tuesday, in the corridor, on the way to the handover, and Ruth read it back to him herself, out loud, both times, at his request, because a document that goes to a registrar should be one somebody can say out loud.

“That is fine,” she said, “except the third line, and I will tell you why the third line is a lie and it is not because of me. *Term expiring.* It is not expiring. It was read out at ten o’clock last night and it runs to six o’clock on the thirtieth, and if you send that to a registrar on Friday it will say on the first of April that a term has expired, and the review will read that as a gap in the service, and there is no gap, and the reason there is no gap is that four people read five lines out loud to each other in a corridor at ten o’clock at night and two of them are paid for it and two of them are not.”

“Then I will change the line.”

“Change the line and then come and read the third line to Mina, because she is the record and she will want to have read it before it goes, and she will tell you it is wrong, and she will be right, and you will have to change it twice.” Ruth put the page down. “And while you are standing there, read the whole thing to somebody who is not in the department, because I have been in this building nineteen years and I can tell you that the day a document leaves this corridor and nobody outside the building has heard it read is the day it stops being true.”

He read it to Margo Doré on the Wednesday, on the towpath, in the rain, at ten past nine in the morning, and Margo read it back to him in her own voice, flat, at speed, twice, and then told him what was wrong with it.

“Not the words,” she said. “The words are all true and I have not got a single one to argue with. That is the problem and you should be frightened of it, because I have been arguing with men from the city for thirty years and I have never once run out of things to say to a piece of paper before I got to the second page.”

“Then say the one thing.”

“There is no *is* in it,” said Margo. “You have written two hundred and forty words about that street and there is no *is* in them anywhere, and I have read it twice and I could not tell anybody what the water on this street is for, and I could not tell them who is responsible for it, and I could not tell them what happens to it on the first of April. And I am not saying you have lied to me, because you have not, and I am saying that you have written a true document about a thing that is not there, and that is what a thing that has no name looks like from the outside, and that is what it is going to look like to the review, and I would like you to sit with that for a minute before you say anything at all.”

The stand was open.

That was the other thing on the towpath at ten past nine on the Wednesday, and Jonas did not see it for about two minutes because he had his head in a piece of paper.

Margo Doré had come out of the loft with a bucket under her arm and was standing in front of the green pillar with her hand on the tap, and the tap was running, and the water coming out of it was the colour of a weak tea.

“Since Monday,” she said. “I came down at one o’clock on Monday to close it, because the trap had packed on Sunday night, and I put my hand on it and it did not ask me anything. It just came on. It has been coming on since, in about four minutes an hour, and I have been putting buckets in the stand and taking them up and the buckets are brown and so is everything, and I have turned the stopcock off twice and the stopcock is the county’s and the stopcock does not have anything to do with the tap, and I have been standing here for two days with a spanner and a wrench and a flat piece of slate and I am not going to break a tap that nine houses drink from.”

“Which plate?” said Jonas. “What does the plate say?”

Margo turned round and looked at him, and Jonas understood, about a second and a half too late, what she was about to tell him.

“It says nothing,” said Margo Doré. “That is what it says. It has said nothing since Monday. It does not ask me a question any more. It used to ask me a question.”

He got the rag and the brush off the back of the pillar and cleaned the face of the plate, and it came up out of the silt and the rain in the way those plates do, and the five lines were where they had been on the seventeenth of March, in the same flat register, and the five lines said:

> **PRECEDENCE MENDED. THIS POINT ANSWERS TO THE HOLDER ON THE ROTA.**
> **A CLAIM FROM OUTSIDE THE ROTA DOES NOT OVERRIDE THE HOLDER AND DOES NOT CANCEL THE ROTA.**
> **AN EMERGENCY DRAW FOR CLINICAL REASON IS PERFORMED AND ENTERED ON THE ROTA.**
> **THE HOLDER MAY REFUSE ANY DRAW. A REFUSAL ENDS THE DRAW AND NOT THE ROTA.**
> **CLAIM: THE HOLDER KEEPS THIS POINT TRUE UNTIL THE SILT TRAP IS CLEAR. UNPAID.**
> **TERM: 14 DAYS FROM THIS READING, OR THE COMPLETION OF THE BARRIER WORK, WHICHEVER IS SOONER. NO CARRYING FORWARD. RENEWED ONLY BY THE HOLDER ON THE ROTA, READING IT ALOUD TO THE NEXT HOLDER ON THE ROTA.**

“Whichever is sooner,” said Jonas.

“Whichever is sooner,” said Margo. “The barrier work finished on Friday at about half past one in the afternoon, because the flap was hung and the stops were set and that was the end of it, and half past one on Friday the twenty-first is sooner than the thirtieth, and that is what I wrote, and that is what you said, and I read it back to you twice and you said it back to me twice, and I did not write down the sooner date because I did not want to write a second date on that wall in front of a woman who has been nothing but careful in her life.” She put the bucket down. “So the term went on Friday afternoon. And since Friday the holder is nobody, and the rota does not answer, and the thing that wins when the rota does not answer is the mains, because the mains has been there since before any of us and it does not have a term on it, it has a *pipe*.”

Nobody spoke for a while, on a street with fifty-nine doors, in the rain, at ten past nine in the morning.

“So the answer to your question,” said Margo, “is that the water on this street is now the water authority’s, in the sense that nobody on this street can stop it being anybody’s, and the boil is coming back on the inner face of that bank because the flap is shut and the trap is packed and the whole of the lowland is full of the water that is coming out of that tap into a bucket and being carried up a path by two people, and I have not told the exchange, and I have not told Otie, and I am telling you because you wrote the sentence.”

“I did write the sentence,” Jonas said.

“You did write the sentence, and you wrote it in a kitchen at about half past four in the afternoon on the seventeenth of March with a woman of sixty-three holding the pen, and I held the pen, and I would like it understood that I am not saying it to make you feel bad, I am saying it because in eleven days I have learned that when something goes wrong in a room, the man with the credential in it is the one everybody looks at, and I have been that man in every room I have been in for thirty years and I have never once been able to do anything about it, and it is a miserable thing and now it is yours, and I am sorry for you and I am not going to help you carry it.”

“There is a second thing,” said Jonas, “and I am going to say it before I say the first thing, because I do not want the first thing to be the reason I say the second one. I did not notice. I was on that path on Friday morning and on Saturday afternoon and I have not been down it since, and I did not telephone, and I did not think about it, and it lapsed on Friday afternoon and it has been open for five days and I found out at ten past nine this morning because a woman with a bucket told me. That is not the clause’s fault. That is mine, and I am not going to give it to a piece of writing.”

“You wrote *whichever is sooner* and I agreed to it.”

“I wrote it because a sooner expiry is the honest shape of a thing,” said Jonas, “and I did not think about who would notice it, and the answer is that a woman with a bucket noticed it and not the man who wrote it, and that is not a system.”

“It is a rota,” said Margo. “It is a rota and it worked. It is the first rota I have ever had that told me something. There is no phone in it. Nobody rings the department on a Tuesday. You have to be here, and I am here, and I have been here since Monday, and I have been the one holding the stand open and the trap packed and the water brown, and I have told nobody for three days because I did not want to be the woman who is always the one, and I would like it noticed that I have been exactly the woman who is always the one and I did it silently, and that is the fourth time in a month.”

She picked the bucket up.

“Now you are going to ask me to put my name on a piece of paper and say that this street is a service, and I am going to say no, and I want to say it before you ask, because I have watched you write four documents this week and I would rather be one step ahead of you for once in my life.”

“I was not going to ask you that.”

“You were going to ask me whether the *rotas* could be it,” said Margo. “Which is a different question and a cleverer one, and I have had two days to think about it, and here is the answer. The rota cannot be it either. The rota is a board on a fence with forty slots on it and thirty-two crosses and a green pillar with a bucket on a chain, and a county man can put a form in a box about a fence, and the county did not take a form in a box about a fence in order to be difficult. They take forms in a box about fences because that is the only shape a fence has. A rota is a practice. A service is a thing somebody is answerable for, and the answerability is not a practice, it is a name, and a name on a document is what I have refused for thirty-two years and I am not going to start at sixty-three because a man from the city has been in the rain.”

Pia Santos came down the path at half past nine with three bags on the handlebars and no gloves, and stood at the top of the street and listened to about forty words of it, and then she said the thing she had come down to say and had clearly been carrying since Tuesday.

“No,” she said. “You are not taking a statement and you are not taking a number and you are not taking anything out of that bucket, and I have been up this path for four days waiting for you to ask me for one of those three things so that I could say no, and you have not asked, and that is the first time in a month that you have wanted something and not asked, and I would like it recorded that I noticed.”

“You did notice,” said Jonas.

“I notice everything and it is not a gift, it is a job.” She put the bags down on the wall. “Here is the demand, then, because somebody has to say it and you cannot and Aurrie will not and Margo has told you and Hector has told you and Otie has told you, and it is going to have to be me, and I am going to say it once and then I am going to go to work.

“If the city says a tap is plant, then a plant is nobody’s, and nobody means the water authority will come and fix it. Nobody means the county will come and fix it. Nobody means the water comes back on Thursday instead of on Monday. Nobody means the boil comes back on the inner face of that bank and takes the wall off the back of number twenty-two, where a man of seventy-eight lives who I have swept the road for and whose gate I can open.” She had not raised her voice once. “And nobody has been able to make anybody come and fix anything on this street in thirty-two years, and the reason is that we have never once been in a position to make anybody do anything, because we have never been in a position to be *served*. That is the word. I got it from a lawyer on the telephone on Sunday for nothing, because I am nineteen and I am not a lawyer yet, and she said: a claim with nobody on the other end of it attaches itself to the nearest person or place that can be reached, and if there is nobody, then it attaches to the man who was last there. And you were last there.”

Nobody said anything.

“So that is the demand, and it is the only one I have,” said Pia. “Not a count. Not a survey. Not a statement. *Who is the other end of it.* Somebody in this city with an address and a door and a name that a letter can be left with, who can be told that the water on this street is theirs to answer for, and who can be that without putting one of us in a list.”

“Anybody can be that,” said Jonas. “That is the whole difficulty. Any of you can be that, and it takes one signature, and it is one signature from a woman of sixty-three or a woman of sixty-one, and it is exactly the thing you have all refused for thirty-two years, and you are right to refuse it, and I am not going to argue you out of it, and I am not going to be allowed to.”

“Then who,” said Pia.

“That is the decision I cannot make and neither can you,” said Jonas. “There are four bodies in this city that can be served on a matter like this and every one of them has a reason that is better than mine. The hospital has a site administration with a board that sits on the ninth and a service manager who has been honest with me all week. The borough has a drainage department and a highways office and a man who lends a pump if a name goes in a box. The department has a series 6 file with a reporting officer in it whose name is on a classification that says *non-civic plant*, and if I withdraw that classification before the first, in the file, with my name against my own words, then the nodes stay in scope, and a review that finds two nodes in scope has to ask what they are for, and that question goes back out to this street.” He stopped. “And that is me making myself the answer again, which is what everybody who has met me has told me I do, and I have been told it four times in a fortnight by three people and one shop steward and I have not done it once on purpose and I am not sure that is better.”

“Withdraw it, then,” said Bo Trimble, from the top of the wall above them, where he had been sitting for some time. “Withdraw the thing you wrote, and then the review asks, and then they come here, and then it is my fault that they came here, because I am the one who is standing in front of them, and I am seventeen and I am on the water rota and I have been on it since I was eleven, and I have just spent four days watching a tap run brown into a bucket and I would rather it was a county officer with a clipboard than a man from the city with a form, because a clipboard I can talk to and a form I cannot.”

Nobody answered him, and that was the end of it, and the reason it was the end of it was that all six of them were standing on a wet street in the rain at half past ten in the morning with a bucket of brown water at their feet and none of them had said the sentence out loud, and the sentence was the one about a service, and it belonged to a person, and there was no person, and that was the finding, and it had cost three days of brown water and a bank that is going to need four more tonnes of stone in a fortnight.

Jonas went up to the Old Ward at half past four on the Wednesday and stood at the board, and the rain had stopped and the glass had been cleaned, and the map was still there with two filled circles in the bottom left-hand corner and no name beside them.

**CENTRAL ROOT — HOLDER: —**
**RECLAMATION SEASON MAINTENANCE REVIEW. RESPONSES CLOSE 28 MARCH. REVIEW 1 APRIL.**

And under the two lines about the review, four inches apart, in the flat register, the things he had caused:

**6-L/14, 6-L/19 — RECOMMENDED CLASSIFICATION: NON-CIVIC PLANT. OUT OF SCOPE IF ACCEPTED.**
**6-L/19 — LOCAL TERM ISSUED 17 MARCH. HOLDER: ROTA. EXPIRED 21 MARCH, 13:30.**

That second line had been added that afternoon by the board's clerk, who had come out of the building at some point in the twenty-four hours since Margo Doré had telephoned the department's own service line to ask a technical question about a plate and had been told, by a person, that a term had lapsed.

Jonas stood and looked at the two lines for a long time. A term he had made, in a kitchen, with a woman's name in the issuer field and a rota in the holder field, had lapsed at half past one in the afternoon on Friday the twenty-first of March because it had been written to expire sooner, and it had not been noticed for five days, and the reason it had not been noticed was that the only person who could have noticed was the only person who was not in a building, and he had been in a building, and there was a shop in the building with a yellow board on it and a woman in it who had told him three times.

And on the board, directly above both of his lines, in the same type, in the same register, the city said the same thing about a root it had been saying for a month:

**CENTRAL ROOT — HOLDER: —**

Two blank holder fields, a hundred yards apart in the same register, one at the centre of the city and one at the end of a street that was not on any map, and the only difference between them was that one of them had been empty for thirty years and the other had been empty for four days because a woman of sixty-three had trusted a clause.

He got out his pad, and wrote nothing in it, and put it away, and telephoned the shop instead.

“Bea. It is me. Two questions and then I am going to go and do something I do not want to do, so I would like to ask them while I still have somebody to ask.

“First: can a term be renewed by somebody who is not the issuer and is not the department, where the renewal clause says *renewed only by the holder on the rota, reading it aloud to the next holder on the rota*? Not can it be signed. Can it be *renewed*. Because I have read it for four days and I think I have been reading it as somebody who cannot do it, and I would like to be told by somebody with a bench and a tape measure that I am wrong, because I have been wrong once this month in a way that gave nine houses brown water.”

Bea Osunde took about four seconds.

“You are not wrong,” she said. “You are exactly right, and you have been right since the seventeenth, and the reason you have been reading it as somebody who cannot do it is that you have never once in your life written anything that you could not keep, and this is the first thing you have written that you are going to have to put down and stand next to, and that is why you keep trying to pick it up again.”

“That was one question.”

“Second question,” said Bea. “Yes. Because the other one is about who answers for the water, and I have been in this shop nineteen years and I have watched forty of you, and the answer to *who answers* is not a person, you idiot, it is an institution, and there is a service manager in Saint Orra west who has had a thing on his desk for eleven days that he could sign and has not signed, and there is a district nurse in a loft who has a tin, and there is a woman of sixty-three with a board on a fence, and none of them will do it, and you cannot make them, and you are not supposed to be able to.”

“I know I am not.”

“No, you do not, because it is only half past four on a Wednesday and you have got a form to write on Friday. Go and see the service manager.”

Jonas Mercer stood at the Old Ward board for another minute, and then went and found a telephone, and put in a call to the west site administration, and asked for Gunnar Halvorsen, and when the call went through he said, “I need an institution to answer for a water service on a street that has no address, and I have got until the twenty-eighth, and I am not going to pretend I know how to arrange that, and I would like to be told what you need me to bring.”

Halvorsen took a long time about it, and then said, “Bring the thing the woman on the rota wrote on the wall, and bring nothing else, and do not bring the boy, and do not bring me a form. And bring it before the ninth, because I have a board on the ninth and forty minutes of *matters arising* and I am spending one of them on this, and I am not promising you a cheque, I am promising you a minute, and a minute is a thing you can stand on.”

Jonas wrote *the ninth* on the back of his hand in biro because he had no pad, and then took the biro out of his pocket and put the hand down flat on the board, which was a ridiculous thing to do and which he did anyway.

And in Saint Orra, at the west clinic, at a quarter past ten that night, a man who had made a term once and could not make it again stood at the end of a corridor while a woman of some years and a man of twenty-six read five lines out loud to each other, out loud, together, and then went and sat down.

“You have not read them,” said Ruth Nwosu.

“I read them at ten to ten.”

“You read them at the door on your way in, which is not the same, and which I have watched you do eleven times, and which is the reason I say this.” She did not look up from the board she was writing the times on. “Read them again properly, all five, in the middle of the corridor, where the person on the trolley can hear you, because in five weeks somebody is going to be on that trolley and they are going to ask who is holding this door, and I am not going to be sixty-eight.”

He read them again, in the middle of the corridor, all five, out loud, at a quarter past ten at night on the twenty-sixth of March, with a boy of twenty-six listening for errors, and Ruth Nwosu said them back to him word for word, and the log says *22:00. Handover. Both holders read term aloud. Ticks in both boxes.*

And four streets and one unlisted service away, on a fence at the top of a road with fifty-nine doors, a board said forty, and thirty-two slots were crossed, and there was nothing in the stand but a bucket on a chain, and a term that had been written to expire sooner had expired, and somebody was going to have to say five lines out loud to somebody else on that board before there was any water in it, and the man who had written them was going to be a hundred miles away, and had promised in a kitchen in writing that he would not be the one who renewed it.
