# Chapter 0042 — What the Night Has For

The Methodist hall on Carrow Lane has a kitchen the size of a church porch and eleven people sleeping in it, and on Monday the fourteenth of April it had eleven people in it for the second consecutive night and nowhere for a third.

They are the residents of Brine Wharf. The hostel was paid for fourteen nights, the last of them the night of the twelfth, and from the thirteenth nobody has paid for anything, and the arrangement that has replaced it is that Joseph Anum, who is eighty-one and lived in flat nine from 1991, sleeps in the vestry, and that Yasmin Rohan has a list of eleven households and a rota for cooking that is not a rota, it is a text group, and that about four of the eleven have gone back to Cooper's Reach in the daytime to wash and come back, because the building is sealed at the front door and not at the back and everybody has understood that without discussing it.

Jonas Mercer came down the lane at eleven in the morning with a roll of drawing paper in a tube, because he had been told there was no accommodation and had decided, in the shop, on Saturday, that the answer to no accommodation was logistics.

Nell Ardent was in the kitchen with a mug and a folded copy of the appeal form and the particular exhaustion of a person who has been doing this for nothing for a week.

“Mr Mercer,” she said. “If you have come to offer us something, I have to tell you in advance that I will not take it, and if you have come to fix a door, there is nothing here to fix and the doors are not the problem.”

“I have come about two thousand four hundred sandbags,” said Jonas.

Nell Ardent put her mug down.

“Say the rest of it.”

“They are in a lock-up on Marsh Lane. Two thousand four hundred sandbags, sixty foam rolls, a portable pump, a generator and a hundred and forty blankets, and they are eleven miles from the low ground, and the low ground is where a flood would come, and I have been in this city for five weeks and every time I turn around there is a store of somebody’s neighbour’s flood kit behind a door with a clock on it.”

“The store opens at seven in the morning,” said Nell Ardent, “and closes at seven in the evening, and the keyholder is a contracted night man on a seven-to-seven clock employed by a security firm, and he is not Mr Rohan and he is not a resident and he has never in his life heard of a flood kit.”

“He is the keyholder.”

“He is a man with a key and a timesheet and a company,” said Nell Ardent, “and if he opens that lock-up on a Tuesday because a technician from a department he has never heard of asks him to, then at nine o'clock on Wednesday morning a controller will ask him a question about a breach of the contract, and he will put that lock-up on a list of things he did not open, and in six months somebody will use it. That is not me being difficult. That is a *contract* and it is the only thing in this city that has ever worked in our favour and it works by not being broken.”

“And if I went to him as a person rather than as a department.”

“Then you would be a man who came to a gate at half past six in the morning and asked a stranger to open a locked building on a Tuesday, and he would say no, and he would be right, and you would have spent a fortnight establishing that the store is reachable by asking.” She picked her mug up. “Write it down. It is a real answer and it will be one when somebody proposes a way round it in June.”

He asked about the lorry then, because he had thought about the lorry on Saturday for twenty minutes, and Nell Ardent said that the cliff-side lorry was a Thursday vehicle on a Thursday rota, driven by a man who is a Thursday man, on a contract that does not exist on a Tuesday, and that the last time it had gone up the lowland path it had gone up it in March with four tonnes of stone and come back with a bent tail-lift.

“All right,” said Jonas Mercer.

“For what it is worth,” said Nell Ardent, “the panel is on the twenty-third. The hearing is on the twenty-third. I am appearing for the Cooper’s Reach residents’ association and not for the city, and I have said so on the record, and the fourth line of the form has one name in it and it is not mine. The £2,000 bond is payable if we lose and we are going to lose, and the £340 came off a widow’s account in February and I know that because she told me and I did not write it down.” She turned the form round. “Do you want the only useful thing anybody has said to me this week, or do you want to go and look at a chapel?”

“What is it.”

“Joseph Anum is eighty-one and he has been asked twice this month whether he is willing to have his flat described as *at risk* on a plan, and he has said no twice, and both times he was asked by a nice woman with a clipboard who was doing her job.” She put the form in her bag. “Tell whoever is building your nine that the count is not free. Everyone thinks the count is the paperwork. The count is the day somebody comes with a clipboard and asks a person to be on a piece of paper, and after that the person is on it forever, and there is no unlisting anybody.”

Saint Orra platform four at ten past two in the afternoon on a Monday, and Kofi Sarr had twenty-one years of it and had said yes to being a witness to a platform in about four seconds in a yard in the first week of April and had never explained why.

“The second question again,” he said, before Jonas had got the tube out of his coat.

“Which one.”

“The one that is not a question.” Kofi Sarr was looking down the platform at nothing. “On the seventh a man came. Very good coat, no tools, a laminated card on a lanyard, and he was pleasant, and he said he was doing a review of continuity arrangements across the district, and he asked me what a platform is, and I told him, and then he asked me how many people I wave through a morning and whether I could produce a list. And I said no. And he said *of course not* and wrote something down, and on Friday he came back with a different lanyard and asked about the bay that is not on the timetable.”

“He has a card.”

“He has a card,” said Kofi Sarr, “and I have been at this platform for twenty-one years and I have never seen anybody wave a card at it, and I am telling you about it because on Saturday a man is going to wave it at something and I would like it written down that I said so on Monday.”

Nora Bell had been at the Saint Orra crossing for nineteen years and had a hut the size of a telephone box and a timetable of her own that was laminated and had four columns on it, and she came out of the hut at half past three on the fourteenth of April and did not come out far, and stood on the traffic side of the barrier rail with her back to a trolley signal as she always stood, and looked at Jonas Mercer and at the tube of drawing paper under his arm and said:

“If you have brought me a clipboard I am going to be extremely rude and I will do it now so that you can put the tube back on your shoulder and we can both enjoy the rest of it.”

“I have brought you paper,” said Jonas, “because you said to bring my own paper, and there is nothing on it but a table, and you can write on it or not write on it and I cannot take it off you.”

He put it on the flat top of the barrier machine and took his hands away.

She did not pick it up for about a minute. She had the shift to run. At four o'clock the tram came and she waved it and watched it go and came back.

“Right,” she said. “Go on.”

“Same thing I said to you in the yard. I need nine people in nine places who can each say what one service is, who is holding it, when it ends, and what the claim is afterwards, and I have six of them and one refusal written out in full, and the ninth is a hospital and the seventh is a street, and the seventh is a no and the ninth is a no, and I am not going to lie to you and tell you either of those is coming back.”

“All right,” said Nora Bell. “What am I.”

“A crossing. The barrier end and the west-lane approach.”

“Whose crossing.”

“Mine is not the right question —”

“It is the only question there is,” said Nora Bell, “and you have not asked it, and you have not asked it because you have got eight lines on a piece of paper and you want this one filled, and I have watched men do that to me for nineteen years and it is the reason I said I would take the week.”

She picked the paper up then, and turned it round so it was the right way up for her, and read it, and Jonas stood there and let her read it, and this was the part he had not prepared for, because he had prepared for her to ask whether it was worth as much as a wet Tuesday in her hand and he had an answer to that, and she did not ask it.

“Right,” she said. “Here is the hole and you have not seen it, so I am going to tell you and you are not going to like it.

“I am not a holder of anything. The lights are held by a schedule. The schedule has a holder and he is in an office in Marsh Lane and he is not here and he has not been here since I started. I latch a gate by hand every morning at six and I have done nineteen years of it and if the gate is down it is because I put it down, and if it is up it is because I put it up, and nobody has ever written my name on any of it, and no piece of paper in this city is asking me to. So put me on line one. What happens is that a man in an office finds out that a crossing has a witness on it, and the first question he asks is *who is the witness*, and the second question he asks is *what is the witness*, and the third question he asks is what did she see this morning.”

“And if you said no,” said Jonas, “I would say it was a no, and your refusal would be in the next line, and the man in the office would be holding a list of nine people and one of them had said no.”

“That is not the part,” said Nora Bell. “The part is that I have waved at about two hundred people a day for nineteen years and I have never written one of them down. Not one. And on the seventh of April a woman with a good voice and a clipboard asked me how many people I wave through in a morning and whether I could produce a list, and I said no, and she wrote something down, and I have thought about that every day since, because I have been *asked* for a list, and if I am on your paper then somebody will ask me for one again and they will ask me properly, because I will be a person who has agreed to say what a thing is for, and the answer to that question is a list.”

She held the paper up and it did not go back.

“So here is what I will do, and it is mine and you do not get to write it down until I have said it twice. I will be a witness to the barrier end and the gate I latch, and I will say what a crossing is and when it closes, and I will not say who crosses it, and if somebody asks me who crosses it they can ask the man with the card in his coat and he can have a list.”

“That is the second condition I have been given this week by somebody who was not in the same room.”

“Of course it is,” said Nora Bell. “You are the third man this month. The first was in March and asked me how many people I wave through. The second was on the seventh and wanted a list. You are the only one who has come with his own paper and put it down where I can reach it, and I have noticed that, and it is not nothing, and it is not enough either, and here is the not-enough: I have been offered a seat.”

Jonas did not say anything.

“Council flood forum. Twelve places, one of them mine, they said, because of the barrier end. An annual allowance and a badge and a meeting on the twenty-eighth at nine in the morning, and they are very serious people, and the badge is a nice badge. I have said no to it twice. The first time I said no and the woman wrote it down. The second time the man who came had a laminated card on a lanyard, and he did not ask me how many people I wave through, which I noticed, because the other one did, and he asked me who I would telephone if the barrier end went down and could not be reopened.”

“What did you say.”

“I said I would telephone whoever was in my hut,” said Nora Bell, “and he wrote that down too, and he was extremely pleasant, and I have thought about him every day since and I do not know why.”

The tram came. She waved it. It went.

“Fourteen April,” she said, to the hut, and not to him. “I will do it. I will say what a crossing is and when it ends. And I will not have a list, and if one of yours comes asking me for one then that is your fault and not mine, and I want that written down somewhere by somebody who is not you, because you are the third man and you have a face on you that I have seen before and I do not know where from.”

“I do not know either,” said Jonas Mercer, which was true, and she looked at him for slightly too long, and then went into the hut and shut the door.

At twenty past four he was at the barrier end, on the tram side, with the tube under his arm, when Dev Singh came out of the crossing office with the tide table rolled up in one hand and a wet coat over his arm, and gave him a piece of paper without being asked, which he did four times in five weeks and which is the entire reason Jonas still talked to him.

The river forecast had been a forecast on the seventh. It was not a forecast on the fourteenth.

**SURGE. THURSDAY 17TH, OVERNIGHT INTO FRIDAY. PREDICTED 2.9 M ABOVE SPRING HIGH AT SALTMOUTH. REVISE DOWNWARD.**

Three days. And the weir stand term ran to six o'clock on Saturday the nineteenth, which was two days after the water arrived, on a street with no address, whose trap had not been cleared because clearing it cost two people one Wednesday morning and there had been no Wednesday morning.

“Revise downward,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Three occasions,” said Dev Singh. “It has been revised downward three times in five days and it has been wrong three times. Do not trust the word. Trust the number on the left, and the number on the left came from the river and not from anybody’s policy.”
