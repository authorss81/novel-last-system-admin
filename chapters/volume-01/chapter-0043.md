# Chapter 0043 — The Ninety Seconds and the Week

Low water on the Wednesday was five past five in the morning and Jonas Mercer was at the bottom of the slipway at ten past with a lamp on the end of a length of cane and no bucket, because a man on his own at the bottom of a lowland chamber at five in the morning is not clearing a trap, he is waiting for somebody to find him.

Nobody came.

Margo Doré came down at about twenty past with two flasks and a torch and stood at the top of the slipway and did not come further, because she had a childcare exchange at seven and because she is sixty-three and because it was her call and she made it.

“Right,” she said. “Then we will wait and see.”

“We have until half seven.”

“We have until half seven,” said Margo Doré, “and we are going to be two people short at half seven the same way we were two people short at five past. That is not an accident and it is not anybody’s fault and it has been the arrangement of this street since before I kept the board. The two people a trap needs at low water are the two people who are not free at five in the morning. I open the exchange at seven. Bo is seventeen and he is asleep until six and he will get up, he will get up now, he will get up every Wednesday I ask him, and he will be here for six weeks and then he will be eighteen and then he will be somewhere else and then there will be nobody, and I am not going to spend him on silt at ten past five in the morning for the sixth time in a row when he has a term at that stand and three days of sleep in him.”

“He is on the water rota,” said Jonas.

“He is on the water rota and he is not on the *tool* rota and that is a distinction I made in this kitchen in March and you wrote it on your pad and it is the only reason I have not had to have this conversation with you every week since.”

“You could do it with me,” he said. “Now. It is two people and one morning and I have done it before.”

“Yes,” said Margo Doré, “and I am going to tell you the thing I have never said out loud to anybody including Bo, and I am saying it because you have asked me three times and I am tired of saying no. If you go down that bank at five past five and you get your arm in that box and you clear it, then from that day the nearest responsible maintenance route on this chain is a man who is on this bank at five past five, and when the water comes on Thursday and I ring and say *come*, you will not be a technician coming to help a street. You will be the claim. And a claim is a thing that does not come. I have had a claim before. I had a claim in ninety-four and it was a firm in Salt Row with a telephone number on a form, and it was a form, Jonas. It was not a man in the rain with a lamp on a stick. It was a piece of paper for eleven months.”

The light was coming up over the sheds and the water in the chamber was going down with the tide and the boil on the inner face of the bank was about two metres wide and the colour of a wet pavement, and about a foot lower than it had been in March, and Jonas Mercer stood at the top of a slipway at ten past five in the morning and did not argue with her, because she had not asked him to.

“All right,” he said.

“All right,” said Margo Doré, and gave him a cup of tea out of a flask, and they drank it on the step, and then she said, “Now go and be somewhere useful, because there is a flood coming on Thursday and the two of us have got nine hours to decide where people who do not live here are going to be.”

“Where are they going?”

“That,” said Margo Doré, “is the first useful question anybody has asked me since the first of April.”

Selah Mora had done the return-of-care limb the previous night, twenty-two hundred to six, and had been at the west site at nine in the morning because she had wanted to be in the building when the telephone rang, and Jonas Mercer was at the card table with his coat still on because he had been in the corridor since half past eight, and Mina Okafor put the receiver on the table face up and did not say anything about it, which she does. It rang at ten past ten and it was Gunnar Halvorsen and it took ninety seconds.

“Raised on the fourteenth,” he said. “It has not cleared. It is sitting in the system and it will clear in three or four days and I have no way of making it earlier and I have not lied to you about that.”

“Three or four days.”

“Three or four days,” said Halvorsen. “The line stays where it is.”

“I know the line stays where it is. I have been looking at it since the fifteenth of March,” said Selah Mora. “Mr Halvorsen, I am not ringing you to be rude. I am ringing you because a woman in your corridor put a condition in writing on the second of April and stopped on the ninth because of this telephone call, and I would like you to know that I know that, and that she did not do it to be difficult, and that when it comes I am going to ask her to come back and she is going to say no, and I am not going to argue with her about it either.”

There was a pause on the line from a service manager who has forty minutes of matters arising and a board to sit in.

“Three or four days,” he said, “and I will telephone you myself, and if she says no, that is between her and her own condition and I will not put a word in it.”

“Good,” said Selah Mora. “Because I have watched people put words in it for four weeks and I have been the one doing it.”

The west-clinic care term expired at six o'clock on Wednesday the fifteenth of April.

It expired the way it is designed to expire, which is that it ends, and the plate did not say anything dramatic about it, and the last version of it was read aloud in the middle of the corridor at ten o'clock on Monday the seventh by Ruth Nwosu to Ezra Mbeki and signed, and it ran to six o'clock on the fifteenth, and Ruth Nwosu had told Mina Okafor in a corridor on the fifth and Jonas Mercer in a corridor on the seventh that she would do the fourteenth and would not do the fifteenth, and both of those statements were on the record and neither of them was a negotiation.

At ten to twelve on the Wednesday morning a woman of eighty-four was found dead in bay six with a long time of being the sort of patient where nobody is in a hurry, and the portering needed the care entrance and the ramp and the wide door at the bottom of it, because the mortuary transfer point at the east site has one slot on a Wednesday and it was at one o'clock, and the family's own arrangement was that a man was coming at half past two and the family did not want him to come.

Mina Okafor telephoned Bram Osei's house at ten to twelve. He had gone off at eleven. He answered and said no, and then said why, and the reason was that a door with no term on it is not a door, it is a wall with a handle on it, and he is a porter, and his authority is a key, and a key is not a permission, and if he opened that door at midday with no term behind it he would be doing it on his own and would be asked in about six weeks by somebody in an office to explain in writing why a porter decided on a Wednesday that a woman's dead body was more urgent than the plate said, and he would have to write that letter, and he is not going to be the man who writes it.

Mina Okafor then went and asked Ruth Nwosu in the corridor, and Ruth Nwosu was standing at the end of it in her coat with her bag over the chair, on a shift she had started at ten o'clock the night before and had been on for nearly fourteen hours of, and she said:

“No.”

“Mrs Nwosu —”

“I have said no to the fourteenth and no to the fifteenth in advance and both of those are written down, and you are asking me a third time at eleven o'clock in the morning on the day I told you about, and I said what would happen and it has happened.”

She said it in about eleven seconds and then she said, “I am not going home, before you say anything. I am going to stand in this corridor until six o’clock because I have got a bag and a chair and nowhere to be, and that is a woman’s own time and it is not yours to spend.”

Nobody argued either of them out of it. That is the entire point of the sentence at the top of a rota that nobody in Nacre has ever printed.

Mina Okafor stood in the middle of the corridor for about forty seconds with the box open and the pen not moving, and Jonas Mercer was six feet away and could see her doing the arithmetic of a room, which is the arithmetic of who is physically in it.

Then a voice said, “Is it the ramp.”

Ivo Bamba was coming the other way down the corridor at half past eleven in the morning with a laundry bag over his shoulder, twenty-nine years old, on nights, who had been on since ten o'clock the previous night and was going home at four because the eight patients on the east ward had made a night into a morning.

“Who asked you to be on this corridor,” said Mina Okafor.

“Nobody,” said Ivo Bamba. “Is it the ramp.”

“It is the ramp and the wide door and the mortuary slot at one o'clock and there is no term on that plate.”

“Right,” said Ivo Bamba, and put the laundry bag down on the floor, and said, “Then somebody has to say a declaration and say it out loud, and then I will hold the door, and I am not on your rota and I am not a holder and I am not doing this for the job, I am doing it because that bay is going to sit there until one o'clock and everybody in this corridor knows what that means for a woman of eighty-four.”

The plate on the doorframe said four words and would not say anything else.

**THE DOOR ANSWERS TO NO LIVE TERM. A DECLARATION OPENS IT ONCE.**

Mina Okafor telephoned the on-call and gave the plate number up front before she was asked for it, which is what she has done since the twenty-fourth of March, and Dr. Sanaa Iqbal said, “Give me the words,” and Mina Okafor read the words, and the declaration was given at **six minutes to twelve**, and the door opened at **four minutes past twelve**, and the bier went up a wet ramp with four orderlies and Ivo Bamba at the foot of it holding the door open with his shoulder and not saying anything at all.

Thirty-six minutes from the declaration to the tail-lift of the mortuary van closing at the east site at half past twelve, and the slot was for one o'clock and they were half an hour inside it, and the family's own arrangement had a man coming to the building at half past two whom the family did not want at the building, and the family were told at ten past one instead of half past twelve, and the woman did not go at half past twelve because half past twelve was a man coming with a car and a woman of eighty-four does not go at half past twelve in front of a man with a car.

Marta Sowle was awake, because she is always awake at about eleven, and she came out of bay nine in a cardigan and asked who was holding the door, and Mina Okafor told her, honestly, that nobody was, that the term had ended at six o'clock that morning, that two people had refused and one of them was on the corridor with her bag, and that the door had been opened on a declaration by a doctor and a man with a laundry bag had held it open.

“Say the number,” said Marta Sowle.

“I do not have the number,” said Mina Okafor. “It is not a number yet. I have got a slot for one o’clock and a bier on a wet ramp and a family who are not going to be told anything until this is finished, and I am not going to write a figure in that box that I have not watched happen, because the last thing this box needs is a number somebody guessed at.”

“Then write this down before it happens, because you will want it afterwards and you will not have the time.” Marta Sowle put her hand flat on the table, which she does about four times a year. “The box counts what a family was left not knowing. It does not count how long a door took, and if it ever counts how long a door took then this box is a stopwatch and it is no use to anybody. Nineteen minutes on the third and this one on Wednesday, and the nineteen was a baby and this was a woman of eighty-four, and whoever reads that box in four years will know that both times a doctor was woken up and neither time a nurse was on the door, and that is the whole of what went wrong both times, and it is in your hand and nobody can make it say anything else.”

Mina Okafor wrote the rule in the box and left the figure out of it, and the bier went up the ramp at twenty past twelve with the tail-lift shutting at half past, thirty-six minutes after the declaration and half an hour inside the slot, and the family were told at ten past one, and what went in under the rule was **forty minutes** — which is the wait between a van closing and a family being told anything at all, and the door itself took ten minutes, and those are two different numbers and the box carries the one that hurts, and it was Marta Sowle's rule and not hers, and she wrote that down as well.

Then she sat down for about a minute and a half, and Jonas Mercer, who had been in the corridor since half past ten and had been able to do nothing whatever for two hours, went and stood at the other end of it and did not come over.

Tomas Renn was in the yard of the River Stacks shop at twenty past six on the Wednesday evening with the light off in the office and a sheet of A4 in a plastic wallet.

“Right,” he said. “I have written it up.”

Jonas came out with the tea towels over his shoulder.

“You said you would write it in your own hand on your own paper and put the date on it.”

“It is in my own hand and it is on my own paper and it is dated the fifteenth of April and it is going in the union’s submission to the Standing Committee on the continuity contract, and the committee reports on the second of June, and the submission has to be in by the twenty-second.” He took the wallet off the wall. “Two of the four stewards will not sign it. Ada Kwarteng will not sign it because it reads as though we have stopped asking. And Cuthbert Nkemelu will not sign it because he thinks the last sentence is a provocation, and he is right, and I have not taken it out.”

“Read me the last sentence.”

Tomas Renn turned the sheet round and did not hand it over.

“*The civic term architecture was not the failure on the third of April and was not the failure on the fifteenth, and the record should not be permitted to suggest otherwise.*”

“That is not a provocation,” said Jonas.

“It is a provocation to everybody who has been paid to make it one,” said Tomas Renn, “and I have been the only person in this city asking that question for a year, and I have been proud of it, and it is the same thing Bea told you about the refusal written down in full: it is the one you are proudest of and it is the one in the wrong direction. The moment that sentence is in a document with a date on it, the shop does not have a demand any more. The shop has a *finding*. A demand can be campaigned. A finding can only be cited, and it will be cited by whoever gets hold of it first, and in June that will be a continuity contractor, because it is the only sentence in the world that says *nothing in the design caused this*.”

“Who is going to say it is the people.”

“Everybody. In a year, in a room, in the way you said you would be able to say in a room on the third of April: the city was saved by luck. And I have got there first, and I have got there first in writing, and I have got there first with two of four of my own stewards in a minority report attached, and that is the price, and I have paid it, and I am telling you I have paid it because you are going to find out anyway and I would rather you heard it in this yard than in a committee room in June.”

Jonas Mercer stood in a wet yard and found that he had nothing, for what was probably the fourth time in five weeks.

“Does it answer your question,” he said. “The four in the morning. Two people refuse and the least-bad option is somebody nobody planned.”

“No,” said Tomas Renn.

“Then what is it for.”

“It is for the two in the morning, and it is for the ninth, and it is for the clinic at four o’clock in the afternoon when the boy comes on, and it is so that when somebody eventually writes the rule for the case where everybody says no, they cannot do it in a hurry and in the dark and on the grounds that the design was never the problem.” He put the wallet back on the wall. “Because it *was* the problem, Jonas. Not on the third and not on the fifteenth. On the third the term was live and the door shut at six in the morning because a renewal clause requires two people to be in a corridor at ten at night and does not require anybody to make them be. That is not luck. That is a rule that puts a rota in charge of a promise. And I have written the first half down and I have not written the second half down because the second half of it needs a payroll line and a training afternoon and a naming authority and a written rule, and I have got three and a bit of them and the third one is a hospital doctor’s good intentions, and I am fifty per cent of the problem now instead of sixty, and I would like to go inside and eat something, because it is raining and I have been standing in it for an hour.”
