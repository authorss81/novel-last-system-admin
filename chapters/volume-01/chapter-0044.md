# Chapter 0044 — Nine Names and a Clock

The yard of the River Stacks shop has a parapet on three sides that the rain comes in over and a fourth side that is a wall eight feet high, and on Wednesday the sixteenth of April at half past four in the afternoon, with a surge due in under twenty hours, Jonas Mercer put a piece of hardboard on an upturned crate in the middle of it and nailed the tide sheet to the wall above it with four nails he had to bend.

It was a printed river forecast, wet, with Dev Singh's handwriting across the bottom of it, and on the crate, where six people could read it standing up, he wrote two lines in pencil because he could not hold a pen properly.

**THURSDAY 17TH. OVERNIGHT INTO FRIDAY. 2.9 M ABOVE SPRING HIGH, SALTMOUTH.**
**WEIR STAND TERM 6-L/19 EXPIRES 06:00 SATURDAY 19TH.**

Two dates, four days apart, on the same piece of hardboard, and Jonas found that he could not look at the pair of them for more than about four seconds before it stopped being a fact and became the actual shape of the next two days.

He had clamped a spare plate round the shop's own service riser on the way in, because there is a hinge on the riser in the corner of the yard and he has been meaning to look at it since March, and while he had the box off the plate printed one line, and it is a sentence he has now had off three plates in six weeks and has never once been able to improve:

**A FLOOD IS FORECAST. NO TERM ANSWERS TO A FORECAST.**

They came at half past four and after. Bram Osei, off at eleven and having slept. Bisi Alade, who had shut the market's service side at three and who had a fish knife in her apron because she had forgotten it was there. Emeka Rusk with a strip of old hose over his shoulder. Teo Vlah, who arrived in a van and left the engine running. Yolanda Fofana from Wren Court, who had taken two buses and was in the wrong coat. Ibrahim Sesay, who had walked. Roz Idris, who had said on the telephone at two o'clock that she would come to say something and would not be staying, and who was right about both.

Nora Bell came at five past five, which was forty minutes late, and her reason was the 16:07 and she apologised for it in the doorway, and Jonas thought: *she has been on time for nineteen years and today she is late by five minutes because of a tram and she has apologised for the tram.*

Kofi Sarr came off the platform at five and stood by the gate with his arms folded and did not come in under the parapet, which Jonas noticed and did not comment on.

Dr. Sethi came at six. He is the day-team physician at Saint Orra west and he is the clinic's naming authority, and he had come in his own clothes and had a bag with a laptop in it that he put on the crate and then took off again.

Yvonne Amoah came at about ten past seven, off a late, and stood under the parapet at the back with her arms crossed and said almost nothing for forty minutes, and then asked Jonas Mercer one question, which was whether the barrier end and the ambulance claim were going to be dealt with on the same paper or on two, and she said that she had driven ninety minutes for the answer and that it was the only thing she had come for, and Jonas Mercer had not thought about it at all until that moment and did not have an answer, and told her so, and she said, “Then I will come back,” and she did, on the Friday, with the station's log under her arm.

Mina Okafor came at half past five and stood at the back by the office door with her coat on, and Jonas said she did not have to, and she said she was not here to be anything, she was here because a line on that card had her handwriting on it and she had struck it out herself and she was going to watch what happened to the other eight, and that was the whole of it and she would not say more about it.

Bea Osunde was in the yard because it is the shop and she works there, and she put her bag down against the wall and said, before anybody started, that she was not going to be on it.

“It is not modesty,” she said. “I am grade two with nineteen years and I work for the man who is mending the term. A witness who works for the man who is mending the term is not a witness. She is a second pair of hands with a name on it, and when it goes wrong they will say *she works for him*, and they will be right, and that is the end of the sentence about the two of us being independent, and there is not enough of the two of us to survive me being independent.” She put her hands in her pockets. “I am the ninth on that card if anybody in this yard needs a mechanic, and I am not the ninth witness, and Mr Mercer, if you write me on that card I will take the card and put it in the river.”

“All right,” said Jonas.

“All right,” said Bea Osunde. “And I want to be allowed to sit here. That is not being on it. I am allowed to sit here.”

Tomas Renn was in the doorway for about eleven minutes and did not come in, and put one sentence into the room on his way out, which was that the shop was not a party to anything and that the submission went in on Friday and that the two in the yard who had not signed it were the two in the yard who were not the union’s problem, and then he went.

---

He read the five lines first, to the yard, standing at the crate, out loud, because every one of them had been told those five lines in a room or a yard or a corridor and the yard had not heard them together, and because the one thing he had learned in five weeks was that you could not hand a piece of paper to a person in the street who had not been told what the piece of paper was.

The five lines were the gate term’s five lines. That is what they were and he did not improve them: what the term permits; who is holding it; when it ends; what the claim is; and who may stop it.

Then he read the card out, service by service, name by name, with what each of them had said they would stand for, and the design is that a person may refuse at the moment their name is read, in front of the others, and the refusal goes in the next line, in full, in the yard, where it can be heard.

It took an hour and ten minutes and it was not a meeting. It was a yard with twenty-two people in it and a piece of hardboard on a crate and a rain coming over a parapet.

**One.** Nora Bell, a crossing. The barrier end and the gate she latches, nineteen years, on her own statement. She did not refuse and she narrowed it before she said yes: *I do not say who crosses it, and I want it in the writing that nobody asked me to latch that gate once in nineteen years and that is not going to stop because of this.*

**Two.** A door. Mina Okafor had withdrawn on the ninth and would not be asked again and the yard understood why in about four seconds. Bram Osei took it in the space of a sentence and then asked for a thing to be written, and the thing to be written was that the Thursday he refused in the first week of March stays on the public board where it is, in the same box, with the same words, and that this yard is not a reason to take it off. He said he did not want his Thursday tidied.

It got as far as two and then Emeka Rusk's telephone went off in his pocket and he did not answer it, and then it went off again, and he said, *that is the harbour, and I have to take it, and I am going to say the whole thing so that it is on the record of this yard and not on the record of a telephone call in a week.*

He came back inside after four minutes.

“The overflow chamber at the Carrow Lane outfall does not drain,” he said. “On a rising tide it spills. It spills into the lowland channel, and the lowland channel is nine miles up, and on Thursday night the choice is going to be a hand wheel, and the wheel is mine, and if I put it over the other way it comes down on the drainage that serves Carrow Lane and Marsh Lane and the whole of the Bellow Market service side. I am telling you that on the Wednesday afternoon to twenty people in a yard because on Friday morning it is going to be two o'clock in the morning and nobody is going to be in a position to be told anything by anybody.

“And I am telling you the other thing as well, because there is no point in any of you finding out at half past three. If I hold it, eleven traders down there lose a compressor and a floor of shelving and a day of market on the Saturday, and I have already watched a fish business lose one Saturday in April because of a mend on a plate, and Bisi Adeyemi is standing eight feet from me and knows exactly what I am talking about.”

“Then you have got a job on Thursday night,” said Jonas Mercer, “and you are the only one of us who can do it.”

“Yes,” said Emeka Rusk. “And I have got a card in my pocket from a Wednesday of March that says *a pump*, and a pump is a pump, and I have never in my life pretended a pump was anything else, and if the two of us are still arguing about it at three in the morning then the water decides, and the water always decides and the water does not read cards.”

Nobody in the yard had anything to add to that, and Teo Vlah, who had a socket set in the van and had been listening with his arms folded, said, “I will go and look at the motor on that pump tonight and I will write down what I find and it will be on a piece of paper with my name on it, and that is all I do.”

---

**Three.** A market. Fola Adeyemi had refused in writing and in full and the yard had her four reasons in a hand that was not Jonas’s, and Jonas read them out because a roster that showed only consent was a roster that had been cleaned, and a roster that showed only refusals was a roster that had been made a complaint. Bisi Alade, forty-one, fish, five years in the market, took it with one condition, which she said twice, and which went on the card in her own words: *the mended hatch is in the record and it cost me a Saturday, and on the eighteenth of April the collector's rule eleven is what I say it is, and the Saturday is not a lesson anybody learned.* She said she was not being a spokesperson for eleven traders. She said eleven traders do not need one.

**Four.** A pump. Emeka Rusk read the five lines back twice, slowly, to the yard, and then read them back a third time because Nora Bell told him to, and said the sentence that is on the card: *I would rather be named than be the man who is not there.*

**Five.** A machine. Teo Vlah said *machines only* into the yard before Jonas got there, at volume, so that everybody in it heard it from him, and then he said the rest, which was that a machine is a thing with a fault and a fault is findable at three in the morning and a person is not, and that he had spent the last five weeks in three buildings finding out how many of his machines are within a mile of a hospital.

**Six.** A school. Jonas read the service and said the name and Roz Idris stood up before he had finished the sentence.

“No,” she said. “And I am going to say why in the yard, because if I say it in a corridor afterwards somebody will write *declined*.”

“Twenty years,” she said. “And what I have said for five weeks is that I will witness a service I have been told is failing, and not a service that has been *described* to me as failing by the person who wants it fixed, and the school has been described to me as failing by the man mending it, and there is a flood tomorrow, and the school has been asked to hold people because it is the biggest warm building with a lavatory in it within half a mile, and I am not going to be a woman standing in a corridor telling children that a mechanic with a lamp is as good as the emergency service. No. And I am not angry with you and I am not going to take it up in a fortnight, and the line stays empty if the line has to stay empty.”

It went on the card in full, in her words, read back to her in the yard, and she said it was correct.

And then Ibrahim Sesay, fifty-one, caretaker and portal at Tarn Street Primary for nine years, who had been standing by the gate with a plastic folder under his arm since half past four, said: “I will take the school.”

“You will not,” said Jonas, and then stopped, because the sentence he had been about to say was the sentence he had said to Fola Adeyemi in a market on a Wednesday in March, and it is not a sentence a technician gets to say to a man who has walked across a city for it.

“Go on,” said Ibrahim Sesay. “Say it. I have heard it, somebody has said it to me in a kitchen, and it was a woman in a uniform and she was right. The thing is — I have got a key. I have had a key to that gate since November and I took it on my own out of a shed drawer, and since January there is a name against it on a piece of paper in a school office because the head teacher wrote to the education office and asked them to make it a procedure, and a procedure is a piece of paper, and a piece of paper is not a person, and on a Tuesday in January I opened that gate for a man with a pushchair and there was nobody standing in it but me and a piece of paper in an office.

“So you can have me for a key. I will be the person who opens it and I will not pretend to be the person who holds it, and I am telling you now in the yard so that it is not a surprise at four in the morning: **if I open that gate on Thursday night without anybody telling me in writing that I may, I am trespassing in my own building, and if I do not open it then the school is shut.**”

Nobody in the yard had anything to say to that for a long moment.

“That is not a witness,” said Jonas.

“It is a key and a man who is honest about which of the two he is,” said Ibrahim Sesay. “Put it on the card as a key.”

It went on the card as a key.

**Seven.** A street. The Weir had refused in the kitchen on the seventh, in four lines read back and not altered, and Jonas read the four lines out in the yard, and the yard heard a refusal by a street for the first time, and there was the particular silence of twenty people who have all been told a version of this city in which that sort of thing does not happen.

Yolanda Fofana, who is fifty-four and the named steward of Wren Court in Salter’s Yard, thirty-one rooms and no civic address and a monthly participation fee and a service that Rook demonstrated in a room in March as better than the west clinic’s, stood in the middle of the yard and said the thing before Jonas could.

“Say it,” she said. “Say the sentence you have to say, because you are about to put me next to them and it is not the same thing and it must be said in front of these people and not in a corridor.”

Jonas Mercer said it.

“You are not the answer to the Weir. You are a block of thirty-one rented rooms with a steward and a fee and a clause nine, and you have been in that block for nineteen years and you have paid a subscription to a service, and if this yard is a list then you are on a list next to a refusal and in six months somebody will produce both pages and the two streets will be in the same place for the rest of time.”

“Thank you,” said Yolanda Fofana. “That is the first time anybody has said that out loud and I want it written down that I said thank you for it and not for anything else.” She looked round the yard. “I will take line seven on three conditions. One: my thirty-one residents are told that a card exists, tonight, before anything else, and not on Friday. Two: if anybody on that card is asked for a list of people, the answer is the address of Wren Court and nothing else. Three: the fee does not stop. Whatever happens on Thursday night, nobody in this yard gets free water at my building without the fee being a separate question from the flood, because the second the fee stops, the day somebody stops paying is the day they do not get water, and I have watched that happen in a different block on a different street and I am not doing it here to win an argument in a yard.”

“Line seven is a street with no address,” said Jonas, “and yours is a street with no address.”

“It is,” said Yolanda Fofana. “That is why they fit in the same line, and it is not because we are the same, and I have spent nineteen years being the only private thing on a list in this city and I am extremely tired of being the example of something.”

**Eight.** A platform. Kofi Sarr did not come under the parapet and said his scope from the gate: a platform and never a passenger, and he said the last four words twice, and the second time he was looking straight at Jonas, and he did not say what the second question had been this week, and the yard could work it out.

**Nine.** A hospital. Dr. Sethi had been standing at the back with his laptop bag on the ground at his feet since six o’clock and he had not interrupted one person, and when Jonas read the line he put his hand up before Jonas got to the name.

“I want to say three things and then you can put me on it or not,” said Dr. Sethi. “One: I am the clinic’s naming authority. I put Bram Osei, Ezra Mbeki and Ruth Nwosu on that rota in the first week of March on my own signature. Two: two of them have refused shifts since and one of them has told you she will not do the fourteenth or the fifteenth, and the reasons are on the board, and none of the reasons are my fault and all of them are my rota. Three: if my name goes on a card with a subject on it, then in June the first question anybody asks is whether the hospital’s witness is the man who named the porters. And the answer is yes, and I will say yes, in a room, in a document, and I will not have anybody find it out first.”

He looked at Jonas for a while.

“I am not doing it because it is a good idea. I am doing it because there is nobody else there and I am the man who named the porters, and if that makes me the problem then I would like to be on the list when the problem is looked at rather than when it is discovered.” He picked his bag up off the ground. “There is one thing I will not do and I want it written in the same hand as my name. I will not witness a hospital. I will witness the west clinic, the care entrance, and a roster. There is no such thing as a hospital, there are forty of them, and the day a card says *a hospital* in a room in June it will mean the one the man in the coat wanted.”

“Then line nine is a west clinic,” said Jonas.

“Line nine is a west clinic,” said Dr. Sethi, “and if the man in the coat asks me whether the East Street hospital is covered, you can tell him I said no in the yard and there were twenty people there.”

Nora Bell, from under the parapet, said: “That is the first sensible thing anybody has said this afternoon.”

The card went into Jonas Mercer’s inside coat pocket with eight names on it, three written refusals in the lines they belonged in, one line with a key on it and no holder, and five lines of Mina Okafor’s condition and date and the fact of the withdrawal, in a clerk’s hand, read back to her and correct.

It was half past seven. Nobody had eaten.

Dev Singh came in at about ten past eight with a wet coat and rolled the good copy of the tide sheet out on the crate on top of the hardboard, and put his finger on the left-hand number, which was not the forecast and was not a policy and came off the river.

“Nine hundred and ten millimetres of rise in the eight hours either side of three in the morning,” he said. “High water at Saltmouth is at ten past three. It comes up the estuary faster than the forecast has it and the lowland dip is nine miles up, and the trap is packed, and I have told the fault line and the fault line has told me there is no person available for that trap in any district, and I have been told twice by men in coats that there is a procedure for that, and I would like somebody in this yard to tell me the procedure for a silt trap with no person available in it, because I have looked, and there is one, and it says *county*.”

“There is no county on that street,” said Ibrahim Sesay.

“There is no county on that street,” said Dev Singh, “and I have been to the county and there is a man in the county who has agreed with me in writing, and I have got the piece of paper, and I have brought it here so that it can be put on the crate next to everything else, and I am going home, because it is my daughter's birthday and I have been in this yard since half past four.”
