# Chapter 0032 — What Rook Can Show

Nina Calder read the series 6 response out loud on the Friday morning at twenty past nine, standing at the end of the records counter with her coat still on, and she read all of it, including the parts she had made him write twice, and at the end she said, “That is filed,” and then she said, “And I am not going to say one word about the fourteenth of March, and you are not going to ask me to, and if anybody in this room asks you what you thought about the six words you are going to tell them you thought about them every day since, which is true, and which is not an answer, and which is the only one either of us has.”

The review is on Tuesday. The response is in the file. Nine days of the month gone and the same six words sitting in the middle of the file in his own handwriting, and Royden Achebe’s name under them, and there was nothing at all he could do with that on a Friday.

At eleven o’clock a man called Alden Rook arrived at the shop and shook his hand and asked whether he could buy him a coffee, which Jonas said no to, and then Rook said, “Fair. Then I will stand here for forty minutes and you can ask me questions and I will not answer any of them, and then you can ask me one more and I will answer that one properly. That is a fair offer and I have made it to four technicians this month.”

He was forty-six and he had forearms, and he carried a ledger under his arm, and he was the first person in a month who had come into that shop and not looked at the yellow board first.

“Show me the worst one,” he said. “Not the worst fault. The worst *hinge*. The one that has been sitting. I know it is not the one you would pick, and that is why I am asking you and not your manager.”

“There is a barrier gate on Bridge Street.”

“The barrier gate on Bridge Street took forty minutes and cost us an hour and your lot had it three days.” Rook opened the ledger. “It is not the worst. It is the fourth worst. Come on.”

---

Morrow Fields, Plot 6, a market garden with eleven glasshouses and a pump house the colour of a biscuit tin. The irrigation pump had failed on the fourteenth of January and the department’s log carried the whole story in three columns: reported, inspected, attended. Inspected the nineteenth. Attended the second of March, which was forty-seven days, which was under the department’s own median of fifty-one for that district and therefore not anybody’s fault.

“It has run since the fourth of March at four in the morning every day,” said Rook. “Four hours forty, first attendance, on the fifth. A duty relay, a seized bearing, and a thirty-pound part the department could have bought on the sixth.”

“Because of a dispute about the contract,” said Jonas. “It is not in the catalog. It is in a private addendum written in 2011 and nobody owns it, and there was no change note, so the department cannot order from it.”

Rook wrote that down, not to score it — to keep it. “Then we have agreed on a fact and it is not in your favour, so I will say the other thing and say it quickly. Ivo’s crew came on the fifth because the department had nobody in on the fourth and Ivo works the days the department does not staff him, and he is not paid for them and is paid for them anyway, because if I do not pay him for them he does them anyway and resents me. That is the whole secret of this company and it took me four years to work it out. The company does not do maintenance. It does attendance, and attendance is a person, and a person can be bought, and once you have bought him he turns up.”

“That is not maintenance.”

“No. It is the part of maintenance that actually keeps hinges running, and I have found that the argument in your city is always about the other part, and the other part is the part with the drawing on it.”

---

Tarn Street Primary, west gate, on a corner where the road is uphill and the footfall is heavy. The gate is a power gate for the two hundred and forty children who come and go on foot, and there is a latch, and the latch works, and for six weeks it did not have to.

Jonas had the file open on Rook’s ledger before Rook said anything, because the plate on the gate post had one line on it and the line was in the department’s register.

**6-44 — WEST GATE, TARN STREET PRIMARY. HOLDER: A. MBEKI-GRISWOLD. TERM: CONTINUOUS.**

“A. Mbeki-Gwold,” said Jonas. “December.”

“Adaeze Mbeki-Griswold. Deputy head. Named holder from March last year until the twenty-second of November, when she moved to a school in Kestrel Hill.” Rook turned a page. “Her term was continuous, which is the correct instrument for a school gate — a school gate does not stop at six o’clock and it does not stop at the weekend, and it should not have to be read out loud in a corridor to somebody every six days. You would hate that sentence, Mr Mercer. You are correct to hate it, and you are the reason I have never once offered it to you as a service model.”

“She left. And the term said continuous.”

“The term said continuous, which means *nobody has to renew it*, which means the moment the holder leaves there is no gap and no ceremony and no alarm, because a continuous term cannot lapse. It just stops having anybody in it. And the gate became a latch on the twelfth of November and it stayed a latch for eighty-two days.”

“Two hundred and forty children a day through a manual gate on an uphill corner.”

“Eleven a.m. and three p.m. are the peaks, so call it a thousand a week.” Rook shut the ledger. “Now here is the part I want you to hold on to, and then you can throw it at me if you like.

“We did not fix that gate. We could have, in a morning, for four hundred pounds, and it would have been wrong. We went to the school and we said: the term is continuous, it has no gap in it, and it is empty, and it will stay empty until a person is named, and no technician in this city can name one, because naming is the school’s. And the head teacher put in a paper, in her own words, on a Tuesday in January, and named a porter. The porter is on a payroll. The term has not lapsed since. It will lapse the day she leaves, and she has a deputy, and we have the deputy’s name, and we do not use the deputy’s name without the head asking us to.”

Jonas was standing at a gate post in Tarn Street with the file shut in his hands and something going through him that was uncomfortable and was not a fault he could read off a plate.

“You got an institution to name a person,” he said.

“We got an institution to name a person.”

“That is — yes. That is the thing that holds a term up.”

“It is the only thing.” Rook said it without any weight at all, which made it worse. “Mr Mercer, I have read your file and I am not being clever. A hinge is not held up by iron. It is held up by somebody who is findable. Every one of your records in that ledger of mine that is *held by neither* — and there are two hundred and sixty of them out of two thousand and ninety — every one of them is empty in exactly one way, and the way is that there is nobody to ring.”

The head teacher came out of the gatehouse while he was saying it, and Rook introduced himself and gave her the name and the number and did not ask for a meeting, which Jonas noticed, because it is what a man does when he has already made his point to the only person in the building who can act on it.

Mrs Morah Bonsu, fifty-one, had a lanyard on and a register under her arm and no interest in either of them, and she listened to about a minute of it and then said, “We named a porter on the ninth of January. It took me a morning and a telephone call to the education office and it took two words from a man in a shed, and I have written to the education office asking them to put it into a procedure so that next time it is not a man in a shed.”

“Then I will write to the education office as well,” said Rook, and got out a pen, and did, standing up, on a gatehouse wall.

Jonas asked her one question, and he asked it badly, and she answered it anyway.

“Mrs Bonsu — if your school had no roll, and no address, and the building itself were on a list of things that do not have a civic function — who would you be? Who is the porter? Who stands at that gate?”

She looked at him for a moment longer than the question deserved.

“Then I would refuse,” she said. “I would refuse, and the gate would be a latch, and I would stand at it myself on the way in and on the way out, for as long as it took, and I would not need anybody’s permission, because a head teacher holding her own gate is not a permission. It is a woman holding a door.” She shifted the register under her arm. “And I would be frightened, Mr Mercer. I would not do it for one term. I would do it for a fortnight and then I would find a porter and a rota and I would hand it over at a handover, out loud, to somebody I trust — and I would still be standing on that corner at seven in the morning for the rest of my life, and that is what it costs, and I have paid it, and I am not complaining about it in front of a stranger.”

Rook looked at Jonas for slightly too long.

“That,” he said, “is the sentence I have been trying to sell for four years, and I have never once managed to sell it, and you have just watched a head teacher hand it over for nothing outside a school gate at eleven in the morning.”

---

Gantry Ward, a low shed behind the freight gate with the shutters up, and inside it a room that Jonas had never seen the like of and had to look at for a moment before he could work out what it was.

A board on the far wall, four metres long, ruled into columns, chalked and re-chalked. **OPEN · CLOSED · DIAGNOSED · WAITING PARTS · BACK TO CONSUMER.** Under the headings, a hundred and forty lines and every line a hinge and every line a number and, at the end of each line, three more numbers: days open, days closed, and a figure that was money.

“That is a service floor,” said Rook. “Two hundred and forty people on my books. Forty-one of them are on this board at this moment and nine of the forty-one are not my staff. The nine are municipal. Two are the council’s drainage department and seven are contractors CivicCore sublets to because I could not hire them, and I do not lie about that, and their names are on the board with my name underneath, because if a hinge is mine for four hours and then it is not, somebody has to be able to see whose it was.”

Jonas walked up to it and read it. Behind him, Rook let him.

“Do you know what is on the right-hand wall,” said Rook.

“No.”

“Clause nine.” Rook got out a folder and put a single sheet on the table, face up. “Continuity participation, standard terms. Clause nine is disconnection for non-payment. Not a threat — a procedure. Sixty days’ notice, one visit, a photograph of the meter, supply isolated. I have had it once in four years, in January, to a laundrette in Kestrel Hill over eleven hundred pounds, and I would do it again and I did not enjoy it. The owner had my letter eleven weeks before anything happened and paid on the day it arrived, and we have never spoken about it, and he still has our service.”

“Clause nine on a block of flats is different.”

“It is not, and I will tell you exactly where the difference is, and you are not going to like it, and I have been waiting all morning to say it because I have wanted to show you Wren Court since the first minute.” Rook shut the folder. “Wren Court. Salter’s Yard. Thirty-one rooms above a chandlery in the Tideglass docks, all thirty-one lived in, and the building has no civic address — not a bad one, none. It has never had one. The ward says unassigned, the water authority says unassigned, and thirty-one households have been paying CivicCore a monthly participation fee for a standing hinge, hot water, a working stair light, and a door that has not once failed since we went in, for fourteen months.”

“Signed by whom?”

“By a steward. There is a steward. Her name is on the contract and she is findable at eight in the morning and her telephone number is printed on the card in your hand.” Rook looked at him. “Mr Mercer, Wren Court gets *better service than the west clinic*. I know that. I priced it. I have run the numbers on the two of them side by side and Wren Court is cheaper per household and has a shorter failure time and a named steward and a clause nine. And the reason it has a named steward is that we went in and said: a service needs a holder, a holder needs to be findable, here is the price, here is the term, here is what happens on the last day, and if you do not want it, we go and you are as you were, and the as-you-were for Wren Court in January last year was nine days with no water in thirty-one rooms.”

Jonas had nothing. He stood at a board in a shed in Gantry Ward and looked for the thing he always looked for, which was the place where the promise and the person came apart, and there was not one, and the reason there was not one was that thirty-one people had been asked and had answered.

“That is not a fair comparison,” he said. “You did not find them. They are not — nobody chose to live there.”

“Nobody chooses where they can afford to live in this city either, and that is not a defence of the department, it is just true, and I have stopped arguing it.” Rook was not being combative; he was worse than that, he was being exact. “Here is my argument, and it is one sentence, and I have given it to a council committee, and they wrote it in a report and did not like it.

“A price is more honest than a queue. In your city, a citizen with a failing hinge has no queue and no price, which means he has nothing at all, and the nothing is invisible, and the invisible thing gets whatever the man in the room has time for that day. Mine has a number on a wall. I have never in four years refused a hinge, and there is not one in this city that I have not answered, and the day I cannot answer it, it is written on that board in chalk with a date, and anybody may come and read the chalk.”

“And the day after the contract ends?”

“Everything goes back to a queue,” said Rook. “It always does. There is no version of this where the money stops and the service continues, and I have never pretended otherwise, and the honest thing to say about that is that the queue is a thing everybody already lives in and the price is a thing that can be refused, and refusing a price is a thing a person can do, and refusing a queue is not a thing anybody has ever been able to do.”

Jonas put his hand on the edge of the table.

“Then say the other one back to me,” he said. “A claim survives the responder. If your man goes tonight, the claim on that door does not go with him. It is still owed by somebody, and the plate remembers it, and it can be served. A price stops at the invoice.”

“Then name the claim,” said Rook. “Go back to your street with the brown water and name the claim and tell me who is served with it. I have never in my life received one. Four years, two hundred and forty customers, four hundred and eleven hinge claims, and every one of them was a fault report, because a claim is a thing that somebody owes somebody, and in this city nobody owes anybody. They just need things doing.”

He shut the ledger.

“One more thing, because you have been standing at that board for six minutes without once reading the money column. I noticed. I am not going to use it.

“There are two nodes in your file I will not price. I do not know what they cost to run and I will not guess and I will not send anybody to find out. And I am going to tell you why there is a contract in front of the Assembly in four days and no budget request, and it is because of what you wrote in a box on the fourteenth of March. Not because you were wrong. Because a service with a price on it has a holder and a claim and a name, and a service without one has a classification, and the classification is *non-civic plant*, and the man who wrote it did it in eleven minutes because nobody could give him a name, and I do not blame him for one of the eleven minutes.”

“Somebody could have.”

“Somebody could have, at any point in three hundred years, and nobody has, and I have offered them money, and I am not going to stand here and tell you that a street of fifty-nine people refusing to sign anything is a failure of civic courage.” Rook stopped. “I have read your registrar’s answer and the paper somebody pinned to your board and the minute of the twenty-fourth, and I know about that door, and the last thing in the minute is a time somebody wrote in pencil on a board. That is the best-managed hinge in this city and it is not in my book and I will not have it, because the day it is in my book it is a service, and the day it is a service it is a customer, and you have a woman on that street who has told you what happens to a customer. I am not going to be the man who teaches her.”

“Nobody may put a name to that service,” Jonas said.

“I know,” said Rook. “That is why there is a contract in front of the Assembly in four days and no budget request, and I would like you to sit with that for a second, because I do not think you have.”

“There is a queue,” said Jonas. “You keep saying it as though it were a thing on your side. It is not on your side. It is nineteen days on a board in a shop in River Stacks and it is a woman of sixty-eight reading the same five lines out loud to a man of twenty-six every week because there is nobody else to read them to, and you have just told me a price is more honest than that, and you are right, and I would like it noted that I agree with you, because it is the first time in a month that a man has said something to me about this city that I believe entirely, and it has cost me nothing, and it is going to make the rest of my week much harder.”

“There is always a queue,” said Rook. “You are standing in front of it every day of your life. I would like, before I go, to be allowed to finish one sentence in this city.”

Jonas did not say anything, and Alden Rook put his hand out, and Jonas shook it, and Rook said, “If it ever comes — and I do not think it will, and you are right that it will not — do not bring me up that path first. Bring the street first. I want to be fourth.”

Then he got into the van, and got out again, and stood on the kerb with the ledger under his arm, which meant he was not going to be allowed to leave yet.

“One more, and then I am gone, and I am giving it to you on a pavement rather than in a room with a table. You have four days until that review, you are the reporting officer, and your name is the name on the recommendation. I am going to tell you what is going to happen, because nobody in that room will explain it to you and I would rather you heard it from a man who wants a contract.

“A panel cannot act on the absence of a finding. It can act on a finding. So if nobody makes one, nothing happens — and a file with an open item in it is a file a committee has to clear on the morning it sits, and the cheapest way to clear one is to accept the reporting officer.” He put a finger on the van door. “Royden Achebe has written a remedy. Four lines, dated the twenty-second of March, saying both nodes are non-civic plant and neither carries a civic function, with your name under it.”

“I know what it says under it. It is my name. I wrote the words and he wrote the sentence and between the two of us we made a thing that is on a public board.”

“You have told your own registrar that you have no basis for a finding, and that is true, and it is the most expensive sentence anybody in this city has said this year.” Rook opened the door. “Hold it. Do not lead with it. And when they ask you in that room what those two nodes are for, do not tell them. Tell them you have no basis for a finding, and let the silence sit there, and let them be the ones who have to break it.”

“That is advice.”

“It is the only free thing I have got,” said Rook, “and I am giving it to a man who agreed with me on a pavement about a price, and I am not unaware of what that does to my argument, and I am not sorry.”

He shut the door and went up Marsh Lane and left Jonas Mercer with four days, a sentence he could not put in a document, and a review going exactly the way the worst possible afternoon had predicted.

The CivicCore van was still in the yard at half past five and the barrier gate was still working and the young man with the lanyard was on his telephone eating an apple.

Bea had the bench to herself and a cold cup of tea and the look of a woman who had been waiting six hours for something.

“Did you have an argument?” she said.

“No.”

“Right.” She drank the cold tea. “Then you are the first person in a fortnight to come back from a meeting in this city with no argument, and I want to say something about it and then I am going to bed. It is worth more than his numbers. If you stand up in that room on Tuesday and say *I could not answer him*, the people who want that contract are going to hear a technician admit that the department has nothing, and that is a thing that can be used, and you will not be able to take it back.”

“I am not going to say that.”

“No,” said Bea Osunde. “You have already decided, and you decided it in a room over a tool-hire place, and that is the part I mind.”
