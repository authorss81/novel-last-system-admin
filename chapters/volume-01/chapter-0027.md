# Chapter 0027 — What Is Under the Sill

The correction came at ten past four on Friday morning, the fourth and last of the working days, on a chart that Jonas had taken off the wall of the net loft at half past three because he had not been able to sleep and had gone down to the kitchen to look at the tide table by the light of a torch like a man who has run out of other things to do with his hands.

Margo Doré came in at ten to five for the ebb and found him at her table with the almanac open and the lamp on and the bar, the paint pen, the two-pound lump of a cold chisel and the folded sheet of paper with the tide times on it spread across the corner of the table.

“You have been up all night,” she said.

“No. I have been awake for two hours, which is the same thing at my age and cheaper.” He turned the chart round to face her. “Margo. I have got this wrong and I have had it wrong since Saturday, and I have had it wrong in your house, and I am going to say so before you put the kettle on, and then I am going to tell you what I got wrong, and I am going to be right, and I am sorry about the order those two things come in.”

Margo Doré put the kettle down.

“Go on.”

“The work does not go in a neap.” He put his finger on the almanac. “Saturday I told you the springs are two and a half metres and the neaps are a metre and a bit, and that the water in that chamber does not go anywhere in a spring, and I repeated it back to you as if I had thought about it, and I had not thought about it, I had memorised it off a table. Look at the low water column. On a spring low, which is today, the water goes down to two metres four. On a neap low, which is the twenty-fourth, it comes down to one metre and five centimetres and stops.”

Margo did not answer for about eight seconds, and then she said, “Go on, and do not do the voice.”

“The floor of that chamber is a metre sixty below datum. I have a tape on it and I put a mark on the wall of it with a paint pen on the first morning. One metre sixty.” He was not being triumphant; he had a piece of paper with a number on it and a woman of sixty-three was about to find out that it was wrong, and he could feel the whole shape of it in his stomach. “In a neap it comes down to one-five. The sill beam sits on the floor with its top face a metre ten below datum. So on a neap low there are forty centimetres of water standing over that beam, and it never comes clear of it, and forty centimetres is not a hole a man can get into. In a spring the low is two-four and the chamber empties and there is eight hundred millimetres of freeboard over the sill, and that is a hole a man can get into, and it is the only kind there is at this end of the estuary.”

Margo Doré put both hands flat on the almanac.

“And in a spring,” she said, “it goes down to two-four, and it comes back up at four hundred an hour, and the chamber is two metres deep, and the ladder rungs are set for somebody with two hands and one bucket, and that is four minutes, and that is what I told a room full of people on Saturday morning at twenty to seven in the morning and you all let me say it.”

“You told them something true about the flood. You told them the wrong thing about the ebb.”

“I told them the wrong thing about the ebb,” said Margo, “and I told it in the voice, and I have been telling it since the flood year, and my father told it before me, and I have got twenty-nine minutes before the low and I would like to say the rest of it now, and I would like to say it properly, without performing anything.”

She straightened up.

“Twenty-nine years I have said the work goes in a neap. It is the thing you say on this street. You do not work a chamber in a spring because a spring is when it comes. And I have said it so many times that I never once sat down and asked whether a spring is also when it *goes*, and it is, and it goes further, and I have been standing in the wrong fortnight of the month for twenty-nine years, and nobody caught me because there has never been anybody in a position to catch me, and the reason there has not is that I am the only person on this street who can read a tide table and that has been treated as a reason to trust it, and it has never once been tested by anybody with something to gain from finding it wrong.”

She looked at the two pieces of rotten beam in the yard through the door.

“It has been tested now,” she said. “By a man with a chart. On the last morning we have got. So say the rest of it, because you have not said the rest of it and you have been standing there for a minute.”

“The rest of it is that we have three mornings of bank work done and dry, and the stone is on, and the bank is good, and the bank will be there in April. And we have one morning. Today, the twenty-first, low water at five past five, and it is the deepest low of the month, and the chamber will empty to a metre and fifty and I will be able to get the sill beam out and put the new one in and rehang the flap and set it to shut on the flood. And if we do not do it today, then the next spring low that clears that beam is a fortnight away, and a fortnight is the seventh of the month after, and I have a document that says the two of us have to stand in a room about an answer to a registrar, and the answer is due on the twenty-eighth, and the Reclamation Season review is the first, and I do not have the hands for both.”

“All right,” said Margo Doré. “Then it is today. And I am going to say the thing that has to be said about that, and then we are going to wake everybody up.” She took the almanac off him and closed it. “It means the paper on the wall is wrong. It says *the ebb before the flood, five ebb mornings*, and there is one morning left on it, and the twenty-first at four o’clock, and there are six seats on that morning. One of them has not been filled since Saturday and I am not going to fill it in an hour before low water, and I have crossed another one myself on Monday and I am not going to uncross that one either.”

“I know.”

“And the one that matters is the bank. It is tonight, and the flood is at ten past two in the morning, on the Saturday, and Perdita is going to be on it because Perdita has a lamp and a habit and eleven years of Sundays, and I have not asked her one single question about it because I did not want to hear the answer, and it is ten past two in the morning, and I have never once in my life been the person on a bank and I am not going to start.”

“Nobody is asking you to.”

“I know nobody is asking me,” said Margo. “That is what I have just realised, and it has taken me four days, and it is the whole of what I have learned in four days. Nobody is asking. There is a piece of paper with a ring round a blank space on it and nobody is asking, and I have spent four days waiting to be asked and being extremely careful not to volunteer, and that is a thing I have done on this street my whole life and I have never once noticed myself doing it until a man with a tide table told me I had it the wrong way round.”

The ebb on the morning of Friday the twenty-first went down further than it had gone all week, and it made a noise doing it, and the water went out of the chamber the way water goes out of a bath, with a lip against the concrete, and left the floor standing in the silt with the ladder rungs coming out of it one at a time and the paint mark Jonas had put on the wall standing nine inches clear of the mud.

Margo Doré was right about the four minutes. She was also, Jonas understood, entirely wrong about the four minutes, in the only way a person can be wrong about four minutes: the flood did not take four. It took two and a half, and then it came in over the path, and the flap — which was a disc of green iron four feet across on a frame that Jonas and Bo had shored up on Wednesday with two lengths of scaffold and a piece of road plate — dropped, and came down on the sill with a bang that Bo felt through his boots, and sat there.

And it closed.

That was the whole of the century’s argument on this bank, and it took a second and a half.

“It is shut,” said Margo, from the top, shouting. “Jonas. It is shut. It has shut on the ebb, which is wrong, that is wrong, that is what I have been saying for twenty-nine —”

“No,” he said. “It has not shut on the ebb. Look at the water level.”

She looked. The level inside the chamber was still falling. The flap was hanging in air with nothing behind it.

“It is standing open,” Jonas said, “and it is going to shut on the flood, in about two and a half hours, and that is what it is for, and it has been doing that for eleven years without anybody knowing it, and if somebody has been shutting it by hand on the ebb for twenty-nine years, then the bank has been full of water behind it for twenty-nine years, and that is the boil, and that is the whole of the boil, and it has not been a mystery for thirty years, it has been an instruction that nobody could read.”

He got up the ladder and stood on the bank in the dark with the water going out of the dip behind him and the light coming up behind the boat sheds.

Then they cut the sill out.

That is the part of a job like this that nobody outside it understands: that the finding is not a moment. It is two hours and forty minutes with a junior angle grinder and a bucket and two people swapping every four minutes because the noise in a chamber that size is a physical danger, and the middle third of a beam comes out in three pieces and a great deal more dust than anyone expects, and the water is coming back the entire time, and there is a clock in three people's heads and no clock in the room.

The rot went right through, in the middle, for about six hundred millimetres, and it was honey-coloured and dry in the middle and wet at the two ends, which is how you know.

Underneath the frame, in the corner where the sill sat on the concrete, there was a lead seal.

It was a disc of lead about the size of a pound coin, the size of a coin because a man in a boiler suit had put it there with a purpose and a hole punch and about ten seconds, and it had been pushed up into the angle between the frame leg and the concrete and forgotten about, and it had a die-stamp on it that was as legible as if it had been struck yesterday.

> **6-L/14 · SERVICE · LOWLAND WORKS · XI·17 · S&D IW**

Eleven years. Jonas looked at that for a while and could not make it into anything, and then he stopped trying, and made himself look at the other thing, which was the one he had not been able to stop looking at since Bo handed the beam out of the hole and turned it over and said, *there is writing on the bottom of that*, and had then, correctly, handed the beam to nobody at all and put it down on the grass and stepped back.

The underside of the rotten beam, where the timber had gone to nothing, had a paint line on it in a colour that had gone to rust, in a small neat hand, and it said:

> **DO NOT CLOSE ON THE EBB.**
> **CLOSE ON THE FLOOD.**
> **6-L/14 — LOWLANDS — 8·III**

And on the beam's other cheek, cut with a cold chisel into sound wood at the end, two letters and a punch:

> **E·M**

Bo read it upside down from the top of the hole, over Margo's shoulder, and said, “That is two letters.”

“That is two letters,” Jonas said.

“It is not a signature. It is a fitter's mark. That is what a fitter puts on a thing he has fitted, so that when it comes back in four years somebody can ask him what he did.”

“I know what a fitter's mark is.”

“Then say what the two letters are, because you have had them in your mouth for a minute and a half and you have not said them.”

Jonas Mercer knelt in the mud at the bottom of a chamber at the end of a lowland street at twenty past seven in the morning with a beam in both hands — his left hand on it, his right hand not on it at all, because his right hand has not been able to close properly since a plate at a clinic entrance eight days ago — and he had a piece of painted timber in his hands that said *do not close on the ebb* in a hand that was old when that beam went into a hole in the ground, and two letters cut into the sound end of it, and the seal on the frame with a date eleven years ago on it in the die of a district ironworks.

He said: “E. M.”

“That is two letters,” said Bo.

“Yes.”

“That is not two words. You have not said two words. Every man on this street has been saying two letters for a minute and a half and you have not said two words.”

“Bo,” said Jonas, “the ironworks seal is eleven years ago, and my father has been dead for eighteen. If those are his letters, then the ironworks sent a man whose initials are the same as a dead Continuity Project engineer’s initials, and that is not a fact, that is a coincidence, and I have known it is a coincidence for about ninety seconds and I have been holding on to it for ninety seconds like a man holding a rail on a moving boat, and I am not going to pretend to you that I have not.”

Bo Trimble, seventeen, on the bank above a hole, at twenty-five past seven in the morning, with a rotary hammer running in a bucket to keep the neighbours awake, said: “You have got a drawer with a number in it that came out of the city four days after you fixed a door, and you have never once said whose name that number belongs to, and I have not asked you, because I have watched six people on this street get asked by a man with a form and it did not go well for any of them.” He put his hands in his pockets. “So that is two letters, and it is yours, and I do not know anything about your father, and I am telling you that I am not going to carry your two letters up that path for you, and that if you want to write them on anything you have to ask somebody who is not me.”

“I am not going to write them on anything.”

“You are going to want to.”

“I am going to want to,” Jonas said. “That is what you have just said to me, and you are the first person on this street who has said it, and I want you to know that I heard it, and that I am telling you now so that if I ever do, you have got a date on it.”

The sill went in at ten past eleven, on a bed of new compound, and the new beam was seasoned ash off a merchant's yard in Saltmouth and cost one hundred and forty pounds, and the church hall tin had a hundred and one in it, and Aurrie Nkemdirim found the other thirty-nine out of the exchange tin without saying a word about it at the time, and Margo Doré worked it out on the Sunday from the shoe box and did not raise it, and crossed it off the list of things she was not going to argue about that month. The flap went back on its new pintle at half past one and swung free, and Jonas set the stops on it himself with a spanner in his left hand, and checked it three times, and the third time was because he did not trust the first two.

The barrier at the end of that day, and Jonas put it in writing for the loft's file and for nothing else, in a hand that was very bad:

> **THE FLAP NOW SHUTS ON THE FLOOD AND NOT ON THE EBB. DROPPED AND HUNG OPEN AT 07:38 ON 21 MARCH, ON THE MAKING, WITH FOUR PEOPLE PRESENT. CLOSED ITSELF ON THE FLOOD AT 02:10 ON 22 MARCH, UNATTENDED.**
> **THE BANK IS CUT BACK AND REVETTED WITH 4 TONNES. THE SILL IS NEW. THE PACKED COMPOUND UNDER THE OLD SILL WAS A STOPGAP AND WAS NOT THE REPAIR.**
> **THIS WILL HOLD AN ORDINARY TIDE AND A GREAT DEAL OF RAIN. IT WILL NOT HOLD A SURGE.**
> **THE FIRST SPRING FLOOD AFTER THE WORK WAS THE SAME NIGHT, AND ONE PERSON WAS ON THE BANK, AND SHE PUT HER OWN NAME ON NOTHING.**
> **NO NAME IS ON THAT LINE AND THE LINE IS STILL ON THE PAPER.**

“Perdita was on it,” said Margo.

“Perdita was on it from half past eleven until about ten past four and she rang nobody and she rang the loft at ten past two when the water came over the path and she said *it is here* and hung up, and she has told me that she has never in her life rung anybody at that hour for anything less, and she is not going to be thanked for it, and if anybody in this kitchen thanks her tonight I will leave.”

Nobody thanked her.

And then the argument about the two things that had come out of the hole, which took until three in the morning and which Jonas was not permitted to have.

The lead seal and the beam went into an oilcloth envelope, and the envelope went into a tin, and the argument was not about the tin. It was about who was allowed to know where the tin was.

“You cannot have it,” said Aurrie Nkemdirim, to Jonas, before anybody had asked. “You understand that. You have not come down here to look at a piece of wood with something written on it. That is not a favour, that is a category error, and if you take that up that path in a bag then in a year you will have a departmental file with a maintenance history in it that you built out of a beam, and we will be in it, and every name in it will be one of ours.”

“I was not going to—”

“You were going to write the number in your pad,” said Aurrie, “and you were going to put six-L-fourteen in a departmental document, and it would be a good sentence, and it would be true, and it would put us in a file. And then the file is a reason.”

“It is due on the twenty-eighth.”

“Then it is due on the twenty-eighth without it,” said Aurrie, “and you will write *the maintenance history of 6-L/14 could not be produced*, which is what you already wrote to the registrar, and you will write it twice, and it will be true both times, and nobody will be able to help you, and that is what a no looks like when it costs something.”

Hector Mbatha asked for the tin.

He asked for it in a way that nobody expected, at half past one in the morning, standing up in a kitchen with his coat on, and the room went silent, and he stood there and said the thing he had wanted to say for thirty-two years.

“I have wanted a number since 1994,” he said. “And I have carried a notebook for thirty-two years and I have wanted, every year, for one thing to come up out of this street that had the city’s own stamp on it. And there is a lead disc with the city’s node number on it and a date on it and a district ironworks’ die on it, and I want it, and I am not going to have it, and I want you all to hear me say the second part out loud, because if I do not say it out loud I will think about it for a year and then I will come back for it.”

He put his hand flat on the table.

“The reason I do not want it is that a thing that is held is a thing that is yours. I have watched that on this street. There was a plate on this wall and it was held by a person, and that person was tired, and the plate was scraped and put in the canal, and I have never known who did either half of that, and it has been on that wall for longer than I have been alive. And I have a number in a book in a drawer and I have wanted a district number since before I could drive, and I am telling this kitchen that the difference between a number you count yourself and a number you are given is the difference between a man and a subject, and I am not going to be a subject, and neither are you, and I would like the sentence kept.”

Ottie Beck took the tin.

She took it without a speech, in the way a person takes a thing that they have decided to take, and she put it inside her cardigan against her ribs and said, “Right. It goes in the loft, in the tin with the spares, and it is not in a tin with a label on it, and I am seventy-one and there is not a registrar alive who is going to come up that path and take an inventory of a seventy-one-year-old district nurse's spares tin. And nobody on this street is to tell the county or the department or anybody in Saint Orra that it exists, and if anybody asks, and they will ask, the answer is that the loft keeps a tin.”

“And if they ask the department?”

“Then the department does not know,” said Ottie, “and the department will have to say so in whatever it writes, and that is a thing you can live with, and it is a thing I have been living with since 1998 with four people and a rota and no address.”

And Jonas Mercer, in a kitchen at ten past three in the morning on a street with fifty-nine doors and no district, put his left hand flat on the table and his right hand in his pocket and said the only thing that was his to say, and which was that he was not going to be a person who had been in a hole.

“Write this down,” he said. “On the wall, with the other three names on it. *The sill of the lowland flap at the bottom of the dip was taken out on the morning of Friday the twenty-first of March, in a spring ebb, by six people from this street and one technician from the city whose name the department already has. It was rotten through the middle third. The flap now shuts on the flood. The repair will not hold a surge. There is no name on the bank for tonight or for the next flood. The department's answer about the maintenance history of this node is due on the twenty-eighth and it says the history could not be produced, and the reason it could not be produced is sitting in a tin in the loft and it is not going in a file.*”

“Write it,” said Aurrie Nkemdirim, “and put the last bit in the middle, and leave room round it.”

She did, and it is still there, under the plate with half its face scraped off, in paint that has not yet gone chalky, above three names on a sheet of paper and a name nobody has ever put down, and one more line underneath, in a different hand, in the same paint, which says *21 MARCH. THE SILL OUT, THE BEAM IN, THE FLAP ON NEW STOPS. SIX OF US AND ONE FROM THE CITY. THE BEAM WAS A HUNDRED AND FORTY POUNDS AND A THIRTY-NINE POUNDS OF IT WAS MINE. — A.N.*

Jonas walked up the towpath at half past three with the chart in his bag and nothing else at all in his hands, and the water was coming back over the path behind him and the stand was shut and the stand’s plate had a term in it that was not his, and at the top of the slip he took out his pad and looked at the page where he had written LO/6-L/14 on the second afternoon of his life in this city, in a records room, in a hurry, with a question mark after it, which was the thing Bea Osunde had told him not to do eleven days ago.

He looked at it for a while.

Then he put the pad in his coat and went home to sleep for three hours, and did not write it anywhere, and had to do that on purpose, and it was the hardest thing he did that month.
