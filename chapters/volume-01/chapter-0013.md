# Chapter 0013 — The First Mended Term

Ruth woke him at ten to five by telephone, and by the time he reached the clinic ramp at seven minutes to six, the sky over Saint Orra Avenue was the color of a knife left in water.

She had the black key out and her coat on, which meant she had dressed in the dark, which meant she had not gone home.

“Again?” he said.

“I have not been home,” Ruth said. “I went to my sister’s for two hours and slept with my clothes on. Do not make that a thing.” She held up the log. “Forty-two minutes past five, I wrote a request for attended civic maintenance at this hinge, requested by the night watch, scope and expiry attached, and I read it twice, and if you want to argue with the wording do it now and not at six in the morning.”

“Nobody is arguing.”

“Because you have not been awake long enough.” She stepped aside so he could see the plate. “Sanaa is inside. Mina is inside. Nobody else is.”

Sanaa came down the ramp with two cups of tea and gave him one.

“I have written on the witness line that I am witnessing a change I understand and not certifying that the door is safe,” she said. “I want you to hear me say it while you are still able to hear anything at all. You look terrible.”

“I slept.”

“You slept for a while, then you read a plate, and now it is six in the morning, and a cup of tea is not a person who has been standing on their feet since yesterday evening.” She held the cup until he took it. “That is a professional observation and not a kindness.”

He set out his tools on the ramp lip: the brass probe, a ceramic driver he did not expect to need, a grounding strap, the field reader, and the bond, which he clipped to the inside of the case lid so he could not lose it in the grass.

Then he put his left hand flat on the plate.

The Margin came up fast this time, faster than at the bridge, and the two terms came with it: the engraved rule about a person being present, and the pencil window belonging to a woman with a contract that ended at eleven. Between them, the empty place. It was narrower than it had been the night before, because now there was a page on the counter with words on it that a person had agreed to, and the empty place had something to push against.

He read as far as the edge of what the plate would let him read and stopped there, because past that point the view stops being evidence and starts being an invitation.

> **ONE FIELD EMPTY.**
> **Precedence unset.**
> **The issuer answers for what he writes.**

“All right,” Jonas said. “Ruth, I need you on the probe.”

He had learned this in the worst possible order. The first time, at the bridge, Nora Bell had been chosen because she was steady. The second time, at the pump, Emeka Rusk had been chosen because she knew the machine. This time he chose a woman he had known for two days, who had spent both of them being the answer to a question nobody had asked her, and he told her so, because if he did not say it out loud he would be doing the thing he had been doing since the first morning, which was choosing people for their usefulness.

“You are not useful to me,” he said. “You are the bearer. If the plate asks a question, the answer is yours, not mine, and if it asks me to override you I am going to say no and you are going to overrule me.”

“That is a good speech,” Ruth said, and put her palm over his on the probe. “Do it now. It is one sentence now.”

He braced the grounding strap against the door frame and set the ceramic driver into the empty field, and he wrote the answer to the question the plate had been refusing to answer for thirty years.

*This term answers to no unattended access for the three cases named above.*

The field took it in one movement, the way wet paper takes ink, and the pain arrived before the plate had finished closing.

It started in his right wrist and ran up the inside of his arm, and then it was everywhere at once: the corridor lights going flat and far away, Mina’s voice arriving in one piece from very far off, and Ruth’s hand suddenly the only real object in the world. He heard the building’s alarm strip make a sound that was not an alarm. He smelled hot copper and scorched dust and, absurdly, cut grass. His right hand stopped answering entirely.

He would have gone on. That was the thing about it. If Ruth had not been there, he would have gone on, because going on was the only move he had and stopping was the move that had cost a woman thirty-eight minutes in a taxi.

Ruth’s voice, low and with no doubt in it at all: “Jonas. Let go of it. I am the bearer, and I am telling you to let go of it, and I will still be here in ten minutes, and so will the plate.”

He let go.

The field closed over itself. The three lamps came up in a slow sequence, and the engraving under the paint — *NO UNATTENDED ACCESS* — stayed exactly where it had always been, and beside it a new line sat in the ceramic face in a hand that was not a hand, flat and even and utterly without anybody’s opinion in it. Then a printer slot he had never seen on that plate went tick, and turned, and pushed a narrow strip of paper out into the dawn.

The strip said the term had been accepted, and accepted only the field he had written. It said the term ended at six in the morning six days after the first service window. It said nothing else at all.

He put his left hand on the ramp and found that he could not get up without Mina’s knee against his shoulder.

Sanaa crouched in front of him and took his wrist and turned it over without asking, and he flinched, and she put it down.

“Tell me what you feel,” she said.

“Nothing. From the wrist to the second knuckle.”

“That is a nerve, not a hand. Keep it lifted and do not make a fist with it.” She looked at his face and then at the plate. “What did you write?”

“The precedence. That the term answers to the old rule for the three cases. Not the whole thing. One field.”

“Who is the bearer?”

“Ruth.”

“Who issues it?”

“Me.”

“Who is responsible for the corridor at two in the morning on the sixth day, if nobody is on it?”

He tried to answer and found that he could not, because the answer was on the printed sheet that had come out of the plate’s slot, and the sheet was face down on the ramp, and Mina turned it over with two fingers so that nobody had to bend.

It was a schedule, printed on a strip the width of a till receipt, and it was in the language of every municipal form he had ever been handed, and it had a line in it that was not a duty anybody had discussed.

> **WEST CLINIC CARE ENTRANCE — SERVICE SCHEDULE**
> **Bearer of record: NWOSU, R., night watch.**
> **Window on record: 23:00–06:00.   Window declared by the holder: 22:00–11:00.**
> **11:00–07:00 — HOLDER NOT NAMED.**
> **07:00–23:00 — HOLDER NOT NAMED.**
> **IF NO HOLDER IS ON DUTY: the route is held by the issuer.**
> **ISSUER FALLBACK — MERCER, J.**
> **AVAILABILITY: acknowledged per shift.**

Ruth took the strip out of Mina’s hand.

She read it once, and then she read the second line again, and the color went out of her face in a way that made the corridor colder.

“It has got my night an hour short at one end and five hours short at the other,” she said. “I came on at ten last night and I am here until eleven this morning, and this thing says the door is only mine from eleven at night until six in the morning. So the two hours at the ends of my shift are the hours the record says nobody is here, and the rest of the time it says I am here whether I am here or not.”

“You wrote that window in pencil on the plate. Years ago. Somebody did.”

“I know who did it, and so do you, and it is not the point.” She did not raise her voice, and it made everything in the corridor much more serious. “The term is mended. My hours are not. Those are two holes and you have closed the one that was easier to close.” She tapped the strip with a thumbnail. “Read the sixth line out loud.”

Jonas read it out loud.

“Read the sixth one.”

He read the sixth one.

“Now read the seventh,” Ruth said, “and tell me what it means in a corridor at four in the morning.”

“It means that if nobody is here, the door is held by me.”

“It means the door is held by you whether or not you are in the building, on the strength of a signature you gave at a table in River Stacks.” Ruth folded the strip once and put it in her coat pocket, which was a thing Jonas understood immediately to be a form of seizure. “That is not a schedule. That is a man with a door.”

“It is what the plate will hold.”

“Then the plate will hold it for six days and we will read it every day.” She looked at him for a long moment. “You have made my watch into a job with a name. I do not know yet whether to thank you or bill you.”

The first service window opened at seven.

Mina brought a stretcher up the ramp at four minutes past seven with a woman of about sixty on it, awake, annoyed, and holding a folder of her own discharge papers in one hand, and the plate took the claim, and the door opened in under a minute with Ruth standing on the corridor, and the whole thing took no argument at all.

The second was at a quarter past eight, a hospital supply run returning through the care door with a locked cage of medication for a patient in observation four, put up under that patient’s own care claim, which is the first of the three named cases, and which is why it went through without an argument.

At nine, a laundress from the building next door tried to bring a laundry trolley through the care entrance to the corridor lift, and the plate refused it, and refused it in the correct way, because a laundry trolley is not one of the three named cases, and the old rule about a person on the corridor still held. The laundress was not pleased. Ruth walked out and listened to the entire complaint and came back in and said, “She is right that it is stupid, and she is wrong that it is hers to fix, and I told her so.”

At eleven o’clock Ruth Nwosu signed the handover and put the black key into Mina Okafor’s hand and did not stay for a single extra minute.

“I want that on the page,” she said. “Nineteen years, and I have not once stayed past my own end without being asked. The morning of the bridge test was because nobody could take the key. This morning is because somebody else took the key.”

She was already in her coat when she turned round.

“You will not have me again until you have asked me properly, and I will probably say no,” she said. “Write that down too.”

She was at the top of the ramp when she stopped without turning round.

“And the hours are still wrong. Twenty-three hundred to six hundred, on a plate, in my name, for the rest of the season unless somebody with authority says otherwise. Fix that before somebody’s mother comes up this ramp at ten to seven in the morning and finds a door that thinks I am not here.”

Then she was gone up the ramp and out into the daylight, and the west clinic care entrance stood open behind a bearer with a name on her for the first time in the memory of anybody working that corridor, and Jonas sat on the ramp with his right hand in a sling made of a tea towel and looked at the two lines at the bottom of the strip in Ruth’s coat pocket, ten feet away from him, in a pocket that was not his.

**IF NO BEARER IS ON DUTY: the route is held by the issuer.**

**AVAILABILITY: acknowledged per shift.**
