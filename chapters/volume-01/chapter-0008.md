# Chapter 0008 — The Man Who Filed a Fault

The NDC records room smelled of paper clips and warm toner. It occupied three windowless rooms above the River Stacks shop, where every fault became a file, every file acquired a number, and every number acquired the appearance of a final answer.

Jonas reached it at 3:14 with six photographs, two service logs, one rejected bridge report, and the persistent message that his probation file needed a clean classification.

Nina Calder, the records supervisor, read the message over his shoulder. “Clean classification is not a phrase the department uses.”

“It is a phrase management sent me.”

“Management is allowed to use unfortunate words when it is frightened.”

She had spent twenty years making damaged hinges legible to people who wanted a culprit, a cost, and a form they could sign. She kept a row of paper cups on the windowsill for inspectors who arrived too early and became unpleasant by lunch.

“Show me the hardware portion,” she said.

Jonas put the photographs down in order. The relay. The scorched contact. The old seal. The clinic’s returned acknowledgment. Nina marked the first two **physical** and the third **historical**, then stopped at the access-plate image.

“You are saying the hardware is sound?”

“Not entirely. The relay is repaired. The conflict remains. The plate keeps a route open for an ambulance and an old empty-crossing term tells the same bridge to stay clear. One is a service need. The other is an old promise. The Lattice is honoring both until the overlap becomes a failure.”

“That is a rule conflict, not a mechanical fault.”

“Yes.”

Nina looked at the rejected report on his screen. The records system had accepted his photographs and refused his conclusion. **Incident cannot be closed while a service cause remains unspecified.**

“The system wants you to name a cause,” she said.

“It has one. It is just not the only one.”

“Pick the one that gets the file closed.”

“Hardware only would be false.”

Nina’s mouth tightened. “A false clean file will get your probation revoked. A true unresolved file may do the same thing, just more slowly.”

Jonas did not have an answer that was both honest and harmless. He turned the photographs over. On the last image, the silver ring around the bridge plate held the Continuity Project emblem and its worn initials. He had spent the morning treating I. Vey as a mark that had outlived its manufacturer. The records inventory told a different story.

He searched the municipal index by seal pattern rather than bridge name. The first result was an image from thirty-two years ago: a charter-stone test, a line of access plates, and a title strip reading **CONTINUITY PROJECT—MUNICIPAL MAINTENANCE SERIES**. The initials belonged to the project office, not a company. The plate around the bridge was part of the original series, still carrying an obsolete maintenance clause.

The index beneath it contained a second reference. The record had no readable location. Its district code ended in a cut-off mark, and its fault-filer field was blank. The record number appeared once in an index of lowland service assets, then disappeared.

Jonas copied the code into his private pad. The first four characters matched a service notice in the last folder his mother kept for Evan Mercer. Nadia had not shown him the entire file. She had only handed over the envelope when he asked for the old hardware code, and the paper still carried the smell of a kitchen drawer: onions, dust, and the adhesive of a seal that had been opened too many times.

He had told no one at the NDC that the code might belong to his father. The match was a thread, not proof.

“Historical seal,” Nina said, reading over his shoulder. “Keep the image, but do not turn it into a hidden suspect.”

“It has no readable location.”

“Then it is an index gap.”

“Or the location was removed.”

“Those are not the same.”

Jonas looked at her. “No. But one can cause the other.”

Nina’s face softened, not into agreement but into recognition. She opened a drawer and took out a continuity-assurance form. It was not a private offer. It was an NDC template for a temporary service warranty, the kind of form that let a department call a fault handled if an outside operator accepted the promise. Tomas had warned him about those forms in the shop. A warranty sounded like assistance until one read the clause that made the service conditional on the operator’s schedule.

Nina placed it beside the report. “If you file this as unresolved, management will ask for a continuity warranty. The company will keep the route open longer and the department will owe them a renewal review. That is how the city turns a repair into a favor.”

“I’m not signing a warranty.”

“I know. I am telling you what will arrive after you refuse.”

A clerk knocked on the door. Selah Mora stood outside in a dry coat, holding a sealed olive folder against her chest. Three anonymous consent marks crossed the outer flap beneath the old-canal shelter’s watch seal; no resident name appeared outside. She did not ask to come inside.

Jonas stepped into the corridor.

“This is the custody receipt,” she said. “The completed return-of-care and immediate safety follow-up are written in the local log. The route description and the people who used it are not being copied here.”

“The NDC report can carry a reference.”

“It can carry the reference number and the fact that a local steward holds the full record. My name, the passenger list, and the route stay in this.”

Nina came to the doorway but did not reach for the folder. “The department needs a witness if the record affects a public service.”

“The record affects our service, not your copy,” Selah said. “Each resident can read or correct their own entry. The steward can use the log only to complete the return of care and immediate safety follow-up. It expires at the next route review. If the city wants it later, the resident has to consent.”

“And if there is an emergency?” Jonas asked.

“Then the person in danger gets help through a service someone already holds. You do not turn a sealed folder into a list because you are afraid of not knowing.”

Jonas looked at the seal. The terms were strict enough to frustrate him and clear enough to trust. He had wanted a route he could follow. Selah had given him a boundary.

“We will record a redacted custody reference,” he said. “No names. No full route. No copies.”

Selah nodded. “Write that the dispositions were completed. Do not write that the shelter was safe. The building was closed for a real reason.”

Nina prepared the reference. It contained a receipt number, the date, the service type, and a statement that the full record remained with a local custodian under resident-controlled access. It did not name the shelter, the passengers, the route, or Selah. It did not give the city a new door into the old canal.

When Selah left, the folder went with her.

Jonas returned to the records room with the redacted reference and the I. Vey image. Nina accepted both, then rejected the original classification again.

“Management wants hardware only,” she said.

“Management is wrong.”

“That is not a filing category.”

“It is the accurate cause.”

She looked at the clock, then at his appointment notice in the side tray. His permanent civil-service review had been scheduled in eleven days. The notice said a clean incident record would help. It did not say a true one would be enough.

Jonas changed the report heading to **UNRESOLVED PERMISSION CONFLICT—PHYSICAL RELAY REPAIRED; LOCAL TERM NOT MENDED**. He attached the bridge and clinic timestamps, the attended-window records, the Oren test authorization, the pump inspection, and the redacted custody receipt. He left out the full shelter route, passenger identities, and the residents’ maintenance practice. He added the lowland index reference as a historical note, not a conclusion.

The system accepted the report with a second warning.

Nina printed the receipt. “Your appointment remains pending.”

“I know.”

“Someone will ask whether you can make the city run without a warranty.”

“They can ask.”

“They will ask why you keep choosing the answer that costs you.”

Jonas put the photographs into his work roll. “Because the cheaper answer is usually the one someone else pays for.”

Tomas met him at the stairwell. The union steward had a paper bag in one hand and a spare pair of insulated gloves in the other.

“Management sent a continuity waiver,” Tomas said. “Just the form they use when they want to make a public repair look privately owned.”

“I did not sign it.”

“I know. I came to make sure you did not sign it at midnight after telling yourself the difference was administrative.”

Jonas almost laughed. It would have been closer to a cough.

Tomas looked at the warning number on the receipt. “The night clinic’s western hinge is failing. Same service chain. The watch record is incomplete again. They have a patient transfer after nightfall.”

Jonas checked the time. The day had already spent most of its light, and the next shift would arrive before the city had decided whether the old watch counted as work.

He took the gloves.

“First we inspect the hinge,” he said.

“Then you tell someone what it costs.”

“That is what I do.”

Tomas’s expression did not soften. “Tonight, you do it before you decide the cost belongs only to you.”
