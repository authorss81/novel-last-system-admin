# Chapter 0045 — The Surge Comes on the Seventeenth

The weather came in from the north-east on the Thursday afternoon and it did not feel like weather, it felt like a schedule being carried out faster than forecast, and by nine o'clock in the evening the wind had gone round to the west and the rain had stopped, which on the seventeenth of April was the most frightening thing of all, because the estuary does not flood on rain and floods on a volume of water coming down from four counties at once.

---

On the Thursday afternoon, twenty minutes to five, which is nine and a half hours before the flap shut itself, Aurrie Nkemdirim issued a term, and nobody asked her to.

Nobody could have asked her. The net-loft has never had a number. The plate the loft keeps came off that wall in March and went into the canal, and what is screwed to the wall above the range now is a watch plate that names a flood year and no service and has not asked anybody anything since. There is no term on the door of that building and there has not been one since March, and a building with no number does not get a plate, and that is not a failure of a plate.

What there was on that floor at twenty to five on a Thursday was a forecast and a room full of people who had read it. Two point nine metres above spring high at Saltmouth for the small hours of Friday, ten past three, and a tide sheet nailed to a hardboard in a yard in River Stacks with four nails somebody had to bend, and about thirty people and four children in a childcare exchange that runs in the day. The room at the top of the stairs had Marigold Adjei, seventy-seven, in it, with a chest, who could not manage those stairs twice a day any more.

She did it out loud, standing up, in about four minutes, and she did not write it down, and there is no copy of it anywhere in the city.

“It is not a service,” she said, to the room. “I am saying that first, in front of everybody, because if anybody writes down afterwards that I called this a service then it is a lie with my name on the bottom of it and I will know about it for the rest of my life. It is a door, and a stove, and the floor you are all standing on, and Marigold at the top of those stairs.”

“And what does it do?” said somebody at the back.

“It does nothing for her,” said Aurrie Nkemdirim. “That is the first thing every one of you is going to want and I am telling you now so that nobody stands up in May and says she was promised something. It does not get her down those stairs. It does not put a doctor on that landing. What it does is put a term on my floor until six o’clock in the morning, and if the water comes through that door in the night then there is a person with a duty on the door, and the person with the duty is me, and I open that exchange at seven in the morning, and I am telling you that in advance so that nobody is standing about at seven being surprised at me.”

“You are putting it on yourself.”

“I am putting it on myself because I am the only person in this room who is not here because of the forecast,” said Aurrie Nkemdirim, “and because the moment I put it on somebody else I am a woman of sixty-one making a person carry a duty she has not agreed to, and I did not come up this path to do that.”

Then she read the five lines out loud to them, and about nine of them wrote something down.

Jonas Mercer was in the doorway with his coat on, holding the plate he had clamped to the shop’s own service riser the day before, and he did not read it out.

That was the hard part of it and it took him the whole four minutes. The plate says, in five words on a piece of cast iron, that a flood forecast answers to nothing, and he has had it off three plates in six weeks and has never once been able to improve it, and he stood in a net-loft kitchen on a Thursday afternoon and watched a woman of sixty-one do in four minutes the thing he has spent five weeks failing to do, and the plate in his hand was about four feet from the floor she was standing on, and he put it back into his bag without reading it, because a man who reads that out loud in that room is a man telling thirty people that the woman who has just spoken is not civic, and he was not going to do that to her, and he was not going to do it to himself.

It ran from twenty to five on the seventeenth of April to six o’clock on the eighteenth, which is thirteen hours and twenty minutes, and nobody renewed it, and it is the shortest-lived term in the history of this chain, and it is the reason there is a shape on a map, and neither of those two facts was known to a single person standing in that kitchen.

The lowland dip is nine miles up the estuary from Saltmouth and the chamber at the bottom of it is a concrete box with a four-foot iron flap on a rotten-then-mended sill, and the flap is set to shut on the flood, which is the one thing anybody did right in March, and at ten past two in the morning on Friday the eighteenth of April it shut itself on the tide exactly as it had shut itself at ten past two on the twenty-second of March, unattended, with nobody inside the chamber and nobody on the bank.

The plate on the frame printed one line about it, and Jonas Mercer read it at the top of the slipway with a lamp in his teeth and could not get a signal to clear it.

**THE FLAP IS SHUT.**

It is shut. It is shut on a sill that is a new seasoned ash beam with a new pintle in it, and behind it is four tonnes of stone revetted into a bank that was cut back two metres in March by six people out of a church-hall tin, and the chamber is holding, and the flap is doing the thing it was made to do.

At twenty to five the bank went.

Not with a sound anybody has described afterwards in the same words. It went the way a wet cake goes: the inner face, which had been boiling at about two metres wide since the second of April, took the top two feet and the side of it and went out into the dip in a long grey wall, and the water that had been behind it came through in a level sheet, and the dip filled to within about a metre of the road in about four minutes, and where the bank had been there was a low ridge of what used to be the bank, going away up the channel, and the trees on the embankment at the top of the road going over.

Jonas Mercer had written on the loft kitchen wall in March, in a hand that was not very good, that the barrier would hold an ordinary tide and a great deal of rain and would not hold a surge. It was on the wall behind the three named positions and the ring of pencil round a blank space, and about eleven people read it during the night, and not one of them said anything about the man who wrote it, which was the single most generous thing that happened to him that week.

He was not in the dip. He was on the road at the top with a lamp and a radio and Perdita Sow's number, and the reason he was not in the dip is that Margo Doré had told him on the Wednesday morning that if he went down there at five past five he would stop being a technician who came to help a street and become a claim, and she had been right, and on the Thursday morning he was a man on a road, and that is what he was for.

Perdita Sow was on the bank.

She was on the bank because on the twenty-second of March she had been on the bank at two in the morning with her own lamp and had rung the loft and said two words, and because the ring of pencil on the loft wall still says **THE 22ND, AT 03:00, ON A BANK, NO NAME**, and Perdita Sow has swept that road on Sundays for eleven unpaid years and has never been given a map with it on it and has never been asked to be on anything.

She was on it at twenty to five with a head torch and a length of orange cord, and she was forty-four and she knew the bank better than anybody alive, and the bank went under her from the knee down in about two seconds and she got a hand on the post of the green stand pillar and went four metres downstream in about six seconds before the water found the cut and stood up.

She did not die. She is in a corridor at Saint Orra east at ten past six on the Friday morning with a cut across the front of the left thigh, a sprain of the right wrist, and a dislocated finger, and the dislocated finger is the one she is going to be angry about for eleven weeks. She was in the water for four minutes and she got out because a boy of nineteen on a towpath with a bicycle heard her shouting and put a bike in it and went down the bank on his hands and knees, and she has not been asked his name by anybody and Jonas Mercer heard about it from a man on a platform at half past nine in the morning and wrote it on the back of his hand and did not find out.

The water in the dip took the low block's back doors at about ten past five. Fourteen households at the bottom of that street have kitchens at the back and doors at the front, and between about ten past five and half past six in the morning they were in their front rooms on the road above with what they had.

At five minutes to five the Methodist hall on Carrow Lane had water coming under the front door, which is where the drain from the low street runs, and there were eleven Brine Wharf households in that hall, five nights into not being paid for, and Nell Ardent was there because she had slept there two of the five nights and slept badly.

“I want it on the record,” she said, on a telephone at ten to six in the morning to nobody in particular, “that I told the residents on the first of April that the day we have no building is the day we are an argument and not a household, and this is that day, and I am going to move eleven people and about forty of their possessions out of a Methodist hall at six in the morning with the water coming in, and I do not have a vehicle.”

There were things going on at the same time, which is the thing nobody ever tells you about a flood, that it is not one thing. It is nine things on top of each other for about five hours, and the ones that are loudest are not the ones that decide anything.

The pump was the first thing done wrong.

Emeka Rusk is twenty-six years on the docks and he is the only person in three buildings who knows where the stopcock is, and the harbour's storm overflow at the Carrow Lane outfall is his, and the overflow chamber on a rising tide does not drain. It spills. It spills in one direction, which is to the lowland channel, because that is where the channel is, and the lowland channel is nine miles from the Methodist hall and about a mile from the bottom of the dip.

At twenty to two in the morning Emeka Rusk had the outfall gate on a hand wheel and he could do one of two things with it. He could let the overflow into the lowland channel, which is what it does, and which puts an extra four hundred thousand litres an hour into a dip that is about to be at the top of its bank, two miles below where it goes. Or he could hold it, and it would find somewhere else, and the somewhere else was the drainage that serves Carrow Lane and Marsh Lane and the whole of the Bellow Market service side, because the market is on the low ground and the market has eleven traders' stock in it.

He held it.

He held it for one hour and ten minutes, and the reason he held it was that on Friday the third of April the hatch at the market had been mended and eleven people had been locked out of their own stock for a day and a fish business had lost the next Saturday, which is the day a fish business makes its rent, and he was not going to do that to eleven traders on a night when there was water coming down the estuary. That is a complete reason. It is the best reason available and it was in his mouth within four seconds when anybody asked him about it afterwards.

Bisi Alade found out at ten past three in the morning, from the floor of the market's cold store, where the water was coming in under the loading hatch at about the rate of a bucket being filled.

She got to him on the outfall radio, which is the one on the back of his own pump, and she was fifty feet of cold store in water to the knee and she did not shout, because the radio does not need it.

“Emeka,” she said. “Turn it over.”

“There are eleven traders —”

“There are eleven traders standing in four inches of water in a cold store and the compressor is under that floor and if that trips we have nothing on Monday, and the Saturday I lost you know I did, and I am not doing that to the whole market on the same night. **Turn it over.**”

“Where does it go.”

“Everywhere,” said Bisi Alade. “I do not know. Turn it over.”

He turned it over at twenty to four. It took him eleven minutes with a hand wheel in the dark on a rising tide and he was alone and he is fifty-one and he told nobody until the Monday.

The water went back into the lowland channel. At ten past four the dip, which had been sitting at the top of its bank since about half past three, was four hundred millimetres from the line where the road meets the embankment and still going, and at ten past five it was over that line, and it stayed over it for the best part of two hours, which is where the low block's back doors are, and all of that is on Emeka Rusk's wheel and he never once said so in public and was asked twice by a man in a good coat and said both times that the overflow chamber does not drain and it spills and there is no decision to take in it.

The market lost a compressor, a floor of shelving, and Bisi Alade's and one other trader's paper stock, which is the same trade in April that it is in March, and it will be written up on somebody's form in June as a flood loss, and nobody will write on the form that a pump operator chose the market and a witness told him to choose the other one.

The school opened on a key and shut on a teacher.

Ibrahim Sesay got to Tarn Street Primary at ten past two in the morning because he lives four streets away and because a school is the biggest warm building with a lavatory in it within half a mile, and about twenty people were in the hall by twenty past two, sitting on the floor, because somebody had told a parent on a WhatsApp group at eleven o'clock that the school would open.

He opened the gate on his key, on his own, with nobody instructing him in writing, at about twenty past two, and he did it knowing exactly what he had told a yard full of people on the Wednesday afternoon that he would be doing.

At four o'clock Roz Idris, who was in the hall because she had gone to see for herself and had said she would not be on the card and had been in the hall since half past two, told him to shut it.

“Roz —”

“Shut the gate,” said Roz Idris. “You have no term, you have no head teacher, you have no instruction, and in about four hours a man from the education office is going to arrive at that gate and ask you to explain to him why a school was open with twenty people in it and no register, and you will say *because of the flood*, and he will write *because of the flood* in a box, and that box is going to be the reason this school is a shelter from now on, and it will not be because of the flood. It will be because a caretaker opened a door at twenty past two in the morning on his own key.”

He shut it. It took him four minutes to get twenty people out of a hall and into the wet in the dark, and one of them was an old man of about eighty who had to be carried to the porch, and they ended up on the playing field in a hundred and ten metres of horizontal rain, and the school was shut at about ten past four, and at about twenty past four a woman of about seventy called Teresa Nkemelu came with two minibuses she had hired with her own money and put twenty of them in, and charged the parish nothing, and put it on a form.

The school opened for one hour and fifty minutes and no permission, and closed by a teacher who is not on the card, and every one of those four facts is a fact and none of them is a fault, and the parish's minibuses are still somebody's money.

The platform at Saint Orra bay four is a vehicle bay used by a replacement shuttle when the tram is diverted, and on the night of the seventeenth, with the tram stopped short at Morrey Hill because of water on the permanent way at two hundred metres, Kofi Sarr was asked by a transport control operator on the radio whether he would take the shuttle onto the lowland road to bring people out of the Cooper's Reach and Salt Row flats that had no bus.

He said no.

He said no for a stated reason, which was that bay four takes a shuttle onto a road that runs under the embankment at the lowland dip, and that if a shuttle is under the embankment at three in the morning with a rising tide then it is a vehicle in water, and he was not going to be the man who said yes to that; and he closed the bay at eleven minutes past two, with a bar across it, in front of a transport control operator who did not ask him why and who has a form for it.

Closing bay four was right. It is the reason no minibus of CivicCore's or anybody else's went under the embankment that night and lost a driver.

It is also the reason that the only wheeled way out of the lowland that night was gone, and that about sixty people from the low block walked four and a half miles in the rain because the vehicles stopped at the top of the embankment and would not go down the slipway, and it is the reason Nora Bell stood in a wet road at the tram end for eleven minutes.

She kept the westbound trolley signal from twenty past three until half past four, standing in the rain at the tram end of the Saint Orra crossing with her back to the traffic as she always stood, and in eleven minutes a signal that Dev Singh is permitted to keep for a limited period before rail control objects became a signal that rail control objected to, and the crossing went dark, and a queue of about forty vehicles built behind her, and at about half past four a rail control supervisor came out of the crossing office, put his hand on the machine, did not take it off, and said to a woman of forty-six in a soaked coat that she was relieved of her post for the remainder of the night and would please go home or stand somewhere else.

“I have stood in that road for nineteen years,” said Nora Bell.

“Yes,” said the supervisor.

“Right,” said Nora Bell, and stood eight feet away in the rain, and watched the queue she had created, which was not hers, and which had forty vehicles in it, and which solved itself at about twenty past seven, when a man from public works walked down the slipway with a tape in his hand and said the road was passable at the third gate, two hours and forty minutes after she had taken the crossing off a machine, and there is a form for that.

She did not go back on the crossing. It was Thursday and it was the seventeenth of April and there were eight hours of daylight to find out what would happen in the morning.

At twenty to two in the morning on the eighteenth of April a woman in a good coat and no tools came up the ramp at Saint Orra west with a laminated card on a lanyard and a folder, and stood at a door that was not hers and did not go through it, and waited to be spoken to.

Ceri Vaughan is forty-five and is CivicCore's continuity counsel and she made a four-part offer in a room over a tool-hire place on Marsh Lane on the twenty-seventh of March, and recorded it as refused, and she had not been in the building since and had not appeared on a board and had not been in a newspaper.

“Mr Mercer,” she said. “Good. You are here, that saves me an hour.”

She had a document in the folder and she gave it to him rather than reading it out, which he noticed.

It was headed **DETERMINATION OF EMERGENCY CONTINUITY — SAINT ORRA CIVIC HOSPITAL (WEST) CARE ENTRANCE**, and it was made at 11:20 that morning by the CivicCore Consortium under paragraph 11 of the Saint Orra service agreement of 2019. Paragraph 11 had two halves and Jonas Mercer had read both of them in April, in a room over a tool-hire place on Marsh Lane, when he was looking for a clause he agreed with. The first half said that where a civic node's term has failed, the party holding the standing continuity authorisation may assume the maintenance and the operation of that node pending determination. The second half was a definition, and it is a normal one, and Jonas Mercer had read it and had not understood why it was there.

The second half said: *a node is in failure where its civic term has not been continuously in force for a period exceeding twenty-four consecutive hours.*

The reason was on the second page and it said: *the term issued on plate 6-41 in March, as renewed on 24 March and on 1 April, was not continuously in force between 06:00 on 15 April and 22:00 on 16 April, a period of forty hours.*

“Which is true,” said Jonas Mercer.

“It is entirely true and I have checked it twice,” said Ceri Vaughan. “That is the point of a determination. It is not an accusation. It is a record of a state, and the state is that a care entrance serving a hospital stood without a term for forty hours in the middle of a flood warning, and that your own agreement and my own agreement and every agreement between the city and anybody else in this business says that a term which is not continuously in force for more than a day is a node in failure, and it is not a lawyer's phrase, it is a maintenance guarantee. I cannot sell an unbroken service on a promise. A surge is coming in at ten past three, the public has granted emergency authority through this agreement and has twice declined to end it, and as of eleven twenty this morning that care entrance is maintained by CivicCore, and my technician is on Marsh Lane and can be here in twenty-two minutes, and I would rather you telephoned the registrar and had him say the word than have me stand on this ramp for an hour.”

“Your technician cannot be here in twenty-two minutes,” said Jonas, “because he is on the lowland and the low road is under water and the second gate was not passable until this morning. I saw him go down at half past ten last night.”

“Then he will be here when he can.”

“He is not going to be here at all, and I am not going to stand here and tell you that he is, because he is not, and there is a plate on that doorframe and you may read it.

“The fault line cleared that door at six o'clock on Wednesday evening. Ruth Nwosu read the five lines at ten o'clock to Ezra Mbeki, who holds until eleven, and Dr. Sethi read them back a second time because a clinic clerk who set a condition in her own hand on the second of April had withdrawn on the ninth, and a term that is read aloud at a handover needs somebody there who is not a holder to hear it, and there is nobody else in that building who reads them.

**The term was live. It ran from 22:00 on Wednesday the sixteenth to 06:00 on Tuesday the twenty-first of April, and it had a holder and a witness and a claim and a precedence, and it was on a plate, and the plate on the doorframe said nothing at all, which is what a plate says when it is in good order.**

“I am not going to argue with you about the forty hours, because the forty hours are the whole of your case and they are correct, and I have been the man who made that forty hours and I will put that in a record today if you want it in a record. The term ended on its own terms at six o'clock on Wednesday morning because it was written with a window on it, and nobody could require anybody to be in a corridor at ten o'clock at night to renew it, and a woman who had told me on the fifth of April that she would not do the fifteenth was entitled to say no and was, and I did not have a rule to make her do it, and there was no rule in March and there is no rule now.”

Jonas Mercer stopped, and then said the sentence that is the actual answer, and he has thought about it since.

“But that is your clause and not the city's, and you have built a maintenance guarantee into a contract and it is a good one and I would sign it if I were on the other side. It measures whether a term is *continuous*. It does not decide what a term *is*, or what a person in a corridor is, or what a door is for, and a node whose term ends on the end written into it has not failed at anything. A node with a lapsed holder is not a node with no holder. That is the sentence I said in a room on the first of April and it is the whole of the dispute and you were not in the room, and the registrar is, and you may telephone him yourself, and I would rather you did, because I would like this in a record in your handwriting and not in mine.”

Jonas Mercer telephoned the registrar's line at twenty past two in the morning from a corridor on a ramp in the dark.

Oren Pell was in a car on the Salt Row embankment with the water at the tyres and answered on the second ring and listened to about ninety seconds of it without interrupting once, which was the first time in five weeks that Oren Pell had let anybody in this city talk for ninety seconds.

“Read me paragraph eleven,” he said. “All of it. I have not got the second half and I should have.”

“There is no second half.”

“There is a definition in the Saint Orra service agreement and you have quoted me the whole of your authority to me over the last four minutes and it is one sentence, and somebody wrote a second sentence under it in 2019 which I read in March in Mr Rook’s office and did not think about again, and it says: *a node is in failure where its civic term has not been continuously in force for a period exceeding twenty-four consecutive hours.* Is that in your determination.”

There was a pause on a telephone at half past two in the morning in a car on the Salt Row embankment.

“Mr Mercer,” said Oren Pell, “read it back to me.”

Jonas Mercer read it back to him.

“Right,” said Oren Pell, and his voice did not change at all. “Now. Both things are true and you are going to have to hold both of them, and neither of us is going to enjoy it.

“The first one is yours. A term that ends on the end written into it has ended. A node with a lapsed holder is not a node with no holder. A term which has ended on its own terms has not failed, and I have written that into a record at half past two in the morning from a car, and I will minute it and I will put my name on it, and it is the finding and it is correct, and it is about the fourth time this year I have had to write it down.

“The second one is theirs, and it is also correct, and it is better written than mine. Your twenty-four hours is a maintenance guarantee and it is a good one, and it is the reason nobody in this city has ever been paid for a door that did not open. It measures whether a term is *continuous*. It does not decide what a term *is*, and it cannot, and a contract between a city and a contractor does not get to define a word the city uses in the same sentence everywhere else.

“Here is my finding, and I am giving it to you in the order it has to be read. The term on plate 6-41 was not continuously in force for forty hours. That is entered and I am not going to pretend otherwise, and Mr Mercer is right that he made it. Therefore the determination of the seventeenth of April was within the authority of the party that made it, and I am suspending it — and I am suspending it on a different ground from the one I had ready at half past, and the different ground is this: *a contractor’s contractual definition of failure is not a civic determination of the state of a node, and if it is, then every schedule in this city is the government of this city, and I will not minute that and neither will you.*”

“If it had not been renewed on Wednesday night —”

“Then the door would have been shut at ten past three this morning, and Mr Calder would have written *no holder* in a box, and there would have been a woman of ninety-one in bay nine who had to be moved in a flooding corridor anyway on somebody’s own authority, and the record of it would have said *moved by a doctor at four in the morning on her own signature*, and that is worse than anything in the agreement. I have written that down as well. I have written both halves down, Mr Mercer, in the same record, in the same minute, in the same hand, at the same time, which is the only thing I know how to do.”

A pause. Water against a tyre.

“Mr Mercer, tell her no. Not in a strong voice. She has a folder and a laminated card and a technician on Marsh Lane and she is doing her job properly, and if you shout at her she will produce a copy of paragraph eleven to a committee in June and it will be the only page anybody reads, and I would like to be in the room when it is.”

“I am not going to shout at her.”

“Good,” said Oren Pell. “And one more thing, and then I have to drive. This is not a victory and do not let anybody in that corridor put it in a book as one. A determination is a piece of paper with a date on it, and the date is the seventeenth of April, and it is on the register, and it is going in front of a committee on Saturday morning, and in June the argument will be that the city had nobody on the night of the seventeenth of April. That argument is now on the record in their favour, with a witness, and I gave them the witness by being in a car on Salt Row instead of in a room with the paper in front of me, and I have done that about four times this year and I have run out of ways of not doing it.”

He hung up. Ceri Vaughan was standing four feet away with her folder closed and she had heard none of it and did not ask, which Jonas Mercer noticed and would remember.

“Your term is live,” he said. “The plate on this doorframe has a term on it that started at ten o’clock last night and ends at six o’clock on Tuesday morning. Two holders read it to each other at that handover and a third read it back, and the registrar has written two findings into a record at half past two this morning from a car, and both of them are against you. You may put the determination in your folder and take it to the committee on the second of June, and it is in the register with a date on it, and I am not going to pretend to you that it is not.”

“That,” said Ceri Vaughan, “is a completely correct answer and it is the best result anybody could have got, and I would like to say two other things and then I will go and find out whether my technician is above water. First: the public has granted emergency authority. Not tonight. Through the agreement of 2019, twice declined, standing. And second —” she held the folder a moment longer than she needed to — “I was required to make a determination where a term was in failure by my own definition, and my own definition is the one I was required to use, and I made one at eleven twenty this morning, and it is dated today, and in June somebody will be able to point at the seventeenth of April and say that a hospital’s care entrance stood without a term for forty hours in a surge and that only a contractor turned up, and both halves of that sentence are true, and the true half that is inconvenient is the half that says only a contractor turned up, and I did not enjoy writing it and I wrote it well, because that is worse.”

Jonas Mercer stood on a wet ramp at Saint Orra west at ten to three in the morning and had nothing at all to say to that, because she was right and she had said it out loud, and she was going to leave and take the only evidence of the night with her.

At twenty past six that morning, in the corridor, at the card table, with the box open and the pen in her hand, Mina Okafor wrote four lines into the public box, in the same box, under the nine minutes of the twenty-fourth of March and the nineteen of the third of April and the forty minutes of the fifteenth, and none of them were about the door, because the door had a live term and a witness and worked, and the one about the door said: *care entrance open on a live term. Two holders, one witness, five lines read aloud at the handover. No declaration required. No delay.*

“There,” said Mina Okafor, capping the pen. “That is the first good line I have ever written in that box and it is going to look like we solved it, and in June somebody will take that line and put it next to the forty minutes of Wednesday and the nineteen of Thursday and say *it was handled*, and it will be false, because the reason it is a good line is that a night watch who had been on since ten o’clock had not gone home, and a clerk who was not on the rota was in the building for her own reasons, and a doctor said *I am not doing it because it is a good idea* and meant it as a warning, and nobody organised one single hour of it, and none of it survives Wednesday.”

“Then what survives Wednesday,” said Jonas.

“Nothing,” said Mina Okafor. “That is the sentence. I set a condition in my own hand and I kept it and it took the second witness off that card, and on Thursday night the second witness was replaced by a man with a laptop bag who did it for the wrong reason and will be shouted at in June, and if you have put my name anywhere near that as a success then I will take it off, and I am not doing that for the reasons you think, I am doing it because I told you on the ninth of April that I would stop, and I stopped, and I am still here, and those are two different facts, and I told him to be the first to say it so that it was me who said it and not him.”
