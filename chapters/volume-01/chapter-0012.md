# Chapter 0012 — The Door's Terms

The west clinic’s care entrance was a service door at the bottom of a ramp, and Ruth Nwosu came down the ramp to meet him carrying the log under one arm and a length of blue painter’s tape in her hand.

“Twenty-one twenty,” she said. “I am forty minutes early and I will be standing on this ramp until eleven in the morning, and I would like both of those facts on the page, because the board says I finish at six. Go on.”

“I am here at your request.”

“I wrote *request* at the top of tonight’s page,” she said, “because your suspension says a named bearer initiates and I would like the page to say it before you start touching anything.” She held the log out and let him read the line in her square hand. **21:12 — maintenance requested by R. Nwosu, night watch, for inspection only.** “There. Now it is a request. If anybody comes looking for who opened this conversation, it is not you.”

Jonas unpacked the bond and laid it on the counter inside, where the high windows would keep the paper flat. Then he laid out three things beside it: a printed copy of the clinic’s own gate terms as the health authority still published them, the municipal index entry for the hinge, and a blank maintenance form.

Ruth watched him do it. “You have brought a lot of paper to a door that has a key.”

“The paper is how I find out whether the door and I agree.”

Above the ramp the corridor ran back behind reception, past the linen cabinets, with a floor rail set into the vinyl and a fire latch that had been chained open since the spring. It was, in the ordinary way, a good corridor in a converted insurance office. At its far end, past the treatment bay, the corridor turned once and became the ward corridor, and a stretcher had to be taken round that turn at a speed a person can control, which is a thing people learn by doing it and not by reading about it.

Ruth took him down to the plate.

It was set into the wall at knee height on the ramp side, ceramic, about the size of a paperback, older than the building’s furniture. Three lamps sat in a row along its lower edge. A brass frame around it had been painted over at least twice. When Jonas brushed the grime away with his thumb, an old line of engraving came up under the paint.

**NO UNATTENDED ACCESS — WEST CLINIC CARE ENTRANCE**

Below it, scratched through the plate’s face rather than engraved, somebody had written a time in pencil: **23:00–06:00**.

“It says no unattended access,” Ruth said. “It has said that since my mother worked here. It is not a lie. Nobody should be able to walk into this building through that door at two in the morning without somebody on the corridor knowing about it.”

“And the pencil.”

“Nobody alive knows who wrote that, and the plate has been reading that window for as long as I have been here.” She took the black key off her chain and held it a hand’s width from the ceramic, and the third lamp came up white. “And it is wrong. The pencil says eleven at night to six in the morning. I start at ten at night and I am on this corridor until the morning watch signs for it at eleven. So for six hours of a thirteen-hour night the plate has me standing out here on my own with the record saying there is nobody here at all, and it has had it that way for nineteen years, and nobody has ever once asked the plate whether the window fits the woman.”

Jonas put two fingers on the frame and looked.

The Margin came up slowly, the way it did when a hinge had been quiet for hours and had to be persuaded. The plate was not one thing. It was two things laid on top of each other with nothing in between. The engraved term reached for the door and said *a person must be present*. The pencil window reached for the same door and said *and here is when the person is*. Between them there was a place where the record should have said which promise wins, and there was nothing there at all, which is the shape a seam makes from the outside.

He had read that shape six times in ten days. It felt like the bridge, and it was not the bridge. It was small, it was local, it sat in one room, and the entire weight of the fault was a question of grammar.

He let go of the frame before the taste came. “Two terms. No order between them. That is all it is.”

“Does it open?” Ruth asked.

“It opens. It has always opened. The problem is that the record has to know who is opening it, and the record cannot tell the difference between a person who is on the corridor and a person who is merely standing in the doorway with a valid piece of paper.”

Mina Okafor came down the ramp with a transfer board under her arm and the particular expression of a clerk who had been sent for and had brought her work.

“Am I witnessing this,” she said, “or am I being consulted, because the second one takes longer and the first one gets you in trouble?”

“Both,” Jonas said. “I need to see what the plate does with a real claim, and I need you to tell me what verification means in this building, because I suspect those are not the same and I would rather find out now.”

“In this building verification means somebody with a duty has looked at the person and the person has not objected,” Mina said. “It is not a document. Nobody has ever given me a form for it.”

He wrote that down exactly, in her words, and underlined the sentence about forms.

Then he turned the page over and, because he had a blank maintenance form and nothing else to do with it, wrote six headings down the left margin in his small square hand: **holder, scope, witness, term, claim, precedence**.

Ruth leaned over his shoulder.

“That is a shopping list.”

“It is what a permission is made of. Six things. If one of them is missing the whole thing has a hole in it, and I would like to know which of the six is missing before I start.” He put the pen on the first line. “Holder. Ruth Nwosu, night watch, twenty-two hundred to eleven hundred, by a key and a lamp. That one exists, in pencil and in her.” He wrote it.

“Scope.” He looked up the corridor. “That is the list that does not exist. Nobody has ever written down what that door is for. I asked the hospital’s own index this afternoon and it says *service access*.”

“Which means the door,” Ruth said.

“Which means a door, which is not a purpose.”

“Term.” He put a dash in the fourth box. “There is no end anywhere in that plate. That pencil is the closest thing, and it is in the wrong hand, and it does not match the woman.”

“Witness,” he said, and reached for the third box, and then stopped, because Ruth had gone very still beside him. “Two people, and the plate is not asking for two people. It asks for a key.”

“It asks for a key because a key is the only version of this anybody has ever been able to prove,” Ruth said. “Put two people in that box and see what happens. Half the women on this corridor have been here longer than the plate has and you would not be able to prove a single hour of any of it.”

He wrote a question mark in the third box and left it there.

Mina put her hand on the second box. “Claim. I have been sitting here for ten minutes listening to three of you describe in detail what you would do if that door was shut, and not one of you has said what happens to this building if it stays shut. Nobody is paying for a closure. That is the box with nothing in it.”

Ruth made a small sound that was not quite a laugh. “Say that one to my manager.”

“I will say it to whoever will listen to it.”

“Precedence.” The last box. He left it empty for a long moment, and then he wrote nothing in it at all, and turned the page round so that Ruth could see six boxes with their contents, four of them real, one of them empty, and one of them with a question mark in it.

“That one,” he said, tapping the empty box with the pen. “The other five are bad. That one is the hole.”

Sanaa Iqbal came in from the treatment bay at ten past ten with her sleeves pushed up and a chart under her arm.

“I hear there is a document,” she said. “Show me the scope line.”

Jonas showed her the scope line, and she read it and made a small unhappy noise.

“Too broad,” she said. “It says the gate is held for care. It does not say *whose* care, or how urgent, or for how long. A gate that opens for care in the abstract is a gate that a clerk can defend and a lawyer can attack and a patient cannot use.”

She put her chart on the counter. “Here is what this building actually needs between ten at night and six in the morning. One: an emergency coming in. That is not a permission question, that is a door, and I want it open in under a minute with no argument. Two: a transfer that a doctor has decided is necessary, where a bed and a named receiving shift exist. That is the category the city calls non-urgent and my patients call Tuesday. Three: a person coming back from here to a home or a shelter that has a named local steward who will keep them. That one gets refused most and matters most.”

“It is refused most because it has no document,” Mina said. “There is no such thing as a return-of-care form. I write a line in a book.”

“Because there is no such thing,” Sanaa agreed.

Jonas looked at the two lists on the paper. “All three. That is the scope. Anything narrower will produce exactly the thing you just described, and anything broader will be a door you cannot close.”

“You are describing every request the building has ever made in one term,” Sanaa said. “That is not a scope. That is a complaint.”

“And you are describing three different services with three different risks.”

“I am describing a woman who has been on this ward since eight in the morning and needs the theatre list at six, and I am describing a man who has nowhere to go home to, and I am not going to rank them for a plate.”

Ruth spoke from the ramp.

“No,” she said. “You are not. I am.”

They turned around. She had come back up without either of them hearing her.

“I am the one who stands in that corridor,” Ruth said. “If the gate opens for a routine dressing at two in the morning, then for eleven minutes the emergency approach at the far end of this hall is behind a stretcher and a screen and me, and I am the only person standing there. So urgent only. That is not a moral judgment. That is the number of people I am.”

“You cannot be the only person,” Jonas said. “The board says you are one woman on a key.”

“The board says a great deal about the number of people I am,” Ruth said. “It says six o’clock.”

Jonas turned back to the plate. He had opened his field reader and the Margin was waiting behind his eyes, and what he wanted — he could feel how much he wanted it — was to write a term broad enough to cover all three lists and let the plate sort them out. A term that said *care* and let care decide what it meant. That was the shape of the bench plate’s silence all over again, and if he had done it, every person in this corridor would have signed something they had not agreed to.

The line on the bond under his thumb said: *at least one named living person who has read this bond and can state, from memory, what the term permits.*

“If I write it broad,” he said, “the person who is on this corridor at two in the morning is the person who decides who is urgent. And if the record does not have their name, then the record is being run by whoever is nearest, and tonight that is me.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

“Then we measure it,” Ruth said.

They took a spare trolley from the corridor stores, put a folded blanket on it to give it weight, and walked it down the corridor to the ward turn and back. Twice, once at Mina’s pace and once at a porter’s pace. Eleven minutes, said Mina’s watch, and the corner by the linen cabinets took two of them. The emergency approach was blocked for all eleven.

Ruth took the painter’s tape off her belt, walked to the far end of the corridor, and put a strip across the emergency doors.

“Eleven minutes,” she said. “So urgent is not a judgment call at two in the morning. Urgent is a measurement. And a transfer that can be done without standing in this hall for eleven minutes does not need the care door at two in the morning, and can have it at nine.”

That was not the term he had come in with. It was better than the term he had come in with, and it had cost Ruth something to say it in front of people.

The call came at eleven fourteen.

Mina put her hand on the board. “That is a scheduled return-of-care coming off the ward list. Not urgent. Home by midnight, local steward waiting, no form.” She looked at Ruth. “Say it again for the record, because the plate is going to ask me whether it is urgent and I would like my answer to be a person and not a category.”

Ruth said: “The answer is that the person is not urgent, and the duty is real, and the corridor is empty, and I am here.”

Mina pushed the claim. The plate took it, looked at it, and turned ceramic white.

> **Verified transfer. Not urgent.**
> **Scope unclear. Precedence not stated.**
> **Which promise answers is not written down.**

The lamps went out one at a time. The care door stayed shut, with a corridor light burning behind it and three people standing in front of it, and for the first time since Jonas had started reading permissions he understood that a fault could be a perfectly legible sentence that nobody had ever agreed to write.

He got his breath back and sat down on the ramp edge with his hands on his knees. The copper came and went in one wave and left him hollowed out.

“All three limbs,” Sanaa said. “With Ruth’s condition, Mina’s definition, and a clause about the corridor.”

Jonas put the pen on the counter. The handover page for the morning shift was still clipped to the board at the top of the ramp, and he turned it over, and on the back of the shift sheet he wrote what the west clinic care entrance was for.

> **WEST CLINIC CARE ENTRANCE — LOCAL ACCESS TERM**
> **HOLDER:** the named watch or care worker on duty at the west clinic.
> **SCOPE:** the care entrance may be held for a person with a verified clinic care claim; for a verified clinic transfer where a bed and a named receiving shift exist, and which does not close the emergency approach; and for a return-of-care handoff named by a local steward, from the receiving threshold to the assigned bed, for that transfer only.
> **WITNESS:** two named people at every handover who have read this term and can say when it ends.
> **PRECEDENCE:** this term answers to NO UNATTENDED ACCESS for the three cases above. Where this term and any premises, freight, or opening-hours term conflict, this term controls the care entrance. Every other use of that door stays as it is.
> **TERM:** from the first service window, 07:00, to 06:00 six days later. It ends there. It does not carry forward by itself.
> **CLAIM:** the issuer maintains this term from the moment he issues it until a named successor steward accepts that maintenance, or the term expires. If the term fails without a bearer present, the route narrows to emergency only and the issuer answers for the delay.
> **IF NO BEARER IS ON DUTY:** the route is held by the issuer.

He read the last line out loud after he had written it, heard exactly how it sounded, and left it there, because it was what the plate would accept and he had no successor to write instead.

Ruth read the whole page. She got to the last line and stopped and read it again, and then she said, “That is a man’s line, not a clinic’s line.”

“It is the only thing the plate will accept today.”

“Then we find out what it does tomorrow,” Ruth said, and signed the holder line, and dated it.

Mina countersigned as the record, and wrote the time in the corner in the small upright figures the clinic used on everything. Sanaa signed as an informed witness, and under her signature wrote, in her own hand, that her signature carried her agreement with the scope and not with any claim about the gate’s safety, which is a distinction Jonas suspected he would need several times in the next six days.

At twenty-three thirty-eight the clinic line rang and Selah Mora answered.

She listened to the page read out to her. She listened twice.

“Give me the return-of-care limb only,” she said. “Not the others. I will hold it from ten at night, when a handoff is actually due and a steward is named for it, and only for that. Bellow Market’s name goes nowhere near a term about a hospital door.”

“That is a narrow window,” Jonas said.

“I am one person and a kettle,” said Selah. “Narrow is what I have.”

She gave a consent for the limb and refused the rest of it, in the same breath, and told them both.

Ruth pinned the page to the handover board and wrote the first handover entry beneath it, because a term with an expiry had to be somewhere a person would actually read it at eleven in the morning, and Mina added the page to the book of records, and Jonas put the bond’s hinge line and the page’s first line next to each other on the counter and found that they said the same words.

The door behind them stayed shut until morning, which was correct, and which felt, at ten to midnight on a night when somebody was sleeping two corridors away who had come in short of breath, and somebody else was packing a bag for a shelter that did not exist on any map, like the exact shape of the problem.
