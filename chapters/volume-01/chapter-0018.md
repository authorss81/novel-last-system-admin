# Chapter 0018 — The First Bearing Trail

Bea Osunde unlocked drawer four of the wall cabinet in the River Stacks shop and found Continuity Project maintenance cards in eight cardboard boxes, and it took her twenty minutes to find the right one, because the cards had been filed by series and then, at some point in the last decade, by somebody who had never opened the drawer.

“Series six,” she said, and set the box on the bench under the strip light, and put her own hand flat on it before she took it off. “Nineteen ninety-odd. These are the ones they pulled out of service and never destroyed, which tells you what the department thinks about history.”

There were about two hundred cards, each the size of a postcard, each with a stamped heading, a series plate number, a maintenance date, and a hand-written line in ink that had been chosen, Jonas suspected, for a century.

He found plate 41 in the fourth row. The west clinic care entrance. The service plate had the same I. Vey seal around the lower screw that the bridge had, and a serial number in the Continuity format, and Jonas felt the ordinary small thrill of a man finding a thing that matched a thing.

Then he turned to the back, because the request had said *chain*, and the back of a series card held the chain.

**SERIES 6 CHAIN. LOCAL NODES.**
**6-38 River Stacks, cabinet 6-R-14.**
**6-39 Saint Orra bridge, west-lane relay.**
**6-40 Saint Orra bridge, side-hinge service relay.**
**6-41 Saint Orra west clinic, care entrance.**
**6-L/14 Canal Lowline, lowland service node. Address not indexed.**
**6-L/19 Canal Lowline, service node. Address not indexed.**

Two unindexed nodes, in a chain that ran from a relay cabinet in River Stacks through the Saint Orra bridge and into a building where a woman named Ruth Nwosu had finally, three days ago, been given a name and a set of hours in a record.

Jonas put his left hand flat on the bench, because his right hand had begun to shake, and read the lowland entries again.

6-L/14. LO/6-L/14.

He knew that string. He had written it in a private pad in a records room six days ago, after a partial index entry with no location and no fault-filer had come up under a bridge plate, and he had never told anyone at the department that the code matched a hardware code in the family copy of his father’s last file. He had told Nina Calder that there was a historical note. He had not told her what the note was about, because the note was about Evan, and because telling her would have made the number in the index a search instead of a document.

He stood in the rain coming through the shop’s open door and felt the whole shape of the last six days rearrange itself behind his eyes, and then he stopped it, hard, on purpose.

The bridge had been a relay and a fault. The green shuttle with the child and the brass token had come up a separate service lane on its own feed and had never touched any of this. The old-canal shelter had a door that asked a question before it opened. None of those things was in his debt because of anything he had done. They were in his life. The difference was the entire point, and he was not going to lose it now because a number had finally matched.

“Bea,” he said. “These two lowland entries. Has anybody ever asked for them?”

“No. They have no index to be requested by.” She came and looked at the card upside down anyway. “What is LO?”

“I do not know yet.”

“Then do not write LO/6-L/14 in your own pad with a question mark, because you will keep it for eleven years and it will become a fact.” She took the card and turned it the right way up and read it properly. “What did the request want?”

“Maintenance history for the chain. It says the response is required before the review date and the review date is in nine days.”

“Nine days.” Bea looked at the clock on the wall, and then at his hand, and then at the yellow board by the door with the bond still stapled to it. “Your appointment review is in five.”

He had not done that arithmetic himself, which told him something about how the days had been going.

At four he took a bus to the reference library and asked for the street index, and the woman at the desk brought him four volumes in a trolley and then watched him turn them for a while before she asked what he was looking for.

“I am looking for something that should be there.”

“That is not a question I can help you with, love. It is four volumes and an hour.”

He took them to a table by the wall. The lowland sheets were the thin ones, as they always were, because the lowland was the wet ground at the bottom of the estuary and had never been worth ink. He went along them with a finger, and then he copied the six words from the card into the margin of his pad and searched them as if they were the name of a person.

He found the woman at the desk again on his way out and asked her the other question, which was whether a street could be inside a ward and not be in the index, and she said it could not, and then she stopped, and then she said that the last time anybody had asked her that she had been a junior clerk and a man from the water authority had said something about a category error, and that she had thought about that sentence for eleven years, and that she was not going to say anything more about it.

Outside, the water authority sold its standpipe map over the counter for a coin. It showed a dense comb of marks through the northern districts and a sparse scatter down in the lowland, and he could not put a single one of the lowland marks onto a street he had just read about.

He walked to the education office because it was two doors along and open, and the woman at the desk was very polite and very fast, and when he asked whether a household with no registered address could still be inside a school catchment she said that it could not, and that she would need a departmental reference to look at the draft sheets, and that without one she was not permitted to open the file, and that he was very welcome to come back with a reference.

He did not have a departmental reference. That was the whole of what he had learned in the four places he had been that afternoon. A thing can be absent from a filing system because somebody made an error. It can also be absent because there is nothing in the office that is allowed to ask.

At half past five his handset rang and it was Nina Calder, and she had the particular flatness that meant she was reading something she had been asked to read twice.

“The Old Ward maintenance office has put a line on your file,” she said. “They have also, apparently without being asked to, put it on the public service strip. Which means your name is now attached to a request that says *unindexed node in chain* in front of anyone who walks past the board.”

“Did they have to?”

“No. The review is public. The requests are public. It is not a punishment. It is the ordinary way the city finds out what it does not know about itself, and it happens to have picked you.”

“Can the department answer it instead of me?”

“The department has said that the issuer of a series plate is the only person who can speak to the series. That is not quite true, but it is close enough that nobody upstairs is going to argue with it, because arguing would mean admitting that the department does not know what is in its own cabinets.” A pause. “They have also asked whether the issuer intends to attend the review in person.”

He looked at the yard, and the cabinet, and the two lowland lines on a card from a decade before his probation.

“They can say I will be there,” he said. “I have not decided whether I will be.”

That evening he drove out to Bellow Market, which he had done twice in his life, both times in a department van, and both times with a form.

Selah Mora was behind the counter of the market’s night office with a kettle going and a full room behind her, and she looked at him across the counter for a long moment before she said, “No.”

“I have not asked you anything.”

“You have a face on you that is asking.” She set down the cloth. “You want to know if the folder says anything about a lowland service node, and the answer is that the folder does not say, because the folder is a return-of-care and safety-follow-up book for the people living in this building and the yard behind it, and it does not contain a history of the district and it is not going to acquire one because a technician from the city has found a number in a drawer.”

He took the sealed olive folder out of his own mind, where it had been sitting since eleven o’clock that morning, and put it on the counter between them, and did not touch it.

“I have not opened it,” he said. “I am not going to. But I want you to hear me say that I thought about it, in a yard, at ten in the morning, and that I decided not to, and that I would rather you hear it from me than work it out later.”

Selah looked at him for a while.

“Good,” she said. “That is the answer I would have given you anyway and I am glad you arrived at it by yourself.” She refilled the cup she had not offered him. “Now. I will not open the folder. I will tell you what I know without the folder, and then you will go away, because you have been in this room for four minutes and there are eleven people here who have now looked at you.”

“Go on.”

“There is a street,” she said. “Everybody calls it the Weir because of what is at the end of it, and it is not on your maps because it has never been on your maps. There is a net loft on it. There is a water stand on it that the residents run themselves, forty minutes a day, on a rota, because the city has never had a record of a tap. And every morning at about a quarter to seven a courier goes down it on a bicycle with too many bags on the handlebars, and she does not speak to the department, and if you stand at the bottom of that street with a department vest on, she will not stop and she will not be rude to you either, because she is not rude to anybody, she is just finished with institutions.”

“Her name.”

“Pia Santos. She brought Alma back in on the shuttle, and she kept the passenger list, and you did not ask her for it, and she has not forgotten which of those two things you did not do.” Selah put the cup down. “If you want to go down that street, you go down it as a person who has been told no and is going anyway. You do not go as the department with a card. And you do not come back here and tell me what you found, because then I will have to decide whether to lie to my own people about it.”

He drove back across the river with the lowland code in his head and the sealed folder where it belonged, and stopped at the Old Ward bridge because the Old Ward board was lit and he could read it from the road.

**RECLAMATION SEASON MAINTENANCE REVIEW — SERIES 6.**
**UNINDEXED NODE IN CHAIN: 6-L/14, 6-L/19.**
**ISSUER TO RESPOND. REVIEW IN NINE DAYS.**

Underneath it, on the same line, in the same flat register, the gate’s own service log was still running:

**HOLDER: B. OSEI — 06:00. READ AND SIGN AT THE DOOR.**

The two things were on the same board, four inches apart, and Jonas stood in the dark and understood that this was what a claim actually looked like from the outside: not a punishment, not a debt with a shape, but two lines of text that had decided, without consulting anybody, that the man who had written one of them had to be the man who answered for the other.
