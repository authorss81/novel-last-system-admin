# Chapter 0017 — The Existing Bearers

The clinic gave them the conference room above the laundry at nine the next morning, and by ten it contained two porters, a night watch on her way out, a doctor who had named them, a clerk, a physician, a shop steward, a shelter steward who had driven up, and a plywood board with a frame made of offcuts, and it also contained a woman named Marta Sowle, who had come because she was the one who had been on the floor, and who wanted to be in the room where it was decided.

Ruth Nwosu was there because she was on her way out. She had come on at ten the night before and had brought a paper bag of clothes with her so that she could go straight to the sister’s from the meeting, and every twenty minutes somebody’s radio went off in the corridor below and she was on her feet before it finished.

Nobody in the room wanted the same thing, which took most of the morning to establish.

Bram Osei came first because Dr. Sethi had named him and Dr. Sethi had come in at eight to do it in person and to say, twice, that the clinic was the naming authority and not the department, and that if anybody upstairs in the hospital wanted to take it back they could come and stand in the corridor at three in the morning and say it to his face.

Bram was forty-nine and had been on the doors of Saint Orra west for six years, and he had a way of stating a boundary that sounded like a fact about the weather.

“I will hold it,” he said. “Six to eleven, Monday to Saturday, and I will not do Sunday because Sunday is when my granddaughter comes. Before six I am on the bins, and after eleven I am on the bins, and neither of those has ever been a question anybody asked me.”

“Half past one to ten,” Ezra Mbeki said. “That is what I am offering, and I am offering it because somebody has to be here at half past one, and it is not going to be him, and he is not going to be anybody’s answer at three in the morning. I am not a nurse. So it is a line, and the line is: if it needs a nurse, it does not go through this door, and I will say it out loud to whoever is on the stretcher, and I do not care that it makes me unpopular.”

“You said that the night it happened,” Tomas said. “I read the statement.”

“You read the statement,” Ezra agreed. “That is why I am saying it again.”

Jonas had brought the schedule the plate had printed on the morning of the repair, the amended schedule he had drafted in the yard on the day after, and a third version he had started two nights ago and abandoned, and he put all three on the table so that people could see the shape of his failure laid flat.

The shape was: Jonas Mercer, all hours, every day, forever.

“Available,” he said, because it was the word the whole morning had been circling. “That is the question. What does available mean, in a term?”

“Four answers,” Tomas said. “Pick one and defend it. Mine: available means a person is named, has read the term, and can be replaced inside their own shift by another named person. If I cannot be replaced, I am not available, I am the building.”

“Mine is different,” Ruth said. “Available means awake, on the site, and not on my second job. You people talk about being on the rota. Half of this hospital is on the rota and half of it is somewhere else, holding a phone. I am available when I am standing in that corridor with nothing else in my hands. If I have got the linen on my arm I am not available, and you can see it on my arm.”

Selah Mora had driven up from Bellow Market and sat at the head of the table because she was the only person there who had to get back. She listened to all four answers before she gave the fifth.

“You are all describing yourself,” she said. “I will give you the one that matters. Available means somebody can leave. I can leave my kettle. I can close my shop and go home at nine because my sister has the key and the rota is on the wall. In eleven months, when the boy who is in the corridor at four o’clock in the morning gets tired of it, he will not be able to leave, because the term will have grown a shape around his name that has no door in it. I am asking you to build a door in it. Put me on the return-of-care limb between ten at night and six in the morning, name the hours, and pay Bellow Market a retainer for them, because the hours are my staff’s hours and they will be paid out of a market that has no water.”

“We can get a retainer out of the clinic budget,” Jonas said.

“Not you,” said Selah. “The hospital. In writing. If it comes from a technician it is a favor and I have watched what happens to favors in my building when the person who did the favor gets tired.”

Mina Okafor put her pencil down. “Mine is boring,” she said. “Available means it was written down before the shift started, in a place where the person starting the shift will see it before the coffee. If I find out who is holding the door by asking a patient, the system has failed, whatever the schedule says.”

Sanaa had said almost nothing for two hours, and now she said the thing that ended the argument.

“None of those is wrong, and together they are the definition of a person who is alone,” she said. “So the patient is the sixth witness. Whoever is holding that door, the person in the chair has to be able to say who they are, and be able to say *I would rather wait*, and have the waiting be a real answer and not an insult. If the route only works when nobody knows who is standing in it, we have built the same gate with a better sign on it.”

They wrote it in that order: Ruth, Tomas, Selah, Mina, Sanaa, and Jonas last, because he had learned that the last person to speak in a room like this should not be the person with the credential.

> **ROSTER — WEST CLINIC CARE ENTRANCE**
> **B. Osei, day porter — 06:00–11:00, Mon–Sat.**
> **E. Mbeki, porter — 13:30–22:00.**
> **R. Nwosu, night watch — 22:00–11:00.**
> **Return-of-care limb only, 22:00–06:00: S. Mora, Bellow Market. Retainer requested in writing of Saint Orra west: not yet agreed.**
> **11:00–13:30 — NO NAMED HOLDER. The gate is closed in that window unless a named holder is on duty.**
> **Emergency care only in that window may be opened by the on-call clinician, in writing, and every such opening is written on the board and entered in the log.**
> **At every handover the outgoing and incoming holders read this term to each other, and the outgoing holder says the hours, and the incoming holder says them back. Where the route is closed between them, the outgoing holder reads it aloud to the clerk on the desk and signs the box, and the incoming holder reads it aloud on his own before he takes the key.**
> **Any holder may refuse a single use, or a whole shift, without giving a reason. A refusal of a whole shift ends the shift, not the job.**
> **Any patient, family member, or member of staff may ask who is holding this door, may ask for a different holder, and may appeal a refusal to a named person who is not a technician.**

Mina read the fifth line twice, and then read it a third time to Selah Mora, who had not asked her to.

“Not yet agreed,” Selah said. “Leave those words on the page.”

“Someone will read it as a gap that nobody has filled.”

“Someone will read it as a debt that has not been paid. That is what it is, and my staff are the ones holding it, and I would rather a stranger on a corridor can read it for himself than have it explained to them afterwards by somebody who has already spent it.” She put her hand flat on the table, not on the page. “When the hospital writes the cheque, I will ask them to take the words out, and until then the words stay.”

Nobody at the table wrote anything down, and Jonas understood that this was the only line on the page with a price on it, and that the price was still only a promise.

They read the eleven o’clock line out loud several times. The gap was two and a half hours, and Bram said the obvious thing, which was that he could start at half past five instead of six and finish at half past three, and Mina said the obvious thing back, which was that the desk cannot be left at half past eleven either, and the room went quiet for the particular quiet that follows two people saying no in a row to a solution they would both have liked.

Mina said, “The clerk’s window could take eleven to half one,” and then heard herself say it and put her own hand flat on the page.

“No,” she said. “If I hold the gate I cannot hold the desk, and the desk is where a transfer comes from. I would be moving the problem one meter.”

“Who is available at half past eleven?” Tomas asked.

Nobody answered, and Jonas understood — with the specific humiliation of a man who has just watched his own proposal dissolve — that the answer was him, and that the room had known it, and that they had spent the better part of an hour letting him hear it in their silence.

“Then the gap stays,” he said. “And it goes on the board. And the emergency clause is written by a clinician and not by me. And we do not close it with me.”

Ruth Nwosu stood up at ten past ten, put her paper bag of clothes on the table, and said the sentence that ended the morning.

“Eleven o’clock to half past one is two and a half hours,” she said. “For two and a half hours, on five days a week, there is going to be a door on this corridor with nobody behind it, and everybody in this building is going to know that, and it is going to be written on a wall where I can see it when I come on. That is the deal. Do not soften it to make the rest of the page look tidy, because the moment it looks tidy somebody will decide it is a small problem and close the gap by putting a name on it that does not exist.”

Jonas mounted the board himself, low on the wall of the ramp inside the rail so that somebody sitting in a vehicle at the bottom of the hill could read it, in a plastic sleeve, with the telephone number in figures large enough to be read at arm’s length in bad light. The number was the clinic’s own service line, and underneath it, smaller, was the line for the open clinic, and the point of having two was that the first was answered by the building and the second by a person.

Above the phone number, in the middle of the board, he had put the holder of the day and the holder of the next shift, and under the holder of the day there was a box with a tick in it that the holders filled in and signed when they took the key, because Mina had said that a name on a schedule is a rumor and a signature in a box at the actual hour is not.

And at the bottom, in the flat register that the Old Ward board had already taught both of them to read, he had put the cost.

**COST OF THIS TERM: a booked cardiac slot was released at 15:41 on the third day. The next available date is the fourteenth. It is carried here because it happened. It is not discharged by anything on this page.**

Ruth read that last line twice, standing in the corridor with her key in her hand, and then she said, in a voice that had nothing in it but the statement of fact: “That is the first time in nineteen years that anything I have done has been written on a wall and counted.”

“Your hours were on the old board,” Mina said.

“My hours were on the old board,” Ruth agreed. “Nobody counted them.” She signed the roster line, wrote 22:00 and 11:00 in her own block capitals beside it, and put the pen down. “Right. I am going to my sister’s before I fall asleep in a chair, and I will be on at ten tonight, and somebody had better have read that line out loud to me when I get here or I will not take the key.”

She was gone down the ramp by five past ten, and the corridor got noticeably lighter.

They took the gap to the corridor at half past eleven, because a term that had not been tested in a corridor was only a piece of paper, and on the corridor at half past eleven there was nobody at all.

Bram had read the term aloud to Mina on the desk at five to eleven and signed the box, and gone to the bins. Mina had a woman of eighty-eight coming back for a dressing at twenty to twelve, verified, with a bed, with a named receiving shift, not urgent and not an emergency, and at five past twelve she put the claim on the plate and the plate said what it had been saying since the term was written, in four words she could now read from the doorway:

**HOLDER NOT NAMED. ROUTE CLOSED.**

Nobody spoke for a while. Somewhere up the corridor a telephone rang twice and stopped.

“I could call her,” Mina said. “She would go on to the main hospital in an ambulance and it would take forty minutes, and there is a bed, and it is not a catastrophe.”

“You could,” Sanaa said.

“It is not a catastrophe today,” Sanaa said. “So I am not going to spend an emergency on it, because the day I spend them on everything is the day there is no emergency left for the person who needs one. Log it as refused for want of a named holder.”

They logged it. At twenty past twelve Sanaa walked down and wrote one line on the card and gave it to Mina and did not say it out loud, and the plate took the line, and the door opened for the length of the eleven-minute walk and the eleven-minute walk back, and the woman was in a chair on a ward at ten to one, and the entry in the log read *opened by declaration, on-call clinician, gap hours*, and Jonas copied it onto the board in the box, and the box was full of other people’s handwriting by the end of the week.

Tomas kept the room until three, because five people still had to write down what they were each refusing to give. He left behind one instruction, which was that the term could not be renewed by Jonas, by the department, or by a manager, but only by a reading at a handover by the outgoing and incoming holders, and that any member of the shop or the union who believed a holder had been named without understanding the claim could call a stop on the work, and that this would be written into the term as a third line.

“It is not my power to write that,” Jonas said.

“It is your power to ask for it in the term you issued,” Tomas said, “which makes it my power to hold you to, and hers, and theirs. That is the entire mechanism. It is boring, and it works about as often as a washing machine.”

And there was the thing Tomas had put on the table on the third evening and then, deliberately, took back off it: what happens when two people on the rota both refuse, and somebody has to be the least-bad option at four in the morning. Nobody in the room had offered an answer, and Tomas had not asked twice, and the roster had answered it the only way a room answers a question like that, which was by writing down a line that made it worse. He read *a refusal of a whole shift ends the shift, not the job* on the way to the door and said nothing about it, which was how Jonas knew he had heard it.

At ten past one Ezra Mbeki came on, read the whole term aloud to himself in an empty corridor the way the page required him to, signed the box, took the key, and then came up the ramp and asked Jonas whether the sixth line of the plate was still true, and Jonas said that it was.

“That is the only reason I came back,” Ezra said. “Everything else on your page is fine with me. If there is nobody on, the door shuts, and I get called, and I come. If I cannot come, the door shuts and the person who needed it waits and complains at me. That I can live with. What I cannot live with is a version where a man in another building is holding my door, because the day he has a bad afternoon somebody ends up on my corridor and the route will still say his name on it.”

The sixth line said: **IF NO HOLDER IS ON DUTY: the route is held by the issuer.**

Nobody had ever been named to replace it. That had been the point of the original repair, and Jonas had heard himself say it out loud at six in the morning, and Ruth had put the printed strip in her coat pocket.

Marta Sowle, who was ninety-one and had come in her coat because she did not intend to have it described to her by somebody else, said the thing that decided it. “Take it off,” she said. “I do not understand a word of it, and I would like it off that door, and I would like somebody to be cross with you about it afterwards.”

It took until four.

The witnesses for a second edit at the same plate were not available in the way the first one had been, and Jonas made a point of saying so out loud to the room before he touched anything. He could work on one hinge and one rule at a time. What he was about to do was not a promise; it was a subtraction. He was going to take the issuer out of the fallback and put a declared reduction in its place, and if the plate would not take it, then the page would stand as it was written and the door would keep staying open for him.

Nobody in that room had asked him to. Three of the people in it had already refused him on the record inside four days, and one of them had refused him in a kitchen, and the fifth had come to the meeting so that it would not be done in a room she was not in.

He anchored on the plate. He put the brass probe on it. He held the strap down against the frame with his right hand, because Ruth had told him on the second night that the right hand was the honest one and the left one was the one that learned, and his right hand could not feel the ceramic at all, and he worked anyway.

Ezra Mbeki stood on the probe. Ezra had read the term, could say when it ended, knew the claim, and had told the laundry and the card table and the porter’s rest room in that order, while putting his coat away, that he was doing it because the alternative was doing nothing and because he had read what was printed under the term about who goes into the corridor if it fails.

The field took it more slowly than the first one had, and it hurt in a different place. The grey line on the inside of his right wrist, which had been cold and unremarkable for two days, opened further and went on past the wrist, up the inside of the forearm to somewhere below the elbow, and for a moment he could not hear the room at all. He had understood by then that it was not an injury. Injuries do not wait for you at a plate. Then it stopped, and the plate printed its narrow strip again.

> **FALLBACK AMENDED.**
> **IF NO HOLDER IS ON DUTY: THE ROUTE IS CLOSED.**
> **An emergency opening must be declared in writing by the named on-call clinician, entered in the log, and written on the public board.**
> **Term unchanged. Issuer unchanged. Claim unchanged.**

Sanaa read the strip twice, because the middle of it was hers.

“That is me,” she said. “Every day between eleven and half past one, if somebody is brought up that ramp, somebody has to telephone me and I have to say the word and write it down. And on a day when I am in a resuscitation room with both hands full, it will not get answered, and somebody will stand there with a stretcher and no answer.”

“Yes.”

“I am saying yes,” Sanaa said, “and I want it in the record that I said yes knowing that. And I want the number on that board to be a person’s number and not a desk.”

Marta Sowle read the new line twice through her glasses and said, “That is better. That one I could understand.”

Nobody thanked him. Ruth was not there to be thanked, and the cost of it was going to be in his own arm at three in the morning when he could not sleep, and there was nothing on the plate that would let him pay it early. When he sat down on the step outside the ramp the corridor carried on without him, and the door stayed shut behind him because there was nobody on it to open it.

At ten past four the Old Ward’s maintenance office sent Jonas a request, and it went to him because his name was in the issuer field of a plate that belonged to a series, and the request was four lines long and entirely ordinary.

> **RECLAMATION SEASON MAINTENANCE REVIEW — SERIES 6 (CONTINUITY PROJECT, MUNICIPAL MAINTENANCE).**
> **Requesting officer: issuer, west clinic care entrance, series 6 plate 41. Unindexed nodes in chain: 2. Response required before the review date.**

He read it twice, standing in the yard at River Stacks in the rain with a sanding block in his left hand and his right hand in his pocket because it still would not close properly.

*Six days had been the length of a term on a ramp. It was now the fourth of six, and the term did not contain one word about a node on an embankment, and the notice did not contain one word about a door.*

He went and found Bea Osunde and asked her whether the shop had a series index, and she said the shop had a wall cabinet with the original Continuity Project maintenance cards in it, filed by series, and that grade III was not cleared to open drawer four, and that his name would not be in the cabinet and that she was not going to leave the keys in a yard in the rain for a man who could not close his own hand.

Then she went and got the keys, because she had decided he was going to do it anyway, and because nineteen years of grade II had taught her that a man who cannot be given a rule will build one.
