# Chapter 0020 — The Flood-Gate Choice

They came back up the path into the loft kitchen at ten past seven and found the big room dark with the lights on over the tables, because Aurrie had sent the children home an hour early for once in their lives, and the argument started there, in a kitchen that smelled of toast and chlorine, and it ran for three hours.

“What do you want me to do?” he asked.

“No,” said Pia.

“It is a legitimate question.”

“It is a question you already know the answer to, which is why you asked it.” She did not sit down. “You want to know what we want so that you can do it and go away. If you ask four people in this room, you will get four answers, and then you will do the one that is easiest, and in a month you will say *I asked them*. You did not ask us. You asked us what you should do and then you took the answer away.”

The four of them who had been in the room an hour earlier were still in the room, and Pia was at the end of the table with her arms folded where she had been, and she had said nothing at all since the door. Aurrie Nkemdirim at the head of the table. Margo Doré with the cardigan back over the clipboard. Bo Trimble, who had not gone home and had put the torch on the table within reach. And, from seven o’clock, a short woman of seventy-one in a cardigan and rubber boots who had come with a plastic bag of apples and been introduced by nobody at all, and who sat down, and said nothing, and ate an apple.

“I am going to tell you what I have and have not seen,” Jonas said, “because I do not want anybody acting on my figures tonight that I have not been down there to check. From the path I have seen a wet patch on the inner face of that bank about the size of a doorway, and Bo has had a torch on it for four minutes, and there is a concrete chamber down there with a flap in it. That is what I have. I have not put a hand on any of it.”

Margo Doré turned her pencil over. “Then what do you actually know, because I have got a rota on that fence with thirty-one crosses on it.”

“I know that a boil on a canal bank is never the thing itself and always a report of a thing,” Jonas said. “It means water is coming up through the fill behind the bank instead of running off it, and the fill is not supposed to be full, and it is full because there is a chamber at the bottom of it with a flap that is supposed to shut when the tide comes over the path. I am betting that flap has not shut in a long time, because nothing on this street has the money or the tide table to shut it, and everything on this street has the reason to want it shut. When the tide comes up over the embankment and that flap does not shut, the lowland side fills from the canal side, and it finds the weak place, which is that bank, and then it finds the chamber, and then it goes looking for somewhere else to put the water. Which is the rest of us.”

“How long?” Margo said.

“Before a surge? I cannot tell you. The next tide that comes up over the path, or the one after, or the one in March.” He put his left hand on the table. “What I can tell you is that if the flap cannot shut, the bank is not a wall any more. It is a lid.”

Nobody spoke for a while. Bo got up and turned the tap in the kitchen off, which was, Jonas thought, an extremely good piece of timing.

“All right,” Aurrie said. “Three things on this table, and one of them has to be chosen, and I am starting because if I do not start I never will. The first is that we apply to the city for a proper service. A culvert, a main, a standpipe on the register, a term with a name on it, a review date, a department that has to answer the telephone. I have wanted that since 1994.”

“Ninety-four,” Hector Mbatha said, from the doorway. He was seventy and he had come in without anyone noticing and stood with his coat on. “My mother is in that house.”

“I know. I want it,” Aurrie said. “I am not going to stand here and pretend that not having a district is a thing we are proud of. It is not. It is a hole in the floor and we have been standing over it since before the children were born. But I am also going to tell you the price, because I have been the one saying *we should ask* for thirty-two years and I am tired of saying it without the rest. The moment we apply, there is a file. The file has a survey. The survey has a number of houses on it, and the number of houses on it is a list, and the list is made by somebody who counts rooms and does not ask who sleeps in them. And there is a deputy registrar somewhere with a clipboard who has been waiting thirty-two years for a building on this street that he can put an order on, and I have watched that man close a shelter that was falling down and be right to do it, and I have never once been able to explain to my own people why I did not want him to come here.”

“Then the second thing,” Margo said. “Temporary. We take the county pump and four tonnes of stone and we hold it for this tide and the next one. Nobody writes anything down. It is a favor from a county office to people who flooded and a favor can be refused next year, and I would rather have a favor I can lose than a plate I cannot.”

“The third thing,” Ottie Beck said, and set the apples in the shoe box and looked at Jonas while she said it, “is that we carry on as we have been carrying on, and that Jonas comes on Thursday and looks at the flap and tells us what he sees, and Bo and I and Margo do the work, and nobody in this building signs anything.”

She had a way of speaking that made a room feel like a ward round, and Jonas had the distinct feeling of being examined by somebody who had spent a lifetime deciding who was lying.

“Doctor,” he said carefully.

“I was a district nurse, and I am seventy-one, and you may call me Otie, and I have run a Wednesday clinic in that room since 1998 with a rota of four people and no address.” She turned one of the apples over in her hand. “Twenty-eight years of Wednesdays. In that time the chest complaints I have seen have gone up, the incomes have gone down, and the number of forms has gone up, and the address has not once improved a single one of them. I say that as a woman who wanted the address too. I am not saying the day we ask is a bad day. I am saying that the day we ask, we get a number, and the number is how they find you, and after that everything is a service of the number.”

Pia spoke for the first time since the door.

“There is a fourth thing and none of you have said it,” she said. “We can apply, and be counted, and get a culvert. Or we can refuse, and lose the culvert, and keep the rota. Or we can take the county pump for one tide and pretend to ourselves that the county is a favour and not a first step. Those are the three. But there is also the thing where we apply, get the culvert, get the count, and then in four years somebody comes with a plate and a renewal and asks who is on duty at two in the morning, and this time we are the ones who have to answer it, with a number.”

“Aurrie.” Hector had not moved from the doorway. “You have wanted that number since before I was born.”

“I have,” Aurrie said.

“So have I,” Hector said. “That is not an argument for it. It is the reason we are having this argument in a kitchen at ten at night instead of a meeting.”

“That is exactly what you want,” Hector said, after a moment, to Jonas. “Except that you are describing the fault instead of asking who fixes it.”

“That is fair.”

“I thought so.”

They argued for another hour and Jonas listened to all of it and did not say a word, and at some point he became aware that Bo Trimble was watching him with the same flat attention Bo had given him at the flap gate, waiting to see whether a man with a deadline would now produce a plan that made Bo’s four hundred millimeters of cut-back pipe somebody else’s problem.

At half past nine Jonas put both hands on the table — the left one flat, the right one half-curled and useless — and said what he had come to say, which was the part that was his.

“I am not going to tell you which of the three to take,” he said. “But you are going to do this work in a fortnight, and I am the person who is going to be asked in eight days to answer for a node on that embankment, so I am going to tell you exactly what the work costs and who has to be standing in it, and then I am going to go away.”

He put a sheet of the clinic's legal pad on the table and wrote with his left hand, badly.

“The flap will not be repaired by a man with a spanner. Bo says there is no hinge left on that pintle and that the sill has gone in the middle, and Bo has been down there more times than I have, so I am going to price his version and not mine, and I will know by midnight whether he is right. If he is right, then to cut the bank back and set a new sill and a new flap you need three things. The chamber pumped dry and held dry, which is a county pump and a diesel generator and somebody awake all night. Four working days, minimum, with the tide table, because you can only work on the ebb and you get two hours of it. And either a marine crew of six out of the city at nine hundred a day, or six people from this street — and I have to tell you that the six people from this street will do it worse and get hurt doing it.”

“Say that again,” Margo said.

“You will get hurt doing it.”

“All right. That is useful. Nobody had said it out loud.”

“If the city ever puts a term on that flap, one of you has to be named on it, somebody else has to be able to say when it ends, and somebody has to be answerable for the night it fails. That is all. Three names and an end.” He put the pen down. “I am not offering to be any of them. I have been all of them, and I have a released cardiac slot on a public board to prove what that does.”

Aurrie looked at the sheet for a long time.

“Write the three things down for me,” she said. “The application, the temporary, and the carry-on. On paper. So that when somebody in ten years says *we could always have asked*, there is a piece of paper that says we had three choices and that we picked one in a kitchen in front of the children’s coats.”

“I will do that after I have looked at the sill.”

“You will do it before you leave this room,” she said, “because in ten years you will not be the one holding it.”

They went out to the chamber at twenty past ten with a torch and a length of rope and the last of the ebb under them, and Bo went down first, on a rope, because it is his street and his fault.

The flap was a disc of green-painted iron on a frame, and when Jonas put the heel of his left hand on it and pushed, nothing moved at all, and he could feel the rust scale come off onto his palm and the whole frame shudder slightly and stop, jammed, exactly as he had suspected. He got the end of the rope round the frame, took the strain with his weight and not his right arm, and had Bo take the other end, and on the third pull the pintle moved a quarter of an inch and made a noise like a knife in a drawer.

“There,” he said, “is the whole of the fault. Pintle is solid, and the sill is not. It is rotten through the middle third on both faces, which means it is rotten in the middle, which means there is nothing left in there to spread the load. And it is worth about a hundred and ten pounds of ironwork and eight hours of somebody’s hands, if somebody can get a scaffold and a torch into a box that is a metre and a half wide. It will not hold a surge. It will hold an ordinary tide, if it is packed properly, for a season.”

“So we can get through the winter,” Margo said.

“We can get through the winter if it is packed properly and if nobody drives a van over the bank.”

Bo went into the boat shed and came back with a half-used tin of bedding compound that had been on a shelf there since somebody shuttered a window, and Jonas had to be talked out of using it, because the tin had a date on the bottom of it, and Margo went up to the church hall and came back with a fresh cartridge instead and set it on the stone without comment. Then they packed the sill with it, one face each, and it took the better part of an hour because the compound has to be worked into the joint and not laid on it. They talked the entire time about the four hundred millimeters of cut-back pipe, and by the end Bo had agreed to say in front of Aurrie, on Sunday, that the coupling would hold for two years if the length above it were right, and Jonas had agreed to bring a compression coupling and a gasket on Thursday in his pocket and nothing else.

Above them, on the dark, the green standpipe and its forty-slot rota board stood with a great many crosses on it, and behind that the net loft with its light on and a plate on the kitchen wall with half its face scraped off, and nine houses with blue doors and brass tokens hanging beside them, and the whole of it held in place, at that moment, by a woman with an apple in her hand and a boy with a crescent callus and a thing that had been in a rota for longer than anybody could remember and had been cut off a plate by somebody who did not leave a name.

“Bo,” Jonas said, as they came back up the path. “The one who is a boy. Why does the loft not have a plate?”

Bo wiped his hands on the tea towel. “We had one. It kept asking who was on duty at two in the morning, same as your clinic door, and nobody here was, and we got tired of explaining to a machine that a person was tired. So it came off the wall and it went in the canal.” He looked at the cloth in his hands. “And somebody scraped the other half of it, and I did not do that, and I have not asked, and neither should you.” He handed the towel back. “Do not make it into a story. It is not ours and it is not about us, and that is all I am going to say about it.”

On the towpath, with the bicycle and the three bags and the dark water on his left, Pia Santos waited for him until he had got his jacket out of the bag.

“I am going to say one thing and then I am going to go home,” she said. “You found that number on a card in a drawer because of a door you fixed, four days after you fixed it, and not because of the folder, and not because of the shuttle, and not because of your father.” She watched him not answer. “I have watched six men walk down here with a reason that would have sounded better than that one. You have got the worst possible reason and the right one, and I do not know what to do with you, and I am not going to decide tonight.”

“The response is due in eight days.”

“Yes. And you cannot answer it alone, and that is not my fault, and it is also not the end of it.” She got on the bicycle. “Thursday. After six. Bring the coupling. And do not bring the roll.”

Then she went down the towpath, and the bollards and the low chain-link and the three hundred meters of a street that had been blank on four official records went past her, and Jonas stood at the top of the towpath with a wet right hand and a card in his pocket and a name he had promised not to use, and listened to the water working under a bank that was, tonight, still holding.
