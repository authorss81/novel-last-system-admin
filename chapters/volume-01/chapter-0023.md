# Chapter 0023 — The Tide Table

The tide was out at five minutes past five on Saturday morning and Jonas got into the chamber with a torch, a tape, a folding rule, a bar, and Bo Trimble holding the rope above him, which was not a position Bo was on any rota for and which Bo had made absolutely clear by saying four times, on the way down, that he was not the rope man and was only holding it because there was nobody else at five in the morning.

“Jonas.”

“I know.”

“You have said the words ‘be careful’ to me once, on Monday, on a bank, at night, and you were wrong then.”

“That was different. There was water moving.”

“There is water moving now,” said Bo, “at about a metre an hour, and it is coming back, and in two hours and twenty minutes it will be coming back at about four hundred, and if you are under that sill when it is, you are not a technician, you are a thing the chamber is full of.”

The chamber was a metre and a half square and two deep with a ladder rung set into the wall at intervals that had once made sense. The flap was a disc of green-painted iron on a cast frame, half out of the water, with a fortnight of silt lying on the lower half of it in a shape like a leaf.

Jonas worked from the bottom up, and he said what he found out loud, because Bo was the witness and a witness who cannot repeat the finding is not a witness.

“Sill beam. Cast or dressed, about a hundred and fifty deep, two hundred wide. The outer face is sound. The inner face is gone from the centre for about six hundred millimetres, and it is not thin, it is *hollow*, and I can put my whole hand in it. That is not rot at a surface. That is rot through the depth.”

“How long.”

“If you want the truth, I do not know, and neither did the man from the van in a coat.” He got his fingers into it again. “I can tell you it is not this season. It has been like that since before the compound went on, and the compound on Monday night is sitting on top of six hundred millimetres of nothing, and it is holding because compression compound is a very good glue and a very bad beam.”

“And the flap will not shut.”

“The flap will not shut because the pintle is a solid lump of rust, and I want to be careful here, because Bo has been right about this since Monday and I have only caught up.” He put the flat of the bar against the frame and worked it, gently, and the frame moved about a millimetre and stopped with a sound like a knife being drawn through a drawer of cutlery. “There is a thing I did not expect. There are two sets of holes.”

Bo did not follow.

“They are the bolt holes in the frame, going into the wall, and they are the ones somebody made when this pintle was fitted. The other set is over here, forty millimetres out, and they are the original ones, and they are bigger, and they are the only ones that go into sound concrete.” Jonas put the tape on them and read it into the torchlight. “Somebody took the original pintle out and put in a pintle that did not match the frame, at some point when there was a reason and no money, and that is why the flap has never sat square, and that is why it has never shut properly, and it is probably why the fill behind that bank is full.”

“So it was somebody’s fault.”

“It was somebody’s job at some point and there was nobody to pay for it and nobody to ask,” Jonas said. “That is not the same as fault and I am not going to let anybody in this kitchen make it the same as fault, because the second time I have watched a thing like that happen on this street the sentence *somebody’s fault* was used to stop anybody naming a body.”

He came up the ladder at ten past six with silt in the creases of his knees and the bar under his arm, and on the wall of the chamber, above the water line, about eighteen inches down from the ladder head, there was a plate.

It was the size of a postcard. It was ceramic, and it had been bolted to the concrete, and there was a crust of silt over the whole of it, and Jonas went down and got a rag and a soft brush from the loft and took the crust off it with his left hand while Bo held the torch, and the water came back up the chamber behind him the whole time and they both watched it do it.

The plate came up out of the silt the way a photograph comes up in a tray, in pieces, and what it said was this.

> **6-L/14**
> **LOWLAND WARDS — FLAP CONTROL, LOWLAND DIP**
> **TERM: not legible**
> **HOLDER: —**
> **CLAIM: not legible**

Underneath, when he had got the last of the silt off and had the flat of the brush on the ceramic, there was a fifth line he had not expected, in the same flat register as the gate’s own log and the department’s answer, worn almost through.

> **IF THE FLAP IS NOT SHUT, THE BANK IS NOT A WALL.**
> **NO HOLDER IS NAMED FOR THIS NODE.**

“That is a municipal plate,” Bo said.

“That is a municipal plate,” Jonas agreed.

“It has got your number on it.”

“It has got a number on it,” Jonas said, “and I am going to say the whole of the other thing now, before anybody in this building finds it on a board in the city and has to wonder whether I was ever going to mention it. That number is a node in a chain of plates, and the chain runs from a cabinet in River Stacks through the Saint Orra bridge to a clinic door, and the plate at the clinic door is mine, because I wrote a term on it, and the answer I gave to a registrar yesterday says in writing that a department officer is going to come and look at this plate before the end of the month.”

The kitchen, which had been noisy, went the particular quiet that a kitchen goes.

“Before the twenty-eighth,” Margo Doré said.

“Before the twenty-eighth.”

“So that is the answer then. We have been found.”

“No,” Jonas said. “You have been *numbered*. Those are not the same and I am not going to be the man who tells a street it has been found when what has happened is that a piece of paper in a drawer finally reached a desk.” He put the rag in his pocket. “The plate has been here the whole time. The chain has been here the whole time. I have known about the number since Wednesday. I did not know where it was until yesterday at six this morning, and I did not bring a form.”

Bo looked at the plate for a while.

“You went down there at five in the morning to look at a rotten beam,” he said, “when you have known since Wednesday that they are coming.”

“I went down there at five in the morning because there is a thing wrong with a beam and I am the person who was asked to look at beams,” Jonas said, “and if I had come at noon with a form you would have had to talk to a form.”

Nobody thanked him, and Ottie Beck, who had come in from the clinic room with a cup of tea at half past six and had been listening from the doorway, said the thing that changed the shape of the morning.

“Right,” she said. “Then somebody is going to have to tell me something, because I have been standing in that doorway for ten minutes and every one of you has been talking about a beam and a number, and not one of you has said the word *when*.”

She came in and put her cup down on the table next to Margo's cardigan-covered clipboard, and Margo took the paper tide table out of her coat and unfolded it on the table, because Ottie Beck had asked a question that could only be answered with a table, and everybody in the room knew it.

It was a printed sheet, four columns, black on grey, from the harbourmaster's office, and it had been folded and unfolded so many times that it had gone through at the folds.

“Right,” said Margo. “Here is the month. And here is the only part of it that matters, and it is the only reason anybody can do this job at all, and it is a neap.”

She put her finger on it.

“Floods at the eighth, the fifteenth, the twenty-second, the twenty-ninth, and the sixth. And the springs are the big ones, and the big ones are the twelfth to the twenty-second, and after the twenty-second they go quiet again until the end of the month. The range in a spring is two and a half metres between high and low and in a neap it is a metre and a bit, and the water in that chamber does not go anywhere in a spring. So the work is done in the neaps, and the neaps are the twenty-second to the twenty-sixth, and inside them you get two ebbs a day, and I am going to tell you why only one of them is any use.”

“The one you can see,” said Jonas.

“The one you can see.” Margo turned the sheet a quarter turn. “Morning ebb, every day of it, low water between five past five and half past five. Evening ebb, low water about twenty to eight, which is dark, and which I am not having anybody working a chamber in the dark at the bottom of a dip in a flood year, and I have not worked in a flood year, I have worked in thirty-one ordinary years and I am not starting.”

“So the working window is the morning ebb,” said Jonas. “Five past five to half past seven, and that is two hours and twenty minutes, and that is the whole of it.”

“Two hours and twenty minutes,” said Margo. “And on the flood you go out, and the flood comes in at four hundred an hour at the spring and about a hundred and forty at the neap, and the chamber is two metres deep, and the ladder rungs are set for somebody with two hands and one bucket.”

“Four minutes,” said Jonas. “From the sill to the top rung, at the neap. Three at the spring.”

Nobody said anything for a while, and then Bo said, quietly, to the table, “That is the answer to the question about who is in the water. That is what it is. It is four minutes and one of us on a rope, and the person on the rope is standing at the top with a lamp, and the person with the lamp is the person who does not go in.”

“And the lamp is in the loft,” said Aurrie, “and the loft is a hundred and forty metres from the top of the chamber, and I have run this exchange for nineteen years and I know every single thing in it and there is not a lamp in it that will throw a beam forty metres down a slope in fog.”

“Then the lamp is a problem we solve this week,” said Wesley Kpomah.

“No it isn't,” said Ottie. “It is a problem you solve by *not being there*. The lamp is not the problem. The lamp is the least of it.” She turned her cup round on the table. “Here is the thing I came in to say, so say it and let us get on. Two hours and twenty minutes a day for four days, which is nine hours and twenty minutes, and I want you all to sit still and understand that number, because everybody in this room has been talking about four days as though four days were a long time. Four days of tide is nine hours and twenty minutes of working. Everything else is waiting.”

“Then the crew is paid for the waiting,” said Jonas.

“Yes,” said Otie. “The crew is paid for the waiting, and that is the whole difference between the two things on the table, and it is the reason I am going to say the second thing and then I am going to go and do my clinic in four days’ time whether or not the water is out.”

She looked at Margo, and then at Jonas, and Jonas understood that she was going to ask the question he had thought he had come down here to ask, and that he was not going to be allowed to ask it.

“Tell them the two prices,” she said. “Properly. Both of them. And do not do the thing where you make one of them sound braver.”

Jonas put his left hand on the table and wrote with it, badly, on the back of a piece of the loft's paper.

“The marine crew is six men out of the city with their own gear and their own insurance and a foreman who will not let anybody in that chamber on a tide they have not seen. Nine hundred a day. Four days, because you pay them for the waiting, and they come on the Monday and the stone goes down on the Tuesday. So that is three thousand six hundred, and I have a hire on a four-inch diesel pump at four hundred and seventy-five a day for the four days, and four tonnes of stone delivered to the top of this street is three hundred and forty, and there is a skip and a lorry for the rotten sill and the silt and that is four hundred and ten, and the county will do the mason two days at six hundred if we can get him, and the flint and the haunching and the new gasket and forty bags of bedding compound is two hundred and eighty.” He stopped writing. “That is seven thousand one hundred and thirty, and that is if the pump comes, and the pump does not come unless a name goes in box four of a green form.”

“And six of us,” said Hector Mbatha.

“And six of you,” Jonas said. “Six of you, four days, and the money for the stone and the skip and the materials, which is one thousand and thirty, and you have,” he looked at Margo, who had the answer before he asked, “eight hundred and twelve between the loft tin and your water tin, and a hundred and one in the church hall tin, and that hundred and one is harvest supper money.”

“Seven hundred and forty and the church hall has one hundred and one and it is harvest supper money and it has been in the tin since September because we have not had a harvest supper.”

“Then the gap is six thousand two hundred and it does not close by anybody being careful.”

“No,” said Hector. “But it closes by six people, and six people cost nothing, and that is the arithmetic and it is a lie and I am the one who is going to say it is a lie, because I am seventy and I have been on a bank in my life.”

They argued for a long time and Bo let them all get most of the way round the table before he put his hand on the middle of it, which he had never done before and which stopped everybody.

“Six of us will do it worse,” he said. “That is what the man said and he is right and nobody has argued with him and everybody is going to argue with him now because there is no money. So let us do the arithmetic that has not been done. Six of us do it worse, and I want to know worse in what, because if it is worse in *hours* then we are not worse, we are slower, and slower is not the same as worse. A worse repair is one that does not shut. A slower repair is one that takes six mornings instead of four. And we have the sixth and seventh of next month as far as I am concerned, and the neap after this one is longer.”

“It is longer and it is a month away and there is a flood on the twenty-ninth in between,” said Margo.

“Then the fill is wet from the twenty-ninth to the fourth and we do it in the second neap,” said Bo, “and the man is right that we will get hurt, and I would like to hear somebody say where.”

That stopped the room for about four seconds, which on the Weir was a considerable thing, and Bo took it.

“Not from the chamber,” he said. “From the bank. You cut the bank back two metres and you have got four tonnes of stone and no scaffold and no ladder, and you have got nine households, and one of the nine is a seventy-year-old man with a bad hip, and I have watched that bank come off a section of it twice. Somebody goes on that bank. That is where the hurt is, and it is not the tide, it is the bank, and nobody has written the bank down on anything.”

Which was true, and got about nine seconds of silence, and was then taken apart by Margo Doré, who had been looking at the almanac since he put it on the table and who now turned it round and put her thumb on a column.

“No,” she said. “No, that is not going to happen, and it is going to happen because the three of you have been arguing about money for an hour and none of you has read the sheet in front of you. The neaps are the twenty-second to the twenty-sixth. The eighteenth is in the middle of the spring. I have been telling you for twenty-nine years that the chamber is a neap job and I have just heard myself agree to four mornings of it, and the only reason I agreed is that there is four hundred pounds in a tin.”

“Which is the reason and it is an honest one,” said Aurrie.

“It is an honest reason and it is still not a plan.” Margo took her thumb off the column. “So we do not get to have it both ways. Either it is a neap job and it is the twenty-second to the twenty-sixth and the chamber does not open until then, or it is a spring job and it is the eighteenth, and if it is a spring job then the flood on the twenty-second at two in the morning lands in the middle of it, and we will be standing on a bank in the dark with a lamp.”

Nobody liked either of those sentences.

“I am not choosing between them tonight,” said Margo. “I am going to bed. I have got a table and it says what it says and it has said it since 1998 in a drawer in the loft, and I have read it out to this room more times than anybody in this room can count, and the reason nobody ever checks it is that I am the one who reads it and I have never once had anybody stand behind me and read it as well.” She squared the sheet. “Tomorrow. Four in the morning. Come and find me and I will read it again, and whoever wants to check me can check me, because I have been wrong about things on this street before and I would rather be caught than believed.”

Aurrie Nkemdirim picked up the card with the coupling specification still on it, the one she had signed and dated on Thursday, and looked at it, and then put it face down on the table in front of her.

“No,” she said. “No, Bo. I know what you have done. You have made a plan and you have made it out of money and you have made it so that it is the only thing on the table. And the man is right that you are better with a wrench than I am, and the man is also right that six of us will get hurt, and neither of those things means the plan is ours. A plan made out of a gap in a tin is a plan made out of your age. Every time one of you two has come to me in eleven years with a plan that came out of a shortage, the plan has been for you to be able to do the work and for me to say yes to it. You have not noticed. I have noticed every time.”

“I was not trying to—”

“I know you were not. That is why I can say it without being angry.” She turned the card over again. “So here is what I will agree to, and it is not your plan. Tuesday the eighteenth. Four days. We will do the work and we will pay the skip and the stone and the pump, and we will put the stone down whatever box four says, because the stone is the stone and four tonnes of it lying at the top of this street is a wall. And on the eighteenth I will read the tide table out loud in this kitchen at four in the morning to whoever turns up, and if there is nobody, the work does not happen, and the chamber stays shut with compound on it and we wait for the next neap. And you will not hold that against me in a month.”

Nobody spoke.

“And the rest of it,” she said, “is not mine. It is Margo’s box and Margo’s name and Margo’s night, and I have said the thing I am going to say and I am going to go and open the clinic room.”

She did not go and open the clinic room. She went and stood at the window with her back to the room for about half a minute, which was longer than the room deserved, and then she turned round.

“Sit down,” she said. “All of you. Because there is one more thing and it is the only thing in this morning that I have not said and it is the thing that is actually going to happen.”

Jonas sat down.

“The county will lend the pump,” Aurrie said, “if a name goes in box four. And the person whose name goes in box four is a person who will be responsible for a machine on a bank in the dark four times a day for four days, and that is what Ottie said, and it is true. And I want it minuted in this kitchen that I have been the one saying *we should ask* since 1994 and that I have never once in thirty-two years asked for a pump, or a culvert, or a service, or a letter, and I have never asked because I could not find one box to put my name in that was about water. Every box has been about the district.” She put her hand flat on the table, not on any paper. “So here is the whole of what I want, and then I will be quiet. I want to ask for a pump, and I want to be asked, properly, by somebody with a form, in front of Bo, and I want to be able to say no in the room where the form is, and if I say no, the form goes back in the envelope and nobody on this street is ever asked again by me. Can that be arranged or is it a mad thing to want.”

“It can be arranged,” said Margo, “and it will not help, and I am going to say why and then I will get on with the eighteenth.”

Bo put his hand on the table again, and this time he was not going to let it go, and Jonas watched a seventeen-year-old decide that he was going to be the last one to speak and get it right.

“You are all wrong about the pump,” he said. “All of you. And I am not saying you are wrong about the money. I am saying that as of yesterday morning there is an officer of the city coming to look at a plate at the bottom of that chamber before the end of the month, and that officer needs a pump, because you cannot inspect a rotten sill in two feet of water, and the department will hire a pump, because the department hires things all the time. You have all been arguing about the county as though the county were the only door in the wall.”

“No,” said Margo.

“Jonas —”

“You have already told them.” Bo stood up. He was not loud. “You said it at six o’clock this morning, in this room, in those words, and Margo heard it and Aurrie heard it. You have written the number on a wall in a public building and your name under it. So do not sit there and be shocked that somebody has noticed a door. If he asks them for a pump on Tuesday, they will say yes, because it is four hundred and seventy-five a day against a hole in the bank, and the price of the pump is that somebody writes a location in a box in a form in an office, and by Friday there will be a line in a book in River Stacks that says *lowland dip* and after that it does not matter what we do with a green form.” He looked at Aurrie. “That is the price. You asked for the box. I am telling you the box is not the only box.”

The kitchen was very quiet.

“Right now,” Bo said. “Four days, from the eighteenth. And the man in the water is not decided and the lamp is not decided and the name in the form is not decided, and I have said the only thing I came in to say, which is that the bank is where the hurt is, and the bank is not on your tide table, and the bank is not in the county form, and the bank is the only part of this that nobody in this room has written down.” He picked up the pencil. “Can I put the bank on the paper.”

Aurrie Nkemdirim looked at him for a long moment.

“Put the bank on the paper,” she said, “and put it in the middle, and leave room round it, because I have been in this kitchen for fifty years and I have never once seen a thing written down in the middle of a page stay there.”
