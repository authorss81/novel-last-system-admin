# Chapter 0041 — The Ninth Is Not a Place

He went up the towpath on Monday afternoon with the card in his coat and a pencil in his right hand, which he could not hold, so it was in his left.

There is a thing about asking people for things that Jonas Mercer had learned in about four weeks and had not learned well: the asking is not the difficult part. The difficult part is that you have to stand in front of the person for long enough after you have asked, in case they change their mind, and there is nowhere to put your hands.

The Weir stand is a green pillar with a bucket on a chain and a forty-slot board nailed to the fence beside it, and the tarp is off the intake grille and lying over the grass on the upstream side, and the water in the bucket is the colour of weak tea, and when he put the bucket under the tap and turned it, the plate printed one line and it took about four seconds to come through the paint.

**THE INTAKE IS SILTED. THE POINT IS SERVING FROM A SILTED INTAKE.**

That is the whole of what a plate can say about it. It does not say how the water is to be drawn, or who will clear the trap, or what a silting intake does to a person at half past five in the morning, and it does not mention money, and it printed it while he was holding the bucket and went back to asking the rota a question that the board on the fence answers with a pencil.

Margo Doré came up the path at about ten past two with a towel round her shoulders and read the line off the pillar over his shoulder, and said, “Two months,” and went to wash a cup.

“Six weeks,” said Jonas.

“It is two months and I have counted every day of it since the twenty-second of March, and you are going to tell me it is the tarp.” She put the cup in the sink. “It is not the tarp. The tarp keeps the leaves out. The leaves were never the problem. The problem is that the trap is a box of silt the size of a bath and the main is four inches and the silt goes in at four inches and stays there, and the last two times we cleared it by hand in one morning, and the morning before that it was the two of us and the Wednesday, and the Wednesday we could not do it because the childcare exchange had a child with a temperature and I was the one who sat with her.”

“All right,” said Jonas.

“You are going to write that down.”

“I am going to write down that nobody came on the Wednesday.” He took the card out. “That is the whole of what I came for.”

And then he said it, badly, standing at a standpipe in the rain, which is where he had planned to say it and knew he would not.

Aurrie Nkemdirim was in the exchange doorway with a folding chair half up in her hands and she had heard him from about ten feet, which is the distance that everybody on that street hears anything.

“No,” she said.

“I have not asked you yet.”

“You have been holding a card for a month,” said Aurrie Nkemdirim, “and you have had two blank lines on it since the fifth of April, and you have walked up here four times since then without writing a name, and you have come today in the rain with a pencil in the wrong hand, so I am going to save us both about four minutes.”

He said it anyway. That is what he had come to be able to do and it took him about ninety seconds and he had it down to the length of a long sentence, and the sentence was: a term is not a person, a claim needs a counterparty, and in a flood or a surge the Lattice has to be able to ask somebody what a service is for and who is holding it, and the people who can answer that for a street are its own residents, and he had six and needed nine, and the seventh line was a street, and hers was the street.

She put the chair down against the doorframe, which she does not normally do, and looked at him, and her voice did not go up at all, which was worse.

“Sit down,” she said, “because I am going to say this once and I would like to be sitting down while I say it, and then you are going to leave.”

He did not sit down.

“Then say it standing up.”

“All right. Standing up.” She looked at the card and not at him. “A name on a list is not a name on a list, Mr Mercer, and I have known that for longer than you have had a card. A name on a list is a place to send a letter. That is the entire difference and it is everything, and there is nothing else in it. In March there was nothing about this street that a letter could be sent to, and the letter went nowhere, and eleven flats down the canal were on their own because of it, and I slept. And on the first of April a man in a good coat stood up in a room and said *this document establishes the service* — and he did not use my street's name, and that is the only reason I am not currently a ward in somebody's report.

“If you put a name on the seventh line, then in June a committee will ask who the seventh line is, and to ask, they will have to write to somebody, and to write to somebody they will need a place, and the place will come from the list, and the list will be the place. And then this street is a service of the city with a service address and a named witness and a claim on the nearest person who can be reached, and every one of those four things will arrive within a year, and I have watched two districts get that in my lifetime and I am not running an experiment on my own street in April with a man holding a pencil in his left hand.”

“You have already got a document in a city file,” said Jonas. “The loft paper. It went to the Assembly on the first and Sore certified it and nobody is named on it.”

“Yes,” said Aurrie Nkemdirim. “And on Wednesday I am going to read it to a hospital board and I am going to read it sitting down, and that document has no address, so nobody can write to it, and if it had one, in six months somebody would use it as a service address for something they wanted, because that is what service addresses are for, and I have watched that happen at Bellow Market to a co-operative with a good cause. It is a door. You are asking me to put a door in my own wall.”

He did not argue. He had been told on the Friday night in the shop that the ninth would cost somebody and had spent five days deciding in advance that it was going to be a stranger.

“All right,” he said. “No. And I am going to write it down in full, and I am going to say what you said, and I am going to say you said it and not that you were nervous.”

“That would be worse than not writing it down,” said Aurrie Nkemdirim. “Write what I said. It is a no. It is not a no in the way yours is a no, which is a man being careful. It is a no from a street that has one thing and you have come to take it.”

And from about ten feet away, from the doorway of the cook-house across the water, Pia Santos said, “And the same from me, and you are not to write that twice, because writing it twice makes it two noes, and it is one no, and if you write it twice a hearing will say there were two people on the seventh line and ask me to produce her.”

He wrote it once, on the step, in the rain, with his left hand, which made it crooked. Four lines, and then he read it back to Aurrie Nkemdirim and asked her whether she wanted a word changed, and she said no because a request to change a word is a negotiation and this is not one, and he put the card back in his coat.

“Two,” he said, going down the path. “It is going to be two by Wednesday.”

The corridor at Saint Orra west on Monday the seventh at ten to ten at night is eleven metres long and it is the only room in Nacre where a term gets read out loud in the middle of the floor, and Mina Okafor was at the card table with the box open and the pen in her hand because it was a Monday, and Ruth Nwosu was at the end of it in her coat with her bag over the armrest of the chair she was not going to sit in, and Ezra Mbeki had come in early and was taking his coat off, and Dr. Sanaa Iqbal came in at five past ten with a bundle of discharge letters under her arm and a coat over her white one, because she had been at the east site and had come across.

Jonas Mercer stood at the card table and asked her in front of two people, because Bea Osunde had told him that walking into rooms and asking people for things was the actual job and there was no instrument for it, and if he was going to do it he was going to do it where the answer could be written down.

“The ninth line is a hospital,” he said. “I am asking you to be it.”

Mina Okafor’s pen stopped.

Sanaa Iqbal did not answer immediately, which he had expected, and she did not say no immediately, which he had not, and he understood afterwards that the length of that pause was the whole of the chapter and that he had walked into it.

“Where,” she said.

“There is a table. Nine services down the left and a name against eight of them and one line with nothing on it, and the one line says *a hospital*, and your name would go on it with what you will stand for and what you will refuse, in your own hand, the same as Mina put hers.”

“No,” said Sanaa Iqbal.

And then she put her hand flat on the card table, which she does when she is about to be exact, and gave him the reason in four sentences and did not give him an inch in them.

“The door on this corridor opens when a named clinician declares an emergency in writing, and it has opened twice this month on my declarations, nine minutes on the twenty-fourth and nineteen minutes on the third, and both numbers are on that board with my shift in them. If my name is on a card with a subject on it, then in June, when somebody reads that card, they will read a document that says a doctor is both the person who decides whether the care entrance opens and the person who certifies the rule that lets it open. That is not a small thing. In a hearing that is a conflict of interest on its face, and it will be the only thing anybody will read, and every declaration I have ever given will be re-read as something I certified about myself. So no.”

“That is the right answer,” said Jonas, “and it is not the reason you just gave.”

“No,” said Sanaa Iqbal. “That is the reason I just gave. The other reason is that you have had my sentence since the twenty-ninth of March and you have used it three times to somebody who asked you a direct question, and the last time you used it you did not come and ask me, you told a panel of six men that a category cannot be told no, and you were quoting me, and I was not in the room, and I read the minute afterwards with two other people.”

There is a thing you learn about being right in a corridor. It does not go in like a slap. It arrives about two seconds late and it arrives as an apology you cannot make because the person who is owed it is standing in front of you holding thirty discharge letters.

“Sanaa,” he said.

“No, let me finish, because if I stop now I will be a person who changed her mind in a corridor, and I have been one of those twice in this job and it cost somebody both times.” She took the bundle off the table. “Are you going to ask me one more question or are we going to stand here.”

He asked her one more question, and it was not on the card and it was not the job.

“Have you been trying to get a second clinician on that roster.”

“Yes,” said Sanaa Iqbal. “Since the twenty-fourth of March. I have four refusals from consultants and one outright no from the education office, and the education office said no because a second on-call is a rota line and a rota line is money, and the money came off the department when the participation fund withdrew, and I have not told you any of it because you would have made it a thing about a card, and it is not a thing about a card, it is a thing about a rota with a hole in it.”

“It was a thing about a card,” said Jonas Mercer.

“Yes,” said Sanaa Iqbal, and went down the corridor to the lift, and Ruth Nwosu watched her go and then said, “Well. That is one more than I was expecting and it was the right one.”

At ten o'clock Ruth Nwosu read the five lines. Ezra read them back. Ruth signed the box. Mina Okafor wrote the times.

**New term to 06:00, Wednesday 15 April.**

Jonas asked her the one question he had permission to ask, once, and got it in four words.

“Will you do the fourteenth.”

“No,” said Ruth Nwosu. “I have told Mina and Mina will write it down and I am telling you so that it is on your side of the table as well. My back is a thing for a doctor in Salt Row and I have said no to the fifteenth and I have said no to the fourteenth, and if you ask me again in eleven days I will say no again, and if you ask me a third time I will stop telling you in advance.”

The arrears were not mentioned. They had not been mentioned since the twenty-fourth of March and Jonas had stopped expecting to be told and had not stopped hoping to be, which is a different thing and not a better one.

The Saint Orra west site board sits on the first floor at ten in the morning and takes forty minutes of matters arising, and on Wednesday the ninth of April it took an hour and ten, because a woman of sixty-one read a piece of net-loft paper out loud from a chair.

She read it as it is. That was the condition and it was the loft's condition and not hers: one side of a sheet of ruled net-loft paper, four paperclips, a patch of tape over a seam, no letterhead, no door number, no list of houses, and nobody named on it. She held it in both hands at arm's length because her glasses are for reading and not for holding, and she read the whole of it at the speed of a woman who has practised a sheet in a kitchen twice, and she did not stand up.

The second line of the minute says: *Item 4, submitted by Councillor Sore under the standing order, a document received by the Assembly on 1 April. Read to the board by the author. The author sat throughout.*

Halvorsen had asked, in the first week of March, for the paper and nothing else, and he got it, and he had asked for it again on the Monday and Selah Mora had told him what it was in one sentence in a corridor, and he did not ask again.

Gunnar Halvorsen is a service manager with a hospital to run and eleven thousand letters' worth of backlog in the returns and a capital programme that is a hundred and ninety thousand over on a heating plant, and he came in at five to ten with a folder and read the paper when it was read and asked two questions about it, both of which were about money and neither of which was about the street. *Four tonnes of stone.* *Who paid for the beam.* The answers were that the stone had not been bought and that a woman of sixty-one had paid thirty-nine pounds out of a tin that runs a childcare exchange. He wrote both down, and he did not ask whose street it was, and Jonas understood that this was a decision and not a courtesy.

“Item six,” he said. “Return-of-care limb, Bellow Market, twenty-two hundred to oh-six hundred, carried unpaid since the fifteenth of March. The line on the board reads *not yet agreed* and it comes off when the cheque clears, in this office, in front of Ms Mora, and it is not coming off before that.”

“Give us a date,” said Selah Mora, from the back.

He looked at her for a moment longer than he needed to.

“Minute eleven-twenty, seconded and passed. The cheque is raised on the fourteenth. If it does not clear on the fourteenth you will know on the fifteenth, and I will telephone you myself, and you can put that in the minute, and I would rather you did not have to.”

“That is not a date,” said Selah Mora. “That is a man promising.”

“That is a service manager,” said Gunnar Halvorsen, “and I have a board to sit in ten minutes and no money in it.”

Councillor Iven Sore was in the back of the room and did not speak, and afterwards, in the corridor, she said one thing to Jonas, which was that she had been in that gallery on the first of April and had counted forty-one people and nineteen of them had stayed for her own motion, and that she had learned something from the number nineteen, and that she still thought the contract should be passed in June.

And at twenty-five to four that afternoon, in the corridor, at the card table, with the box open and the pen in her hand, Mina Okafor struck out the second line of a job card in a nine-line list and wrote underneath it, in her own hand, and turned the card round so that he could read it, which she had never done in nineteen years and which is the only time in this whole business that anybody has handled that object like evidence.

*2. A door. — M. OKAFOR. WITHDRAWN 9 APRIL, 15:35. THREE CONDITIONS WERE SET ON 2 APRIL. THE THIRD WAS A DATE. THERE IS NO CHEQUE.*

“I am not angry,” she said, “and I want that written in whatever you write down about it, because people will tell you later that I was angry and it will sound better. I said on the second of April that I would be a witness to this city’s services for exactly as long as a woman was being paid eight pounds an hour for nothing on a rota that is on a door in Saint Orra, and on the ninth of April there was no cheque, so I have stopped. That is all it is. It is a condition with a date on it and I have kept it, and if I had not kept it you would all have had nine of something and one of it would have been me.”

He said, “You are still the clerk.”

“I am still the clerk,” said Mina Okafor. “I am not the witness. Those are two facts and you are not allowed to use the second one, and I have known a man use the second one about four times this month and I am telling you now so that it is me who said it first.”

She put the pen in the tray and squared the box, and the plate on the doorframe behind her, which had said HOLDER NOT NAMED at ten o'clock that night and had been true for about eleven seconds and false for the rest of the night, said nothing at all, which is what plates do when the people who can answer them are not in the room.

He read the five lines out loud in the middle of the corridor afterwards, to nobody, standing where they are read, because he had been doing it since the seventeenth of March and it was the only thing on that card that was his.

Nobody made him and nobody thanked him for it.
