# Chapter 0028 — A Separate Kind of Pressure

The water came over the path at ten past two on the Saturday morning and it did what the almanac said it would do, which was nothing dramatic at all. It came up over the tarmac of the boat sheds, along the front of nine houses, past the net loft's step, and into the dip, and it lay there in the dark being a flood, and at ten past two a telephone rang in the loft kitchen and Perdita Sow said two words into it and hung up.

The flap shut at ten past two as well. That was the whole point of the four days of work and it worked: Perdita heard it from a hundred and forty metres up the path, the same bang Bo had felt through his boots, and the bank on the lowland side of the dip stopped moving and began, slowly and visibly, to go down.

The barrier held. It held because the sill had gone in eleven hours before it and the pintle was four hours old and the flap was on stops that Jonas had set three times, and it held for four hours and ten minutes, and nobody was hurt, and the boil did not come back.

Aurrie Nkemdirim wrote all of that on the wall in the morning in the same paint she had used for the four names and the ring round the blank space, and under it, in a second line, she wrote what had actually happened, which was the other half.

> **21–22 MARCH. THE WATER WAS OVER THE PATH FROM 02:10 TO 06:20. THE FLAP SHUT ITSELF. THE BANK HELD.**
> **THE STAND WAS SHUT AT 01:00 ON FRIDAY AND WAS NOT OPENED AGAIN UNTIL SATURDAY, AND THERE WAS NO WATER IN NINE HOUSES FOR THIRTY-ONE HOURS, AND ONE HOUSE HAD A CHILD WITH A FEVER IN IT AND NO WATER TO WASH IT WITH.**

“It is the second line that matters,” Aurrie said, to nobody, at nine in the morning, with a brush in her hand and the words still wet. “The first line is a thing that happened. The second line is a thing that we did.”

Nobody argued about the first two lines. Then they argued about the stand for two hours, and Jonas was in the middle of it, and it was the worst four hours of his month.

The stand had shut at one o'clock on Friday morning, which was correct. The silt trap had packed at half past twelve and the person in the first slot on the rota was a woman of seventy-eight called Delphine Ofori, who had drawn at one o'clock, seen the water come out of the spout brown, and closed the stand herself, and crossed the slot, and written the time on the board. She had done everything the term required of her. She had done it alone, at one o'clock in the morning, without asking anybody, because the term said the holder decides and the holder was her, and the term was still alive that morning, and she is the reason it was still alive that morning.

And then at half past two, ninety minutes later, Ottie Beck came down the path in the rain with a basin and a towel and needed water, because a woman in number twenty-two had a child of four with a fever of thirty-nine and two and the child needed to be sponged and the child needed a clean cloth, and there was no clean water in that house because the stand had been shut and the bucket on the chain was empty.

And there was no way to open it.

Not because of the flood. Because of the term.

Jonas had mended one field, and the field was precedence, and the line he had put in said that this point answers to the holder on the rota and that a claim from outside the rota does not override the holder. And the holder was Delphine Ofori, who had gone home at one o'clock. And there was a second line under it, and the second line was the emergency one: *an emergency draw for a clinical reason is performed and entered on the rota.* Performed — by the holder. And the holder was in bed four hundred metres up the street in the dark with a fever coming off her own grandchild.

“You wrote a rule that needs a person,” said Bo Trimble, at about eleven o'clock on the Saturday morning, standing at the top of the stand with his hands in his pockets and a piece of paper in his jacket that he had not shown anybody. “That is all it is. It is a rule that needs a person, and you did not put a person in it, because you could not, because I would not let you, and because none of us has put a person in it for thirty years. And so it was half past two in the morning and there was a child with a fever and the rule said *the holder* and the holder was in bed.”

“I know.”

“Then say it properly, because you are nodding again, and nodding is how this happened.”

“I wrote a good term,” Jonas said. “It is a small term and it is bounded and it has a witness and it has an expiry and it has a claim and the holders can refuse, and it is the best civic instrument I have ever made and it did not get a basin of water up that path at half past two in the morning, and I do not know how you want me to say that without it sounding like I am blaming the term.”

“I am not blaming the term,” said Bo. “I am blaming the fact that we have not got a person at half past two in the morning and that is not your fault and it is not the term's fault, and I want it written down in those words, because in a month somebody is going to come and ask why there was a plate on the stand and a rule on the plate and no water, and the answer must not be *because a technician wrote a rule*, because the technician did not write the rule, a woman of seventy-eight closed a tap, and the technician wrote down which promise answers, and that is all he did.”

Ottie Beck had sponged the child with water from the loft's own kettle and the boiler that had not gone out, and had boiled and cooled and carried, and had done it for four hours, and said so once, in one sentence, at two o'clock, and then never mentioned it again.

And the second thing that happened on the twenty-second of March was that Ottie Beck did something that none of them had expected, and which Jonas was told about ninety minutes later on a towpath, and did not understand until the following week.

She had written it on the back of a sheet of the loft's paper, in a hand of seventy-one, and taken it to the Old Ward, and pinned it to the public board under the glass canopy between the ferry office and the Assembly's public doors, at eleven o'clock in the morning, with four paperclips and a piece of tape over the seam.

It was called, at the top, in capitals, **THE LOWLAND DIP — WHAT WAS DONE AND WHAT IT COST.** It was one side of a sheet of paper. It had no letterhead, because there is no letterhead, and it was not signed by anybody, because nobody on that street would sign anything, and it had one sentence in the middle of it in a slightly larger hand than the rest.

> **WE ARE NOT A DISTRICT. WE HAVE NEVER BEEN A DISTRICT. WE ARE A STREET WITH WATER ON IT AND A FLAP GATE AT THE BOTTOM OF IT. WE ARE NOT GIVING A LIST OF HOUSES AND WE ARE NOT GOING TO. IF YOU WANT TO KNOW WHAT WE DID, COME AND STAND IN IT, AND WE WILL SHOW YOU THE WATER LINE, AND YOU MAY WRITE IT DOWN.**

Underneath that: the work, the dates, the six people, the one technician from the city whose name the department already has, the three hundred and forty pounds of stone, the two hundred and eighty pounds of flint and haunching and gasket and forty bags of compound bought with money out of a church hall tin, the six hundred and twenty pounds spent, the hundred and forty pounds for a beam of seasoned ash of which thirty-nine was Aurrie Nkemdirim’s own money, seven hundred and sixty pounds spent against the nine hundred and thirteen there had been, the skip not hired because the chamber emptied on the ebb, and nobody on that street paid for any of it except the six of them and a tin in a church hall, the rotten middle third, the two sets of bolt holes, the fact that the flap had been shut on the ebb by hand for twenty-nine years and that this was the cause of the boil, and the two lines about the flood, and at the bottom, in the same hand:

> **THE ANSWER THE CITY GAVE ON THE FOURTEENTH SAYS IT WILL SEND SOMEBODY TO LOOK AT THE FLAP BEFORE THE REVIEW. WE ARE TELLING YOU THAT THE FLAP IS ON THE WESTERN EDGE OF THE LOWLAND DIP AND THAT IT IS REACHED BY A FOOTPATH AND THAT WE HAVE SAID SO ON A PUBLIC BOARD BECAUSE A PUBLIC BOARD IS THE ONLY PLACE ANY OF THIS IS TRUE TO ANYBODY ELSE.**
>
> **THE ONLY THING WE ASK IS THAT NOBODY PUTS A NUMBER ON US THAT WE DID NOT PUT ON OURSELVES.**

It was up for two hours and eleven minutes before anybody in the city did anything about it, and in those two hours and eleven minutes it did more damage than Jonas Mercer had managed in eleven days.

The first thing it did was make the review item real.

Royden Achebe's classification recommendation was dated the fourteenth of March, lodged on the twenty-second at ten past two in the afternoon, and it went into the series 6 file, and it is four lines long and it is the single most consequential piece of paper in this whole business, and Jonas has never seen the original because it was issued under his own reporting officer's number.

> **RECOMMENDATION — SERIES 6, NODES 6-L/14 AND 6-L/19.**
> **Both nodes are non-civic plant. A flap gate and a standpipe. Neither carries a civic function, a service obligation, or a responsible holder. Recommend both are classified out of scope of the Reclamation Season maintenance review and closed as municipal plant with no service claim.**
> **Reporting officer: MERCER, J., issuer, series 6 plate 41. Grade III. Access to 6-R-14 withdrawn pending review of the review.**
> **Author: R. ACHEBE, field services.**

That is what it says, and the sentence that matters in it is *neither carries a civic function*, and Jonas wrote it.

He had written it himself, on the Friday, at twenty to one in the afternoon, two floors above a shop, in eleven minutes, on a form with a Post-it on it, and he had initialled the time beside it, and it was the best piece of work he has done this month and it is the reason that paper is on the board.

He had done it because a blank in a box is a hole with a line round it, and because the alternative was to write *the maintenance history of this node could not be produced* twice, and because the flap has no holder and no term and no service obligation and nobody's name is on it and the city cannot produce a person to be responsible for it, and because that was true, and because it was the only true sentence available to a man with four days' notice of a review and a question in a box that wanted six words.

He had also been told, in a kitchen on that street, by a woman of seventy-one, that the number is the leash, and by a woman of sixty-one that the survey has a list in it, and by a man of seventeen not to write a name in a plate. He had heard all of it, on the same evening, at the same table, out loud. He had written the six words anyway, at a desk, on a Friday afternoon, in a city, because the box wanted six words and because he was the reporting officer and because he did not have anybody's permission to write anything else.

**Non-civic plant.**

And the consequence of the six words arrived at half past three on the Saturday afternoon as a clerk on the telephone, and it arrived with perfect courtesy.

“Mr Mercer. Field services. Your recommendation has been accepted in principle and it is going to the review as a recommended classification, and if the review accepts it then both nodes come out of scope on the first of April and there is no pre-review inspection, because you do not inspect plant.”

“That is not what I—”

“I know exactly what you wrote, sir, it is on my screen and I have read it, and I am not going to discuss it with you. What I am going to tell you is the other thing, because you asked me to let you know when a recommendation is accepted in principle, and I am letting you know. The acceptance is subject to the review and the review is on the first, and the review is public, and your name is on the recommendation, and the recommendation says *non-civic plant*, and there is a piece of paper pinned to a board in the Old Ward that says the opposite in eleven lines and it does not have your name on it and it does not need it.”

He put the telephone down. He put it down and then picked it up again and dialled Oren Pell, and Oren picked up on the ninth ring and said his name, and Jonas got four words out.

“The classification says non-civic.”

“Yes,” said Oren Pell. “I have read it. It went past me at four o'clock because everything on that file goes past me at four o'clock, and I have got about nine words for you and I am going to spend them now so that you can spend the weekend on them instead of me.

“It is wrong. Not because a flap gate and a standpipe are not plant, because they are plant, and plant does not have a district. It is wrong because you have described what the thing *is* and filed it as what the thing *is for*, and a piece of iron in a chamber is a piece of iron in a chamber, and the water that comes out of the tap at the top of that street is a service and the city is the only body in this estuary that can run one, and you have just told the review that there is nothing on the other end of it, and the review will believe you, because you are the reporting officer and your file is the cleanest thing in the chain, and I have never in twenty years seen a recommendation go through a review on a stronger file.

“And here is the second thing and it is worse. If the nodes are plant, then they are nobody's, in law, in a file, forever, and a piece of plant that is nobody's is a piece of plant that fails in a flood and gets called a defect on a plant asset schedule, and I will sign a defect, and I will have nowhere to serve it, and I will put a date on it, and the date will be the day I did it. I said that to you in a room on the fourteenth and I did not enjoy saying it and I have thought about it every day since. You have given me the exact thing I asked you not to give me, and you did it in eleven minutes at a desk, and I am not going to be the man who tells a board that the man is a coward. He is not a coward. He is worse than a coward. He is careful.”

“Then what do I do.”

“You withdraw it,” said Oren. “Before the first, in writing, in the file, and you put your name against your own words and you say *I withdraw, and the reason is that I did not have the standing to make the finding, and I have since spoken to the people who live there, and they have told me what it is, and I am recording that I asked them and that they answered me and that the answer is in a piece of paper on the board at the Old Ward.*”

“They will not say that. They have refused to give me a list, a number, a name, and a signature.”

“Then you write that,” said Oren Pell. “You write that the local custodians of the node were asked for a statement of function and declined to give one, and that the reporting officer has accordingly no basis for a finding, and that in the absence of a finding the node should remain in scope until somebody with standing makes it. That is not a withdrawal. That is a refusal to classify, and a refusal to classify is a thing an issuer is allowed to do, and it is the only move on this board that does not put your name on a lie and does not put a county officer in a kitchen on the twenty-second of March asking a boy of seventeen what his mother’s door number is.”

He hung up.

Jonas sat in a yard in River Stacks with a telephone in his hand for a while.

Then he got the departmental van out and drove down to the towpath, and Pia Santos was at the bottom of it with her bicycle against a railing, in the rain, at four o'clock on a Saturday afternoon, in the exact place where she had told him the four things.

“You have been to the board,” she said.

“I have been to the board.”

“And it is up.”

“It is up.”

“Then you know that your name is on the other one,” said Pia, “because Royden Achebe’s is on the board as well, in a shop window at the Old Ward, the recommendations go up in the shop window, everybody who has a recommendation goes up in the shop window, and there are four of them on there and three of them are about a bridge. So your street is in a shop window on the same page as a bridge, and a woman I have never met has pinned eleven lines under it saying *we are not a district*.”

“Yes.”

Pia took her gloves off and put them in her jacket, and did not sit down on the wall, and Jonas understood that whatever happened next was going to take about ninety seconds and that he was not going to be able to influence any of it.

“Here is what is going to happen on the first of April,” she said. “The review will take your six words and close both nodes, and it will do it in a room with a hundred and forty people in it, and the hundred and forty people will be on the last road out of Nacre and a woman from CivicCore’s press office will be at the back, because they come to everything now and they have started coming early. And when it closes, the water on that street stops being a question the city has been asked and becomes a thing that never was, and Margo’s rota will be a rota on a fence, and Otie’s clinic will be a clinic in a room, and my mother’s door will be number thirty-one on a piece of paper in Saltmouth that is nine years old. Nothing will be taken away. That is what I want you to understand before you go up that path with a face. Nobody is going to come with a clipboard and take anything. It will just stop being on any list, and the city will not know it is there, and that is a thing you cannot appeal, because there is nothing to appeal against.”

“I know.”

“You do not know, you know the shape of it.” She got her gloves back out. “So I am going to tell you the thing I have been carrying up that path for two days, and then I am going to go home, and you are not going to be able to do anything about it.

“We are not going to help you withdraw it. That is the answer and I have had it since about eleven o’clock this morning and I have been waiting to see whether you would come down here and ask, because if you came down here and asked I would have to think you were doing it to be allowed to.”

“I did not come to ask.”

“You came to be told that you cannot,” said Pia. “Which is not the same, and I have known the difference since I was twelve, and you had better be as good at it as you are at putting a plate back on a wall, because in about a week one of us is going to have to stand up in a room and say the word *citizen* out loud, and I have decided it will not be me, and it will not be Bo, and it will not be a woman of seventy-one holding a tin.”

She got on the bicycle.

“Go and ask the department what a refusal to classify looks like on a form,” she said. “And then take it to somebody who is not the registrar and not the manager and not a technician, and find out whether a woman of sixty-three who has kept a water rota for thirty years can put her own name on a piece of paper about a tap, and what that costs, and who it costs it to. And when you have found out, come up that path and tell us, and we will decide, and it will not be you, and I am going to bed.”

She went up the towpath in the rain with three bags on the handlebars and fifty-nine doors behind her and a city that had just, on the strength of eleven minutes at a desk on the fourteenth of March, agreed in writing that none of it was a service.

And up at the Old Ward, on the public board, four inches under a sentence about a central root field with no name in it, there was a line that had been added at half past three that afternoon, in the flat register, that nobody in the building had written by hand and that anybody on that street could read from the far side of the glass:

**SERIES 6 — NODES 6-L/14, 6-L/19 — RECOMMENDED CLASSIFICATION: NON-CIVIC PLANT. REVIEW 1 APRIL. OUT OF SCOPE IF ACCEPTED.**
