# Chapter 0037 — One Name Is Not Enough

On Wednesday morning the department had to service a gate that CivicCore had fitted on Tuesday, because the closure on the barrier at Bridge Street had a hold-open that fails every forty minutes, and nobody had a part, and the part has a triangular flange and a captive nut and it is not in the department catalogue and it is not in any addendum, and there is a note in the file that says *repairable under existing maintenance* in a hand nobody in the shop recognised.

Bea had a special open-ended spanner and a roll of PTFE tape and forty minutes, and Jonas could not hold the plate square with his right hand, so he got a wedge of roofing slate and jammed it under the bottom rail and held it there with his forearm for the whole job while Bea took the spanner to the captive nut and discovered that the captive nut was a bolt of proprietary thread and the spanner slipped twice.

“They will not sell you the part,” Bea said, at the second slip.

“They will sell us four hundred of them, at a price, on a schedule, in June.”

“That is what I said, only ruder.” She put the spanner down and sat back on her heels. “Jonas. You are doing the thing.”

“What thing.”

“You are being good at something for four days and you have not told anybody, which is how it starts.” She wiped her hands. “I have watched you since the fourteenth. You got a term off a plate you do not own, you put a name on a rota and then you found out you could not renew it, and every single thing you have done since has been a thing you did quietly that worked, and I have had three people in this shop ask me whether you were all right.”

He did not answer. Down at the crossing, at half past eleven in the rain, Nora Bell had her umbrella hooked over the rail and her back to the traffic, and Jonas stopped on the wrong side of the bollard and asked her straight out, which he had thought about for two days and it still came out badly.

“I need somebody who can stand in the road and say what a door is for.”

“All right,” said Nora Bell. “Go on.”

“Not today. In a month, or in a flood, or on whatever day the thing needs saying. I need nine people, in nine places, who can each say what one service is and who holds it and when it ends, and I cannot say that term to any of them twice without a rule, and I am not the person who writes the rule, so I am going round asking.”

“Right,” said Nora Bell, and then she said, “And what happens to me?”

He had known it was coming and he still had nothing.

“It is a public record,” he said. “If there is a flood and the Lattice has to choose between rules, then the record says who stood witness, and the person who stood witness has agreed to a duty and can be called on it, and — yes — if you are the witness and the thing goes wrong, the claim lands on the person who made the claim, and that is me, and it does not land on you, and I know that and I have written it down and it is on a bond in the shop with my name on it, and I am not the first person to say that and I am not sure it is worth as much as a wet Tuesday in your hand.”

Nora Bell looked at the traffic going past for a while.

“I have stood in that road for nineteen years,” she said. “Somebody with a clipboard comes to me four times a year and asks me about a gate. You are the first one who has said *I need you* instead of *I need you to sign*, and I have noticed that, and I want you to know I have noticed it, because it is not nothing, and it is not enough either.” She turned round. “You can get me an answer in a week. In a week I can be told what a witness is and what I am agreeing to and what I can refuse. In a flood I have got about ninety seconds to decide whether to stand in it. You are asking me to do the ninety seconds part without the week. That is the trade and I am not refusing it, I am telling you I have noticed you made it, and I will take the week, and then I will tell you.”

“The fourteenth,” she said, before he could answer. “Not Thursday — Thursday I have the school run and my granddaughter and I am not thinking about civic anything. The fourteenth, half past three, at that end of the barrier, and bring your own paper, because I do not write on other people’s paper.”

He wrote it on the back of a job card at the shop: **N. BELL — TUE 14 APRIL, 15:30, BARRIER END. BRING OWN PAPER.**

And then he had one name and eight lines, and Bea took the card out of his hand.

“No,” she said.

“It is a job card.”

“It is a job card with a woman’s name and a day and a time on it, and the day you start writing names on things that are not work orders, Mr Mercer, is the day it stops being a job card.” She read it. “Nora Bell, the fourteenth, the barrier end. That is fine. That is a person and a day and a place, and if it never happens nobody is hurt. The second one will be a name and a day and a place and a subject, and then it will be a list, and when it is a list somebody will produce it in a hearing, and Mr Mercer — a hearing is the one room in this city where a list is not a list.”

“Better than nine nobody.”

“I know it is better than nine nobody,” said Bea. “I want to be very clear that I am not telling you it is a waste of time. I am telling you that the ninth one is going to cost somebody, and that you are going to find out who it is in about a week, and that you will not be able to put it down.”

Bellow Market at one o’clock on a Wednesday is the only building in Nacre where three hundred people will queue for something that is not a service, and Fola Adeyemi has run the griddle at the back of the cook-house for twenty-two years and has a queue that starts at the door and turns.

She is fifty-nine and she heard him from about six feet away, over the flat-top, with a spatula in her hand, and did not stop cooking, and let him get the whole request out before she answered any of it.

“No,” she said.

“I have not asked yet.”

“You have had it in your face for a minute and a half, and you are about to ask me to stand somewhere in a flood and be a person who can say what a thing is for, and I have watched you do this to four people since Tuesday because you are frightened, and I am not unkind about it, I am busy.” She turned a slice over. “That is the fifth refusal you have had this week and you have got one of them, and I would like you to know that I know what my refusal costs you, and I am going to give it to you anyway, and I am going to give you the real reason so that you stop coming back to me with better arguments.

“I have a gas account. I have a supplier who can take the whole unit out on a Thursday if I do not tell him by Wednesday. I have a lease with a landlord who owns four other cook-houses and gets a valuation every second year. I have a licence from the borough with a name on it. I have a rota with eleven people on it and two of them are pregnant and one of them has a tribunal in April.” She put the spatula down. “That is four things that can be served on me on a Tuesday, in four different rooms, in four different buildings, and I have spent twenty-two years learning to say no to all of them, and it is the only skill I own.

“You do not need a cook,” said Fola Adeyemi. “You need somebody nobody can dismiss. And I am not that, and you knew it when you came in, and you came in anyway because you are doing a round and I am on the round.”

“I am sorry.”

“Do not be sorry. Come and eat at eleven and do not make a thing of it and do not ask me about a flood again until you have somebody the borough cannot ring.” She picked the spatula up. “I will tell you what I would do if I were you. I would go and find the man who comes out at night with a torch and a mobile telephone with four per cent battery in it. Nobody can serve that man in the middle of the night, and he does not know that yet, and that is worth more than a cook-house.”

He ate at eleven and did not ask her about a flood.

Saint Orra west, half past three in the afternoon, and the corridor outside the card table where Mina Okafor was writing up the Thursday returns, and there was a red line across the box on the public board outside the door that said **06:00 NO HOLDER** and had said so since the twenty-fourth, and Jonas had walked past it eleven times that week and had not said anything to her about it, and she brought it up.

“You have been avoiding me,” said Mina Okafor.

“I have been not-knowing-how-to-start.”

“That is the same thing with a better name.” She squared the board. “Now say it.”

He said it. It took him four minutes and he had it down to about the length of a long sentence, and it was: a term is not a person, a claim needs a counterparty, and if a service is going to be mended in a room with a stranger in it, then the stranger has to be able to say out loud what the service is, when it ends, and what happens after, and the stranger has to be somebody who will still be standing in that room when the four-in-the-morning happens.

“Why me,” said Mina.

“Because you are the record. You are the person who writes down that a door opened at twelve past twelve and why it took nine minutes, and you are the only person I know of who has turned a number that was against us into a thing that is on a board. On the twenty-fourth you put nine minutes in a public box on purpose and told me it was information and not a complaint. That is the whole job. That is exactly the whole job.”

“Do not put it like that,” said Mina Okafor, “because that is the version that makes me feel good, and I have had four of those this month and I have said yes to one of them and I am still working it out.”

She put the pen down.

“Three conditions, and they are in a document, in my hand, and you are going to read it and not touch it. One: every term I witness is read out loud in this corridor, at the handover, by the holders, in the same five lines, in the same voice, and if a term is ever read somewhere else I stop. Two: nothing goes on the public board with my name on it that has not been on the board already, and by that I mean I am not a signature, I am not a person who can be produced. I am a person who wrote down what happened. Three, and this is the one that matters and it is the reason I am going to say yes at all: the return-of-care limb is still on that board reading *not yet agreed*, and the words come off when a cheque is written by west site administration, and I will be a witness to this city’s services for exactly as long as a woman is being paid eight pounds an hour for nothing on a rota that is on a door in Saint Orra. On the ninth of April, if there is no cheque, I stop.”

“That is a condition with a date on it.”

“That is a condition with a date on it and it is not clever and it is not a trick and it is the only kind of consent I have ever been able to give anybody in nineteen years of being a clerk,” said Mina Okafor. “I will sign that, in my own hand, and bring it to you, and then you go and find me eight other people, and I hope to God you do not find them all in one building.”

Bea was still in the yard at six with the gate closed and the PTFE tape on the bench, and he put the card on the bench with two names on it and eight ruled lines and the word *witness* at the top, which was new and which he had written on the Thursday morning before he had anybody to put under it.

“The fourteenth,” said Bea, reading it upside down. “You have got Nora Bell for the fourteenth and Mina Okafor for a date in April. That is two out of nine, and the ninth is going to cost somebody, and I am not going to say it again.”
