# Chapter 0038 — What Two Refusals Look Like

At ten o’clock the five lines were read out in the middle of the corridor by Ezra Mbeki, who had them by heart, and Ruth Nwosu read them back to him, and Ruth signed the box, and Mina Okafor wrote the times, and the plate at the doorway printed the only sentence it had printed since the fourteenth of March and which everybody in that corridor had stopped seeing.

**HOLDER NOT NAMED. ROUTE CLOSED.**

Except that for about eleven seconds it was a lie, because at the moment of printing Ruth Nwosu was standing on the other side of it holding a black key.

Mina wrote: *22:00. Handover. Both holders read term aloud. New term to 06:00, 8 April.*

---

Ruth Nwosu refused the Wednesday night before she got in, and she refused in the way she refused things, which was to do it in writing on the board at twenty past two on Wednesday afternoon and then to hand-read it back to Mina Okafor so that the reason would go on the board as a refusal.

“Because I have been on four nights out of five this week,” she said, “and last night I was on from ten at night until eleven this morning and I went home and slept until two, and this afternoon I have got something for my back from a doctor in Salt Row and I am going to take it at nine o’clock and go to bed.”

“The term is renewed,” said Mina. “It runs to the eighth.”

“Then it runs to the eighth without me in it and that is not the same thing and you know it is not the same thing, and I have taught you the difference.”

“Mrs Nwosu —”

“Do not do that,” said Ruth. “Do not do the voice. I have read that term aloud to four people in this corridor and I have argued for it every single time, and last night at ten o’clock I argued for it in front of a porter who came in with a mop, and I would do it again tomorrow night if there was anybody left to do it to. I am refusing the shift. Not the job. Not the term. The shift. And if you want to write *my hours on this record say twenty-three hundred and I have read them back twice and they still say twenty-three hundred* in the box again tonight, write it in my own hand this time, because it is my box.”

And Ruth Nwosu went home at half past two on the Wednesday afternoon and got the bus.

Selah Mora rang the desk at twenty past four.

“I am not doing the limb tonight,” she said, before Mina had finished saying good afternoon. “I know it is only eight hours and I know it is only four nights running and I have done it every night this week, and I am not doing it tonight, and I am not going to tell you why on this telephone, and if you want the reason write to my address and I will put it in an envelope.”

Then she said, into the pause: “No, I will tell you the reason, because you will otherwise think it is a demand. My mother has a chest and my daughter has nobody, and I have put her with the woman at the exchange who has four of her own and took her at eleven o’clock at night without asking me a single question, and I am not going to be the one who says thank you for that in a corridor in front of a doctor, so I am saying it here to a clerk at a quarter past four in the afternoon and then I am going to go and collect her.”

The Wednesday night handover happened at ten o’clock with one holder instead of two.

Ezra read the five lines aloud in the middle of the corridor, at Mina’s insistence, standing at the card table rather than in the corridor because there was nobody in the corridor, and Mina said them back to him, and he signed the outgoing box, and then he put the black key in the drawer and said, “I am not locking it. I am putting it in the drawer because the drawer is where it goes.”

“It is not going anywhere,” said Mina. “The route closes at eleven because the day holder leaves and there is no incoming holder, and it has said so since the seventeenth of March, and the plate will say the same thing it said at two minutes past twelve at midday on Monday the twenty-fourth.”

“It will say it in about eight seconds.”

“It will say it in about eight seconds.”

And the plate said the four words again, in the same flat register, with the same full stop.

Ezra Mbeki did not go home. He sat down on the bench by the lift with his coat on for about four minutes and then went and did the bins on the ward, because he was twenty-six and his mother was in the ward at the east site and he had been going there since her operation on the eleventh and there was nobody else to go, and in the small hours of a night in March he had moved a woman of ninety-one out of a scan room on his own and had written down that he knew before he moved her, and he had not been able to put that down since, and it turned out that the way you put it down is by refusing a shift in the afternoon to a clerk who writes everything down.

“Mina,” he said. “If I am not here in the morning, and Ruth is not here tonight, and there is nobody else — write it as what it is. Do not put *porter unavailable* or *no cover available*. Put *offered, refused, twice*, because that is what happened and in a year somebody is going to come and ask why that corridor was shut at five in the morning and I am not going to be a *no cover available*.”

“It goes in as what it is,” said Mina Okafor, and wrote it, and Ezra Mbeki went home at ten past ten with a term that had three more days on it and nobody to hand it to.

---

At six o’clock in the morning a car came up the ramp at Saint Orra west with its lights on and stopped, and a woman of about thirty came out of the passenger side and opened the back and got in and started undoing a seatbelt, and a young woman of nineteen came out of the driver’s side with her coat open and one shoe off and said, to nobody, “She’s at the end. She’s been at the end since about two. I couldn’t — I couldn’t find anybody, I’ve been on the phone since eleven.”

The ramp light was out. It had been out since the Monday, and it was on a thirty-second timer that had a relay in a box at the top of the ramp which Jonas had opened and looked at on the Tuesday and had been told by the estates office that the part was on order.

Mina Okafor was on the ramp in ninety seconds because she had come back for her notes at half past four, which she had told nobody about and which she would be insufferable about for a week.

“Push it up,” she said. “Get her up. I am going to telephone the on-call.”

The plate said four words and would not say anything else. There was a live term, renewed the night before by two holders reading it to each other, witnessed, in the record, unexpired, running to six o’clock on the eighth. It made no difference whatever. A term is a promise and a promise is not a pair of hands, and the hands were in a car park in Kestrel Hill and a corridor in Bellow Market, and the only instrument in the entire civic apparatus of the city of Nacre that could open that door at six in the morning was a named clinician in writing.

Mina telephoned the west site switchboard and asked for the on-call and gave the number of the plate.

Then she telephoned the mobile, because the switchboard had said she was on the ward.

Dr. Sanaa Iqbal answered on the second ring.

She was not in a resuscitation room. She was, at that moment, at a kitchen table in a rented flat in Kestrel Hill with a chart under her elbow, a cold cup of tea, and a school-holiday week’s worth of discharge letters that nobody had done because there was nobody to do them, because she had volunteered, at nine o’clock on Wednesday, to do them at home, on her own sofa, at her own hour, in her own time, for reasons she had not examined.

“Yes,” she said.

“Twenty-odd, in labour, at term, no midwife, brought in by a neighbour, arrived at six o’clock, and the plate says holder not named and there is nobody on the rota, and I need a declaration, and the last one I asked you for took nine minutes and I am telling you the number up front so you know what I am asking for.”

“That took nine minutes because I was with a family,” said Sanaa. “I am at a table. Say the declaration.”

Mina wrote it out and read it back and put the plate and said it twice more. The declaration was given at twelve minutes past six and the door opened at nineteen minutes past six.

It took six minutes to get the trolley up a dark ramp with a dead light and no handrail on the east side, because the handrail came off in March and had not been rebolted, and there are six orderlies on nights and two of them were on the east ward and one of them, Ivo Bamba, came and pushed a shoulder under the rail and held it in place all the way up so that the person on the trolley and the woman holding her hand did not lose the wall. Jonas Mercer was in the corridor. He had been in the corridor since four o’clock because he could not sleep, and he could not have opened that door for anything in this world, and he did not try, and there was nothing for him to do, so he went up the ramp with the trolley, on the off side, and pushed.

Marta Sowle was awake.

She is ninety-one and she is on the west ward and she has not slept a whole night since February, and she came out of bay nine in a cardigan over a nightdress with her slippers on and a hand on the wall, because she had heard wheels on that ramp, and wheels on that ramp in this building have meant every single time since the third of March that the door had not been open when it should have been. She got as far as the card table, where Mina Okafor was standing with the telephone still in her hand, and the two of them looked at each other.

“Mrs Sowle —”

“Who is holding the door,” said Marta Sowle.

“That is what I have been telephoning about. The plate says holder not named, I telephoned the on-call, she gave me a declaration at twelve past six, the door is open, and she is in the corridor.”

Marta Sowle stood in the middle of that corridor in her slippers and thought about it for about four seconds, which is a long time for a woman of ninety-one standing in a corridor at six in the morning.

“Then put that on the board,” she said. “Not my name. Put *holder not named, door opened on a declaration, nineteen minutes* in the box, in the morning, where the nine minutes are, because in four years somebody is going to write a piece of paper about that door and they are going to find a box full of other people’s minutes and not mine, and mine will be the one that says nobody was holding it, and that is the true thing, and it ought to be the true thing on the wall.”

“It will go in the box,” said Mina Okafor.

“And do not put the issuer’s name back on it either,” said Marta Sowle. “He took himself off that door for me and I want him to stay off it, and if he has to be asked every time then he has not taken himself off it. He has only moved.”

The baby was out at thirty-one minutes past six and the mother was nineteen and both of them were fine, and it was a perfectly ordinary disaster of the kind that goes into a corridor conversation the next morning and is retold for years.

Jonas found Mina Okafor at the card table at ten past seven writing it up.

“How long,” he said.

“Nineteen minutes from the car. Six minutes up the ramp. Declaration at twelve past six. Baby at thirty-one minutes past six.” She wrote it all down in the same hand as the twenty-fourth. “Do you want the number or do you want the sentence.”

“Both.”

“The number is nineteen. The sentence is that the plate did exactly what the twenty-fourth of March said it would do, and the reason it closed is not a fault and it is not a design error, and it is not anybody being cruel.” She put the pen down. “Two people said no yesterday and both of them were entitled to and one of them went home at half past two because of a back and one of them has a mother with a chest. That is the whole of it. That is your two refusals, Mr Mercer, and there were no rules to break because there are no rules, and you have just watched it work anyway, on luck, on a rota with a hole in it and a woman at a kitchen table in Kestrel Hill who was doing discharge letters because she had volunteered to, and if she had been asleep it would have been twenty-two minutes, and if she had been in a resuscitation room it would have been thirty-one, and none of those three numbers is in anybody’s design.”

---

He telephoned Tomas Renn at ten past seven from the corridor.

“I am not telling you I have an answer,” he said. “I am telling you what happened at six o’clock this morning so that you can do with it what you want.”

“You have rung me at ten past seven in the morning,” said Tomas, “on a Thursday, which means you have not slept, and I am not going to ask. Go on.”

Two people refused. The plate said holder not named. The term was live and renewed and made no difference. A doctor answered a telephone and a clerk called a doctor and the door opened at nineteen minutes and the baby was out at thirty-one. And the answer to the question was: Mina Okafor, who was not on the rota and was in the building for her own reasons, and Ivo Bamba, who held a handrail, and Dr. Iqbal, who was not on any rota at all and was at her own kitchen table because she had said yes to something else.

“That is not an answer,” said Tomas Renn. “That is three people and a piece of luck and it worked, and I am telling you the thing that you already know and I am not going to be gentle about it, because it is seven in the morning and you have had five hours’ sleep in two days and there is a woman on a ward who needs to be told about that door.

“That is not a rota, Jonas. That is a rota with the whole shift taken away and replaced with three people who happened to be somewhere else. It worked on Wednesday night because Ruth Nwosu has a back and Selah Mora has a mother and both of them said no on purpose and were right to, and on Tuesday it will be somebody else’s mother, and on a bad Saturday in June it will be three, and there is no version of four in the morning that this city can survive on the grounds that somebody was at a kitchen table.”

“Then what is the answer.”

“There is no answer,” said Tomas Renn. “I have been shouting about it since January and I am still shouting about it and I have made it my own instead of the roster’s and I told you that on the twenty-fourth and I was right to say it to myself, which is not the same as being right. There is no answer. I have never had one. The only thing I have is a stop-work clause and a refusal clause, and neither of them says who holds the door, and this morning a doctor who has not slept opened it for a stranger in a corridor and she will be the last person in this city to admit that she is the last person in this city.”

He did not put the telephone down.

“Write it up,” he said. “Not to me. To yourself, in your own hand, on your own paper, and put the date on it, and do not put my name on it and do not put hers on it, because if it is in my hand it is my problem and I have already had that, and if it is in hers it is a complaint and she is not complaining, she has told you in writing that she is not complaining.”

“I will write it up.”

“You will write it up, and then in about four months you will be in a room where somebody is proposing the obvious thing, and somebody will say *we solved it in April*, and you will be the only person in the room who knows that we solved it in April because two women said no and a doctor happened to be awake, and you will have to be the man who says that, and I am asking you now, at seven in the morning, on a Thursday, to decide in advance whether you are going to be able to say it.” A pause. “That is the fourth question. I asked it on the twenty-fourth. You are not answering it now either, and I am not asking you to. I am asking you to hold still and notice that I have now asked it four times and got nothing, and that a doctor at a kitchen table has answered it in nineteen minutes with her own body, and that is the answer I have been refusing to accept for a year, and I am going to bed.”

Ruth Nwosu came on for the night at ten o’clock and did not go near the ramp, and Jonas asked her once, in the corridor, whether she was all right, and she said, “No,” and he said, “Right,” and wrote nothing down.

And Selah Mora was at the interchange at a quarter past eight the next morning with her daughter on her shoulders and a packed lunch for both of them, and she would not look at him, and Jonas did not ask her the reason, because she had written it in an envelope and he was not going to be the man who asked twice.

On the Saturday he went back with a length of flat bar, four bolts and a spanner, and he rebolted the handrail on the east side of the ramp, which is not a plate and does not need a witness and for which nobody had ever once refused him permission, and it took him twenty-five minutes, and afterwards he went and told Bea Osunde the number, and Bea wrote nineteen minutes and thirty-one minutes on the yellow board next to the bond with no comment at all.
