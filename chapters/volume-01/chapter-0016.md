# Chapter 0016 — What the Claim Requires

By seven on the evening of the third day, two people had offered Jonas Mercer a way to keep the door, and one of them had put it in a folder with a seal on it.

He had refused all of them, and by nine he understood that refusing was the part he was best at, and that a man who is very good at refusing is not the same as a man who has fixed anything.

The first offer was Tomas’s, and it had the merit of being honest. The Civic Continuity Workers’ Union had six members in the Saint Orra area. Two of them could read a civic term well enough to describe its scope and its expiry to a stranger. Neither had been trained on a hospital access plate, neither was paid for a shift outside their own trade, and the union’s own rule — written by Tomas eleven years ago after a man died under a scaffold because nobody had known who owned the rope — forbade putting a member on a duty they could not refuse.

“You are asking me for a favor,” Tomas said. “Do not do that. Ask me for an argument and I will give you four of them.”

“What would it take?”

“Four things.” He held up his hand. “A payroll line the department agrees to. A training afternoon somebody pays for. A naming authority that is the clinic, not the department, because a gate at Saint Orra is a Saint Orra thing. And a written rule for what happens when two people on the rota both refuse, which is the question you have not asked and which is the question that will kill your plan in a fortnight.”

“Which is?”

“Somebody has to be the least-bad option at four o’clock on a night when both of them have reasons.” Tomas put the hand down. “I have never solved that. I have only ever watched a city postpone it. You have just built a small version of it, and it has already cost a woman nine days.”

The second offer arrived at twenty to nine as a hand-delivered envelope from Saint Orra Civic Hospital’s west site administration, and it was signed by Gunnar Halvorsen, the service manager, who had the particular defeated courtesy of a man whose entire profession was making gaps disappear on paper.

The envelope contained a **TEMPORARY LOCAL CONTINUITY WAIVER — SAINT ORRA WEST, CARE ACCESS**, and Jonas read it twice before he understood that it would have solved the afternoon.

It proposed that during any period when no named watch or care worker was on duty, **the issuer or a designated municipal officer** should be permitted to open the west clinic care entrance for a verified clinic care claim, and that the hospital would roster its own porter supervisor into the gaps for the duration of the term, and that the waiver would expire with the term.

“Read clause four out loud,” Gunnar said, over the telephone, when Jonas called him. “Then you will tell me I am asking you to surrender something.”

Jonas read clause four out loud. It said: *In the absence of a named watch or care worker, the duty falls to the issuer or a designated municipal officer.*

“Halvorsen,” he said, “this would have opened that ramp at fifteen twenty-two today.”

“Yes. It would have opened it at ten to four when you finally got there, and at two this morning, and at every hour of the four days this term has left. My supervisor would have covered the evening gaps and your man would have covered the rest.” A pause, and then, with less edge: “You understand that I am not trying to bury anything. I have a gap on my rota and I have found a legitimate instrument for closing it.”

“I understand.”

“Then tell me your objection quickly, because I have a night shift to publish and I do not want your technician’s ethics on my board.”

Jonas looked at the sentence. *The issuer or a designated municipal officer.*

He could not argue that it was vague; it was unusually precise. He could not argue that it was untrue. What he could argue — and he argued it badly, and had to argue it twice, and eventually said it in nine words that he was not proud of — was this.

“A category cannot be told no,” he said. “If it says *the issuer or a designated municipal officer*, then on a night when I am ill, whoever is nearest opens the door, and nobody has to be anything, and the record says a person was there. I did that this morning. It already put a patient on a floor.”

Silence on the line.

“Then I have nothing,” Gunnar said. “Which is worse than having an argument. I have no gap-filler and no term and a released cardiac slot in a corridor at nine o’clock at night. What are you going to do?”

“Go and ask the people in the corridor,” Jonas said.

He did not hang up on him, which cost him something, because Gunnar Halvorsen did not hang up either, and for a while the two men stayed on the line not speaking, which is what happens between people who have each been told no by a form.

At ten o’clock Jonas went back up the ramp with the waiver in his coat, because Tomas had told him that a refusal which is not minuted in the record is a preference, and Jonas needed it to be a decision in front of witnesses.

Ruth Nwosu was two hours into her shift and had already read it, because Mina had put it on the handover board when it came, and Mina did that with every piece of paper that came near the corridor.

“Clause four,” Ruth said, before he had it out of his coat.

“You have read it.”

“I have read it forty times.” She came out to the ramp and sat down on the low wall with her key in her hand. “I want to say the thing about my job, and I want to say it in front of you and not in a form.”

“Say it.”

“If that clause passes, then at eleven o’clock at night this duty is not mine. It is the issuer’s, or whoever the hospital says it is, or whoever is nearest.” She turned the key over. “And a thing that belongs to whoever is nearest is a thing with no name. In eleven months the hospital will put a poster up about dignity at work and I will be the only person on this corridor who has never heard the word, because I was not a watch, I was a courtesy. You do not need me to be grateful to you. You need to write down that the hours exist, and you need somebody who is not you to write them down, because a name written down by the man who wants the door is only another kind of favor.”

“The waiver pays for the gaps.”

“It pays for a body. I am not asking for a body.” Ruth looked up at him. “Put my name and my hours and my rate on the page and let the gap be a gap. A gap is honest. A gap with my wages in it is theft.”

Sanaa came out at half past ten with her coat already on. She had not asked to be there and she was the last person in the corridor who could have stayed away, and she had a piece of paper of her own.

“I have written the notice,” she said. “It goes on the wall by that door, in a plastic sleeve, at eye height for somebody in a wheelchair. It says what the gate can do, what it cannot do, that the gate is held by a named person and that the person changes, that a patient or a family may telephone the number on the notice and ask whether a transfer can be received before they are sent, and that a refusal of care may be appealed to a named person who is not the technician.”

“Fine.”

“It also says that on one night in the past week a patient was moved through this door at twenty to three in the morning without a second person, and was on the floor for eleven minutes.” Sanaa looked at him. “Not her name. The event. She is ninety-one and she goes home at the end of the week and she has agreed to that sentence, and she wants to read it before it goes up.”

“Why does she want to read it?”

“Because she has worked in a factory for forty years and she has been moved by people in a hurry, and she says that if it is written down then it happened to the door and not only to her.” She put the notice into his hand. “Put it up tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight, so that whoever is standing in this corridor at four in the morning has read it before they have decided they are the one who can cope.”

At twenty past eight the next morning Jonas was in the records room over the River Stacks shop with Nina Calder and a sheet of plywood he had cut himself in the yard, planed smooth, and framed in offcuts, and he was sanding the corners when Nell Ardent came up the stairs and sat down on the edge of Nina’s desk without being asked, which gave Jonas the distinct impression that she had done it twice before in buildings where nobody had invited her.

She was small, thirty-nine, in a coat that had been expensive and then survived a great deal. She put a card on the desk that said **OPEN COVENANTS LEGAL CLINIC** and a second card beside it that said, in handwriting, *brought by N. Vessey, this morning, re: a door.*

“The Vessey daughter phoned my clinic at seven,” she said. “I have been a lawyer for seventeen years and I have never once been asked to take a case that is a scheduling failure, and I want you to understand that this is not a case, it is a schedule, and I have no remedy for schedules.”

“Then what do you have?”

“The truth, which you will not enjoy, and one piece of advice I am going to give you for free and once.” She put her hands flat on the desk. “The truth is that the record is now public. The Old Ward board printed the released slot at nine o’clock last night with your name on it. Which means the department is now exposed along with you, and an exposed department is a department that will want this settled before anybody sues anybody, and settled means quiet.”

“Before you answer that, there is a letter on your file.” Nina turned a sheet round. “Registrar’s office, this morning. It says that on the second evening of the current term the issuer acknowledged a shift on a gate he is suspended from initiating, and that on the third afternoon the same issuer acknowledged a further shift and used that acknowledgement to open a route. It asks the department to state whether it intends to treat the second act as a continuation of attended maintenance or as a breach of the review order, and it gives me three working days.”

“What is the department going to say?”

“That depends entirely on what the department decides it wants to have happened,” Nina said. “Which is the sentence I would like you to sit with, because in three days somebody upstairs is going to choose, and they will choose the sentence that costs the city nothing, and the sentence that costs the city nothing is that you were correct.”

“I have not signed anything quiet.”

“No. You refused two of them in front of witnesses, which was the correct thing to do, and I am not here to congratulate you, because the refusal has not yet cost you anything and the record is where it will cost you.” She turned the second card over. “Here is the advice. Your name is on that board in the issuer field. That is not only a liability. It is also the only thing standing between you and a version of this in which a twenty-six-year-old porter is the one who signed a card she did not understand. Stay the issuer. Do not give it up in exchange for anything, and do not let anyone offer you a title in exchange for giving it up.”

“And the claim?”

“The claim attaches whether or not there is a lawsuit. Nobody has to sue you for the debt to be real.” She glanced at the plywood in his hands. “What is that?”

“A notice board for the ramp.”

“Good. Make it weatherproof, mount it low, and put the telephone number in large figures, because a person lying in a vehicle at the bottom of that hill is not going to find a small one.” She stood up. “I am not writing you a letter. If I write that the repair was sound, I will be writing a sentence I do not believe, and you will use it, and in four months somebody will produce it against the person who actually put their name on the gate.”

At twenty to ten, Bea Osunde came into the yard with the tool roll in one hand and a piece of paper in the other, and she put the paper on the bench next to Jonas’s sanding block and turned it round so it faced him.

It was in his own handwriting. Fourteen days. Five hours a day, sometimes seven. It covered every gap in the west clinic care schedule and then some, and it included three nights he had written *call in — available 02:00 to 06:00* beside a name that was his own, and at the bottom, in a firm hand that had clearly been written to be legible to a stranger, was the sentence: **if I cannot attend, the shift is covered by Mercer, J.**

“You finished this last night,” Bea said.

“Yes.”

“Who holds the gate on the Thursday you have the dentist appointment?”

“I will move it.”

“Who holds it if you have the flu and cannot read your own handwriting?”

“I set an alert on the plate.”

“And who reads the alert, Jonas, and who telephones the doctor, and who tells the patient?”

He had an answer for the plate, and none for the rest of it, and the silence went on long enough that Bea turned the paper back over and folded it and put it in her own pocket rather than give it back.

“I am keeping this,” she said. “Not as evidence. Because you will write it again.”

He looked at the board he had made, and at the notice in the plastic sleeve, and at the printed schedule still folded in his coat with his own name at the bottom of it, and he could see, with a clarity that was not comfortable, that he had spent two days building four different ways of being the answer and had written them down on four separate pieces of paper.

“No,” he said aloud, to the yard.

“No,” said Bea.

At a quarter to eleven he went back up to the ramp, and Ruth came out of the corridor, and Mina was at the card table, and Sanaa was on the phone to the cardiac suite about a booking in nine days that somebody would have to chase every morning, and he read the page of the term out loud from the top, and then he turned it over and said what he was going to do.

“We are not going to cover this with me. We are going to go back to this corridor tomorrow morning with everyone whose name belongs on it, and we are going to write down what the hours are, and we are going to leave the gaps in. And the released slot stays on the record as a cost that this term produced, and my name stays in the issuer field until a named successor has held that door for five days without me, and says so in writing.”

Nobody applauded. Sanaa said “good” in the voice she used for a patient who had finally taken a tablet, which Jonas understood was not the same as approval.

Ruth turned the black key, and the door opened, and the corridor was empty, and the lamp over the plate was white, and none of it meant anything at all except that at ten to eleven on the morning after everything, in a building that was not his, a woman whose name was finally written down turned a key and let nobody through, because there was nobody to let through, and that was the correct outcome, and it was the first correct outcome he had produced in four days.
