# Chapter 0010 — The City Needs a Name

The attended-service test began with seven people who had every reason to distrust the city’s records.

Nora Bell guarded the bridge. Dev Singh dispatched from the control room. Ruth Nwosu held the west-clinic night-watch key. Mina Okafor received the patient. Selah Mora kept the Bellow Market shelter handoff. Oren Pell monitored the limits of the test, and Jonas stood at the old bridge plate with a maintenance log that named all of them and none of their private histories.

The scheduled window ran from 10:00 to 11:30. One ambulance lane would cross the Saint Orra bridge, the patient would enter the west clinic, and one shelter return would complete at that clinic under Bellow Market’s watch. No other route could be opened. No witness could leave. The old-canal shelter remained closed; Selah’s duty now began at the receiving shelter and did not reopen the building behind her.

Nina Calder had brought the department’s test form. Its first line read **primary fault: hardware**. In the margin she had written, in a careful hand, “close as mechanical if service remains within the window.”

“Remove that,” Jonas said.

“It is a contingency.”

“It is a conclusion before the test.”

“The department needs a classification.”

“The department needs a true one.”

Nina held the pen over the paper. “Your appointment review is in eleven days.”

“Then it can read the same result as the rest of the city.”

The morning was cold enough to make breath visible over the canal. The bridge deck shone under a sky the color of old tin. Nora’s boots struck the pedestrian paving at regular intervals. Dev’s radio carried the ordinary sounds of the district: bus announcements, a delivery driver looking for Loading Bay Four, the hospital heliport clearing a lane. For a moment, the city sounded as if it had never heard of permissions.

At 9:58, the clinic confirmed the patient’s care claim. Dev confirmed the ambulance route. Oren checked the west-lane load certificate that supported the Assembly Emergency Service Test; the failed south span remained outside its scope. Ruth confirmed the clinic’s night-watch duty. Selah confirmed that the shelter return would remain within its existing local claim through the test window; its vehicle movement would close at handoff, while the formal term would close at 11:30. Mina confirmed that the patient’s receiving bed was ready.

Nina held up the registrar’s review slip. “Your suspension remains,” she said. “It lets you service an attended route when the named bearer initiates it. It does not let you open one alone.”

“I can work inside that limit,” Jonas said.

“Write it beside the test scope.”

Jonas looked at the list. “Do not call the routes open yet.”

Nora raised her radio. “Waiting on your word.”

“Open the ambulance lane only when the ambulance reaches the east marker. Selah, hold the shelter vehicle at the local end until the clinic has received the patient. Mina, call the bed before you open the ward door.”

“Less dramatic than the schedule,” Dev said.

“More accurate.”

At 10:00, Dev called the lane. The ambulance rolled across the bridge. The old empty-crossing request appeared on the control strip, pale and stubborn, and the ambulance’s service claim answered it in a stronger white. The side hinge gave one clear note.

The lane held. The patient crossed.

At the clinic, Ruth turned the black key. Mina called the receiving bed, and the west-clinic entrance opened. The stretcher passed inside. Dev marked the ambulance complete, and Jonas felt the first pressure behind his eyes before the bridge plate had finished clearing.

“Anchor,” Nora called. “You have the plate. I have the west post.”

He set the brass probe against the plate and looked at Nora’s hand instead of the crowd. The Margin opened between the two terms. The conflict was still there, but it was no longer a loose shape. The empty-crossing term belonged to the old bridge service. The ambulance claim belonged to the current care route. The separately inspected west-lane hinge supported the current crossing but did not settle precedence with the obsolete bridge term. The failed side route remained outside the test. What Jonas saw was a relationship, not an instruction.

Something brushed the edge of the view. Not a person. A pressure.

Jonas swallowed. “Side hinge pressure rising.”

Dev’s voice came through the radio. “Rising how much?”

“Not enough to close the ambulance lane. Enough to isolate the side route.”

Nora looked toward the pedestrian span. “Can you keep the bridge?”

“Not the side route. The bridge can keep one lane.”

Oren stepped into the control bay. “Then isolate it. Do not expand the test.”

Jonas opened the side-hinge cover. The physical pressure line was pinched where a replacement sleeve had been fitted years before. The ordinary repair was to replace the sleeve and wait for inspection. The civic fact was that the side route no longer had a holder to accept an isolation order. He could not make it obey by turning the screw.

“Existing duty,” he said. “Oren can order a safety stop within the Assembly test. The crew can isolate the failed side route. The bridge lane remains under the ambulance service claim. The clinic and shelter routes remain under their own witnesses.”

“Record that as an attended restriction,” Oren said. “Not a civic edit.”

Jonas nodded. Nora closed the pedestrian gate and set its mechanical latch. Dev called the attended restriction to the dispatch log. Ruth and Selah repeated their own limits from the two service ends. Mina stayed with the patient.

The side gate dropped into its physical stop. It was a safety precaution, not the formal safety closure; the restriction still required Oren’s order and a recorded isolation time.

At 10:17, the shelter return reached the bridge approach on its scheduled clinic run. Selah’s resident could not use the city route while the bridge test was in its isolation window. A street route was available, but it added thirty-eight minutes. Selah offered it as the local choice. Mina confirmed the clinic could accept the delay.

“Record it,” Jonas said.

Nina’s pen stopped. “The delay is not a fault in the hardware.”

“It is a cost of the fault. Record both.”

The shelter return went by street. The ambulance lane stayed open. The clinic entrance stayed open under Ruth’s watch. For the thirty-eight-minute wait, the test continued without pretending that three services shared one owner.

At 10:31, the side-hinge pressure changed. The sound in the plate became a thin whine, and the Margin brightened toward the clinic and shelter terms at once. Jonas’s vision narrowed to the service requests. He could not tell whether the system wanted the clinic to prioritize its patient or the shelter to keep its return. The answer was not in the fault.

He called for the anchor.

Nora put her palm over his on the probe. Her hand was callused, warm, and real. “Look at the plate,” she said. “You are trying to carry three rooms at once.”

Jonas forced his attention to the wet screw beneath his thumb. The physical pressure was high enough to damage the side hinge if it continued. He could isolate the damaged route. He could not make the hospital and shelter terms agree about the hour they had promised.

“Close the side route under Oren’s order,” he said. “Keep the ambulance lane open until the marked end. Record the shelter delay. Keep the clinic watch and shelter watch separate.”

Nora repeated Oren’s order to Dev. The side route closed formally at 10:31. The control strip went from amber to a hard red, but the ambulance lane remained white.

The system did not punish him for refusing to solve everything. It simply continued with the choices people had made.

At 10:55, the shelter return finished its vehicle movement and arrived at the clinic through the street route. Mina received the resident, while Selah confirmed the handoff and next watch at Bellow Market. The shelter route’s authorization remained open for the rest of the test; 10:55 ended the run, not the term. Mina recorded the thirty-eight-minute delay. The test’s three routes were still open, but none had become permanent.

Jonas’s nausea returned. He leaned against the bridge cabinet while the city’s noises moved around him: the tram, a school bell, someone shouting about a missing parcel, the river striking the pilings. The sounds did not tell him who to trust. They told him that people were making choices in the same weather.

Nina came to the cabinet. “Management wants the report to say the side hinge developed unsafe pressure and was isolated.”

“It did.”

“Secondary fault: physical.”

“Primary fault: permission conflict.”

“Those words will keep your appointment pending.”

“They will also keep the record true.”

She looked at the red strip, then at the clinic and shelter timestamps. “Nina Calder, records supervisor. I am recording that the side hinge developed unsafe pressure and was isolated under Oren’s safety-stop authority within the Assembly test, the service routes closed at their stated times, and the service claim remains unresolved. I am not certifying the underlying rule.”

Jonas nodded. “Thank you.”

“Do not thank me yet.”

At 11:30, Dev announced the end of the attended window. The ambulance lane closed. The clinic route closed after Mina confirmed that the patient had reached observation four and passed to the named day team. The shelter route closed after Selah confirmed the resident’s arrival and next watch. The side gate stayed isolated, and the test record carried each time in a separate line.

Oren signed the bridge portion. Ruth signed the clinic watch. Selah signed the shelter handoff. Mina signed the patient disposition. Nora signed the crossing. Dev signed the dispatch log.

Jonas had never seen so many signatures on one service and so little authority in a single place.

The management form still had **primary fault: hardware** written in Nina’s careful hand. Jonas crossed out the line and wrote **unresolved permission conflict; physical side-hinge pressure fault and unsafe condition confirmed**. The terminal rejected the change once. He submitted it again with the witness timestamps attached. The warning number returned, but the record stayed open.

At noon, he went to the Old Ward public notice board with the remaining test receipt. It stood beneath a glass canopy between the ferry office and the Assembly’s public doors. For years, the board had displayed bridge closures, water notices, clinic schedules, and maps. Now its maintenance strip had refreshed without a paper update.

The map showed the city’s service districts in pale blocks. At the center, where the ordinary map should have shown a root node, there was an empty field. A small line beneath it read **CENTRAL ROOT—HOLDER: —** and **NEXT MAINTENANCE REVIEW: RECLAMATION SEASON**. The field held no name or call to action, only a line for renewal.

Jonas remembered the pressure behind the old bridge plate. The public board offered no repair instruction. It did not identify the missing duty, the old council, or the term that had been allowed to fail. It gave him a fact and left him responsible for what he did with it.

Nina’s report named the bridge, the clinic, the recognized Bellow Market handoff, the witnesses, and the expiry. It did not name the residents or the old-canal route. The redacted custody receipt sat in the file like a closed door. The I. Vey seal appeared on the historical image, two worn initials in the margin of a system that had been built before his birth.

He was still holding the tool roll when Tomas arrived.

The shop steward took the test receipt from him and read the last line: **No civic term amended. No personal claim created. Routes closed at 11:30.**

“Good,” Tomas said.

“Read the rest.”

“I did.” Tomas tapped the unresolved conflict. “You did not make the side hinge permanent. You did not merge the shelter and clinic. You recorded the delay.”

“The appointment is still pending.”

“Of course it is.”

Tomas looked toward the empty field on the public board. “And now you know the next move is not to put your name in that field.”

Jonas’s hands tightened around the tool roll. “I did not plan to.”

“No. You were starting to see a way to make the blank useful.”

A tram passed beneath the Old Ward bridge. Its bell rang once and faded into the water noise.

Tomas handed the receipt back. “No shared-node edit until you sign a visible responsibility bond. A bond that says what you intend to do, who can refuse, and what claim follows if you are wrong. I will bring it to the shop. You will read it in front of witnesses.”

“What if I cannot sign it?”

“Then you do not make the next civic repair.”

Jonas looked at the blank field. It offered no answer and no permission to become indispensable. For the first time, the absence did not feel like a hidden door waiting for him to find the right key. It felt like work that would have to be answered for in public, by more than one person, without using a stranger’s record as proof.

He took the receipt from Tomas and turned it over. On the back, the test times were already written in three different hands.

“I’ll read the bond,” Jonas said.

Tomas’s expression remained severe, but he nodded. “Good. Tomorrow, after you sleep—if you can.”

The Old Ward board refreshed again. The empty field remained empty. Jonas did not touch it.

For the first time, he accepted that a safe shift and an honest record might be enough for today, even if they were not enough to make him legitimate.
