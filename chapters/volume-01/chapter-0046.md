# Chapter 0046 — The Bounded Handover

The loft kitchen at ten past seven on Friday the eighteenth of April had eleven people asleep on the floor on top of each other, four children on the table, and the exchange's Wednesday clinic — which is a Wednesday, which is the day before — turned into a drying room, and the low block of a street with no address in a converted net-repair building, because the water came over the line where the road meets the embankment at ten past five and went down four hundred millimetres at about nine o'clock and did not come back the same way.

There is no power in the building and there was no power in three buildings either side of it, because the substation on Marsh Lane tripped at eleven minutes past four in the morning, about half an hour after somebody put a hand wheel over, and was off for nine hours and a half, and the cause on the fault report — which arrives on the Monday, from a man who has never met anybody in this chapter — is *surge current associated with the storm overflow gate operation*. It does not say who turned the wheel. There is no field on the form for it.

Aurrie Nkemdirim was doing the arithmetic of who had come up the path and had lost what, and Margo Doré was at the tap with a jug because a tap is the first thing anybody looks for, and the water in the jug was the colour of weak tea and everybody in the room looked at it and nobody said anything, because it is the water from a standpipe whose intake is silting and everybody has known that since the seventh of April and one person put it in a document on Monday.

Bo Trimble was seventeen and asleep sitting up with his back against the boiler.

There is a thing that is very easy for a man from a department to think and that is wrong, and Jonas Mercer thought it at about ten to eight that morning and then watched it be wrong for the next three hours: that if you have got a bucket and a rope and a lamp, you are useful.

At half past eight he took the bucket and the rope and the lamp down to the dip on his own, because the chamber was going to be emptying all morning and there was about two hours left of daylight ebb, and the flap was shut on the flood and the bank was in the channel four metres downstream and there was a branch of it against the wall, and a silt trap needs clearing.

He was in the chamber for about nine minutes. He got the top eighteen inches of silt out of the trap and could not shift the rest, because the rest needs a shovel and a bar and two people and about three hours, and there is nobody on that street at half past eight in the morning on a Friday who is not standing in their own kitchen drying something. He came up the slipway with about nine inches of black silt in a bucket and put it on the step and did not tell anybody, and it is not in any record, and it bought a street about a fortnight of brown water, and it is not a repair and it is not a claim and it is a bucket and a boy.

The Saint Orra bridge at twenty past twelve on the Friday afternoon has a west lane, a westbound trolley signal, a tram deck and a barrier end, and the failing structure is on the south span, which is isolated and has been since the tenth of March and is staying that way. The west-lane hinge is at the west approach, under a cast cover the size of a tea tray, and it is the hinge Public Works passed in the first week of March, and it is the only one on this bridge that Jonas Mercer is allowed to work on and the only one that will let him.

The fault is a seam and it is two years old and it is the oldest thing he has ever touched: the barrier end's pedestrian closure term and the ambulance service claim have no order between them, and on a normal day it does not matter, because on a normal day the barrier keeper is on the same side as the ambulance. On the seventeenth of April, with two feet of water on the deck, the barrier keeper said the west lane was closed at four minutes to four and the ambulance service claimed it at six minutes past three, and the plate did the only thing a plate can do with two true instructions, and the deck was closed and claimed at the same time for fifty-eight minutes, and there is a fifty-eight in the Saint Orra crossing log for Friday the eighteenth of April, and Dev Singh wrote *both* in the box and would not be moved off it.

Behind that, further in, where nothing in the world can get at it, there is the obsolete empty-crossing clause, which says the west lane is to be kept clear during ambulance service, which was written when the west lane belonged to freight, and which Jonas has refused to touch in five weeks because it is not the fault and because he was told about the cost of reading a thing into a minute on the first of April.

Dev Singh is a dispatcher and has been at the crossing for a long time and has never held anything with his name in a field. He had been thinking about that for four days and he said so before anybody started, standing on the west approach with his hands in his pockets.

“I am not doing this for the flood,” he said. “I am doing it because I have got a bar and a schedule and a signal and in fifty-eight minutes on Friday morning I did not know which of the two I was. And if you mend this in a way that makes me the man who is always on at the crossing, then you have not mended anything, you have found the nearest pair of hands, and I have watched that happen to four men in this borough and two of them are off.”

“Nobody is permanent,” said Jonas Mercer. “That is the part I am actually here to say, and I would like to say it before I touch the cover rather than after. It is one field. It is precedence and nothing else. The issuer is you, because a plate will not take a role without a name and the barrier end is yours and the west-lane schedule is yours. The claim is yours, unpaid, and it is yours until you hand it on. It is not mine and it does not become mine because I am standing here. And I cannot renew it, and if it ends, it ends, and there is no version of this in which I come back in nine days and put it right.”

“Then whose is the ambulance side,” said Dev Singh.

“Yours and hers, together, and neither of you can be made to be on the bridge without the other, and either of you can stop it.”

Yvonne Amoah is forty-one and is the Saint Orra ambulance duty officer and has eleven years, and she had not been asked anything by Jonas Mercer in five weeks and had been in the room at the yard on the Wednesday and had said almost nothing. She had come to the bridge on the Friday morning on her own time and had brought the station's log, and she said:

“I want it in the words, in the writing, that the ambulance claim does not go onto that deck unless the barrier keeper and the duty officer both say so at the time. Not both beforehand. **At the time.** Because that is what happened on Friday morning: both of us were in a vehicle at nine miles an hour with water at the axle and neither of us had said it, and fifty-eight minutes happened, and I would rather be stopped on a bridge than be a second opinion in a car.”

They did it at twenty past twelve with the rain stopped and the deck wet and the wind going round.

Jonas got the four bolts out with a bar and a cloth so as not to scar the casting, and the right hand would not close on the spanner, so he jammed a wedge of roofing slate under the cover and held it with his forearm the entire time, and Nora Bell held the lamp, because her scope is the barrier end and the gate she latches and no other part of the bridge, and she held it above the plate and read the terms aloud as they printed.

The first thing that printed was the handover.

**THE BRIDGE BARRIER END AND THE AMBULANCE SERVICE CLAIM ARE HELD IN COMMON. NEITHER ANSWERS TO THE OTHER. EACH MAY STOP THE WEST LANE. THE LANE IS USED ONLY WHEN THE BARRIER KEEPER AND THE AMBULANCE DUTY OFFICER HAVE BOTH AGREED AT THE CROSSING. A HANDOVER IS A FRESH ACT OF BOTH, SPOKEN, IN DAYLIGHT, AND THE PERSON RECEIVING IT ANSWERS FOR IT.**

Dev Singh read that out twice and then handed it to Yvonne Amoah at twenty to one, in daylight, out loud, in front of four people, and she took it and said it back, and at ten past one she handed it back to him, out loud, in daylight, in front of the same four people, and the record has two handovers in it in fifty minutes, and Dev Singh said that the two handovers were the entire point and that he would fight anybody who tried to take the second one out of the book.

The second thing that printed was the claim and it was short.

**CLAIM: THE BARRIER KEEPER KEEPS THIS LANE TRUE WHILE THE WATER IS ABOVE THE LINE ON THE POST. UNPAID. THE CLAIM IS THE HOLDER'S AND NOT THE ISSUER'S AND NOT THE MENDER'S.**

“You have put my name on it,” said Dev Singh.

“I have put your name on it because it is yours,” said Jonas Mercer. “That is the part of the last five weeks I have understood and the part I would like you to keep.”

And then the plate printed a third thing, and it is the only thing he was there to say, and it is the only thing the hinge was ever going to print about a handover.

**A WITNESSED HANDOVER IS RECORDED AMONG THE HOLDERS. IT GRANTS NOBODY AN OFFICE.**

Yvonne Amoah looked at it for a second and then said, “That is the first time one of these has told me what it is for.”

Dev Singh, on the west approach, with the log open on the barrier, said the thing that was under all of it and that none of them had asked him for.

“It is still in the file,” he said. “The empty crossing. It has not been read into anything and it has not been touched and it is still in that file with its own number on it, and on a Tuesday in June with no water on that deck it is still the reason an ambulance loses eleven minutes at this bridge, and I am not going to stand here on Friday afternoon and pretend that a surge took it out of the file. That is the next one. That is mine, and it is not a flood, and nobody is going to pay me for it.”

“Then it is on the card,” said Jonas.

“It is not on your card,” said Dev Singh. “Your card has nine things on it and one of them is a key. That one gets a file and a name and a date and a fight, and it will take me eighteen months, and I would rather have it on a file.”

Oren Pell's record is not a minute. That is the distinction and Jonas Mercer had heard it made, and had not understood it, until the registrar said it to him at ten past seven on Friday evening in a car park in the Old Ward where there was nowhere else to stand.

A minute is a list. A minute is a thing that a panel reads and a gallery listens to and a member can be reported as having caused. A registrar's record is a thing that describes a state of a node, entered under a duty, and it does not name a person unless the person is the state.

Oren Pell was sixty-one and he had been a deputy registrar for nineteen years and he had a manila folder on the bonnet of a car and a floodlight on a tripod because the Old Ward board is under a glass canopy and the light in it is on a timer.

“Four lines,” he said. “And I am going to read you two of them and the other two are for the file and you can have the file in June.”

He read the plate first, which is the condition, which is Jonas's line, the one he declined to read into a minute on the first of April at 09:42 in front of a statutory panel and six members and forty-one people in a gallery.

> **IF THE FLAP IS NOT SHUT, THE BANK IS NOT A WALL.**

“For the record,” said Oren Pell, “that line is not new, that line is eleven years old, that line is under a service seal stamped in the same year and by the same works as a piece of brass that came out of a market hatch in April, and I have not connected those two things and I am not going to, and neither is anybody else in this car park.

“Now the two lines for the file. One: this line was available to be recorded on the first of April of this year and was not, on the ground that a minute is a list, and that ground was a reporting officer's ground and not a registrar's ground, and I have read the minute and the reporter is the man standing in front of me and I am not going to argue with him about it in a car park. Two: a civic work with no holder and no issuer, recorded, is an unclaimed burden, and an unclaimed burden attaches to the nearest responsible maintenance route on the chain, and the nearest responsible maintenance route on the lowland chain is not Mr Mercer, because the department has already assigned him a claim in the series 6 file on the twenty-ninth of March, and a registrar may not attach two unclaimed burdens to one route without a determination, and I have made the determination and it is the second line of the second page and you may read it.”

Jonas read the second page.

> **UNCLAIMED BURDEN — LOWLAND WARDS 6-L/14, CHAMBER AND BANK. ATTACHED TO: THE INTERIM LOWLAND MAINTENANCE POST, SERIES 6. HOLDER: ROYDEN ACHEBE.**

“He accepted it four minutes ago,” said Oren Pell. “By telephone, from a yard in Gantry Ward, in about nine words. I put it to him as a question and he said yes, and then he said a thing that I am going to repeat to you because I think you should have it, and then I am not going to discuss.

“He said: *Then it is mine in a way the post is not. I took the post in a room in April on the argument that a man can hold a plate and a department cannot, and I have held it for seventeen days and it has a number on it and nothing under the number, and this has got four tonnes of stone and a flap and a man who wrote his own classification on it, and I will hold that, and I am telling you that I would rather have had it before I had the post.*”

The light on the board came on at eight o'clock, fifty minutes after Royden Achebe had said his nine words out of a yard in Gantry Ward, and the caretaker who puts the board up every evening walked past the two of them without stopping and looked at the paper in Jonas Mercer's hand for about a second and a half and went on in, and the board carried the things it has carried since March, and Jonas Mercer read it standing up the way he has read it a hundred times.

**CENTRAL ROOT — HOLDER: —**

**6-L/14 — NON-CIVIC PLANT. ACCEPTED. NO CIVIC CLAIM ATTACHES TO THE CONTENTS OF THE CHAMBER OR THE BANK.**

**6-L/19 — NOT ACCEPTED. REMAINS IN SCOPE PENDING INSPECTION. NO INSPECTION SCHEDULED — NO LOCATION OF RECORD.**

**6-L/21, LOWLAND REFUGE STORE 1, COOPER'S REACH — HOLDER: —. NOT REACHED ON 17 APRIL.**

The last line of it, in a box, four words and a time, in Nina Calder's typing and nobody else's hand:

**6-L/14 — UNCLAIMED BURDEN ATTACHED 18 APRIL. SEE HOLDER.**

And Royden Achebe's name, in a different box, which is how he is a man and not a department, and which is how a thing that took Jonas five weeks to refuse has been settled for a man who has not been here for any of it, in nine words, out of a yard, on a Friday afternoon, while his crews were on two nodes in Salt Row that Jonas has no clearance for and has not been to.

Tomas Renn's submission went in at fourteen minutes past nine that morning, four days early, with a covering note, and it is in the public papers of the Standing Committee on Civic Continuity in the folder for the second of June, and at about two o'clock on the Friday afternoon a CivicCore continuity officer telephoned the shop and asked whether the union would consider a joint working paper on continuity arrangements, and Tomas Renn said no in a sentence, and he did not tell anybody at the shop, and Jonas Mercer found out about it on the Saturday morning from Bea Osunde, who had been in the yard when the telephone rang.

He did not go and find Tomas about it. He stood in the yard for a while with a piece of flat bar in his hand and worked out, quite slowly, that the man who had been the only person asking in this city for a year had gone, in one morning, on purpose, into a document, and that he had done it so that the sentence in it could not be taken back or softened by anybody who was paid, and that he had not told the shop, and that he had not told Jonas, and that this was the correct decision and that Jonas Mercer would have made the other one.

Councillor Iven Sore came up the towpath at about half past seven in the morning on the Saturday because Jonas Mercer had sent her the tide sheet on the Wednesday evening with nothing on it but the numbers and the two dates, and because she had read a registrar's record at about seven o'clock that morning, in her office, alone, and had had to sit down.
Aurrie Nkemdirim decided whether she came up, and Aurrie Nkemdirim decided she came up, and she did not decide because Jonas asked her to.

She was not asked twice, and nobody in the kitchen had to be told anything, and the one thing about the Saturday morning that Jonas Mercer noticed and wrote down later on a bit of card was that a councillor of the Civic Assembly had walked ninety metres of towpath on her own and had been let in by a woman of sixty-one who had spent five weeks refusing to let anybody in.

She came in and put her coat on the back of a chair and stood in a net-loft kitchen with a woman of sixty-three holding a jug, a woman of seventy-one by the range, a boy of seventeen asleep against a boiler, and about sixty other people who had got up, and she said, “Thank you for letting me in,” and Margo Doré said, “We have not got a chair for you that is not got water on it,” and Sore said she had stood in worse, and sat down on a sack of something.

She got about eleven minutes.

She said that she had wanted the condition line on the first of April and had been refused, and that a registrar had written it into a record at ten past seven on Friday night, and that she had read it in her office at about seven o'clock this morning, in her own chair, and had to sit down.

“A term and a flap and a sill,” she said. “And a bank. And a paragraph about the bank being a wall. And a date. That is the entire civic record of a service that two thousand four hundred sandbags and a Thursday clinic depend on, and it has taken a man to lose a bank to write down what a bank is for, and I sat in this room on the second of April and you offered me a form.”

“We did not offer you a form,” said Margo Doré. “You asked for a form. There is a difference and it is the whole difference and you are about to ask me the next thing and I am going to answer it the same way, so you may as well not have stopped.”

“Right,” said Councillor Sore. “Then here is the next thing. I am going to be honest with you in about four sentences and then I am going to go and sit in a car.

“I have wanted the contract passed in June since the twenty-ninth of March and I have said so in a corridor to that man and I did not mean it as a threat, I meant it as a worry. My view is that a city cannot keep naming people one at a time until the water comes. And on Thursday night the water came, and on Thursday night I was at home, because I am not on any of this, and eleven people were in my Methodist hall at six in the morning with no heating and no hot water and no building, and a man came into the hall at about half past four and gave me a piece of paper about it, and I have had it in my coat since, and I would like to put it on your table and I would like you to tell me whether it is a claim on you.”

It was a folded sheet from a CivicCore continuity officer, headed *ASSISTANCE OFFER — COOPER'S REACH AND THE LOWLAND — RETROSPECTIVE PARTICIPATION*, and it offered to fund the hall for eleven weeks and to hold the marshal costs, and it was dated the seventeenth of April, and it had been produced on the night of the surge by somebody who did not know that eleven people were in the building.

“Read it,” said Pia Santos, from the doorway, and Sore read it out loud in about fifteen seconds, standing up.

“No,” said Yasmin Rohan.

That is everything she said and she said it in a laundry room on the thirty-first of March and she said it again in a net-loft kitchen on the eighteenth of April and it is the same sentence in the same form both times, and there are eleven people in this room who heard it the first time from a different woman and a completely different set of circumstances, and nobody in the room found it strange.

“Right,” said Councillor Sore, and folded it up, and put it in her coat.

And then she asked the question, and it was not the question she came with, and Jonas Mercer was standing by the door and had no warning of it at all.

“Pia Santos,” she said. “On the twenty-ninth of March, on the bottom of a slipway, you said *not a count, not a survey, not a statement — who is the other end of it.*”

“Yes.”

“I am the other end of it,” said Councillor Sore. “I am the member whose office received your paper on the first of April and who has read the registrar’s answer and the series 6 file and the review’s findings and the Brine Wharf appeal, and I can tell you that on the other end of it there is no one. Not because people will not answer. Because I have looked, in the only way anybody can look, which is by asking a department for a record, and a department cannot give me a record of a place that has no address, and if it could, the record would be a form with a number in it, and the number would be the answer, and it would be the wrong one.

“I can tell you that a 6-L/14 has been classified as plant and that no civic claim attaches to the chamber or the bank, and that a 6-L/19 is in scope pending an inspection nobody can schedule, and that a flap gate failed on Thursday night and a registrar has written down why, and that in six weeks a committee will be asked whether a contractor may hold a node when the holder has lapsed, and nobody in that room, including me, will be able to say what is at the end of the Lowland Works chain, because the chain has a service number and the service has no location of record and there is no other end of it at all.”

She stopped. Pia Santos, who is nineteen, stood in the doorway of a net-loft kitchen with a wet towel in her hands and did not say anything for about eight seconds, which is a very long time.

“That is the answer,” she said.

“It is not an answer, it is the absence of one.”

“It is what we have been asking you for since March,” said Pia Santos, “and the answer has been the other end of it for thirty-two years and you are the first one to come up here and say it out loud, and I am not being kind about it, and I am also not going to pretend it is nothing, because you came, and you asked, and you did not ask for a number.”

Councillor Sore looked at the ring of pencil on the wall above the shelf of cleaned cups, which has a blank space inside it, and she did not ask about it, and Jonas Mercer understood later that this was the single most important thing that happened in that kitchen and that he was the only person who could tell that it had happened.

Then she said: “I am not going to promise you anything. I have learned more in nine minutes than I have in four months of committee and I am not going to turn round in a kitchen and be a different person in a chamber, because the moment I do that you will never believe a word off any of us again. I am going to go back to the Old Ward and I am going to do what I can with what I have got, which is very little, and I am going to tell you before I go that I do not think the contract should be passed in June any more and I am not going to explain why in here.”

“Thank you,” said Aurrie Nkemdirim.

“Do not thank me. Nothing has changed. I have just stopped being the only person in the room who has never seen this floor.”
