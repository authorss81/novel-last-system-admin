# Chapter 0950 — Under The Raised Trough

Thursday the thirty-first of October 2024, at the trough standing against the back wall of number twelve with the yard in shade. Under a raised trough.

**A STONE TROUGH STANDS AGAINST THE BACK WALL OF NUMBER TWELVE RAISED AT EACH END ON A PIECE OF SLATE STOOD ON END, WHICH LEAVES A RUN BENEATH IT THE WHOLE LENGTH OF THE TROUGH, AND THE HAND GOES UP INTO THAT RUN WITH THE THUMB HOOKED OVER THE TROUGH'S OWN FAR RIM AND THE THUMB PRESSED DOWN, TO FIND OUT HOW DEEP THE RUN IS AND WHAT LIES ALONG THE BOTTOM OF IT.**

The trough is a single block of stone about five feet long and two feet across at the top, hollowed out by a man who had a week in the yard and a chisel and no instructions. And it stood on the ground at the foot of that wall for a hundred and thirty years before it was lifted.

Nobody lifted it for anything. It was set up on the two pieces of slate because a trough that stands an inch off the ground can be tipped out and a trough that sits on the ground cannot, and the man who put it up wanted the option and then wanted nothing at all else.

The hand goes up under the trough with the thumb hooked over the trough's own far rim. And the thumb presses down on it, so that the far side of the run is the side that gets measured and not the near one.

The rim is worn. A stone trough that has been handled for a century and a bit has a shallow dish worn into the top of the rim about the length of a hand, deepest where the buckets went and never quite gone back to flat anywhere along it.

Under that dish the hand goes in flat. The run is three inches deep at the middle and shallower towards both ends where the trough beds down on the slates, and it is closed at each end by the slate itself, which stands across the full width of the trough's own underside and leaves nothing at all at either corner of the run.

---

What is along the bottom of the run is the flags the trough stands on. And they are dry and pale and dusty in a band the width of the trough, and the dust in that band is a different colour from the dust outside it because it has not had the weather on it for a century and a bit.

There is a leaf skeleton in it at the middle, dried flat, with the veins still in it, and it is a beech leaf and it is finished from here. Leaves do what that one has done over three months in a dry dark place, and what happens under a trough is the same thing that happens on the sill of an open window.

There is grit under the stone ends of the trough where the block was levered up and let down again, a line of it an inch deep and pale. There is a snail in the left-hand corner that does not move when the flags are put down on either side of it.

The two pieces of slate are brittle along every edge and grey all over. And they stand straight because slate on end is strong on its short dimension and because neither of them is a foot high.

The trough is against the wall because a trough is always against something, because it takes a great deal of space and because the ground beside a wall is the flattest ground a yard has. There was a standpipe on that wall once and it is a capped stub now with a lead plate over it, and the trough was set under the stub because a trough under a tap is the only arrangement that makes any sense at all.

A stone trough weighs more than anything else that size in a yard. Set down on two slates an inch high it is not difficult to tip, because a trough tipped on its long edge rolls rather than lifts, and a man can get a shoulder under the near rim and walk it over. The slates are for that and for nothing else, and they have been doing it for a hundred and thirty years without being moved.

Slate was used because it was there. It came off the same roof as everything else that has been rebuilt on that side of the house, and the pieces are the sizes slate gets cut into when there is a broken edge to be made do with, which is why neither of them is square.

A trough is a vessel and the making of one is a piece of work in its own right, and a trough made by hand is always a compromise between a block big enough to take a pail and a block a man can lift. The hollow goes down from the top face and gets wider as it goes, because the chisel is not a machine and the man working it goes round the block in courses and leaves a taper of the man's own making on it.

That taper is the reason the underside of this trough has a flat in the middle and a slope down each side of it. The flat is the part that has been near the ground for a hundred and thirty years and the slopes are the parts that have not.

The stone is a hard blue-grey sandstone from the same quarry as the kerbs on that side of the street, and it is not brittle through the body of it. It goes crumbly at the top edge, where the chisel left the arris in the first place and where a hundred years of buckets have been knocking the arris down, and the two kinds of face are about a foot apart and can be told apart in the dark.

The inside of the trough is smoother than the outside of it by a long way. It has an inch of rain in the bottom of it and nothing in the trough but that, and the smoothing came first from the chisel and afterwards from everything that has been put into it and taken out again.

---

Her sister is standing in the mouth of the back passage with her back to the yard wall and her face to the passage, and she is not going in and she is not coming out.

"The tables were out at the hall at ten."

"The tables were out at the hall at half past nine."

"Then the van came before the tables were out, which is backwards."

"The van comes when it comes and the tables go where they are put."

They stand there in the mouth of the passage a while with the yard behind them. The fingers had come down out of the run before either of them was in the passage, and the trough above that mouth of the passage has been stood on by a woman who came out to hang washing and a man who came out to look at the roof, and neither of them has ever looked underneath it.

A postcard has come for the woman at number twelve and has not come for anybody else on that side of the street. And it is from a niece who sends word about twice a year, and neither of them makes anything of it.

Nothing has been done to that trough since the two pieces of slate went under it, and the run along the whole of its length has been open to the air of that yard and closed to the light of it ever since. The flags at the bottom of it are dry all the year and pale all the year for the same reason.

The run under that trough is three inches deep at the middle and shut at both corners, and its floor is a band of dry flags a hundred and thirty years pale.

Under that trough there are three inches of dry air, a leaf skeleton lying flat in the middle of the flags and a snail in the left-hand corner, and a hand hooked over the far rim finds the dish in the stone, then air, then the flags, and the whole of that waits, pending a reason that has not come.
