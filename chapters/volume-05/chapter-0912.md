# Chapter 0912 — Over The Kerb Notch

Saturday the twenty-fifth of May 2024, at the kerb of concrete at the mouth of the bay at the lock-up before the street wakes. The saw cut across the top of a concrete kerb.

**SHE HAS A KERB OF CONCRETE AT THE MOUTH OF THE BAY AT THE LOCK-UP WITH A NOTCH IN ITS TOP AND SHE MEANS TO PRESS THE HEEL SIDE OF THE THUMB INTO THAT NOTCH WITH FINGERS SPREAD AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS AT THE BAY MOUTH FROM THE CONCRETE WITH A RAG IN HER POCKET.**

The apron round that kerb was poured with the bay and has been swept every working morning since, while the kerb itself has kept its line.

It is a kerb about four feet long, with the top worn round where tyres brush it and a notch cut in the middle about a finger wide.

She wants what the notch is cut down into, where the broom never gets and the rain stays longest.

A cut in a crown goes deeper than the crown does, because the top only carries weather and the cut carries what the weather has been cutting with.

So the thumb heel side sinks into the kerb notch with fingers fanned across the top.

Sand at the head of a cut rubs the skin dry while the concrete round it keeps the night's chill against the hand, and the notch drags where the loose of it lines the sides.

The heel of the thumb goes in past the first joint and finds sand packed hard in the bottom of the cut, pale and parched, gritty throughout, with a rusted nail stub lying aside.

She bears down harder, and the sand slides a fraction under the thumb and then jams, and cut sand tightens the further it is driven because every grain in it is broken open.

Below the surface sand there is more sand of the same make, going down past the reach of the thumb, and both flanks of the cut drop sheer to it with no ledge between.

The woman who books work is out at the bay mouth from the concrete with a rag in her pocket, and she does not take it out.

She stood at the mouth with her hands in her coat pockets and looked at the kerb and then at the shutter and back again, and she walked a little along the kerb and back, and then she went back to the office steps with the rag still in her pocket and left the mouth where it was.

A notch like that one is put in a kerb for a wheel to run in, and this one has had a barrow run in it every week since the bay was first cleared.

Concrete carries grit in its body from the day it is poured, and every grain of that grit was in the way of the saw blade, and the blade gave out on the second pass rather than on the first.

A blade that gives out leaves the flank torn instead of sawn, and those torn pores have stood open through the seasons since and taken yard dust into their mouths.

The notch is a saw cut and not a cast one, and a cast kerb would have had that dip in it from the mould with its arrises still sharp.

It was made years back, when the bay was to have a second shutter and the second shutter was to run along this kerb on small iron wheels.

That shutter was cut short in the shop and stood a long while as a bench by the office door, and in every year since, nothing has gone near the notch but a barrow wheel and the weather.

A cut like that loses no strength. Showers pass down through it and away, the flanks turning shadowed before they harden across the seasons, till the notch belongs to the kerb as a chip belongs to a cup.

Filling comes from above. Yard dust near the mouth of the bay, apron sand toward the middle, and wind-borne grit settling deepest where the runoff stops.

Deep grit rests slack on top and dense below, and the dense part is as hard as the pour it sits in.

The whole mass has drawn back from the flanks by a hair throughout, because grit shrinks as it dries while the pour shifts with cold, and that hair of draught is what airs the notch through damp spells.

She tries the thumb afresh, and the loose sand and the packed sand tell differently at once, the loose giving under the nail and the packed refusing it.

A cut carrying loose sand will take a screw and keep it for weeks. The threads bite into the grains and the grains grip back, and the purchase lasts until the first soaking spell softens them.

A cut carrying packed sand will take no iron at all. The grains swell in showers and shrink in sun and shift the iron weekly until it drops out of the cut.

A cut with dense grit in the bottom will take a bolt once the grit is out of it. Steel then reaches the solid pour, which spreads the strain, and the kerb bears it the way it bears a wheel.

The notch stayed open and the bolt went elsewhere, because packed sand in the bottom of a cut will not take iron and the sand was packed before anyone thought to try it.

The crown of the kerb runs level to the sky the whole length of it, sound throughout, with no crack anywhere along the top.

Concrete that has stood under standing water takes a tint from the lime meeting the dust in it, and the tint sinks a little way in and then halts where the pour goes hard.

Damp pour smells of earth after showers and the smell goes out toward midday as the face dries, and it stays longest inside the cut where no sun gets at it to bake it out.

So the notch rings shadowed all day while the crown shows its own dampness only along the drip course, and the notch gathers what the crown sheds and keeps it where it stands.

---

A saw goes through concrete faster than through stone, and it goes through it duller every hour it works, so the iron of the blade ends finer than the iron of the shop that made it.

That is what the flaked edge on this notch is, iron that gave out twice on grit and would have cut stone all day.

The stone chippings in the pour show flat where the saw met them, with the flat faces bright against the grey, and each flat chip throws water off instead of drinking it.

Chippings lying flat like that stay true through the winters, while the ones that tilt in their bed work loose by spring.

The gate chains lie coiled on wall hooks beside the office doorway, with the links rusted together where showers have pooled inside the loops. A chain stretches by about one link in ten years under barrow strain, and that much is enough to make it drag.

Chains that drag polish the pour in arcs, and chains that hang rust solid inside the loop, and the arcs on this yard tell which gate is worked every day.

The padlock on the side gate hangs with a brass body gone green along the joints of its case, the shackle bright where hands polish it and dark where rain spots it.

Shackles wear thin on the pull side and keep their thickness on the other, so a shackle tells you which way a gate is opened most.

The yard gates swing on strap hinges, wide at the post and narrow at the leaf, and each one sits true where iron meets timber and casts a shade that pales in sun and darkens after showers.

A true hinge swings without complaint and an askew one sings until oil stills it for days, and half the hinges in this yard sing on a cold morning.

Roof spill braids round the grit in bright threads after showers and parts at the kerb, deepening where the pour dips and thinning where green rims the bend by the pipe.

That bend weeps long after the clouds have gone and keeps that green going through rainless spells, which is why the far corner of this apron never dries in a week.

The bolts through the shutter guides sit in holes rimmed with rust, with the iron dark round each one where rain follows it in.

A bolt that sits true to its hole draws the guide tight without bending, and a bolt that beds edge on works its hole oval by the end of the first winter.

The boards of the office stack meet their stickers flat to flat, and that admits draught through the gaps instead of sealing them in.

Boards lying flat on stickers dry level by dusk, and boards lying flat on the pour keep the damp underneath them till the next rainless spell.

She takes the thumb out of the notch, wipes the sand off on the flank of the kerb, and stands back in the middle of the apron where the concrete is level.

By that light the notch shows as a dark score cut in a flat top, and the pour lies flat to it on all four sides, and the score goes down further than the eye can follow.

A barrow wheel could run in that score tomorrow and would find it packed at the bottom, and it will go on being packed at the bottom until the loose grains lying over it work down and settle, once the wind has finished shifting them about.
