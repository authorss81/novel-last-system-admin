# Chapter 0912 — Over The Kerb Notch

Saturday the twenty-fifth of May 2024, at the kerb of concrete at the mouth of the bay at the lock-up before the street wakes. The notch in the top of a kerb.

**SHE HAS A KERB OF CONCRETE AT THE MOUTH OF THE BAY AT THE LOCK-UP WITH A NOTCH IN ITS TOP AND SHE MEANS TO PRESS THE HEEL SIDE OF THE THUMB INTO THAT NOTCH WITH FINGERS SPREAD AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS AT THE BAY MOUTH FROM THE CONCRETE WITH A RAG IN HER POCKET.**

The kerb came with the bay when the apron went in, and the apron round it has been brushed a hundred times while the kerb has kept its line.

It is a kerb about four feet long, with the top worn round where tyres brush it and a notch cut in the middle about a finger wide.

She wants what the notch sits over, where the broom never reaches and the rain sits longest.

A notch in a crown reveals beyond its face, for the face carries wear while the notch carries what wear cuts.

So the thumb heel side sinks into the kerb notch with fingers fanned across the top.

The concrete feels cool on the skin, the way yard concrete stays cool till morning, and the notch drags where sand lines it.

The thumb bears down near half an inch before meeting firmness. Not concrete. Sand compacted within the nick, pale and parched, gritty throughout with a rusted nail stub aside.

She bears a touch harder, sand sliding a fraction beneath before jamming fast, cut sand tightening the further it is driven.

Deeper sand underlies the surface sand, descending beyond thumb reach, nick flanks plunging sheer sans ledge beneath.

The booking woman paces to the bay mouth over concrete, rag pocketed and untouched.

She stood at the mouth with her hands in her coat pockets and looked at the kerb and then at the shutter and back again, and she walked a little along the kerb and back, and then she went back to the office steps with nothing in her hands and left the mouth where it was.

A kerb with a notch cut for a wheel is a common thing at a mouth, because a barrow is wheeled in and out, and each wheeling leaves its own mark.

The notch went in on the day the shutter was hung, with the same saw that cut the guides, and the saw ran true for the guides and chipped for the kerb, which explains the rough flanks where the blade broke out.

Concrete cuts clean when the pour sits flat to the saw, and chips it when the pour lifts at one corner, and the kerb lifted that day on grit under it.

That day sits years back in yard memory, when the bay was to take a second shutter and the second shutter was to run on the same kerb with small iron wheels.

The second shutter was cut short in the shop and used for a bench by the office door through many winters, and the notch was left open to the weather.

A kerb with an open nick loses nothing of its strength. Showers pass through and away, the flanks turning shadowed before hardening with seasons, till the nick belongs to the kerb as a chip belongs to a cup.

Filling arrives overhead. Yard dust near the mouth, apron sand toward the middle, wind-borne grit settling deepest to rest where runoff quits.

Deep grit rests slack above and dense below, the dense part equalling the surrounding pour for hardness.

The mass has drawn away from the flanks by a hair throughout, for grit contracts drying while the pour shifts with cold, leaving that hair of draught to air the nick through damp spells.

She tries the thumb afresh to learn the sand. Slack above, compacted beneath, the compacted zone shadowed where showers pooled.

A shallow nick carrying slack sand accepts a screw for weeks. Threads bite the grains and grains grip round, the purchase enduring till the first soaking spell loosens it as the kerb perspires.

A nick carrying compacted sand accepts no iron. Grains swell in showers and contract in sun, shifting iron weekly till it drops.

A nick with dense grit beneath accepts a bolt once the grit is spooned out. Steel passes to solid pour which spreads the strain, letting the kerb bear it as it would bear a wheel.

Hence the nick stayed open and the bolt went elsewhere. Whoever sawed it marked the chipping, saved the bolt for bench work, and abandoned the nick to skies.

The kerb crown remains flat to the sky, sound throughout and uncracked along its length.

Pour shadowed by standing runoff owes its tint to lime meeting dust, the tint sinking briefly before halting at flat hard pour. Damp pour carries an earthy odour following showers, fading toward midday while the face dries flat anew. Odour lingers deepest within the nick, beyond reach of baking sun.

Hence the nick rings shadowed while the crown shows it solely at the drip course. The nick gathers shutter drips and retains them, the crown shedding them down its cheeks.

---

A saw chips concrete faster than stone because concrete carries sand in its body from pouring day, and each grain dulls the iron edge a fraction more with every pass.

On this notch the edge went on the second pass, so the sides tore instead of cutting, and the torn pores have stood open through seasons since and gathered yard dust in their mouths.

The stone chippings in the pour show flat where the saw met them, with the flat faces bright against the grey, and each flat chip throws water off instead of drinking it.

Flat to the blade like that the chips sit true through winters, while ones that tilt in work loose by spring.

Gate chains lie coiled on wall hooks beside the office doorway, links rusted together where showers pooled inside the loops. Chains stretch a link per decade under barrow strain, lengthening just enough to drag. Dragging chains polish the pour in arcs while hung ones rust solid, revealing which gate labours hardest through winter. The padlock on the side gate hangs heavy with a brass body gone green along the joints of its case, shackle bright where hands polish it and dark where rain spots it. Shackles wear thin on the pull side over decades, while bodies endure, telling which openings earn daily use.

The yard gates swing on dark strap hinges, wide at the posts and narrow at the leaves. Each hinge sits true where iron meets timber and casts shade paling in sun and darkening after showers. A true hinge swings without complaint, while an askew one sings till oil stills it for days. Roof spill braids round grit in bright threads after showers and parts at the kerb, deepening where the pour dips and thinning where green rims the bend, which weeps long after clouds pass into evening, nursing green through rainless spells.

The bolts through the shutter guides sit in holes rimmed with rust, with the iron dark round each where rain follows it in. Bolts sit true where the head meets the iron, and each head keeps a collar of grit that washes off in rain and gathers again by evening. A bolt that sits true to iron draws the guide tight without bending, while one that beds edge on works oval by winter.

Office-stack boards meet stickers flat to flat, admitting draught rather than sealing it. Boards resting flat on stickers dry level by dusk, while those resting flat on pour retain under-damp till the next rainless spell.

She draws the thumb out of the notch and rubs the sand off on the side of the kerb, and studies the top from the yard for what the line gives away at midday.

From overhead the nick presents as a dark score in flat pour, square and correct head-on, flat concrete hugging it all round.

Seen slantwise the nick shows wide at the top and narrow below, where the saw broke out on its way through, while the kerb crown stays flat round it through sun and shower.

Square above and narrow beneath, the base sawn when the pour lay flat and true suiting iron best for a season, once wind-settled sand is spooned clear.
