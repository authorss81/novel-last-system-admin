# Chapter 0845 — Dust In The Joints

Saturday the sixteenth of September 2023, along the front run at number twelve from the time the dew lifts off the gate until the street has gone quiet, and the dust gone out of every joint is the whole of that Saturday.

**THERE IS DUST GONE TO POWDER IN THE JOINTS OF THE RUN BETWEEN THE GATE AND THE STEP AT NUMBER TWELVE AND SHE MEANS TO BRUSH EVERY JOINT OUT AND LAY FINE DUST BACK INTO IT WITHOUT LIFTING A FLAG, AND HER SISTER IS AT THE PASSAGE WINDOW AND SHUTS IT AGAINST THE DUST.**

That run is the line of flags from the gate to the front step, laid across the full width of the front in May and bedded in sand brushed into the joints and brushed over the top of them afterwards.

The sand has not gone anywhere. It is still in the run. It has only stopped being sand and become powder, which is a different thing with the same weight.

She does not repack it. Packing under a flag wants a lath and a boot and a long afternoon, and the run was packed in May, and the one flag that had gone loose is packed and has sat still since. What has happened since is that the top of the bedding has dried and come away from the sides, and a joint with powder in it is a joint with nothing in it, because powder goes down with the first rain that finds it and takes the bedding down with it.

She starts at the gate end, which is the end most walked on, with a stiff yard brush, a pan, a bucket, and a pad of sacking to kneel on.

Each joint gets the brush on it from the house side towards the gate, so the dust is pushed out of the joint and away along the run and not back down it into the next one. It comes up off the flags in a thin grey smoke and hangs in the still September air, and where it has been standing since May it goes up in a puff and settles again on the flags she has already done.

She goes over each joint twice and the second time takes much less.

Under the dust the joints show what they are. Along the middle of the run the bedding is still firm and pale and takes the brush tip and stands, and there is nothing to be done there and she goes on. At the gate end and at the step end the brush tip goes in and the joint takes it, and where it takes it the colour goes from pale to dark, which is the old sand at the bottom of the joint and not a great deal of it.

Where the brush is swallowed to its full width the joint has lost everything above the firm part. She goes over them as she goes and there are nine like that on the run, four of them open enough to lay a finger flat across.

She puts no fresh sand into the nine. Fresh sand dropped into an open joint falls to the bottom and sits there as a plug, and a plug in a joint with firm bedding under it is worse than powder, because a plug is a thing a boot can work out, and once it is out the joint is wider than it was before and takes more.

What a joint on a level run wants is a blanket. Fine dust laid into it and worked down, so that the grit off the flags and the next shower of rain sit on the blanket and not in the bedding. Dust on a level run stays where it is put, because there is nothing running along it to take the dust out again.

So she sweeps the faces first, and does it dry.

The film on a flag face is not dust like the joints. It is the fine soil that blows up off the front bed and the green that comes with it and the grit off the path, all of it baked on through a summer and lying in the tooling marks. A stiff brush lifts it in a film that comes away whole where it is thick and in one stroke where it is thin, and it does not come up in the air at all.

That film goes into the pan and the pan goes into the bucket, and there is a great deal of it, because a run of this length carries the dust of a whole summer on its faces and every bit of it is fine and grey and has been through a season.

Her sister is at the passage window with the sash down to the sill and the cloth in her hands, and calls out of the opening once about the dust on the sill outside.

She shuts the window while the band person is still working at the gate end, and goes through to the back of the house with the cloth, and the window stays shut until the brushing is over the house end of the run.

She goes back along the run with the bucket and the short-handled brush and puts the dust into the joints a shallow sweep at a time, drawing it off the pan and letting it fall, and then tapping the back of the brush down the joint so it packs of its own accord.

Worked in, the dust goes down into the joint and stays down. Tipped in from above it stands up in a ridge along the middle and washes out of the joint in the first rain, and she is not doing the second thing.

She works from the gate end towards the house so the last joints she does are the ones nearest the wall, and the dust from the last few pans is the finest, because she has swept the faces twice by then.

The last joint is the one under the house wall and it is a different joint from all the others. There is moss in it, a green strip of it about the length of her hand and the width of a thumb, sitting in the joint and running on under the wall.

She does not take the moss out. Moss in a joint under a wall is not a weed to be pulled, and it is doing a job: it is keeping the bedding under that joint shut and letting the water that runs off the wall sit above it instead of going into it. A joint scoured bare under a wall takes the wall's water in, and it takes it every time it rains, and the bedding under a bare joint by a wall goes soft and settles and the flags over it rock by the spring.

She goes round the moss with the brush and leaves it standing, and the dust goes in either side of it and up to it and stops there.

The test is the back of her hand along the joint afterwards. Where the dust has stayed down below the top of the flag the joint will take rain, and where she can still feel the dish of it under her fingers the bedding is showing and there is not enough dust in it yet. She goes back over three of those with another sweep each and the hand tells her different.

She thinks about the run while she works, how a joint is not a gap between two stones but a piece of ground about the width of a thumb that happens to be in the shade, and shade is what keeps it damp, and damp is what keeps sand sand. Three months of September sun at this house has had the lot of it, and what she is laying back is not mending a gap. It is putting the shade back where it belongs by putting something over it.

The kneeling pad goes hard under her knees before she is a third of the way along, and she turns it, and later folds it in two and goes on.

---

The bucket goes to the compost at the side, and the pan and the brush go in the shed, and the sacking pad is rolled and hung on the gate.

She stands at the gate when the street has gone quiet and looks down at what is there. A run of flags blinded pale with dust lying in every joint below the level of the stones, and one green strip of moss left standing in the joint under the house wall.
