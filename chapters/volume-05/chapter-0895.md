# Chapter 0895 — Behind The Standing Stone

Friday the twenty-second of March 2024, at the heap of field stones along the side of the yard at number twelve while the light is still low on the wall. The gap behind a standing stone.

**SHE HAS A HEAP OF FIELD STONES ALONG THE SIDE OF THE YARD AT NUMBER TWELVE AND SHE MEANS TO PUT THE FINGERS OF ONE HAND UP BEHIND THE BACK EDGE OF ONE STANDING STONE WITH THE PALM TURNED AWAY AND THE TIPS CURLED OVER IT AND HER SISTER IS AT THE BACK GATE COMING IN WITH A BASKET OVER HER ARM.**

The heap has been along that side for longer than the fence beside it, and the fence has been mended twice while the heap has sat where it was put down.

No barrow tipped it there. It is a row of stones laid up by hand, two deep at the foot and one deep at the top, with the faces out and the backs in, and the whole of it about knee high and about six feet long.

She wants the one at the end of the row, where the row turns the corner by the gate. That stone stands up out of the heap with its face to the path and its back to the soil, and there is a gap behind it where the next stone has slipped down.

A gap behind a stone is the one place in a heap where a hand can go and learn something, because the front of a heap shows what was picked up and the back of it shows what it was picked up off.

So the fingers go up behind the back edge of that standing stone, with the palm turned away from it and the tips curled over the top of what is behind.

The stone is cold through the fingertips, the way field stone stays cold until the sun has been on it an hour, and the back of it is rough with lichen on the upper half and smooth underneath where it has rubbed on the next stone.

The fingers go up about two inches and stop on something soft. Not stone. Soil, packed firm between the two stones, with small roots cut clean across where they went in last summer and died back in winter.

She works the tips a little to one side, and then a little to the other, and the soil gives about half an inch under the nails and then stops firm again.

What stops it is another stone. The back of the heap, leaning in the other way, with its face turned into the heap and its back to the earth bank behind.

So the standing stone at the end of the row is not standing on the ground at all. It is standing wedged between the stone in front of it and the stone behind it, with soil packed in the fork of the two, and the soil is what keeps it upright.

Her sister comes in through the back gate from the garden side with a basket over her arm and shuts the gate after her with her foot.

She came down the path with the basket banging on her knee and stood at the end of the heap and looked at what was being done, and after a bit she turned the basket over onto her hip to keep the weight off her arm and stood there a while longer.

She was told the basket was for the cuttings by the shed and that the shed door was open, and she went on down the path with it and left the gate shut.

---

A heap like that is put up for a reason that has nothing to do with building. A field heap is where stone goes when it comes out of a bed, and a yard heap is where it goes when it comes out of a path, and the heap along that side is both.

The stones at the foot of it are flat and wide, the sort that come up when a spade hits them in spring, and the stones at the top are round and hard, the sort that roll out of a bank after rain.

Field stone goes firm in a heap in a way that quarried stone does not. Quarried stone has fresh faces that take water and throw it off, and field stone has old faces with the pores already full of soil, so it sits down into itself and the soil between locks it.

That is why the heap has sat through two fence mendings without being knocked over. The foot of it has sunk about an inch into the earth, and the earth has come up round the foot stones, and the whole row now stands on a footing of its own making.

The gap behind the end stone is about three fingers wide at the top and nothing at the bottom, where the two stones meet on a bed of grit.

Grit gathers where two stones meet and on nothing else, because rain takes the fine soil down through the heap and leaves the coarse stuff where it catches.

The fingers find the ridge of that meeting through the soil if they are pushed, and they can be, which is how the wedge came to sit where it sits.

A stone wedged like that will take a winter. Cold gets into the soil in the fork and lifts it a hair, and the thaw lets it down again, and each year the stone sits a hair lower between its neighbours.

In three winters the end stone has gone down about half an inch, because the paint mark on its face that was level with the next stone is now half an inch below it.

The paint mark is from the day the gate was painted, when the brush touched the stone and left a spot of green on it.

That spot is the only measure on the whole heap, and it says the heap is settling and not spreading. Green paint chalks off in sun within two summers, so the spot fading yet still seen says the mark sits sheltered by the gate post shadow.

The soil in the fork is firm and dark and full of small white roots that have gone soft with winter and will come again in May.

What lives in a heap lives in the back of it. A wren has nested in the middle of that row for two years, and the moss on the north side of the foot stones is thick enough to take a thumbprint.

The wren in the middle of that row and the moss on the north side and the green paint spot on the face leave that stone where it is. A stone caught in a fork with soil packed round it moves only when the soil goes out of the fork, and the soil goes out only when water takes it.

Water off the shed roof runs down the path and along the foot of the heap in wet weather, and it has cut a channel about an inch deep past the third stone from the end.

Past the end stone the channel turns out to the gate, because the end stone stands a little proud of the row and throws the water off.

So the end stone guards its own footing by standing out of line, and the row behind it takes the wash instead.

The timber of the gate is firm and the iron of its hinges is firm, and the stone of the heap is firm where it stands out of the wet.

She takes the hand out from behind the stone and brushes the soil off the fingertips on the palm of the other hand, and looks at the row from the path to see what it shows from the front.

From the front the end stone looks like the tallest stone in the heap, standing a hand above the rest with its face to the light.

From the back it is the shortest, wedged down between its neighbours with only the top of it showing over the soil.

The row gives a tall stone at its end from the path and a low one from the shed side, and the second of those is the truer of the two, because a stone caught between its neighbours with soil packed in the fork of them stays where the three of them were put.

She will leave the heap where it stands through another summer, with the end stone wedged and the soil firm round it, and the row settling a hair each year under its own weight when the thaw comes and the water runs off the shed roof past the foot of it.
