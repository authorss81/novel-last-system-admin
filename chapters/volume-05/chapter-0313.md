# Chapter 0313 — Six Days, Five Rooms, and Nobody

Thursday the thirteenth of April 2017, from about half past ten in the morning, a kitchen at the back of a parade in Tideglass, a shop on that parade, a kitchen in Corrance Lane, a corridor in a building by the water, and a hall off that parade.

**HE WAS IN FIVE ROOMS ON SIX DAYS AND NOBODY PUT ONE QUESTION TO HIM AT ALL IN ANY OF THEM, AND HE SAID SOMETHING TO A WOMAN IN A CORRIDOR ON THE THURSDAY AND SHE SAID NOTHING BACK, AND HE SAID SOMETHING ELSE TO A MAN ON THE TOP DECK OF A BUS AT TEN PAST FOUR, AND THE MAN WENT BACK TO HIS PAPER, AND HE WENT HOME.**

It is six days and not one, and the five rooms are the same five, and the reason for the six days is that the man whose kitchen he does was away from the Monday and the woman who owns that kitchen did not want the man back in there at the end of it, and so the lad was given the Friday as well.

The hot water on that side of the parade comes off a timer and on a Saturday morning the timer is set wrong and the water is not on until about a quarter to nine, and on the Monday and the Tuesday and the Wednesday it was on when he came in at half past seven.

On the Tuesday the woman who runs that shop shut the breakfast at about ten and said, "Chairs up."

He took them up, seven of them, the same seven, and carried them through and set them in a row on edge where they go, and then went back for the eighth one.

He stopped there in the middle of the floor with a chair in each hand and waited to be asked where that one went as well.

"Top of the stack," said the woman who runs that shop, to the till, with her back half round.

He set the eighth one on top of the others, turned the same way up and leaning at the same angle they were all leaning at, and she looked at the pile for a second, and said two hundred years, exactly the way she had said it before, and then said, "There's a bit more on the Saturday if the weather holds."

He said aye.

On the Thursday she said, "They're not doing the delivery on Saturday, so come in at half past eight and not ten."

He said aye, and she said nothing else, and there was a man at the end of the counter with a paper and a pen all morning who did not say one word to him from one end of the day to the other.

He had been in that shop, that kitchen, that hall and that corridor, day after day since the middle of January, and in all of that time two people had asked him one thing each, and he knew them both, and the second of those two questions had been about which of two places he did first.

On the Thursday, going past the end of the table in the hall with the tray for the third time, he stopped for about a second and a half in the gap between that table and the door, on purpose, with the tray in his hands, and the man in his forties was eight foot away and looked up and looked back down at his cup.

Nothing came.

The man who works nights had the ring at the back of that kitchen at about ten at night, and there was a pan at the back of that cupboard, up on the shelf above the lids, none of which it went with, and a code was stamped into the base of it, and the lad saw it there on the Monday and on the Tuesday and did not touch it and said nothing about it to the man who works nights, and that man said nothing to him about it, and neither of them has said one word about it to this day.

In Corrance Lane the man and the woman at that table were the same on all three days and the plates were the same, and on the Wednesday there were more of them, because a man had brought two of his own in off the back, and nobody at that table said one word to the lad about the plates or the man's two or the fact that he had carried them in.

At the building by the water, the first floor, the stop tap had been off from about half past seven on the Saturday until about half past seven on the Monday, and on the Wednesday there were three bottles of washing-up full standing on the landing by the wall, because the woman of about fifty-five had had no water in that building for a day and a half and had done her washing up in what she had carried up.

He came down that corridor at about ten past four on the Wednesday with a mop and a bucket and the bucket was going over the bottom of that landing about two foot from those bottles, and he stopped, and put the bucket down, and carried the three bottles one at a time down to the bin store at the bottom of that stair and put them on the shelf, and came back up and mopped.

The woman of about fifty-five came out of the room at the end of that corridor while he was finishing and said, "I'll be in."

He said aye.

She looked at the landing where the bottles had been.

On the Thursday at ten past three he was on that corridor with the mop, and the woman in the room next door along, counting past the fire door, came out because he was in the way of the fire door with a bucket, and stood against her own frame with her hands in front of her, the way she does.

"There was a blanket in that cupboard," he said. "Under the stairs. The one on the landing, the door of it stands open. There was a fire blanket in it in a bag, and there's a bar of iron under it, and there's a key on a hook on the back of that door."

She did not say anything.

"A man's been and took the blanket out," said the man of about eighteen. "I saw it on the chair outside your door on the Saturday and then on the Monday it wasn't on the chair."

The woman in the room next door along stood against that frame and said nothing at all, and looked at him, and then looked at the wall at the end of that corridor.

"There's a key on the hook as well," he said. "You don't have to lock it. Nobody does."

And she said nothing, and he took his bucket through the fire door and down the stair and out into the daylight, and she drew her own door to behind her so that it stood open the width of a hand.

That blanket is on the end of a bed in that room with the light on, and the woman in that room cannot say a word about it, because the only way she could say a word about it is to say where it went, and she has not told anybody in that building and is not going to.

---

About half past seven that night he was in that hall with the tray in both hands, and he went the round of that room, and every hand at that table came at it and took a cup, and two of those hands said what they wanted in theirs, and the man in his forties was at the long end with his hands flat either side of a cup, and the man in his sixties was at the window, and the man from the parade was sat with a cup held in both hands, and the woman who works in a shop had her chair turned away from the window, and the woman of about fifty-five was in early, and the young woman of about twenty was at the far end and said nothing through all of it.

Nobody asked him anything.

He went round that room four times with that tray and by the fourth time there were about six of them left in that hall, and the fitting above the middle of that table cut out, and neither of the two at the other end of the room went out, and the woman of about forty-two with the key said the meter would want seeing to before the summer, and the man in his forties said it would, and that was the whole of the conversation of that evening.

He went through to the kitchen at about ten past nine and washed that tray out and filled it from the cold tap, because by then the hot had gone, and the clock on that wall runs a quarter of an hour out and has done all winter.

---

The bus up the parade was half full and he got on it at the bottom and went up to the top deck, and a man near the front had a folded paper and was looking at it the way a man looks at a thing he has decided to be bored by.

"That step's still down," said the man of about eighteen.

The man looked up.

"Aye," said the man of about eighteen. "Outside the shop that's shut. At the low end. It's been down since before Christmas and it's not anybody's to be doing and I've not done it and I'm not saying I'm going to."

"It's the one with the lino on it," said the man.

"It is."

"Aye," said the man, and went back to his paper.

**AND A BOY WENT DOWN OFF THAT BUS AT THE STOP AT THE TOP OF THE PARADE WALKING, AND THREE BOTTLES OF WASHING-UP ARE ON A SHELF IN A BIN STORE AT THE BOTTOM OF A STAIR IN A BUILDING BY THE WATER, AND A SAUCEPAN IS BEHIND TWO LIDS ON A SECOND SHELF UNDER A SINK.**
