# Chapter 0611 — The Paper With the Twenty-Eight Names On It

The Monday of the twenty-first of December 2020, from about seven in the morning until about ten at night, in this borough.

**HE HAS BEEN COPYING OUT A WAITING LIST IN A CANTEEN BEFORE ANYBODY ELSE IS IN SINCE BEFORE THE SUMMER, AND IT HAS TWENTY-EIGHT NAMES ON IT IN HIS OWN HAND WITH A LINE UNDER EVERY SINGLE ONE, AND HE PUTS THE PAPER IN THE FILE THIS MORNING AND TOOK IT OUT AGAIN AT TEN O'CLOCK AND IT IS IN HIS COAT.**

He is a man in his mid fifties. He is not in a position to do anything about any of them. What he has is a piece of paper and a habit and half an hour at seven in the morning in a canteen.

---

Here is the paper, and the shape of it is the whole of the chapter.

There is a list of waiting at the place where he works. The list is not his and he cannot change it and he has no part in it, and the only thing he is allowed to do with it is to look at it, and looking at it is part of the job.

He has got into the habit of copying it out.

He copies it on a sheet of A4 from a pad, in his own hand, in a canteen at about seven in the morning before anybody else is in. He writes the twenty-eight names down the left in the same sequence they are on the board, and then he goes back down the column and puts a line under every name.

Not a date. A line. Under every single one, the same line, in the same place, in the same way.

He has been doing that since before the summer.

The reason he started is not noble and he has said so, in his own head, more than once.

He started because the first thing he wrote down, in the June, was a name and a number beside it, and the number was the week it was expected to go through, and he has never in his life been able to keep a sequence of weeks in his head. So he wrote it out. And having written it out, he found that he could not stop.

The reasons he has collected since are these.

One: he is better at copying a thing out than he is at keeping a thing in his head, and that is true of him at work and true of him at home and true of him in a church hall.

Two: a line under a name looks like the beginning of something, and it is not, and he wrote them anyway.

Three: he has never told anybody that he does it.

Here is today.

He was in the canteen at about ten to seven and it was dark and the heating was on and the vending machine was making the noise it makes.

He had the list off the board in his head, which is a thing he is good at. He wrote the twenty-eight names and went down the column and put a line under each one, and he was about halfway through when somebody came in.

That is the thing that has happened once before, in about the September, and it is why he now sits with his back to the door.

The man who came in is in his early twenties and has not been there long and he does the job at the far end and he does not know what a waiting list is.

And he said, "You're in early."

And he said, "I'm always in early."

And the young man said, "You're here before the lights come on."

And he said, "The heating's better early."

And the young man got a tea and a packet of something and sat down at the other end of the table, and after a bit he got up and went out, and the sheet of paper was face down under his cup where he had left it.

He did not think about it for a while. He made a second tea and drank it and looked at the light through the door.

Then he thought about it, and here is what he thought, and it is the thing he has thought about most since the September.

Nobody was going to take it. Nobody was going to read it. There are twenty-eight names on it in his own handwriting and they are the same twenty-eight names that are on a board on the first floor, and the man who came in does not know a single one of them, and the paper was face down under a cup.

And he thought: nothing happened.

And he thought, at about twenty past seven: that is the sentence. Nothing happened. I have been copying this out since the summer and nothing happens.

And he got the sheet and turned it face up and looked at it and the lines were all still under the names and the twenty-eight names are still the twenty-eight names, and the half he had done was as good as the whole.

And he put it face down again and took his coat off the back of the chair and went up.

---

He put the sheet in the file at about ten past seven.

The file is on a shelf in the office and it is a lever-arch file with about six other things in it and he has known for some time that nobody has ever opened it and it is not locked.

He put it in at the back, behind a folder of paper about something that stopped in about 2018.

And he took it out at about ten o'clock.

Here is what is between seven and ten.

He worked. There is a thing he has to do on a Monday morning that takes the best part of a morning and does not need him to think, and he did it well, and a woman in her late twenties who works with him asked him a question about a form at about half past eight and he answered it in a way that made her write it down, and he was pleased about that in a way he did not show.

At about nine he made a tea he did not want.

At about ten he went back to the shelf.

He has thought about the shelf a good deal and he has worked out this about it, and it is the closest thing to a reason he has got.

The file is where a document goes when it is finished.

He is not allowed to finish it. That is not the rule and nobody told him that; the rule is that there is nothing to say to him about the list at all. But a lever-arch file with a thing in the back of it is a thing that has been dealt with, and he knows that is what it looks like from the outside, and he has known it since the first time he did it in the June.

So putting it in the file is not tidiness. It is him, alone, in a canteen with the heating on, putting a piece of paper somewhere that means he has finished with it.

And then he takes it out.

He took it out at about ten and he did not go back to the canteen. He took it back to the office and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, and it is in the inside pocket of his coat now, and he has had the coat on and off four times since and it has been in the pocket every time.

And here is what he has worked out this morning, at about four in the afternoon, and this is the last thing and it is the whole of it.

The reason he cannot put it down is not that it matters.

He would be able to carry it to the end of his life and it would make no difference to a single one of the twenty-eight. He has checked that thought properly, more than once, and it holds.

The reason is that if he stops, then the twenty-eight are a number on a board and nothing else, and he has spent the summer copying them out at close range in his own handwriting and he is not going back to that without getting something in exchange.

He does not know what he wants in exchange for it. He is aware that there is no such arrangement available to him and has been aware of it since the summer.

And he is fifty-odd and he is not going to get anybody else to look at it, and so he is the only person who ever wrote the twenty-eight names out by hand, and if he stops then that stops as well.

He is fifty-odd. He has decided that it can stop whenever. He has not decided when.

He was in the canteen again at about nine in the evening and he had the paper on the table face up and the lines under the twenty-eight names, and he wrote the twenty-eight again at about half past nine on a fresh sheet and put a line under each one and put that one in the file too, and then went and took the first one back out of the file and put it in his coat, and locked up at about ten.

**HE HAS TWENTY-EIGHT NAMES IN HIS OWN HANDING WITH A LINE UNDER EVERY ONE AND HE PUT THE PAPER IN THE FILE AT TEN PAST SEVEN AND TOOK IT OUT AT TEN AND WROTE ANOTHER ONE AT HALF PAST NINE AND PUT THAT IN AS WELL, AND THE FIRST ONE IS IN HIS COAT.**
