# Chapter 0672 — From Inside The Van

Wednesday the sixth of October 2021, in this borough. He is in his van from morning until midday with a load of frames for the estate, and the whole of the morning is a road end he does not turn into.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES STOPS HIS VAN AT THE END OF THAT ROAD ON A WEDNESDAY WITHOUT GETTING OUT, SEES A GATE STILL BACK AGAINST A COAL SHED WALL, KNOWS THAT THE ONE PERSON WHO COULD SEE HIM HAS HER BACK TO THE GLASS, AND DRIVES ON, AND NOBODY SEES HIM.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is on his own all morning. A lad from the estate talks to him for twenty minutes and asks him nothing at all.

---

He leaves the unit with the frames roped and sheeted, and the shoebox with the stove element in it still on top of his own bench where he cannot come in without seeing it, and the under-bench drawer still open with a new brush in a plastic bag flat along the back of it.

He wants to deliver the frames and get back without making the road end into a visit, because getting out would make it a visit, and a visit would take back what the woman said she would do.

He takes the ring road in the early light with the traffic thin, and the van pulls a little to the left on the long straight.

The turning for that road comes up on his right about halfway through the run, with the houses set back and the coal shed walls showing at the ends of the gardens. He went past it once, in the last week of August, and did not slow down, and that was the right call and he made it without any trouble at all.

This morning he slows without meaning to, and then he stops, with the engine running, just past the turning, where he can see down the short road to the side of number twelve without blocking anything.

---

The gate is still back against the wall of the coal shed out of the way. He can see the pale line of it against the dark wall, and the path clear in front of it, and no figure at it and no car on the path and no lights on in the front room.

At the kitchen window under it there is a figure with her back to the glass, small with the distance, not moving much, the way a person stands at a sink. She has not turned round. She has no reason to; the road is empty, and there is nothing on it but a van going slowly past a corner in the early light, and she is a woman of seventy-eight eating breakfast or washing a cup, and a van is a thing that goes past.

He sits with his hands on the wheel and looks for all of half a minute.

What he has is this. The gate is open. She did it. He never found out whether she managed it, because he did not ask, because asking would have been the end of the arrangement, and now there is a man in a white van sitting at the end of her road in daylight, four feet from her gate, and she has her back to the glass and does not know he is there, and nobody else on that road is awake, and nobody on that road is looking.

Nobody sees him. There is a woman at a window eleven yards from a gate she had to strip in August, and she does not see him, and he is going to sit here and let that be the case, and it is the only thing he has wanted since June and he is going to have it.

A bus comes up the road from the far end and goes past, and he can see the tops of the heads on the top deck through the window, and about half of them are looking at nothing, and none of them looks at a white van that is not doing anything at the end of a road.

His right hand is on the door handle. He does not remember putting it there. The handle is cold and it moves a quarter of an inch when he takes the weight off it, and if he took the weight off it properly the door would come open and the noise of it would be enormous in that street at that hour.

He takes his hand off and puts it on the wheel again and puts both hands on the wheel, which is what a man does when he is waiting for a light and is not going anywhere.

If he gets out now he takes that off her and there is no way to put it back.

---

She put her hand over her mouth in a kitchen and said she would do it, and she did it in four hours on the seventh of August with a bad brush that got into the socket, and both palms cut inside, and she never asked him for a penny, and he never put one in front of her, and there is no invoice anywhere in the world for that afternoon and there never will be.

He watches the figure at the window for a few seconds longer, and it occurs to him, sitting there, that he does not know what her face looks like. He has been in her kitchen three times. He could not tell you this morning whether she has glasses.

He puts the van into gear and drives on, and the road end goes out of the mirror in about two hundred yards, and he does not look at it in the mirror afterwards, because looking in a mirror at a thing you have just decided about is a different decision.

---

The frames shift a little behind him and the rope creaks, and he steadies them at the next lights with a hand back over the seat.

He delivers to the estate and helps carry the frames in with a lad from the house, and they stack them in a front room with dust sheets down, and the lad offers tea and he says he will take it.

They drink tea standing up with the frames against the wall. The lad is about nineteen and has been doing this since the summer and is better at the carrying than he is at the talking.

"This lot's for the Rushden place?"

"Rushden place. Eight doors and a pair of frames for the kitchen."

"You doing the whole house?"

"Doors and kitchen. There's a man from a company come in the spring to look at the rest of it, and I don't think they've done anything about it, but there'll be a lot of it here in the spring. There's a fella said something to my dad about it."

"What did he say?"

"About the roof."

"And you know?"

"Everyone knows," the lad says. "Everyone on this estate knows. There's three of us had ceilings come down and nobody's told anybody, because if you ring them and ask they say they'll come out and look, and then they come out and look and then nothing happens for a year."

He hears all of that and puts his cup down on the sill of the frame he has not started.

"Right," he says.

"You'd not ring them?"

"I'd want to see the house first. And I wouldn't ring on my own account with nobody behind me, because then it's me against a company and me's a man on his own with a van."

"You've done the same as us, then."

"No," he says. "I've not done anything. I've had a look and I've a unit and a van and nobody's asked me for a roof, and a man's not a roof."

"That's deep."

"It's not deep. It's not ours." The lad grins. "You want to see it though, the Rushden one? The man would want you to see it. He'd want somebody in the trade to tell him straight."

"I'll see it in the spring when I'm here anyway."

"No, you wouldn't. You'd be busy."

"I'll see it in the spring when I'm here anyway," he says, and finishes the tea, and the lad does not push it, and the two of them stand in a front room with dust sheets down and eight doors leaning against a wall that has a damp patch in the corner nobody has mentioned.

Then the lad asks how long the fitting will take and he says about as long as it takes and not a figure, because he has learned since July not to put a figure on a thing in front of people.

"That's no answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

"Fair enough," the lad says, and looks at the damp patch, and does not ask about it either.

---

He said nothing in a kitchen on the sixteenth of July to a woman who asked him how much is that, and decided it in about four seconds while he was still looking at the drops, and still thinks it was the right thing.

He said about ten minutes for a job that is not ten minutes. He told a woman on the shop telephone on Monday that a cupboard door was about ten minutes, and she came back into it and made it a quarter of an hour, and he let her, and it is still doing him the way a stone in a boot does.

He will not say a figure now unless he has to.

---

He drives back the other way so he does not pass the turning again, and he gets to the unit before midday with the van empty and the ropes coiled.

He puts the canvas bag with the leather bottom and the stone in it in the footwell, with the stranger's brush still in the side pocket since the twentieth of July, and he leaves the under-bench drawer open as it was.

He works the afternoon at the bench on a sill for another house, planing the back true so it sits dead flat, the way a kitchen table at number twelve stands dead flat on a shim planed and sawn flush under the window-side foot.

He works it in a quarter of an hour and then does not put it on the rack, and stands looking at it, and it is the best piece of work he has done in a month and it is going into a house in a street off the ring road that he has never walked down and whose name he does not know.

The lad gave him a card on the way out of the estate, a letting agent's card with a boy on it in a shirt, and said, "That's for the man. He's always in. You ring that and say you're from the joinery side of it and he'll give you a straight answer." He has it in his shirt pocket and he does not know why he is keeping it, because he is not going to ring a letting agent about a roof, and a man who will not ring a letting agent about a roof cannot go into a house and stand in a front room and say the word damp out loud.

He turns the sill over and looks at the face of it, and it is flat enough to put a cup on, and a cup is what you put on a table at number twelve four times a day, and he did that joint with card and no glue in about a quarter of an hour in the summer with a woman watching and neither of them talking, and it has not moved, and he has never been back to see it, and now he cannot go back, because going back would mean the visit, and the visit would take the gate off her.

No Wednesday has been booked. Nobody has asked why the second line is what it is.

The table's leg is back in dry with a card on the joint, no glue. He knows the feel of that joint without touching it, because he saw it from the doorway in July and did not ask who put it there.

And a man who has been in her kitchen three times sat at the end of her road in the morning with the engine running and let a woman at a window stand with her back to the glass, and she has had four hours in August and a man in a van in October and she has had neither one of them in the same day, and he arranged that, deliberately, on purpose, in daylight.

---

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES HAS SEEN FROM INSIDE HIS OWN VAN THAT A GATE HE HAS NOT TOUCHED SINCE JUNE IS STILL STANDING OPEN, AND HAS LET A WOMAN AT A WINDOW GO ON WITH HER BACK TO THE GLASS, AND HAS DRIVEN ON WITHOUT GETTING OUT, AND NOBODY ON THAT ROAD SAW A THING, AND AN OPEN DRAWER WITH A NEW BRUSH IN IT IS STILL OPEN.**
