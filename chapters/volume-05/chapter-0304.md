# Chapter 0304 — A Hard Frost

Friday the tenth of March 2017, from about ten past seven in the morning, a parade of shops in Tideglass, and a hall off it.

**THE BOROUGH DID WHAT A BOROUGH DOES ON A HARD MORNING, AND A BOOK LANDED ON A SHELF OVER BY THE WATER AND CAME OPEN A FINGER'S WIDTH UNDER SOMEBODY'S SLEEVE, AND WHAT WAS IN IT WAS A LAMP WITH ITS BULB OUT, AND NOBODY IN THAT ROOM LOOKED AT IT, AND AT HALF PAST NINE THAT NIGHT A MAN STOOD UP IN A HALL AND SAT DOWN AGAIN.**

There was a hard frost on that parade at about twenty to seven in the morning, the kind that has the sun on one side of every roof and black ice in the shade of every wall, and it had gone by about midday and the boards had come up wet.

The shop that shuts early put its shutter up at about twenty past seven, which is about an hour later than it used to be, and the woman who runs it stood on the pavement in the sun with her hands in her pockets while the roller went, and the radio shop next door had its own shutter half down and a man inside it with a radio on the counter and not the sound up.

The man who comes down with the shovel was at the corner by the bank at about ten past seven and put grit down the gutter side of it and carried on up the parade, and that grit bin at the top of Peverell Street stood there with a crust in the bottom of it that had not been there on Wednesday and that would be gone again by the middle of next week.

At the bottom of Peverell Street a white van with a fridge in the back of it came up level with the second house on the left, stopped, turned round in about thirty foot without reversing at all, and went back down the street, and nobody waved at anybody.

At the end of Marsh Lane a van with no sign on it stood outside a house with its back empty and a tarpaulin folded over the top rail, and it had stood at that end of that lane on about four of the six days of that week.

---

In the building by the water, at about half past ten in the morning, a man and a woman carried a box of files in off a corridor and set it on the desk in the room where the desk is. The shelf with the books on it is on the left of that desk. It takes four feet and it has about sixty books on it and one of them is the register.

He had one end of the box. She had the other.

"Books or behind the door," she said, and put her end down.

"Books," he said.

She set them on the shelf as they came out of the box, which was not any way of doing it, and the register went on third from the end, and the sleeve of her cardigan caught the front edge of it on the way past and pushed it about a finger's width out over the edge of the shelf, so that its last page stood open in the air with nothing holding it.

Nothing has ever been written in the margin of that page. What is written last in that book is a lamp for a yard, its bulb entered under a column headed maintenance, and that bulb went out about November two years ago and has not been changed, and what sits under that entry is the line the paper makes by itself, coming down out from under the writing and going on and then stopping about two inches short of the bottom edge of a board on a book about that deep.

The days between the day that lamp went in and this Friday morning are eight hundred and thirty-five.

Neither of them looked at it. She put the empty box down on the floor and folded the flaps in, and he said the lift was out, and they took the box down the stairs between them and left it in the passage, and she went back up and pushed the shelf door to, which does not latch and has never latched, and the register stood where it stood with that last page open to the air on the edge of the shelf until a woman who was not in that room at about a quarter past ten pushed a binder against it without knowing what was under it.

---

There is a corridor on the first floor of that building and a room at the end of it with the light on in it, and a mark on the inside wall of it about a foot long that has gone the colour of the ceiling, and a door on the inside of which there is a hasp that went on at the end of October.

In the yard under that gable end there is a chair against the wall with a blanket thrown over the back of it, and about six foot away from it there is a bath standing on its side against a wall, and the ground under the chair has dried and gone hard and gone white, and there is rain in the bath on the side that faces out, which is a thing that happens to a bath.

A man came into that yard at about a quarter past two with a bag of tools and went out through the side passage without stopping, and did not go round the back, and did not see either of them.

That hall was full from about half past seven and the meter was on and about nine of them were in it.

The woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall had left her coat on the back of a chair and had been in early. The man in his sixties had the window and a cup of tea in his hands. The man in his forties came in in the middle of the evening with a scrap in his pocket that has a figure on it and that he has not shown to anybody. The woman of about fifty-five was in before the man in his forties. The man who works nights came in still in his coat. The woman who works in a shop sat with her back to the window. The young woman of about twenty was at the far end with her hands round a cup. The man from the parade came early.

Four of them have been going round that table since the twelfth of January and not one of the four has moved since that day, and there is no putting one of them beside another, and nobody in that room has ever been able to say what the four of them have in common except that they are four of them and they are on a table.

The woman who works in a shop swung her chair round and said, "There's four. Nobody's added to it and nobody's took any off it, and the one that came off a street is still the one that came off a street."

"That's the whole of it," said the man in his forties, to the boards of the table.

"It is."

The man in his sixties watched the parade through the glass and said that it was the coldest it had been in that window since about Christmas and that the lamp on the bank was doing something odd with the frost on it, and nobody asked him about the lamp.

At about half past nine the man from the parade stood up.

The young woman of about twenty looked at him. The woman who works in a shop looked at him. The man in his forties set his cup down.

He stood there for about two seconds, and then he sat down again, and picked his cup up, and drank his tea, and said that the tea in that hall went cold in about four minutes every week since the summer and that he did not know how anybody managed to drink it.

"He'll ask it Thursday," said the woman who works in a shop.

"He'll ask it Thursday," said the young woman of about twenty.

The meter ran out at about ten past ten and the padlock was put on it after that, and the chairs went up, and the woman of about fifty-five went out last but one, and the light in that porch was on when the door opened and went off about twenty minutes later with the street lamp outside it.

**AND AT TEN PAST TEN ON THAT FRIDAY MORNING A BINDER WENT AGAINST THE OPEN EDGE OF A BOOK ON A SHELF IN A ROOM BY THE WATER, AND A WOMAN SHUT A DOOR THAT DOES NOT LATCH BEHIND HER, AND THE MAN WHO COMES DOWN WITH A SHOVEL WENT BACK UP THE PARADE WITH AN EMPTY SPADE, AND THE QUESTION IS STILL A QUESTION.**
