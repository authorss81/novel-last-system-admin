# Chapter 0819 — The Knock That Was Not The Draught

Saturday the tenth of June 2023, in his own office cabin at that unit for the whole of that Saturday, and a window frame that has dropped a quarter of an inch and knocks in any wind at all is the whole of it.

**THE WINDOW IN HIS OWN OFFICE CABIN AT THAT UNIT HAS A FRAME THAT HAS DROPPED A QUARTER OF AN INCH AT ONE CORNER AND KNOCKS ON ITS STOP IN ANY WIND, AND HE MEANS TO CLOSE THE GAP, AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN STANDS IN THE OFFICE DOOR IN THE AFTERNOON AND SAYS TWO THINGS THAT ARE ABOUT SOMETHING ELSE.**

That cabin is in the corner of that yard, a timber shed about eight feet by ten with a felt roof and a bench out in the yard and nothing else in the yard at all.

It has one window in the side that looks along the frontage. It is a wooden frame in a masonry opening, an old frame, put in when the cabin was made and left to get on with it for a good many years, with a top-hung leaf on a rebate that closes against a stop.

Since about April the leaf has been knocking.

Not shutting hard. Knocking — a hard flat sound, two or three of them in a gust, and then nothing for a while, and then again. It is the noise a cupboard door makes when it is not quite shut, only louder and worse, and it comes from the top corner of the frame and not from the bottom.

That is the first thing he knows before he has looked at anything, and he has been living in that cabin all day every day and has been putting up with it since about April because it is not a noise you can do anything about quickly and because a man who owns a yard is not going to take a window out for a knocking noise.

---

He starts at about the middle of the morning with the leaf open and his back to it.

The gap at the top is the gap he can see. The leaf has about a quarter of an inch of daylight along the head of the frame at the corner nearest the frontage and not one single thing anywhere else along it.

So that is where the draught is, and that is where he intends to put a strip.

He has a roll of a foam draught excluder in the drawer of that bench out in the yard — a strip about an inch and a half wide and an inch thick, sold by the length for exactly this job, and it goes into a rebate at the outer face of a sash frame and it closes the gap against air in about a minute.

And that is the wrong job and he knows it is the wrong job before he has got it out of the drawer.

A draught in a frame is a gap that is the same all the way round or a gap that is worse at the bottom, because wind comes at a window from below and above and the stop on a top-hung leaf is what stops the air, and air does not care about the top corner.

Air gets in at the bottom of a window. Everybody who has ever put a strip in a sash frame knows it is the bottom corners and not the head that let the cold in.

So this is not a draught. Something has moved.

---

He gets the leaf out of its rebates with a dismantle knife and two blocks of wood and puts it on the bench out in the yard on a blanket.

Then he looks at the frame itself, and there it is.

The frame is fixed with four tabs. Two at the bottom into the sill and two at the head into the lintel above, and the bottom two are solid — you could hang a car off that frame at the bottom.

The head of the frame has one tab in it at the left, going into the lintel, and that one is in sound material and it is tight.

The other one is four inches away, and it is in a hole in the lintel that has a lot more room in it than the tab is.

He puts a finger in it and there is a quarter of an inch of nothing round that tab, all the way round it, where there ought to be brick.

Somebody put a fixing in that hole at some point, and somebody has taken it out, and the tab was left in its hole afterwards without being packed back to the lintel. The tab is still sitting in the opening with nothing behind it. It is the only thing on that head carrying the top of that frame, and it is carrying nothing at all.

The frame has dropped at that corner by however much it has dropped and it has twisted, and the whole weight of the top of the frame is sitting on one tab and on the sill at the bottom.

That is the noise. A frame that is heavy at the top corner and light at the bottom is not draughting, it is chattering, and it chatters when the wind gets behind the leaf and the leaf moves a thousandth of an inch and finds the stop.

He takes the frame out altogether.

Four tabs out, one set of fixings, the frame brought in through the door and laid on trestles in the yard, and the opening left with the felt visible behind it and about a quarter of an inch of night coming through the top corner and about three of the felt going in the yard.

Then he does the part that takes the afternoon.

The lintel at that corner is old brick, and the hole in it is the hole the missing fixing was in, and it goes into a joint.

You do not put a fixing back into a joint in old brick. So the tab comes across — about three inches along the head of the frame, into the middle of the lintel, where there is a whole brick under it — and it is drilled and plugged and screwed properly and squeezed up until the frame is back where it was, square, on both diagonals, with the head tight from end to end.

That is the repair and it takes about an hour.

He has to get at it, because a top-hung leaf on a frame with a quarter of an inch of daylight will put a leaf through the wall behind it as it closes, and there is no frame that is out of square and has a good side to it. So the frame is taken off the bench and cut through the two sides with a saw and the pieces are joined with two lengths of hardwood the width of the frame and four bolts, which is the oldest repair in the world and is the correct one.

The woman from two doors down comes to the office door about the middle of the afternoon and stands in the doorway while he works, and she does not come in and she does not look at the frame twice.

"Have you got a bit of that left over," she tells him. "The blue stuff. For the kitchen."

"There is a bit of it in the box under the bench," he tells her. "Take the whole roll. I am not going to want it."

"You are not," she tells him. "You are going to want it in the autumn."

And she goes, and that is the whole of what passes between the two of them about that Saturday, and neither of them has mentioned the window.

He puts the frame back the same way it came out, and the tabs go back into the lintel including the one he has moved, and the frame is on the lintel at both ends now and the diagonals are equal.

He can feel it before he gets the leaf back in. The frame is firm at that corner where it used to give if he put a thumb on it.

And the leaf still does not shut properly.

He goes back to it about an hour before the light goes and puts the leaf back in its rebates and closes it, and there is about a quarter of an inch of daylight at the head of it all along.

That is correct and he does not touch it.

An old frame in an old opening drops about a quarter of an inch over twenty years and the whole of the industry is built around the fact that it will, and a leaf that shuts hard against a stop is a leaf that will swell in the winter and jam in the summer. That quarter of an inch is the allowance. It is not a fault in the frame and it is not a gap in the frame and it is not going to be packed.

What is not going to be packed is that corner. He leaves it exactly where it came from.

He opens the leaf and looks into the rebate. He has the strip in his hand and he stands there with it.

The whole thing is a strip of foam, a quarter of an inch thick, an inch and a half wide, in a long brown paper packet, and it is the right thing, and it would close that corner and about four inches either side of it against air, and it would do in about a minute with a knife.

And it does nothing at all about the frame, because the frame is not moving any more, because the frame is on the lintel at both ends and the diagonals are equal, and a strip in a sound frame is a thing in a frame.

He puts it back in the drawer under the bench with the rest of it.

What the corner needed was a fixing, and he had the drill out before the middle of the afternoon and that took an hour.

He comes out of that cabin at the end of it and there is a wind coming up the frontage, not a big one, the kind that comes off a roof in the afternoon, and it goes past that building the way it does about twenty times a day.

There is nothing in that window knocking.

Not once. He stands in the yard and listens to it go past about four times on purpose, waiting for it, because he has been waiting for it since April and now he wants to hear it not happen.

The frame is still a quarter of an inch low along the head of it where it has been since before he came, and the leaf is still a leaf on a stop with an allowance in it, and the corner is open about a quarter of an inch and the strip that would close it is in the drawer under the bench.

That corner is not a draught. It was never a draught. It was a frame with one fixing in it instead of two and the wind making a noise off the top of it twice a week in a gust, and he has been standing in that cabin every working day since April listening to a window talk about something it was not talking about.
