# Chapter 1013 — The Last Morning And The One Moment

Undated in this draft.

**THE ROOM BEHIND THE MIDDLE BAY HAS FOUR CHAIRS AND A STOVE IN IT.**

The room behind the middle bay at the lock-up is where the men eat and it is nine feet by seven with a deal table down the middle of it that four men push their knees under, four wooden chairs that do not match each other, a stove in the corner with a flue going up through the roof and a kettle on the top of it, and a hatch in the wall through to the bay that is propped with a length of batten. The shutter on the bay side is down and has been down since the spring. The table top is not square with its own legs, because the leg at the bay end sits about half an inch further out than the one behind it, and every plate ever put on that top has gone towards that gap and stopped there, and there is a groove worn along that line where fifty years of plates have come to rest.

He was at the end of it on the side nearest the hatch, four feet off the head of the table, a good half hour before that yard came alive on the last morning of the season, because the woman at nine had told him to be in that room and he had agreed to it, and he got there early on purpose. The woman who keeps that book came in with it under her arm and the woman at nine behind her, and then the sister from number ten, who had been six weeks on the water and came down it three days ago, and the four of them stood about in there for most of an hour without anybody doing anything at all, and he stood with his fists shut in his pockets and did not sit down.

"She has asked to come off," the woman who keeps that book told them, "and the price has been put to her, and she is here, and she has not moved her mind."

"I have not moved my mind." She put her hand flat on the table by the groove and left it there. "And I would like it read out with me in the room."

"It will be read out." Then to the sister: "You were down there for six weeks and there is work against you that you did not do here."

"I did it. It is in the book because it was asked for."

"It is on that page because you asked for it yourself, which is the only way anything ever gets on it." She opened the book on the table, and from where he stood he could see that the ruled column had three names against it and no more, and two of them were women, and the third was the sister from number ten, and the rest of the page down to the bottom was ruled and empty.

---

Nobody offered anybody a cup of tea. The kettle was on the stove and it had been on the stove since they came in and it was not a thing anybody was going to be offered, and on and off for a decade he had never stood in a room at that yard when a season shut, and it is a room nobody invites a man into.

They read it out. The six weeks first, and then the woman at nine's name, which came off because she had asked for that and not because anybody had taken it off her, and then the woman with the book read the rest of the season's work off that page out loud so that everybody there had it, which is a thing she does at the end of every season and which nobody there had ever thought to describe as reading out.

He had about a minute and a half, and he knew it to a second, because he had stood at that desk four times since the summer and could time that woman any day of the week off his own two hands. He worked the whole of it out twice while she was turning the page. He could put a hand on the edge of that table and ask, in front of the two of them and the woman who keeps that book, for a line against work he has done this season on this frontage. It would be the right ask. It is the only sentence in this borough he has wanted to put to anybody since he was twenty-one and it takes nine words.

He did not do it, and he wants it understood that he did not do it because of the arrangement and not because he lost his nerve at the last moment, which is what a man who is not frightened always tells people afterwards. The arrangement was this. A man who asks in front of other people is a man who wants to be seen asking. She has one column. And the part of it she could not get onto a page about him two days ago was whether he had come up that yard for her or for himself, and asking there would have answered that in front of three women in the wrong direction.

"Go on."

"Nothing."

"You have been standing there for a minute and a half with your hands shut in your pockets and there is nothing," she put across to the woman with the book, "and put that down if you want a thing to put down."

"That will not go on the page."

"I know it will not. I am not after the page. I am after you knowing I was asked for it, because I am the only person in this room who was ever going to ask him for anything, and I did not ask him, and I got a person into this room instead."

What he thought about afterwards, and what he has not been able to put down anywhere, is that the hardest part of it was not the minute and a half. The hardest part was the twenty minutes before, when he stood there and let four women who could all see him not ask anything, and did not explain why he was not asking either, because explaining would have been asking with extra words round it, and the extra words would have been for the woman who keeps that book.

Then he was out in the yard with the shutter down behind him and the weather coming off the bay roof in a run at the kerb, and he crossed the middle of it and stood on the far side and thought about a season closing on that page with three names on it and one name taken off and the whole column ruled all the way down the rest of it. He will be a man who was in the room. And the way a person finds out he is not a name on a page is not by being refused and not by being asked, and it took him the length of a nail to find that out, and it cost him nothing whatever, which is the part he has not got over.

He went back in at the end, an hour after the others had gone, and asked the woman at nine one question, which was whether she would have asked him if she had not found out that he was coming, and she told him no, and then she told him why, which is that she had spent fifty years learning the difference between a man who asks because somebody put him in a room where asking was possible and a man who asks because he has found out where the asking is, and that on that morning she had looked at a man standing at the end of a table with his fists in his pockets who had not asked, and that she knew by then that it was the second sort, and she had asked the wrong man to be in the room.

---

He slid the flat of a palm along that table top towards himself with the grain of it, and the far end came and the near end did not, which is a fault in a table and not a fault in a man, and there is nothing else in there he will ever be allowed to lay a hand on.

That table is still standing where they left it with the four chairs pushed back away from it, and it will be a long time before anybody in that yard knows how it came about that a man stood at the end of it for a minute and a half and asked for nothing.
