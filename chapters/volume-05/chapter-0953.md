# Chapter 0953 — At The Tilted Kerb

Tuesday the twelfth of November 2024, at the kerb at number nine with the wind off the fields. At a tilted kerb.

**A GRANITE KERB RUNS ALONG THE EDGE OF THE GRASS AT NUMBER NINE WITH THE BED UNDER ONE OF ITS ENDS WASHED OUT, SO THAT THE KERB SITS DOWN AT THAT END AND OPENS A WEDGE BENEATH IT, AND THE THUMB PRESSED BACKWARD AT ITS OWN JOINT GOES INTO THAT WEDGE WITH THE FIRST AND SECOND FINGERS SET DOWN BESIDE IT, TO FIND OUT HOW DEEP THE WEDGE GOES AND WHAT IS IN THE BOTTOM OF IT.**

The kerb is the length of that side of the strip and it was laid when the house was built, on a bed of old lime, and it has been walked past and carted past and never lifted since. The end that has dropped is the end nearest the field gate, and it has dropped about a third of an inch.

Which is nothing at all until a hand goes under it, and is the whole of the story of that edge. The kerb is nine inches deep and four inches across the top, and the bed was cut to its full width so that the kerb is not sitting on the ground anywhere along that line at all. The thumb is bent backward at its own joint and pressed into the wedge under the low end.

The first and second fingers are set down on the flags beside it as a check on how wide the hollow is where it goes in. The bend of a thumb is the useful part of a thumb here. A thumb pushed in straight has the width of a nail at its tip and finds nothing at all under a third of an inch of drop, whereas a thumb bent back at its first joint puts the whole pad of it down flat into the gap and takes the depth of it in one go.

The thumb goes in at the level of the flags because a third of an inch is exactly what a thumb's own bend is. The hollow is that deep at the end of the kerb and shuts to nothing about eight inches along, and the fingers on the flags outside the hollow find flag the whole time and no hollow anywhere under the rest of that kerb.

Not one grain of lime is left inside the hollow. What is in there is the lime that was in the bed, washed out and lying as a soft pale powder about a quarter of an inch deep, and below that powder the ground, which is firm and does not come away under a thumbnail.

Behind the kerb is a line of stones set on edge, and they are what the water comes to when it comes off the field, because grass slopes towards a wall and a wall slopes towards a kerb. That line of stones has been taking the run-off of that field since before the house was built and it does the same job now, and the kerb is on the other side of it from the grass and takes nothing of that water.

A kerb is bedded so that it cannot go anywhere, and the bed is what stops the kerb working along its length, where the bars of a wheel bear hardest.

The bed under this one was old lime, and old lime laid thin along the edge of a strip is the softest thing in any kerb line there is. It goes off and it hardens, and once it has hardened it will carry a cart all day, and what it will not do is stand up to water running along the line of it for four months of a wet winter.

That is what happened at this end. The water off the field comes over the grass and finds the lowest line, and the lowest line is the line of the kerb, and it goes along the joint at the kerb's edge and into the bed and takes the lime out of it, and when the lime is gone the bed is a soft line under a beam that has nothing to stop it going down.

The kerb was set out from the gate and worked back along the strip a yard at a time. And the bed was cut for each yard as it went, which is why the bed is under every foot of that kerb and not only under the ends.

Nothing is put back into that bed and nothing is taken out of it. The kerb has simply followed the line it lost, and where the flags have been laid since, they were laid to the kerb and not to the bed.

---

The arris at that end of the kerb is blunt all the way along the last two feet. And at the very end it is chipped away to nothing where a barrow wheel has caught the corner of it, and the granite there is brittle along the chip and goes to powder under the pad of a thumb.

Granite itself is not brittle. A chip of it is hard and grey and takes the print of a thumb and keeps it, and the difference between that and the mortar in an old bed is the difference between a stone and a powder, and a kerb that is chipped at the top arris loses the same shape whichever of the two it was cut from.

The line of set stones behind the kerb goes down into that grass and none of them has moved either. And the kerb has tilted towards the line of stones and not away from them, which is where the water was.

A hollow under the end of a kerb is a wedge and not a gap, because the kerb has turned about one of its long arrises rather than dropped bodily, and the turning is what closes the void within about eight inches. A kerb that had sunk flat on its bed would have opened a space all along its length and would have had to be dug out to get at.

The bed is intact for the other forty feet of that kerb and has not moved a thousandth of an inch. The lime in it is hard and pale and comes away from a trowel as a fine dust and not as a lump, which is what old lime does and what new lime does not.

The woman who does her shopping is standing at the mouth of the lane with the wall of the house at her back and a folded paper bag in one hand, and she is not coming up to the path.

She stands there for a while and looks along the lane towards the field gate and along the front of the wall. And once she shifts her weight from one foot to the other without going any nearer, and she does nothing else at all.

The thumb was back at her side before she reached the mouth of the lane. And the hollow is still a third of an inch deep when the wind takes her along the front of the wall.

The paper bag goes with her and the lane is empty afterwards, and where she stood at the mouth of it the grass is not flattened because she never stood on the grass at all and only on the metalling of the lane, which is not worn by standing at the end of it but by everybody who comes through, and the kerb is at the same angle at the same end of it, and nothing else about the boundary between that strip and that field has altered by a hair in the whole of that.

The kerb keeps its forty feet of true bed and its two feet of blunt arris and its third of an inch at the field end. The thumb finds a third of an inch of nothing, a quarter of an inch of powder, and ground under that.

A wedge of washed lime eight inches long lying in it at the field end of that kerb, with firm ground under that and the very end of the arris gone off at the corner where a barrow wheel caught it. That hollow is left as the water made it, despite every cart on that lane going over the end of it a hundred times a year.
