# Chapter 0550 — Half Past Seven

The Sunday of the first of March 2020, and about two hours of it, in one street of this borough.

**SHE HAS BEEN OUT ON THAT STREET AT EIGHT EVERY MORNING SINCE THE FOURTEENTH OF FEBRUARY AND THERE ARE NINE OF THEM DOING IT NOW AND SHE HAS NOT TOLD ANYBODY IN THAT STREET THAT IT STARTED WITH HER, AND THE MAN WHO IS THERE BEFORE HER HAS NEVER BEEN THERE BEFORE.**

It came down overnight on the Thursday of the thirteenth and by the morning of the Friday there was six inches of it lying unevenly against the walls of the whole borough, more in some streets than in others and more against the walls than in the middle.

The woman in her early forties went out at half past seven with a plastic bucket, a stiff brush and two tubs of salt, and she did the length of her own side of that street, and the salt went down in a stripe about as wide as her boot, and it took her about half an hour.

She did it because her own front door is the one you cannot get through, and because she was up anyway, and because when she had been round once there was still nobody else out.

On the Saturday morning four other people were on that street before eight. By the third week there were nine, and there has never been any arrangement and there has never been a conversation about it, and the only thing anybody has ever said to anybody about it is the sort of thing you shout across a street at seven in the morning, which is about the weather.

The salt is the only thing anybody in that street has spent anything on and nobody has ever divided it.

She buys hers on a Saturday at the place at the end and she carries it back in two trips, because a tub is heavy and she is in her early forties and has a shoulder that nobody in that street knows anything about.

The man in his late sixties has been buying enough for the whole street since the twenty-fourth of February and has not told anybody he is doing it and has not been asked. There are three tubs of it stacked in the recess by the number thirty at eight o'clock every morning, and by the last week there were four of them out before anybody had started, and by then anybody who wanted one had worked out where they were and where they were not.

Two of the nine have tried to pay their own way for it. One of them offered him a five-pound note in the second week and he did not take it and said one word. The other one put a note on the door of the number thirty on the Monday, and it was still there on the Tuesday, and on the Wednesday it was in his outside jacket, and neither of them ever mentioned it again.

In the third week it was cold enough that the salt did very little, and nine people were out at eight, and four of them were out again at about ten. That is the first morning anybody did that street twice.

Nobody arranged the second hour. One of the nine looked at the same stripe at ten and it had not gone, and the other three had looked at it too, and the four of them were simply there.

That is how the tenth one arrived, and he has not been back since, and the man in his late sixties saw him once and said nothing to him, and nobody in that street knows who he is.

Nobody counts. There has been no count of it in that street since the middle of February and there is not going to be one. If you had stood at the end of it on any morning and asked how many people were out, four different people would have given you three different numbers and all three would have been close.

---

He came at about half past seven on the Monday of the twenty-fourth.

He is a man in his late sixties. He lives at the number thirty, which is on the other side of that street and further up, and he has lived there since about 1979, and since that snow nobody in that street had seen him out before nine.

He came at half past seven with a spade that is not his, and he has come at half past seven every morning since, and the two people who were already there when he arrived did not know him either and neither of them said anything.

On the first of March she came at eight, as she comes, with her bucket, and he was about thirty yards up the street with his back to her, working the same stripe, and there were the other seven of them between them and at either end.

She did her own side and then she did the middle, and by about half past eight they were within thirty yards of each other, and they stayed that way until about nine.

She stopped and put her bucket down and said one thing to him.

"They're saying it'll be worse tomorrow."

He did not answer that. He carried on with the stripe he was on, and she picked her bucket up and carried on with hers, and the eight of them finished about nine the way they do.

On the Monday there is another fall and nine of them are there again, and on the Tuesday there is another fall and nine of them are there again, and the stripe goes black by the Wednesday, and nobody in that street has said one word about who started it.

The man in his late sixties works his way down his own side and stops at the step of the number thirty and looks back up the street at what the other nine have done. It is not better than it was on the Friday of the fourteenth. It is black, and it will be black until the end of the month, and there are nine of them, and he has never once asked where it started.
