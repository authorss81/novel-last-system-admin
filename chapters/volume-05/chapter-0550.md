# Chapter 0550 — Salt, and a Man an Hour Early

The Sunday of the first of March 2020, and about two hours of it, in one street of this borough.

**SHE STARTED IT ON THE FIRST MORNING AND BY THE THIRD WEEK THERE WERE NINE OF THEM, AND HE HAS NEVER COME BEFORE AND WAS THERE AN HOUR EARLY, AND SHE CAME AT EIGHT AS SHE ALWAYS DOES, AND THE ONLY THING ANYBODY SAID WAS ABOUT THE WEATHER.**

It came down overnight on the Thursday of the thirteenth of February and by the morning of the Friday there was about six inches of it lying unevenly against the walls of the whole borough, more in some streets than others, and more on the side of the streets than in the middle of them.

The woman in her early forties went out at half past seven with a plastic bucket and a stiff brush and two tubs of salt, and she did the length of her own side of that street, and the salt went down in a stripe about as wide as her boot, and it took her about half an hour.

She did it because her own front door is the one you cannot get through, and because she was up anyway, and because nobody had done any of it by the time she had been round once.

On the Saturday morning four other people were on that street before eight. By the third week there were nine, and there is no arrangement and there has never been a conversation about it, and the only thing anybody has ever said to anybody about it is the sort of thing you say across a street at seven in the morning, which is about the weather.

The salt is the only thing anybody in that street has spent anything on, and nobody has ever divided it.

She buys hers on a Saturday at the place at the end and she carries it back in two trips because a tub is heavy and she is in her early forties and has a bad shoulder that nobody in that street knows about. The man in his late sixties has been buying enough for the whole street since the twenty-fourth and has not told anybody that he is doing it and has not been asked, and there are three tubs of it stacked in the recess by the number thirty at eight o'clock every morning and nobody has ever moved them, and by the last week of February there were four of them out before anybody had started, and by then anybody who wanted one had learned where they were and where they were not.

Two of the nine have tried to pay their own way for it. One of them offered him a five-pound note in the second week and he did not take it and said one word, and the other one put a note on the door of the number thirty on the Monday and it was still there on the Tuesday and it was in his outside jacket by the Wednesday and neither of them ever mentioned it again and that is the end of that as well.

In the third week of February it was cold enough that the salt did very little, and nine people were out at eight, and four of them were out again at about ten, and that is the first morning in a month when anybody did that street twice.

Nobody organised the second hour. One of the nine looked at the same stripe at ten and it had not gone, and the other three had looked at it too, and the four of them were simply there.

That is how the tenth one arrived, and he has not been back since, and the man in his late sixties saw him once and did not say anything to him, and nobody in that street knows who he is.


She does not keep a list. Nobody in that street keeps a list, and she has been asked by nobody whether she is the one who starts it, and she has not told anybody that she starts it, and it began with a bucket and a stripe of salt and no speech.

What is true is that the nine of them do not all turn up at the same time. Two come at half past seven. Three come about ten to eight. The rest come whenever it gets grey, and the woman in her early forties is one of the ones who come at eight, and she has been eight o'clock on every morning since the Friday of the thirteenth of February.

There is no reason for that either.

---

He came at about half past seven on the Monday of the twenty-fourth.

He is a man in his late sixties. He lives at number about thirty, which is on the other side of that street and further up, and he has lived there since about 1979, and in the fortnight since the snow nobody in that street had seen him come out before nine.

He came at half past seven with a spade that is not his and he has now been coming at half past seven every morning since, and the two of the people who were already there when he arrived did not know him either and neither of them said anything.

On the Sunday of the first of March she came at eight as she comes, with her bucket, and he was about thirty yards up the street with his back to her, working the same stripe, and there were the other seven between them and at either end.

Nobody counted anybody. Nobody has counted anybody in that street since the middle of February and nobody is going to, and if you had stood at the end of it that morning and asked how many people were out there, four different people would have given you three different numbers and all three of them would have been close.

She got to him at about a quarter to nine. Neither of them has put anything to the other, and the two of them have worked within thirty yards of each other since the twenty-fourth of February.

"It'll be black by the middle of the week," she said.

He said, "It'll be black by Wednesday."

That is the whole of it. She took her bucket back across the road and he carried on and the eight of them finished about nine o'clock, the way they do, and on the Monday there is a second fall and nine of them are there again, and on the Tuesday there is a second fall as well and nine of them are there again.

**SHE HAS DONE IT EVERY MORNING SINCE THE THIRTEENTH OF FEBRUARY AT EIGHT AND HE HAS DONE IT AT HALF PAST SEVEN SINCE THE TWENTY-FOURTH, AND THE ONLY SENTENCE THAT HAS PASSED BETWEEN THEM IN THE SIX DAYS SINCE HE STARTED IS ABOUT WHEN THE SALT WILL BE BLACK, AND THE STREET IS NOT BETTER AND IS NOT WORSE AND NOBODY HAS SPOKEN ABOUT IT.**
