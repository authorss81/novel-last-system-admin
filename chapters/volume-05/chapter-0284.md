# Chapter 0284 — Everything As It Was

Friday the thirtieth of December 2016, from about ten past ten in the morning, a building by the water in Tideglass, a parade of shops, the low end of Peverell Street, and a hall off the parade.

**SOMEBODY WORKED OUT A NUMBER IN A BUILDING BY THE WATER ON FRIDAY MORNING AND IT IS STILL NOT WRITTEN DOWN ANYWHERE, AND AT A TABLE THREE ANSWERS WENT ROUND AND NOBODY IN THAT ROOM CAN COMPARE THEM, AND NOBODY IN THIS BOROUGH PUT A NAME TO ANYTHING IN THE LAST WEEK BEFORE THE YEAR TURNED.**

Nell Ardent went into the building by the water at about ten past ten because there was a line out of the register she wanted for a form of her own, for a job, and she wrote the line out on the form and squared the paper against the edge of the desk and shut the book.

She opened it again straight away, because she does that. Her finger went down the column to the bottom of the written part and stopped where it stops, and the last thing written in that book is a yard light, and the bulb in it is in a column headed maintenance, and it is out and has been out since about November two years ago.

Out from under that last line there is a ruled line that runs on towards the foot of the page. The book is about two inches deep and that line stops about two inches short of the edge of the board. She did the sum on the corner of the form, in the white space she had left, with the butt end of the pen, and it came to seven hundred and sixty-five days.

She shut the book and shelved it again among the others where it lives, and the register did not move. The margin opposite that last line is empty. It has been empty for about two years and there is still no name in it.

---

The parade shut early that week. The shop that shuts early put its shutter down at one on the Friday and it did not go up again until the fourth of January, and four other shops on that parade went to about the same hour on the Friday, and the boards went up over the windows on the Saturday morning, a man in a van with three sheets of hardboard and a ladder, and the boards went over the bank and the shop that sells radios and the shop with the trade counter, and the woman who runs that shop stood on the pavement and watched him put one over her window and did not go inside and did not help and did not go away.

At about half past ten the man in his thirties came up the parade with a piece of batten over his shoulder and stopped outside a shutter and stood there a minute and then went on down the parade to a door that was open, and he was let in and he was in there until about two.

At the top of Marsh Lane a bin came out at about a quarter to twelve and went down to the end of the road and was brought back, and at the bottom of Peverell Street a man put grit at the corner by the bank with a shovel, and did not go up that street at all, because that street is not on his round and never has been, and there is a light on in the front room of number forty-one and the door is shut and the letterbox on that door has still got a soft leaflet in it from the summer.

What was left on the first floor of that building was cleared out on the Tuesday and the keys went back on the Friday.

Two men carried an office chair and a metal desk out of the room at the end of the first-floor corridor and put them on the landing by the fire door, and then carried them down and out and set them down by the bin store, and they did not say one word to each other about any of it, and neither of them was asked to do any of it and neither of them knocked on a single door on that floor, and one of them said to the other as they came past the corridor that there was still somebody up there, and the other said, "Aye," and they went down the stairs.

The woman of about fifty-five is in a room at the end of that corridor on a month, and there is a hasp on the door of that room and it went on at the end of October, and she was in it with the light on under the door, and neither of the two men knocked and neither of them had any idea it was there and neither of them was going to go and find out.

The man in his thirties had a job on at about half past three on the Friday, cutting a shelf out of a cupboard in a house in Corrance Lane, and he was on the pavement outside it with a jigsaw on a battery that was not charged when he got there.

A van came off the bridge behind him at about a quarter to four and stopped, and a man in it put a ladder out of the back and a coil of rope and went into a passage, and the man in his thirties said, to nobody, that that was the third ladder in a week, and the man in the van said, "Ay," and went in, and that was the whole of it.

The hall off that parade was full at about half past seven, the meter on and nobody letting out for the night, and there were nine of them in it, and not one of them had come out for any reason you could put on a piece of paper.

The man from the parade came early. The man in his forties turned up late and stayed about an hour, as he does. The woman with the key had her coat over a chair before her bag was out of her hand. The man in his sixties had a cup of tea and spent the first part of the night looking out of the window at the parade, which is what he has done all winter. The woman of about fifty-five was in by about ten past eight with her bag over her shoulder. The young woman of about twenty came off the bus.

They did the price of a boiler, a bank holiday that had come off at the last minute for no reason anybody in that room could give, and a gate on a lane at the top of a street that two people had stood in front of in the cold in December and had not written one word down.

At about a quarter to nine, at the table under the window at the end, a woman who works in a shop asked whether anybody had heard the register one, and a woman beside her said that she had, and that her aunt had it off a man off a lorry in July and that she had never once asked her aunt where she got it from, and that it was the oldest dated thing the borough keeps.

"There's two of them going," said a woman who works in a parade in a place off Corrance Lane with a name on it. "Somebody's been saying the day's going to be the one. The twenty-eighth of June. That's a good deal better than the register."

"The day's better than the register," said the woman beside her, and gave it across the table without being asked, the way she gave it in October.

"There's a third one going about as well," said the woman who works in a shop, and looked round to see whether anybody had heard the third one, and there was a pause of about a second at that table, because there had been a third one in that hall on the sixth with about eight of them in it, and it had come back now with a shape on it that it had not had then, and nobody at that table could say who had put the shape on it.

"It's not ours," she said. "It'll be one of theirs. Somebody over that water will open a drawer in a building they have never been inside and find a sheet with our postcode on the top of it, and that will be the one, and the lot of us will be moved or dead before anybody comes and tells us what it was."

"Is that a new one?"

"That's what I mean," said the woman who works in a shop. "It's newer than the other two and it's worse than the other two and it's about the only one of the three that doesn't want anything off anybody, and I'd not bother arguing with it. You'd not be able to get up off a chair about that one."

The man from the parade put his cup down on the table, and said, "Right," and then said, "Well. Which of the three of them is it, then."

He put his hand up after that and said that somebody in that room ought to know by now, and four people at that table looked at the boards of the table, and nobody said anything, and he put his hand down and drank his tea.

Nobody at that table knew. The woman who works in a shop said the third one had come to her off a woman on her own shop floor and that she had not asked where it was from, and the woman who works in a parade said that she had had the day off a woman in her own shop, and the woman who works in a shop said that the register one was the borough's own book and the day was a day, and no one of them had heard the other two said the same way twice, and there was no way of putting them side by side, because they were not about the same thing.

The young woman of about twenty was at that table and said nothing at all through any of it, and drank her tea.

The man in his forties said, at about ten to ten, to the woman of about fifty-five, that she was not long on that room.

"A month," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"Right," said the man in his forties, and went back to the price of the boiler.

Nobody at that table said a word about the month after that or about what came at the end of it, and nobody asked her whether she had anywhere, and nobody in that room had anywhere to put her and nobody offered her anything of any kind.

At about ten past ten the gas was shut off at that meter and the padlock went on after it, and the chairs went up, and the man in his forties carried six of them, and the man in his sixties had carried three and had gone at half past nine.

**AND AT THE BACK OF A PARADE A LAD OF ABOUT EIGHTEEN HAD THE LAST OF A BREAKFAST ON THE DINES AND WENT HOME AT HALF PAST EIGHT DOWN A DARK PARADE, AND A MAN WENT ALONG THAT PARADE AT ABOUT HALF PAST EIGHT PUTTING THE SHUTTERS DOWN WHERE THEY HAD NOT ALREADY BEEN PUT DOWN, AND THE METER WENT ON TICKING WITH THE ROOM EMPTY FOR AN HOUR AFTER THE LAST PERSON WENT, AND NOBODY PUT A COIN IN IT.**
