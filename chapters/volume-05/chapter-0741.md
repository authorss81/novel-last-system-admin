# Chapter 0741 — Two Things Said In One Sentence

Tuesday the twelfth of July 2022, in this borough. He is in his own unit from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a drawer under a bench that has been shut since November and a run that has been one figure in his head since March and turns out to be two.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES OPENS THE DRAWER UNDER HIS OWN BENCH FOR THE FIRST TIME SINCE NOVEMBER AND TAKES OUT A SQUARE AND LEAVES THE BAG WITH THE BRUSH IN IT SHUT ON THE BOTTOM OF THE DRAWER, PUTS THE SQUARE ACROSS THE FACE OF A SIX FOOT RUN AND FINDS THE FRAME TRUE, PUTS THE TAPE ALONG THE TOP OF IT AND GETS SIX FOOT AND A QUARTER INCH, PUTS THE TAPE ALONG THE BOTTOM OF IT AND GETS SIX FOOT, SAYS BOTH OF THOSE OUT LOUD TO A LAD WITH A VAN IN ONE SENTENCE, AND PUTS THE SQUARE BACK WITHOUT WRITING EITHER OF THEM DOWN.**

He is a man in his early forties in his own unit on Tuesday. A lad with a van comes to the roller door in the morning and he speaks to him out there, with the tape and the square out in the doorway.

---

The drawer under the bench has been shut since November and the reason it is shut is in it.

He does not think about the reason. He thinks about the square, and the square is in the drawer with the reason, and that is the arrangement he made in November when he put the brush in a bag and shut the drawer on it and has not had a reason to open it since.

He opens it at about the middle of the morning with the roller door up and the light on.

The bag is on the bottom of the drawer with the brush in it, and the bag is knotted at the neck and the knot has been pulled out flat, and the bag is where he put it, and he does not touch the bag at all.

The square is on the top of the drawer under a piece of newspaper and he takes the square out and shuts the drawer on the bag and puts his hand flat on the oak front for a second, which is a thing he has not done since November and does not decide to do.

---

He goes to the run on the trestles and puts the square across the face of it, at the hanging end first.

The square is a wooden one with a blade in it and the blade has not been reset since the winter, and it reads true. He moves it along the face a foot at a time and it reads true at the hanging end and true in the middle and true at the shutting end.

Then he puts the tape along the top of the run, because that is the order he has done it in since March and because the top is the edge you can see and get at without turning anything over.

Six foot and a quarter inch.

He does it again at the other side at the top, because the top is the one edge and there is only one of it, and it is six foot and a quarter inch there too, and he writes nothing, because there is a shoebox on the bench and a scrap of scribing card in his pocket with a twelve on one side and room on the other, and a quarter inch is not the kind of thing that goes on a card.

---

The lad with a van comes to the roller door and stands in the light with his hands in his pockets.

"Are those the gates you make," the lad says.

"That is what they are."

"My dad has a gate that will not shut."

"Then your gate is not the one that has stopped it."

The lad comes into the doorway and looks down the bench at the two gates leaning along the wall.

"You could come and look at it."

"I could," he says. "I do not hang them either. Hanging wants two of us and there is one of me, and I am not doing roofs, and I told a man that in a yard in the spring and I have not got better at it."

"You told me that in the yard in March."

"I told a man that in the yard in March," he says. "And you have been in that yard."

"I have."

"Then you knew what you were getting."

He picked up the tape off the trestle and turned it over in his hand.

"I make gates," he says. "I measure frames. And I tell people what their frame is, which is a thing nobody else in this trade will sit down and do."

---

He took the square and the tape out into the doorway, which he does not usually do, and put the square across the face of the run where the lad can see it.

"That is a six foot run," he says.

"It looks about it."

"It is true," he says. "It is true at the hanging end and true in the middle and true at the shutting end. I have not had to reset the blade since February and it has not wanted resetting. That frame is the straightest thing I have put my hands on this year."

"And?"

"And it is a quarter inch over six foot at the top," he says, "and it has been a quarter inch over at the top since March, and I have been saying it that way to everybody including myself, and there is one half of it I have never looked at."

---

He went down on one knee at the bottom end of the run with the tape and put it along the bottom edge, at the underside, on the flat where the frame had been squared off at the ends.

Six foot.

He put the tape back along the top to be sure. Six foot and a quarter inch.

Then he did it at the middle, top and bottom, and it was the same at both.

"It is out of square," he says. "It is six foot and a quarter inch along the top of it from end to end and it is six foot along the bottom of it from end to end, and the two are not the same, and a piece of wood like that is not a quarter inch too long. It is a quarter inch out of square the whole way along, and it has been since March, and I have been carrying one figure about for four months and it was half of one."

---

The lad nodded at it and then at the two ends of the run.

"That is two things," the lad says. "I have only ever heard you say one."

"I know," he says. "That is what I have been doing."

He stood up off his knee and looked down the length of the frame the way he looks down the length of one of his own gates.

"A gate on that," he says, "closes square at one stile and stands a quarter inch proud at the other, every time, in every weather, and nobody would ever find out which stile was the fault because they would all go looking at the gate, and it is the frame, and it has been the frame since the spring."

"And you cannot shave it."

"I can shave it in half and have two frames that are each a sixteenth out," he says, "which is worse, and it is not my frame, and it has to be true before anybody hangs anything on it. The frame is the man's. He has had it since the spring and he has not known, and he will find it at the fitting the way he finds everything, and he will not mind."

---

He took the tape and the square back in and put the pencil on the bench where he had put it before, and did not pick it up.

He could tell the man today. He would tell him this afternoon if he telephoned, which he will not do, because a figure that has been in somebody's head for four months is a different thing from a figure that has been put in a man's ear in a doorway in July, and the second one goes straight out of that man's head and into a builder's, and there is no way of calling it back.

"He finds it at the fitting," he says. "He always finds it at the fitting and he has never once minded."

---

The lad went off up the lane with no work, which neither of them minded by the end.

He opened the drawer under the bench again and put the square in on top of the newspaper and shut it, with the bag and the brush in it knotted at the neck on the bottom where they had been all the time, and the roller door went down, and the run stood on its trestles in the dark being true on the face of it and being six foot and a quarter inch along the top and six foot along the bottom, which is one piece of timber and two numbers and no way at all of writing down the first without the second.
