# Chapter 0806 — The Duct Under The Floor

Monday the tenth of April 2023, in this borough. The whole of that Monday is spent at the side of her own house at number ten, and a duct under the floor of her own extension is the whole of what it is about.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES HAS AN AIR DUCT UNDER THE FLOOR OF HER OWN EXTENSION PACKED SOLID SINCE BEFORE SHE CAME AND MEANS TO ROD IT OUT FROM THE HALL END, HER SISTER STARTS AT THE FAR END OF THE SAME DUCT ON THE SAME DAY WITH A DIFFERENT ROD, AND WHERE THEY MEET EACH HAS PULLED OUT A DIFFERENT THING AND THE WHOLE LENGTH BETWEEN THEM IS CLEAR, AND NEITHER OF THEM EVER FINDS OUT.**

Number ten, on that Monday. She is in her early thirties. The extension runs out across the back of that house and its floor is boarded, and under those boards there is a square air duct about nine inches across, built of bricks on edge with a lid of slate on it, that lets air under a suspended floor that has had no air under it since the week it was laid.

---

The duct runs from a cast-iron grille in the wall at the hall end of the extension to a brick headwall at the far end, where it comes up out of the ground into an opening about three feet above the floor with a loose slate leaning against the brickwork.

The grille has a mesh in it that is furred up with dust and paint. She has known since the summer she came that the boards over that duct give differently under a heel than the boards over nothing, and that the extension is colder along that line in a hard frost than it is anywhere else in it, and that the corner of the ceiling above it has been damp twice.

---

The reason it is packed is that it was packed by whoever built the extension, and they packed it on purpose, and there was no reason for them to do it other than that the arisings from the footings were in their hands and the duct was in the ground and nobody was going to cart a barrowload of broken brick two hundred yards for the privilege of a hole in a floor.

So she is going to take the boards up at the hall end and get a coil of flexible rodding into it and pull that arisings out through the length of it.

The coil is on a drum and it is about four yards of it and it is the thing she bought in her second winter here for a different job and never used for that job.

---

She starts at ten in the morning and she has the boards up by eleven, three of them out of the run nearest the house, and the first thing she sees is that the duct has no lid on it. The slate went years ago. There is a lip of slate edge in the ground where it used to be and about four inches of air above the dirt in the bottom of the duct, which is as much as was ever built into it.

She cuts the earth off the bottom with the trowel and gets the coil in on its weight, a bag of sand tied in an old sock, and it goes in about a foot and stops.

The rodding comes back out on the next pull with brick on it. Broken brick, small stuff, the size of a fist, and mortar in with it that has gone to sand, and she works that out into the corner of the room where the boards are stacked and starts again.

---

It is not pleasant work and it is not fast.

She gets about four feet along the duct by the middle of the afternoon, working the coil in eighteen inches at a time, and each time it stops she lets it out a foot, turns it, and goes in again on a different line, and every third or fourth pull the coil brings a lump out with it.

What comes out is what a duct under a floor in a house that has had a fire in it and a plumbing leak and a family in it gets. Broken brick. Mortar. A length of chicken wire with plaster stuck to it. The lid of a jar. A plaster bat the size of a dinner plate with the paper still on the back of it. And about two feet of old roofing felt lying flat along the bottom of the duct with a bird's nest of two seasons built on top of it.

---

Around three in the afternoon the coil stops and will not go again.

It stops at a place where the duct narrows, and there is something wedged in the narrowing that will not come past with a coil, and she can feel it with the coil and she cannot get a grip on it, and she stands on the boards in the extension with a drum of rodding in her two hands and a bag of sand on a string going down a hole in the floor.

There is a length of duct about four feet ahead of her that is clear and there is a length behind her that is clear, and the whole of the middle is a wad of it about two hands across.

She puts the board back. She puts the three boards back the way they were, with the nail marks lined up and nothing of the coil left down there, and she coils it back onto the drum and carries the drum out to the shed and hangs it on the wall.

---

Her sister comes through the side door at the end of the afternoon.

She has come for a length of scaffold tube she left in this shed in the spring, and she stands in the middle of the extension looking about for it, and she says the one about the boards, which are down and in the middle of the floor with a saw mark across them.

The woman of the house is not in the room. She is in the kitchen with the door open and she does not go out to her for anything.

---

The sister finds the tube behind the door and comes back out into the extension with it in her hand, and puts her foot on the boards near the far wall to turn round, and the boards there go down about an inch and stay down.

She takes her foot off and looks at them.

She has knelt down and put her thumb and her forefinger either side of a board and lifted it, and it comes up about the thickness of a coin on the nail heads and there is a square hole in the ground under it about nine inches across with brick courses round it.

That is the far end of the duct. It comes up out of the ground under that board and runs away under the floor toward the house, and there is a slack coil of steel rodding lying in the bottom of it that she does not see because it is dark and she is looking at her own hand.

---

She gets a screwdriver and lifts the slate lip off the dirt, and she has a length of three-inch scaffold tube in her hand.

You do not need anything else. A length of tube pushed into a hole in a floor is a rod, and this one is stiff enough to push through a wad that a coil of flexible rodding bends round, and she gets it in up to her knuckles and leans on it.

It goes four feet, and then it stops.

She works it the way anybody works a bar that has found something. She lifts and brings it back and turns the end of it a quarter and puts it down again, and after about twenty minutes of that the lump comes apart and the tube goes on another three feet, and she feels it go past the place where it narrows and into open duct.

After that it is nothing. She gets the tube in as far as her arm will reach, and then a bit further, and she can feel it move freely under the floor for the length of the room and out the other side into whatever is under the hall-end boards.

She is astonished at it. She tells herself afterward that scaffold tube is a better rod than a coil of flexible wire and that anybody who has tried to push a coil into a packed duct has wasted their afternoon.

---

What she gets out is different.

What the coil brought was building rubbish. What the tube brings is the felt and the nest and the plaster, all of it lying loose along the length of the duct where the rod had shaken it off the walls on its way in, and it comes out in lengths rather than lumps, and there is a length of it about two feet long with the bird's nest in the middle of it that comes up the duct and out of the hole whole.

And at the end there is the point of the tube coming out through the board at the hall end of the room, which is three boards down and has been three boards down since before the morning.

---

She drops a coin of grit and a boot lace and an old nail into the hole to stop the floor complaining about it, and puts the board back down on the nails, and puts her weight on it and it does not go.

Then she stands in the middle of the extension with a length of scaffold tube in one hand.

There is a pad of paper on the window sill with a pencil on it and a bit of the top sheet torn off square at the corner. She tears another strip about as wide as two fingers, has it at the open end of the duct about an inch above the boards, and lets go.

It does not lie there. It goes down into the duct and is not seen again.

She does that four or five times, and every time the paper goes, and the boards under her feet do not feel any different at all, which is what a duct with air in it feels like from the top.

She puts the board down properly with all four nails in it and stands the tube against the wall by the side door where she came in, and goes out through the side door and across the back of the house and out at the yard gate.

The woman of the house is still in the kitchen. The drum of rodding is on the shed wall with the four yards of it coiled back on it. The drawer in the shed with the hammer and the screwdriver and the paper in it is shut, and the ply is leaning against the front of that shed with nothing driven into it, and the felt is still on the shed with its corner up, and nothing in that yard has been touched except a length of tube that came out of it and one board of a floor.
