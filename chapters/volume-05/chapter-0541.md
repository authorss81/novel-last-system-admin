# Chapter 0541 — An Hour Before Her

The Monday of the thirteenth of January 2020, from six in the evening until about twenty to ten at night, on the ground floor of a block of flats on the west side of this borough.

**SHE HAS BEEN IN THAT FRONT PART EVERY MONDAY EVENING SINCE THE JANUARY OF 2012 AND SHE DOES NOT LIVE IN THAT BUILDING AND HAS NEVER LIVED IN IT, AND SINCE THE FIRST WEEK OF NOVEMBER TWO PEOPLE WHO DO LIVE IN IT HAVE BEEN ARRIVING AN HOUR BEFORE HER, AND SHE CAME EARLIER HERSELF TWICE, AND ON TONIGHT ALL THREE OF THEM WERE THERE AT THE SAME TIME FOR THE FIRST TIME.**

That front part of that building is where the post goes, and where there is a set that a man in his eighties has had in there since long before anybody now living in that building was asked about it, and two long low seats that came from somewhere else and do not match.

The programme starts at nine and ends at ten and has been on at nine on a Monday for longer than the building has been on that road. That is not a thing she checked. She has been going since the January of 2012 and has never once looked up what it was called and would not be able to say.

She is a woman in her mid sixties. She lives alone in a flat about half an hour's walk away and she does not want to be in it on a Monday evening, and that has been the whole of the reason since the beginning and she has never given it to anybody either.

She walks in at about quarter to nine. She has walked in at about quarter to nine for eight years and one month. She knows the door and she knows the time and she knows that by then there will be at least one person, and there has been at least one person since she started going.

There was a period of four or five years when there were four or five. That ended in about 2017 and it ended the way those things end, which is slowly and without anybody deciding.

There is one man left. He is in his mid eighties and he has been in that building since about 1988 and he is the one who put the set there, or his wife did before she died in 2015, and he has not in eight years said to her why she comes and she has not in eight years said to him why she does not live there.



The change came in November and neither of them noticed it for about five weeks.

He was in there. She came in at quarter to nine and he was at the end of the long seat with the programme on and the sound low, and there were two people on the other seat.

A man in his mid seventies from the second floor, and a woman in her early eighties from the top floor.

She said good evening. They said good evening. He moved up about a foot so that she could sit down, which is what he has done for years, and the programme went on and nobody mentioned that there was anybody there at all.

She stayed for the hour. She went at ten, as she goes. She walked home and she thought about it the whole way.

She went back the following Monday and it was the same, and the one after, and it was the same for six weeks, and by the middle of December she had worked out what she was looking at, which was that two people who live in that building and have never in eight years come out before nine o'clock were coming out at about half past seven.



What she did about it was twice, and neither of the two times was clever.

On the Wednesday before Christmas, which was not a Monday and which she had not told anybody about, she was in that building at about seven for a reason that was not a reason, which was that she was in the borough and it was cold and that front part of the building is warmer than her flat.

The door was not shut. She went in. It was empty. The set was off and there was nothing burning in there at all except a small nightlight on a stand, and the two seats.

She stood about ten minutes and she turned the set on and she watched about half of a programme she had not come for, and then she turned it off again and went out and walked back.

She did it once more on the Sunday the fifth of January. That one she went in at about twenty to eight with an hour and a half to sit through, on the theory that she had got the time wrong in December.

It was empty again. It was colder than her flat.

She went home again and she was absolutely certain of it on the way, that there was nothing whatever in any of that, and that she would not do it a third time.

So she went back to quarter to nine, which is where she has gone for eight years, and which is where she went on the sixth and on the thirteenth.



What neither of the other two has done is mention it either.

The man in his mid eighties has gone up on a Monday evening for about two years now and has not once come back down early. He has asked her no questions about why she comes, and he has never asked the woman in her early eighties anything at all, and on the thirteenth of January, when she said, "You have for a bit," he said nothing, and she did not repeat it.

Whether either of them has worked out that the other comes at half past seven on Mondays is a question that is not going to get asked, and it has not been asked, and she has had a month to work on whether to raise it herself and has decided twice not to.

What she decided, on the Wednesday before Christmas, standing in that empty front part with a set she had turned on for nobody, is this. She decided that if she had come early three times and found somebody, she would have to decide what she was doing there at half past seven on a Monday, and that there is no answer to that which is better than the one she has now.

And what she has now is that she comes at quarter to nine, and if there is anybody there when she arrives then there is, and if there is nobody there then there is nobody there, and it has not been a thing that either of them has to account for to anybody.

The programme ends at ten. She has known that for eight years without ever having checked it, in the way that people know things about a programme they did not begin watching.

On the thirteenth of January there were two people on the far seat when she opened the door.

The set was on. The sound was low. The man in his mid eighties was at the end of the near seat with his legs crossed and the woman in her early eighties was on the far seat at the other end with a folded cardigan across her lap, which she did not need, because she lived directly above them.

They said good evening. She said good evening. He moved up about a foot.

The programme went on. Nobody mentioned that there were three of them and that it was the first time.

She has been in that front part on four hundred-odd Mondays and this is the only occasion on which she has sat down and there has been space for exactly one move.

The hour passed. At about half way through the man in his mid eighties said, "Same one."

And the woman in her early eighties said, "It's the same one," and looked at her and said, "You come every Monday, don't you."

And she said, "I do."

And the woman said, "You have for a bit."

And she said, "Eight years."

And the woman in her early eighties said, "Well," which is the end of a sentence she did not finish, and the man in his mid eighties said, "You'd think of something," meaning the woman, meaning what she was getting at, and then he turned the sound up about two notches and that was the end of it.

At about ten the man in his mid eighties got up and got his jacket and said good night to the two of them, which he has done for eight years, and went up. The woman in her early eighties sat for another twenty minutes and then said, "It is not on until Monday," and went on, which was not true and which nobody took any notice of.

She went at ten, as she goes.



What she has taken away from it is not a thing she has told anybody and is not a thing she would put in a sentence if anybody asked her tonight.

What she has taken away is that for weeks she thought she was the last one in that building to want to be out of their own flat on a Monday evening, and that for ten weeks she went in at quarter to nine and found two people already sitting there in the dark, and she said good evening to them as if it were the ordinary thing and it was not the ordinary thing at all.

She has not asked either of them why they come early and she is not going to. They will be there next Monday and she will be there at quarter to nine, and if it is eight o'clock instead then it will be eight o'clock instead.

**SHE HAS COME IN AT QUARTER TO NINE EVERY MONDAY SINCE 2012, AND FOR TEN WEEKS SHE HAS FOUND TWO PEOPLE WHO HAD BEEN THERE SINCE HALF PAST SEVEN, AND SHE CAME EARLY HERSELF TWICE TO A COLD EMPTY FLOOR AND WENT AWAY AGAIN, AND TONIGHT SHE CAME AT HER OWN TIME AND SAT DOWN AND STAYED AN HOUR, AND SHE IS NOT GOING TO ASK.**
