# Chapter 0679 — On The Low Wall Of Her Own Garden

Wednesday the third of November 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house at number nine from dark until late with her sister at the kitchen table, and the whole of the evening is a taped parcel on the low wall of her own back garden with a number on it and no street.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES FINDS A TAPED PARCEL ON THE BACK WALL OF HER OWN GARDEN, BRINGS IT INTO A HOUSE SHE HAS LIVED IN ALL HER LIFE, PUTS IT IN THE FRONT ROOM UNDER A STOOL NOBODY SITS ON BECAUSE THAT IS WHERE SHE DOES NOT HAVE TO LOOK AT THINGS, AND DOES NOT GO UP THAT ROAD.**

She is a woman in her late fifties. She is at home all night. Her sister is at the kitchen table for most of it.

---

She comes in from lates late with her coat damp and her shoes loud on the tiles, and her sister looks up from the kitchen table with a mug and a form and a pen in a clip.

"How bad was it?"

"Bad enough. Two of them in."

"Two?"

"And a man who hadn't booked, which was the best of it, because he was the only one who said he was sorry about the parking."

She hangs her coat on the back of the door. The back door is the one she fixed herself in March with folded card packed at the hinge and a hammer and no help at all, and it shuts true now without lifting, and she shuts it behind her out of habit even though there is nothing outside.

Then she goes out to the bin and sees it.

---

It is on the low wall at the back of the garden, on the top course, on top of where the fence meets the brick and where the cats sit in the summer.

It is about the size of two loaves. It is taped round with brown tape in a cross and the tape has gone dark and hard along the edges, and the bottom corner has a grey mark on it that is either dirt or a wet thing that dried.

There is a label on the top of it. Half a label. A printed label with a number on it in the middle and a street above the number, and the street is gone off the paper — not torn, gone, the way ink goes off a wet label, with the top of a couple of letters left in it.

Number twelve.

She stands in the garden in her work clothes and looks at it for long enough that the kitchen light goes out behind her because her sister has turned it off over there for the night.

---

She goes and gets her sister.

"That came in the yard."

"Number twelve?"

"It says number twelve."

Her sister comes out and stands in the doorway with a jumper over her nightdress and her feet off the floor because the tiles are cold.

"That's not for us."

"No."

"Did it come in the back?"

"It came over the wall or it was put over. There's nothing on the front."

Her sister looks at it for a bit.

"There's a woman at twelve?" she says. "I know there's a woman at twelve."

"Yes."

"Is she any good?"

"I don't know her."

"You could take it round."

And there it is again, and her sister has said it four times now and it is the same four times, and the woman at number nine stands in the cold in her work clothes and looks at a parcel on her own back wall.

"No," she says.

"You could just knock."

"I'd not know what to say."

"You'd say I've had this out of my garden."

"Right."

"You'd not have to know her."

And that is where it stops, because the woman at number nine works out, standing there, that she does not know what number twelve is called and does not know whether number twelve is on this side of the ring road or the far side of it, and does not know which of the three of them it is, and has never once in her life gone up that road, and is not going to start at night in November with her shoes already off.

---

She takes it in.

She goes out and gets it with two hands and puts it on the kitchen table while they both look at it, and then her sister says it wants to go in the bin, and she says she knows, and her sister says there'll be a collection on Friday and last time it was the Tuesday, and neither of them knows, and that is the entire of the conversation.

Then she carries it out of the kitchen and through the passage and into the front room.

The passage is short and she goes down it with it in both hands and puts her shoulder through the door of the front room, which sticks in the summer and does not stick now.

---

The front room is the one that has not been done since the spring.

The stool is against the wall opposite the window, three rails oak and one ash, with a chair on one side of it and a settee on the other, and it is not a place to sit. It is a place to look at a thing, and it has been that since August, and nobody in this house has ever sat on it and nobody is going to start tonight.

The fire has not been lit in here since Easter and she does not light it, and the room is cold the way an unheated room is cold, which is a different cold from the kitchen, and it is the kind of cold you can stand in.

She puts the parcel on the floor.

She puts it under the stool, which is against the wall opposite the window, and pushes it back with her foot until the corner of it is under the rail, so that from the doorway you cannot see it and from the window you cannot see it and from the chair you cannot see it.

That is what she does with it. She does not decide to. She puts it under the stool because that is where she puts the things she does not have to look at, and there is nowhere else in that house.

She stands up and looks at the stool again from the doorway the way you check a picture.

A man in a unit off the industrial estate made it in the summer and gave it to her for nothing because she said something in a doorway to another woman in June, or because a woman at the end of a road said something in a kitchen in May, and neither of those two women has ever met the other and she has worked out which of them it was and she has never been able to get at the thank you for it.

The stool is the only thing anybody made in that house and it is in a room with a chair on one side of it and a settee on the other, and there is nowhere in it to sit that is not the front of somebody's house.

And now there is a taped parcel under it with a number on the label and no street, and the room she has never sat in has got a second thing in it that nobody in this borough can account for.

---

Her sister comes and stands in the doorway.

"That's a cold room."

"It's a cold room."

"You've not got the fire in here since Easter."

"You've said."

"You could light it."

"There's no reason to light it."

"You could light it because it's November," her sister says, "and you're stood in here freezing, and you've a fire and a coal shed and there's a man in this road'll bring a bag round the back for you the same as he does for me."

That is true and it is a good argument and it is not an argument that has anything to do with the parcel.

---

"I'll get it checked."

"Checked?"

"Not the parcel. The label." She says it without looking at her sister. "I'll look at the label. There's a number on it."

"What about the number?"

"Nothing's going to happen."

"It might. Somebody might be after it."

And that is the first time her sister has said anything about it at all that is about the parcel, and it is about the parcel, and she said it after three attempts, and she said it late at night with her feet off the tiles.

The woman at number nine stands in her own front room and cannot get at the sentence she wants to say, which is *don't start that*, because *don't start that* is a thing about the stool and the fire and the chair on one side of it and the settee on the other, and it is not about a parcel at all, and if she says it her sister will hear it as being about the parcel and she will be right about the parcel and wrong about everything else.

So what she says is: "I'll see."

"I'll see," her sister says. "That's the second time I've heard that."

And her sister goes back to the kitchen and puts the kettle on.

---

They drink tea at the table with the council form between them and the pen in its clip, and the talk goes to rotas and to a back door that shuts true now and to the price of the bin, and her sister says the bin is going up in the spring and there is nothing anybody can do about it, and she says that is the first sensible thing anybody has said to her all week.

The pad on the fridge has six things on it in the order she would do them, with the first one crossed off twice, in her own hand, and she has never once made herself cross it off properly and the crossing-off twice was the whole of the ceremony, and the second thing on that pad is the front room and it has been second since March and it is a room with a stool in it that nobody sits on, and she has never once put a date on it and she knows exactly why she has never once put a date on it.

She writes nothing down tonight.

---

At late night her sister goes back to number ten with the form in a bag, and the back door shuts true behind her and it makes a sound that goes all the way down the passage at number nine.

Then she goes through to the front room with the radio on low.

The stool is against the wall opposite the window. The chair is on one side of it and the settee is on the other. The parcel is under the stool against the wall and she cannot see it from where she is standing and she does not get down to check it.

She looks at the chair and the settee and the ash leg of the stool and the three oak rails, and she thinks about a man in a unit off the industrial estate who made it and did not say why, and about a gate at the end of a road standing open against a coal shed wall since the first of September with two drop bolts in an apron pocket, and about a house with six things on the back of a piece of paper and no pencil in it.

None of that is in this room. None of it is in this house. None of it has ever come to this house.

There is a tape roll in a drawer in the kitchen of number ten with about four inches of brown tape left on it, out of a roll that came out of a shelf in a house at the end of a road in May, and neither woman has ever said where the roll came from to the other.

And there is a parcel under a stool in a front room in this borough with a number on the label and no street on it, and she cannot go up that road, and she has not gone up that road in all the years she has been in this borough, and she is still the woman who has never gone up that road and she will still be that woman in the morning.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES HAS PUT A THING THAT IS NOT HERS UNDER A STOOL IN A ROOM SHE DOES NOT HEAT, HAS SAID *I'LL SEE* ONCE TO A SISTER WHO HAS NOW SAID IT TWICE, HAS WRITTEN NOTHING DOWN, HAS CROSSED THE FIRST LINE OF A PAD OFF TWICE AS SHE HAS DONE SINCE MARCH, AND HAS NOT GONE UP THAT ROAD.**
