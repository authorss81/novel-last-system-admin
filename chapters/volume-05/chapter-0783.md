# Chapter 0783 — The New Rail On The Store Door

Friday the sixth of January 2023, in this borough. He is at his own unit and yard from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a new bottom rail in the door of his store, and a line of daylight along the head of it.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES HAS THE BOTTOM OF THE DOOR OF HIS STORE GONE SOFT AND MEANS TO COVER IT WITH A SHEET OF HARDBOARD, THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN CUTS THE SOFT PART OUT AND PUTS IN A NEW BOTTOM RAIL OF A DIFFERENT THICKNESS, AND THE DOOR SHUTS ON THE RAIL AND THERE IS NOW A GAP AT THE TOP OF IT.**

He is a man in his early forties at the unit on Friday. He goes and gets the woman from two doors down in the morning, and she comes back with him and does the second half her own way.

---

The roller door is up and has been up all winter. In the yard the gates lean on the wall with the chalk gone off them down to two marks and a bare square, the latch is hooked back over one of them, and the post it is hooked to is all wall at the top and all wall at the bottom with the width of a thumbnail between them at the height of his hip. The staple on the frame wants April. The hole he filled in the grass is standing up proud where he left it. The length of oak is on the bench with its shoulder standing over the planed face, the drawer under the bench is shut, and the bag for his brush is down on the bottom of it where he put it. The stool folds itself in two over by the paint. At the back of the store the cowl is on the air brick and the wall behind it is dark from the floor up, and that is as it was in November.

The door of the store is the problem.

It is a boarded door on three ledges in a frame in the back corner, and it has been shut on a hard floor since he took the unit. It has not shut on its own for about two years. You have to take the bottom edge and lift it, because the bottom of the door has gone into a soft mess about the length of your hand, and the water that comes in under the store door when it blows hard comes straight onto it.

The bottom rail has gone.

It is the lowest horizontal board on the door, between the two stiles, and about a hand's length of it at the hinge end is soft the whole way through. Not thin. Soft. You can put your thumb in it to the first knuckle and it comes out as a wet crumb, the colour of a wet tea tray.

His plan is hardboard.

He has got a sheet of it in the van and he means to cut it to the size of the soft part, screw it over the face of the rail with six screws round the edge, and screw two more into the stiles at each end of it, so the hardboard takes the wear and the door shuts on the hardboard and not on the wood.

---

He goes and gets her.

He shuts the unit and walks down to her door and knocks and asks her, and she puts her coat on over her apron and comes back up the row with him.

She looks at the door for a while without touching it, then gets a piece of stiff card and a pencil out of her bag and puts the card up against the bottom of the door.

"How far up does it go."

"About the length of my hand, and then it stops dead and goes hard again. You can feel it with your hand. There is a line across it."

"Yes. I can feel that." She goes down the face of it with the back of her thumbnail. "And what is behind the soft part."

"The frame."

"Not the frame. Behind the soft part. Is there anything behind it."

"There is the bottom ledge and the frame."

"And is the bottom ledge soft."

He does not answer at once. He puts his thumb on the ledge where it crosses the stiles and presses, and he can push it about as far as the nail goes.

---

"You are not putting hardboard on that."

"I am putting hardboard on that."

"You are putting a sheet of the hardest thing you own on the face of a door, and the soft part is behind it, and the soft part goes on being soft behind the hardboard, and the hardboard takes the weather and the soft wood takes the wet, and you will have screwed a lid on a bucket."

"Then what would you do."

"Take it out."

"Take the whole door out and frame a new one round it?"

"Take the bottom rail out." She says it as though she has been waiting to say it. "The bottom rail is a board between two stiles and it can be out in an afternoon. And I have got one."

"What thickness."

"That is the thing you need to know before I start." She takes her hands out of her pockets. "I have got a rail and it is not the thickness that came out of your door. It is about a quarter thicker. I have had it since the summer and it came off a door of my own and I have been keeping it for something."

---

"Then it will not go in."

"It will go in. It will go in and the door will shut." She is looking at the head of the door now, not at the bottom. "And then I am going to stand back and you are going to be unhappy, and I would rather you had it in advance of me doing it."

"Go on."

"I have been looking at that door since I came in. Your door hangs on three hinges and the leaf has dropped on them over the years, and it has dropped so far that the top of it is up hard against the head of that frame, and there is no daylight anywhere along the top of it. That is why it shuts and it is why it has shut for years."

"Go on."

"The moment you put a quarter of an inch thicker on the bottom of it, that leaf is a quarter of an inch taller than the opening it has hung in for however long it has hung there." She puts her hands about that far apart. "It will shut. It will shut better than it has shut in your life. But it will be riding that much higher on those three hinges, and there will be that much daylight along the head of that door from one end to the other, and a draught coming in there all winter that you can put a sheet of newspaper at."

---

They do it.

She takes the door off the three hinges with a bar and a lump of wood, two pins out of three straps, and lays it face up on two trestles she borrows off the man next door. The bottom rail comes out in four pieces with a chisel and a hand saw because it is already half out of its own, and the inside of it is the colour of a wet tea tray and crushes between two fingers like a crust of bread.

She brings her rail. It is a sound board, quarter sawn, an inch and three-quarters at the thickest part and dressed on the face, and she has kept the arris on it.

She cuts it to length between the stiles, lets it up into the grooves with a mallet and a block, and works on the face with a chisel and a plane until the grooves grip it and it does not move in them at all. She does not nail it. She screws two screws through each stile into the end grain of it with a gap left at the head of each one, because a screw screwed hard into the end of a rail twists it, and then she pares the screw heads off flush with a chisel and files the two spots flat.

Then she puts the door back on its hinges and puts the pins in.

---

It shuts.

It shuts on the rail. He pulls it to and it goes over and into the frame and latches, and he does not have to take the bottom edge and lift it, and he shuts it again to be certain and it shuts again and he has not touched the bottom of it once.

"You will not be lifting that."

"And it is a rail twice as good as the one that came out of it." She is packing the plane away. "That door will shut on that rail for as long as the rail lasts, and it will outlast you."

---

Then he shuts it one more time and stands and looks at it from the inside, in the last of the light through the doorway.

There is a line along the head of the door.

It is not a big gap. It is about a quarter of an inch and it goes the whole width of the door from the hinge stile to the latch stile, and it is dark now because it is dark inside and light on the other side, and when the wind gets up in the yard the strip of paper he put at the head of it goes out of its own accord without anybody touching it.

He puts his hand up into it. His hand goes in flat to the second knuckle along the whole length of it.

He stands in his own store with his hand up inside the head of his own door, and the woman from two doors down is at the other end of the unit with her bag in her hand, and neither of them says anything, because there is nothing to say about it.
