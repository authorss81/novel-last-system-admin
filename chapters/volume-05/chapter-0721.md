# Chapter 0721 — The Bell On The Bench In A Lock-Up

Saturday the thirtieth of April 2022, in this borough. She is at a lock-up from first light until dark, and the whole of it is six bags in a row and a telephone she plugs in to see whether it works, and it does.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES PLUGS A TELEPHONE SHE TOOK OFF A WALL INTO A SOCKET ON THE WALL OF A LOCK-UP TO SEE WHETHER IT WORKS, AND IT DOES, AND SOMEBODY RINGS IT, AND NEITHER SHE NOR THE MAN WITH THE KEYS ANSWERS IT, AND SHE PUTS IT BACK IN THE BAG AND TIES THE BAG.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is in a lock-up all day. A man with keys is at the doors with tea for most of it. She speaks to him in the middle of the day.

---

She starts with the bags because the bags are what the place is for.

Six of them against the back wall in a row, tied at the neck, labels out, nothing written on any of the labels because nothing in this lock-up has ever been in a house long enough to need one.

She goes down the row with both hands and lifts each one to feel the weight, and sets it down square, and does not open any of them. She has not opened one since October. Some of them are somebody else's house out of somebody else's house and there is no version of this job in which that is hers to do.

The heap at the far end is under a sheet, grey canvas, stiff where the frost has been in it, and the boxes under the sheet are in their bays in the order they came in.

On the sheet, at the corner nearest the doors, there is a taped box with the folded bit of bag still over one corner, brown and damp through, and she puts the corner of the sheet down over it the way she put it down on the seventh of January and the way she has put it down every time since.

---

The bag with the telephone comes in with her on the seat and she does not open it for the first hour.

She has the shunting about for half of the morning, and a man with a clipboard comes in the morning about a bay that is not paid for, and she says it is not paid for, and he says it is not paid for either, and he writes nothing and goes.

At about half past the middle of the day she stops and puts the bag on the bench with the bolster and opens it.

The telephone is in it and the lid is in it and the bell is in it, still on its foot of flex with the two bare wires out of the end, and she had wrapped it in a cloth so it would not knock the tins.

She sets the telephone on the bench and the bell beside it and the lid on top of the lid, and she stands and looks at the three of them.

There is a socket on the lock-up wall by the door. It is an old one, brown bakelite, and it has a thing on it for a plug that has not been made since somebody's father was alive.

She has an adaptor in the van because she has had one in the van since she got the van, and she goes and gets it and comes back and stands in front of the socket with it in her hand and does not put it in for a while.

---

Then she puts it in.

She plugs the telephone into the adaptor and the adaptor into the wall and she puts the bell on the bench on top of the bolster and she leaves the flex long enough to reach the cradle.

The line is not silent. That is the first thing she did not expect.

There is a tone in the earpiece. Not a fast one. A slow one, and a proper one, and the number is alive, which it should not be, because this house has been empty since before the spring and nobody told anybody at the other end of it.

She picks up and puts down and picks up again and the tone is there both times, and she says out loud in a lock-up to a sheet-covered heap:

"Right," she says. "Somebody's paying for that line."

---

She leaves it plugged in.

There is no reason to. She knows there is no reason to. It is not her telephone, and the line is not hers, and a line that a shop or a man or a family is paying for, with a telephone on the other end of it somewhere in this city that has no idea where this telephone is, is not going to help her clear a house.

She leaves it plugged in because it is the only thing in this lock-up that has done anything all day.

She goes back to the bags and sorts what is hers from what is not, bag by bag, lifting to feel the weight, setting them in a row with the labels out.

And at about the middle of the afternoon the bell goes.

---

It is not a loud sound. It is a flat ring on the bolster, dulled by the cloth, and it is not the sound of a telephone in a house where somebody wants to know something, and it is very nearly a sound she has had in her ears all morning without noticing, which is the sound of the road, and of a lorry, and of nothing.

It is the sound of a bell that has been lying in the bottom of a case since before the spring, with two bare wires out of it, going on a number that a woman in a lock-up has just made live with an adaptor and a socket on a wall.

It goes about nine times.

She stands at the bags with her hands down.

---

The man with keys comes in from the office with his tea at the sound of it.

"That's ringing," he says.

"Yes."

"That's not ours."

"No."

He looks at the telephone on the bench and the bell on the bolster and the flex going across to the socket on the wall.

"Who's that," he says.

"I don't know," she says, and it is the whole truth and it is the first whole truth she has given him in ten months of Tuesdays and Saturdays, and she does not know why she gives it to him now.

---

It rings again and this time he is standing next to her.

"Somebody's trying," he says.

"Somebody's trying."

"Are you going to get it?"

And here is the sentence, and she stands there and lets it sit there for as long as the bell goes on sitting there, and the answer she gives is the only answer there is in a lock-up with a sheet-covered heap behind her and a folded bag she has not opened since the twelfth of November.

"No," she says. "It's not mine."

And that is true. And it is not the reason, and both of them know it is not the reason, and the reason is that the other end of that line belongs to a house on the far side of the ring road that she stood in on Monday, and there are two pairs of wire on the back of the plate of that telephone joined to each other with tape, and if she says *hello* on this end then somebody on that end is going to say a name, and she is going to have to stand in a lock-up and admit that she has been in their hall with a screwdriver in her hand.

---

The bell stops.

The two of them stand there for a moment in a lock-up on a Saturday afternoon with a dead bell on a bolster, a man with tea, and a woman with a screwdriver in her pocket.

Then she unplugs it.

She pulls the adaptor out of the wall first and the tone cuts dead in the earpiece where it is lying face down, and then she pulls the telephone out of the adaptor, and then she takes the bell off the bolster and puts it back in the bag with the lid and the telephone and the cloth around it, and she pushes it down under the tins and knots the bag and ties the neck twice.

The bag goes back on the seat where it was at first light.

---

She works until the light goes.

She sweeps the floor out of the corners by the doors and round the bays and under the sheet edge and she tips the dust in a bag and ties the bag. She oils the hinges of the doors with a rag from the van because they scream and they have screamed since the spring. She counts the bags again at the end, six, tied square, labels out, nothing on any of them.

She does not open the drawer under the bench. It has been shut since the middle of November with leads and screws in it and it is not her drawer and it never was.

She does not lift the sheet off the heap and she does not lift the corner of it off the taped box with the bagged corner, and she does not open the bag with somebody else's blanket in it, which has been set aside since the twelfth of November, and which she does not open tonight either.

---

The man locks up behind her and she sits in the cab with the engine off and the bag on the passenger seat.

A woman in a lock-up has made a dead number live for the length of a sorting-out and somebody has rung it and has given up, and she does not know who and she will not know, and she cannot ask, and the not-asking costs exactly nothing at all.

That is the thing she cannot get past.

A cistern weeping at the far side of a joist over a landing four streets away costs a woman of seventy-eight a bowl of water twice a day and a tap she will not have anybody near and a room she will not sit in. The felt gone off a shed roof costs a woman in her early thirties a drawer and a piece of paper with three lines on it. A bell under the boards in a house off the ring road cost a man in his late twenties a sentence he did not say to a woman at a door.

A bell on a bolster in a lock-up cost a stranger at the other end of a line the length of a sorting-out and then they gave up.

It is the cheapest thing in this borough by a long way and she cannot think of one single thing she could have done about it that would not have been worse for somebody, and she has driven from lock-ups to houses for ten months and this is the first job she has finished that she cannot put a single mark on.

---

**What happened at the lock-up:** a telephone off a hall wall in an empty house was made live for the length of a sorting-out by a woman with an adaptor and a socket, and somebody rang it, and neither person in the building answered it.

**What was not done:** the drawer under the bench stayed shut, the bag with the blanket stayed tied, the sheet stayed over the heap, and the taped box with the bagged corner was not looked at, and the line is dead again now and the bag is on the seat where it was at first light.

**What cost nothing:** the whole of the damage is that a woman somewhere gave up ringing a number she needed, and she does not know that it is this number, and never will.
