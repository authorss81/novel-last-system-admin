# Chapter 0801 — The Four Pallets Turned

Sunday the nineteenth of March 2023, in this borough. That Sunday goes on her own yard and her own bay from the time the frost first comes off the ground until she cannot lift another one, and four pallets with the blocks wet through in the middle of them are the whole of it.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES HAS FOUR PALLETS STANDING ON EDGE AGAINST A WALL WITH A STACK OF BLOCKS DOWN THE MIDDLE OF EVERY ONE OF THEM WET THROUGH SINCE JUNE AND MEANS TO TURN THEM WHERE THEY STAND, THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK TAKES THE WHOLE FOUR OUT INTO THE YARD TO CLEAR THE WALL, TURNS THEM THE OTHER WAY UP AND PUTS THEM BACK WITH THE BLOCKS OFF THE GROUND ON BEARERS, AND IT IS TWO PEOPLE AND A CLEAR MORNING.**

On that Sunday the shutter goes up over the bay without a sound and stays up all day and nothing goes through it, and the woman who books work is standing out in the yard in the gap the four pallets make against that wall, waiting, because there is a job on the board in that office and nothing on it about today.

---

Those four pallets have been on edge against that outside wall since October.

There are nine blocks in each one, laid flat down the pocket in the middle, and every one of those nine has been wet through in the middle since June, and she has turned her back on them once already and looked at them in the summer and said they were not as bad as they looked, and they were.

The wall they are against is the north side and it is the render on it that wets them, because that stretch of wall never dries: the coping over it has a lead strip that lets go of a bit of the flashing every year about this time, and everything the roof puts on that coping runs down the face of the render and into the back of four pallets stood on edge against it. A pallet on its edge has its bottom boards against the wall with nothing between them and the render, and the water goes into the pocket and lies there, and the middle of every block is as wet in March as it was in July.

She knows which part of it is the water, because she has put her hand in. She put her hand into the middle of the topmost block of each stack one afternoon in the summer and the block was cold and damp right through the middle of it and dry as a stone on the two ends, and she has known ever since that this is not a stack of blocks that got wet and then dried. It is a stack of blocks with a pocket of air round it that has had nowhere to go.

And those blocks are spoken for. They go out on a job on the Thursday, and they cannot go out wet, and a mason will not take one off a lorry and stack it with a stone in weight of water in the middle of it.

Her plan is to turn them where they stand.

She means to tip each one over where it stands so the wet face is up and the dry face is against the wall, and put them back against the wall, and go into the bay.

---

"You are turning them where they stand."

"I am turning them where they stand. There is nowhere else for them."

"There is a yard the length of that bay behind them." The woman who books work has not moved off the shutter. "And you are not going to turn one of them where it stands, because a pallet with nine blocks in it that has been standing on edge since October is not a thing you tip over. It is a thing you get two people to."

"And then what."

"And then you find out whether turning them has done anything at all." She comes down off the shutter and walks out to that wall and puts two fingers into the middle of a block in the top of the nearest stack and brings them out and looks at them. "A block wet in the middle is a different animal from a block wet at an end. An end dries in a week. A middle dries when the air can get at it from both sides, and a block that is lying in a pocket with its own blocks stacked against it on three sides and a wall against it on the fourth is not going to dry in March or in June or in any March after that."

"So turning them does nothing."

"Turning them where they stand does nothing. Turning them out in the middle of a yard with the sun on them does a great deal, and the trouble is that they have to be put back where they came from before anybody can get through that gap with a load."

---

So they take all four out into the yard first, and that is most of the morning.

The trick with a loaded pallet is that nobody lifts it. You take the top of it and walk it back and it goes over on its own bottom edge with the blocks sliding down the pocket and stopping against the bottom rail, and then it is lying on its side with nine blocks in a row on their edges and the pallet face up.

They do that with the first one, and then the second and the third and the fourth, and they stand them in a row out in the middle of the yard with the blocks on their edges and the air going straight through all of them for the first time since the autumn.

The frost came off the yard early and by the middle of the morning the ground under them had begun to come up on the surface, and then it stopped coming up and stayed where it was, which is what ground does when there is nothing coming down on it. The sun came over the far wall onto the middle of the yard and stood there, and there was nothing else in that yard on a Sunday.

---

Then they turn them, which is the same operation backwards, and then they go back into the pocket.

They turn each pallet over on its face and lay it down the other way up, so that the pocket comes up the right way for the blocks to go into, and then they put the nine blocks back into the pocket lying flat. Nine lifts apiece and thirty-six between the two of them, and every block is stone cold and a good deal heavier than it looks.

And the whole of it is done with the shutter up behind them and the yard empty and nobody else in the place, and if it is going to be done it is done on a Sunday morning in March and not on a Tuesday in June.

---

Then they put them back against that wall, and this is where it stops being four pallets against a wall.

There are eight lengths of softwood stacked inside at the front of the bay, cut off square, about four feet long and about three inches by three. Those are the bearers and they have been lying at the front of that bay since somebody had them cut, waiting for something.

Each pallet stands on two of them.

The bearers go down on the ground running out from the wall, two under each pallet with a gap between them, and the pallet comes down across the top of them on to its own bottom edge, so that the whole length of that edge is carried three inches off the ground and the back of the pallet stands an inch clear of the render instead of lying against it.

And the pallets go back with the face that has been against the wall turned the other way, so that what is against the render is the solid deck and not the pocket, and the pocket with the blocks in it stands out into the air and the water coming down that wall goes past the outside of it and drops onto the ground and never gets in.

The bearers are laid two and two down the length of the run and not one under the other, so that the air can go the whole way along under the pallets and come out at the bottom instead of making a tunnel with no other end.

That is the whole of it and it is eight bearers and every one of them in use, and not one of them has anything to do with the blocks.

---

"You have put them up on lengths of timber."

"I have put them up off the ground and off the wall." She stands back from them. "Blocks wet in the middle are not wet because they are stacked on other blocks. They are wet because there is no air anywhere near them, and a pallet jammed against a wall with its pocket open to the rain is the worst of both. You can turn them over as often as you like and the wall puts the water back in the same morning."

"You have had that wall down three times now."

"Three times, and the first two they went back against it the same afternoon they came off it, and there was no reason on earth to take them off." She gets the heel of her hand against the side of the nearest pallet. "This is not about turning them. This is about there being air round them and the ground not being where they are."

---

They go back to the head of the ramp in the middle of the afternoon and she stands in the doorway of the office and looks at that wall.

Four pallets, on edge, the other way up, off the ground on bearers and off the render on air, with nine blocks in the middle of each one that are dry at the ends now and going dry in the middle by the summer.

And the woman who books work goes out of the gate and up the lane and does not turn round.

The woman of the lock-up stands with her hand on the rail at the top of the ramp and looks down that wall a while longer.

She put all four of those out into the middle of that yard on a Sunday and she could not have tipped the first one. There are nine feet of clear ground in the middle of that yard with the sun coming over the far wall onto it, and that is what a pallet with nine blocks in it wants, and there is no making it out of the four feet of gap against a wall.

Under the bottom edge of every one of those four pallets there is now a strip of daylight a foot and a half long, where in October there was nothing to see at all, and it is not a shadow, and there is nothing standing in front of it.

On the Thursday those blocks go out dry at the ends and wet in the middle, and a mason will take them, and in the next wet weekend the same thing will begin again on the same render about this time.
