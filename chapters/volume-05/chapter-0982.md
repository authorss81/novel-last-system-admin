# Chapter 0982 — Where The Handle Broke

Undated in this draft.

**A BROKEN SPADE BLADE LIES IN THE GRASS WITH THE SOCKET EMPTY AND THE STEEL TANG BROKEN IN THE BOTTOM OF IT.**

The blade of the spade lies in the grass by the shed at number ten, on its back, with the socket end pointing up towards the shed and the foot of the blade down in the grass towards the fence. It came off a handle and nobody has picked it up, and it has lain in that place through one dry week and the start of another.

The socket is the tubular top end of the blade, an inch and a quarter across the outside and about four inches deep, and it is made of the same steel as the blade and not welded to it. It is a forging pressed up from flat and drawn, and the wall of it is a sixteenth of an inch and hollow inside from end to end.

The handle is not in it. What is in the bottom of the socket is the tang, which is the iron the handle was meant to be driven onto, and the tang has broken off about an inch above the socket floor and stands up out of it with a break that goes across the metal and slightly up one side.

The break is bright and it is the only bright thing about the blade. Everything else on it has gone: the whole of it is a brown that varies from a dark red on the spade's foot to a pale grey where it has lain in the wet grass and dried in the sun, and the boundary between those two colours follows the outline of the shadow the shed threw across it last week.

The steel of the tang is damp in the bottom of the socket where the grass keeps the sun off it, and the black of it is in the hollow and grey on the outside where it has dried. The blade on the face of it is not damp at all in the middle of the day, because the sun gets at it about an hour after the shed's shadow has moved off.

The shed on that side has its felt short at the front edge, and what gets in behind the felt keeps the ground under it wet to a foot for a week at a time. The spade lies about six feet from the shed and clear of that strip, in the grass that grows between the shed and the boundary wall and does not get mown because nothing gets mown.

The boundary wall there is close-boarded, and the boards lie one over another with a step between each, and the bottom board is off its nail at one end and stands out a finger's width from the one above it. Behind that opening is the hollow of the wall, and it is in the same corner of the yard as the spade, though nothing in the yard connects them.

The gate in that run of close-boarding is a pair of iron leaves on a frame, and the hinge on the near leaf has worked loose, so that the leaf hangs by its top corner and the bottom of it lies in the grass where it fell. The gate post the leaf was meant to shut against stands about an inch to the left of where the leaf now lies, which is how it came to be a gap and not a fault of fitting. The grass under all of it has grown up through the summer and stands about six inches, and the spade lies in it with the socket end standing clear above the blades while the foot of the blade has gone down into them and is not to be seen at all.
---

One fingertip goes down into the socket and is turned slowly against the bottom of it, so that it goes round the inside of the break in a full circle and finds the whole of what is left of the tang.

What is there is a stump of iron about three quarters of an inch high, standing on the floor of the socket and leaning very slightly to one side, and the break across the top of it is not square. On one side it runs straight across and is clean, and on the other it has torn down into the metal in a curve and finished in a long splinter that reaches nearly to the floor of the socket.

Going round the inside of it, the fingertip finds a lip of iron where the tear has lifted the metal off the wall of the socket for about half its circumference, and under that lip there is a hollow no wider than a sheet of paper and no deeper than a card, and in that hollow there is rust powder and nothing else.

The socket is a forging and its wall is hollow all the way down, so the space inside the tang is open at the bottom of the break and inside the wall of the socket there is a second space, a long thin one, running the whole four inches down to where the blade begins. The fingertip cannot get into it, and going down the socket finds the join where the wall was closed at the top of the blade, which is a ridge under the skin like a scar.

Nothing gives. The tang does not move under the fingertip, and the break is not a loose one, and the socket does not turn on the grass at all. All that comes of the going in is a smear of rust powder on the tip of the finger and leave the stump of the tang standing at the same angle in the same hole.

A spade handle is driven into the tang while the tang is hot and the steel is soft, and after that it cannot be taken out without taking the steel with it. Whoever broke this one did it wrong, in that the tang went before the driving was done, and the handle came off it and went wherever it went.

The scar of the driving is still in the bottom of the socket. Four small marks stand round the inside of the tang where the shoulder of the handle bore against the steel, and they are not opposite one another, which means the handle went in crooked and was driven crooked, and the tang was then bent a little off true at the same time.

That bend is the reason the stump leans. The lean is only a couple of degrees, but it puts the broken face of the tang over the widest part of the hollow in the wall, so that rain off the blade goes down the inside of the socket and out through the space where the metal is not. The rest of the blade is stamped on the shoulder with the maker's name and the number, and the stamping has gone soft at its edges where the steel is thinnest, and the number is the one anybody would want if anybody were minded to make another handle for it, which nobody is going to.
---

The sister is standing in the gap where the near leaf of the gate hangs by its top corner only, with the close-boarding on one side of her and the loose leaf lying in the grass on the other, and her hands swinging empty at her sides.

She does not step over the leaf. The spade lies six feet away in the grass by the shed and is not looked at, and not one word is spent on the leaf or on the spade.

"There were potatoes in the market again."

"They were six a pound on Saturday."

"I gave up on it before the spring."

"You have missed a treat then." The talk goes on to a return ticket that was bought in the spring and never used, and to the difficulty of finding out what it is worth now, and neither of the two comes to an answer and neither of them tries a second time.

Her hand goes down towards the loose leaf in the grass as if to lift it clear of the way, and comes back up empty, and the leaf lies in the grass in the same place it has lain in for a fortnight.

The tang stands in the bottom of the socket at the same lean with the same torn edge to it, and there is more iron in the wall of that socket than there ever was in the middle of it.

Under the lip of the tear the rust powder lies in a hollow no wider than a sheet of paper and is packed there hard, so that the rain coming down the blade goes past it and out through the gap in the metal; it will be there for good, and the number on the shoulder is the one nobody will ask for.
