# Chapter 0851 — The Tray And Its Brackets

Thursday the twelfth of October 2023, along that wall in the bay at the lock-up from the time the dew has come off the tray until the office window goes dark, and the tray dusted and every bracket gone over is the whole of that Thursday.

**THERE IS A CABLE TRAY ON THE WALL OF THAT BAY AT THE LOCK UP PACKED WITH THE GRIT OFF THE YARD AGAINST THE CONDUIT IN IT AND SHE MEANS TO DUST IT AND CHECK THE WASHER UNDER EVERY BRACKET HEAD WITHOUT MOVING THE CONDUIT, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS AT THE YARD GATE WITH HER BACK TO THE ROAD AND DOES NOT COME IN.**

That tray runs the length of the back wall of the bay at about the height of a man's shoulder, galvanised sheet folded up the two sides, on brackets screwed back to the wall every six feet or so.

It is a lid. That is the whole of what it is for: the conduit lies in it and the grit of this yard falls into it and stays there, and as long as the conduit is lying in the tray and not on the grit in it, the grit cannot get at anything worth grinding.

Since June it has packed with the yard's finest and hardest and gone off pale, and the conduit has settled down onto it in three places, and where a conduit rests on grit that grit is a cutting edge lying against the sheath under it every time anything is moved in the bay.

She does not move the conduit. It comes out of the tray or it does not come out of the tray, and there is no way of getting it up onto the wall and working on it and putting it back where it was as if it had never moved, and she is not doing it today and would not do it on a Thursday.

So the tray gets emptied and the conduit stays where it is.

The soft brush goes in at one end and runs the length of the tray, and the grit comes forward ahead of it in a wave about the depth of a thumb and goes over the side onto the floor onto a sheet. Working it from the far end does the opposite and drives it against the wall, and she has done that once before on a tray in another bay and remembers it.

It comes out grey and hard and with the grey of the yard in it, and where it has been packed wet and dried it comes off in lumps about the size of a bean and has to be picked out of the bottom of the tray by hand.

She empties the tray in three passes and sweeps the floor under it after each, and the conduit is lying in a clean tray for the first time since the spring and is up off the grit all the length of the run.

Then she goes along the brackets with her thumb under each washer.

That is the check and it is the whole reason she is out there with a tray brush and nothing else, because a washer that has stopped working is not visible from the floor. You go along a run of brackets with a fingernail under every washer edge and you learn more about that wall in five minutes than in a year of looking at it.

A washer that is down and taking the weight cannot be got under with a nail at all. A washer that has stopped has a gap under the edge of it and the gap is where the bracket is moving, and the bracket moves, and the tray comes off the wall.

She does them all. There are nine brackets on that run and nine washers and the ninth is at the far end by the roller door.

Four of them have drawn a ring in the paint.

Not one of those four is a fault. The head has been down on that paint long enough that the paint has been pushed out from under the washer and polished round the edge of it by the washer turning a fraction each time the tray was loaded, and it has made a bullseye about the size of a thumbnail, brighter than the wall in the middle and dirty round the outside.

That is a washer that has been doing its work for years. The paint under it has nowhere to go and has been getting out of the way of it, and the ring it has made is the best thing on that wall.

The ninth bracket is the one she looks at twice.

Its washer is down flat and the screw is tight and the bracket does not move. But the paint around that bracket has been built up over the years by two or three repaintings nobody asked for, and the washer has ended up under the surface of the paint rather than on it, so what she can see from the floor is the head sitting in a shallow dish of its own paint with the washer somewhere underneath.

She leaves it. Bringing that washer up to the surface means the bracket comes off the wall, and the bracket off the wall means the tray comes down, and the tray down means the conduit comes out of it and goes somewhere else until a day with a man who knows what it goes to.

She puts her thumb under it twice anyway to be sure, and it is down, and that is the end of what can be found out about it from where she is standing.

The woman who books work is at the yard gate in the afternoon with her back to the road and both hands on the gate, watching what comes in and what goes out, and does not come into the yard and does not turn round.

She thinks about washers while she finishes off, how a washer is a piece of nothing that is worth exactly what it is for, which is spreading a load over a wider piece of paint than the head of a screw would otherwise touch. Put the load on the paint directly and the paint bruises under the head and the head goes on going down into the wall a fraction at a time until the hole is a slot. Put a washer under it and the load spreads and the paint bruises somewhere it can be seen from the floor and brushed, which is the only place a bruise is any use at all.

Where the conduit crosses the bracket at the far end there is a short length about a foot long and it is pale.

The whole run is pale where the dust has lain on it, bleached to a grey white by a summer of it, and this length is the same pale as the rest of it and then it is not.

She has brushed the tray out along its whole length and the dust has come off the top of the conduit, and where the dust was the sheath is dark. It is the colour that plastic is when nothing has lain on it for four months.

She puts her thumb on it and wipes it across and the dark goes off her thumb and the length stays pale, because the dust has gone into the texture and not merely onto it, and that is what a summer in a yard does to a length of conduit lying in a tray that is meant to be a lid.

The tray is emptied and the conduit lies clear in it the whole length of the bay with air under it, and nothing is resting on it anywhere.

---

The brush goes on its nail behind the bench, and the sheet of grit goes out to the bin, and the tray stands empty along the wall.

She stands in the bay when the office window has gone dark and looks along what is there. A tray clean the length of the wall with the conduit lying in it, and one short length of it by the far bracket where under the dust the sheath is dark.
