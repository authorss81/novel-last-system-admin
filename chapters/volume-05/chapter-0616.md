# Chapter 0616 — The House He Meant

Between about a quarter past ten in the morning and about seven in the evening on Sunday the seventeenth of January 2021, in this borough.

**A BOY IN HER LATE TEENS STOPPED HER IN A LANE BEHIND THE PARADE AND SAID THAT HER HOUSE HAD BEEN PUT UP, AND SHE HAS SPENT THE DAY TRYING TO FIND OUT WHICH HOUSE HE MEANT, AND SHE HAS NOT FOUND OUT, AND SHE HAS STOOD AT HER OWN FRONT DOOR FOR THE FIRST TIME SINCE BEFORE THE SUMMER.**

She is a woman in her mid thirties. She works in a shop on the parade that is shut on Sundays and has been on the rota of that shop since before the summer before last. She lives on a road off the parade in a flat above a shop that she has been in since before the summer.

Here is what he said, word for word, and she has gone over it since.

She had gone down to the shop on the parade to leave a key under the mat, because the Saturday man had gone home without it and the manager had said not to put it through the letterbox.

And the boy was standing in the lane behind the parade with a bag of shopping over his shoulder and a cigarette he was not smoking, and he is a boy in his late teens she has known by sight for most of a decade and has spoken to perhaps six times, and he lives at the top of her own road.

And he said, "They've put yours up."

And she said, "Put what up."

And he said, "Your house. It's gone up."

And she said, "Which house."

And he said, "Yours."

And she said, "Has somebody been to mine."

And he said, "There's a board on it."

And she said, "Right," and he went off up the lane with the bag over his shoulder and she stood in the lane and found that she could not move for a bit.

Here is the thing that has taken the day, and it is not the board.

If it were her own flat there would be one thing to do, and it would be an easy thing, and she would have done it before she got back to the road.

What it is, and she worked it out somewhere about the middle of the morning, is that she has two houses.

One: where she is. A flat above a shop, and nobody sells flats above shops, and no board has ever been put on anything above a shop on that road in the time she has been in it, and she knows the man who owns it and he has never once mentioned anything.

Two: the house she was in until she was nineteen. That is on an estate on the other side of the borough and it was sold before the summer to a couple, and there is a board on it, there has been a board on it since before the summer, and she has driven past it and she does not go up that road.

And the boy said *your house*, and not *your flat*, and did not say which, and by the time she had thought about it he was gone up the lane.

She has been in a café and a car park and her own front room with that sentence since the middle of the morning.

She found him again at about one.

She went up the road on the off chance and stood at the end of it for long enough for it to be odd, and then she went up and knocked on the door of the house he lives in, which is the top house, and a woman in her late fifties opened it and said the boy was not in and had gone out.

She said, "Will he be back."

And the woman said, "He's always back."

And she said, "Do you know when."

And the woman said, "You'll get him."

And she said, "It doesn't matter. It was only about a board."

And the woman said, "There's boards up all over the place now."

And she said, "Did he say which one."

And the woman said, "Which one of what."

And she said, "Nothing."

And she walked away from that door having worked out two things at once, which are that the boy had not mentioned the board to anybody in that house, and that the woman would have said so if he had.

---

Here is what she got out of the rest of it, in order, because she has been through all of it more than once.

One: she went down to the parade at about half past one and along it there is a place that cuts keys, a supermarket, a takeaway, and a betting shop, and the betting shop is shut on a Sunday and the man who owns it is in on the other days, and she stood outside the shutter for a while.

Two: she got her phone out and looked at the house on the estate on the map, which she has done before, and then she did nothing with it.

Three: she went and had a coffee and a sandwich she did not want in a café on the parade with a man at the next table who was eating the same sandwich, and she read the whole of a page of a paper and cannot tell you one thing on it.

Four: and this is the one she has kept going back to, at about half past three in the afternoon, sitting in the car behind the parade.

The boy does not know which of her houses he meant. That is now the likeliest thing, and it is worse than the other version, because it means there was no information.

If he had meant her flat, he would have said flat. Every person on that road says flat. She has lived there since before the summer and she has heard every house on it described by the people who live in it, and nobody says house about the top of that road.

If he had meant the old house, she would know, and she would have done something about it by now.

So he meant one of two things she cannot separate, and the whole of the information is a boy in a lane with a bag of shopping who was repeating something he heard on a Friday or a Saturday and got the word in his mouth from a different life.

She got to her own front door at about four.

That is a fact she will not be able to put down afterwards, and she has already noticed that about it.

She has not stood at that door since before the summer. She goes down the steps and out and she does not stop and she has never once stopped.

She stopped at about four.

And there is no board.

She stood there and looked at the whole of the front, which is a shop window and a door and a step and a rail and a bracket with a sign swinging on it, and there is nothing on any of it, and there has not been, and the woman who owns it was in the shop and waved at her through the glass.

And there is a woman on the till who has never mentioned a board, and a landlord in Leeds who has never mentioned a board, and a letting agent she has never heard of anywhere.

---

Her sister rang at about five.

And she said, "You all right? You went quiet."

And she said, "I'm all right."

And her sister said, "You've gone quiet since about two."

And she said, "I haven't."

And her sister said, "Mum's birthday's in March."

And she said, "I know what it is."

And her sister said, "Are you doing something with the house."

And she said, "What house."

And her sister said, "The old one."

And she said, "Why would I be doing something with the old one."

And her sister said, "Because you asked me last month what the ground rent was."

And she said, "That was for a bit of a joke."

And her sister said, "Right."

And she said, "It was."

And her sister said, "It's been on the market since before the summer, they've had it up and down twice and there's a couple after it, I saw them in the car park in June."

And she said, "The house is sold."

And her sister said, "It isn't."

And she said, "Mum died and Dad sold it and I signed the thing."

And her sister said, "You signed for the loft."

And she said, "Right," and her sister said, "You never asked about the house."

And she said, "No."

And her sister said, "Why are you asking now."

And she said, "I'm not."

And her sister said, "You did just ask."

And she said, "I didn't ask you anything. I said why would I be doing something."

And her sister said, "That's asking."

That is where it stops and it stops there.

There is a boarding across the front of the shop underneath her and a board that would screw to the frame of the door, and she cannot tell from the wrong side of a rail whether a thing like that would go on her own.

There is a house on an estate on the other side of this borough that has been on and off the market since before the summer and has not gone and is still there.

And there is a woman in her mid thirties who has been sure since before the summer that the arrangement was finished, and who at about a quarter past five this evening could not tell you, standing in her own kitchen, whether it was.

She has not rung the estate agent. She has not gone up the estate road. She has not asked the boy anything, and the boy does not know that there was ever a question.

**SHE STOOD AT HER OWN FRONT DOOR AT FOUR O'CLOCK AND THERE WAS NO BOARD ON IT, AND A BOY IN A LANE HAD TOLD HER AT A QUARTER PAST TEN IN THE MORNING THAT HER HOUSE HAD BEEN PUT UP, AND SHE CANNOT SEPARATE THE TWO HOUSES IN HER HEAD, AND HER SISTER SAYS THE OLD ONE HAS NOT SOLD.**
