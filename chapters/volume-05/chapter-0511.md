# Chapter 0511 — She Had a Blue One of Her Own

The Tuesday of the thirteenth of August 2019, and a good part of it was gone by half past eight in the morning. One street and the length of a parade, in Tideglass.

**TWO PEOPLE WERE IN THAT HOUSE ON ONE EVENING IN 1994 AND EACH WENT OUT CERTAIN ABOUT THE OTHER AND A THING THAT WAS LEANING IN A CORNER, AND A QUARTER OF A CENTURY OF COURTESY HAS BEEN BUILT ON THE MISTAKE AND NEITHER HAS EVER BROKEN IT, AND IN MAY A THIRD PERSON TOLD HIM THAT THE UMBRELLA HAD BEEN HERS AND HAD GONE MISSING A LONG WAY FROM THAT HOUSE, AND HE HAS KEPT IT TO HIMSELF SINCE AND HAS GOT AS FAR AS WISHING HE HAD HELD HIS TONGUE IN MAY.**

They are not related and have never been, and they were at that house in June 1994 for about four hours between them on the same evening, and neither of them has set foot in it since.

He is in his fifties and has a trade. She is in her early sixties and does not work.

They have lived within about a mile of each other since about 1996 and have met perhaps three dozen times since, all of them accidental, all of them on pavements, and on every one of those they were extremely pleasant to each other.


Here is the umbrella.

It was blue. It was a long umbrella with a wooden handle and it was hers, and it was propped in the corner of that entrance between a shoe rack and the door, standing up on its end the way a person leaves one.

He saw it at about half past seven. She saw it at about half past six.

He has told himself for twenty-five years that he left that house at half past ten with somebody else's umbrella because he was the worse for what had been drunk, and that a man who walks out of somebody's entrance with a thing that is not his and says nothing about it is welcome nowhere afterwards, and that he has never in his life gone back to that house to put it in the corner where it was standing.

She has told herself for twenty-five years that she left that house at about eight with somebody else's umbrella because she had her coat over her arm and did not think, and that a woman who walks off with a thing that is not hers and never says a word about it is a woman people will believe anything about.

That is the whole of the two beliefs and neither of them is true.


What they built on it is the whole of what either of them has done for twenty-five years, and it is the same in both.

She has been careful about him for twenty-five years. She has never asked him for anything. She has never let him hold a door she could have got through herself, and she has been out with him twice in twenty-five years and stood at the far end both times, and she has not once, in twenty-five years, borrowed anything from him.

He has been careful about her for twenty-five years. He has never asked her a question that he could have asked anybody else, and he has not been in a room with her for longer than about three quarters of an hour since about 2001, and when somebody he knows has mentioned her he has changed the subject, and he did that over a meal with four men in about 2011 and could not afterwards remember why.

Neither of them has been unfriendly. That is what makes it the shape it is. They are pleasant, and quick, and both of them have been pleasant and quick for twenty-five years out of a terror neither of them would be able to name.

---

And here is what happened in May.

He was at a place four streets off on a Thursday evening in May with three men he has known a long time, and one of them said something about a house that is going to be cleared.

Not that house. A different one, and it made him think about it, and about a week later he was in a place where a woman he has not spoken to since 2004 was serving.

He asked her about the umbrella.

He did not ask whether she took it. He asked whether she had ever been in that entrance.

And she said yes, on the fourth of June 1994, about half past six to about eight.

And he said, "There was an umbrella."

And she said, "Was there?"

And he said, "Blue. Long. Wooden handle."

And she said, "Mine was blue. It had a wooden handle. I left it at my sister's that night and it was gone the next morning and my sister did not know it was mine."

And he said, "Right."

And he said, "Ah."

And she said, "Yes."

And that was the whole of it. It was about a minute and a half and there were other people behind her.


He got outside and stood on the pavement.

What she had told him was not that she had taken his. It was that there had been an umbrella that was hers and that it had gone, and that the going of it had never been a thing either of the two women had ever put to anybody.

And what he understood on that pavement, in about four seconds, is that he had spent twenty-five years being careful with her about an umbrella that had gone missing at a house a long way from that one, and that it had never had anything to do with him, and that she had never once said so.

Not that she was innocent. He still does not think that. He thinks she walked off with an umbrella in her coat at about eight in the evening after three glasses and has spent twenty-five years not knowing that anybody else in that borough went away from that corner believing something about her.

Both of those can be true and he cannot hold them at the same time and neither can she.

---

And here is hers, and it is a different thing and it is worse.

What she took from that shop is not the umbrella.

What she took from it is that a man she has been careful with for twenty-five years asked her whether she had ever been in that entrance, and that he gave her the colour and the handle before she could answer, and that he knew about that umbrella.

Which means he went away from that house with something of hers on that evening, or with something he thought was hers, and he has been carrying it for a quarter of a century.

And there is no version of asking him that does not say so.

She has worked this out about nine times since May and each time she has got a little further round it, and what she has got to is that she would have to say the words *I hear you took my umbrella* to a man standing in front of her, and that he would either say yes or he would say nothing, and that both of those are a thing she cannot do at her age in front of people.

So she has done nothing, and it has been about ten weeks.


And on the thirteenth of August they were on the same pavement, and it was the fourth time in about two years, and here is what happened.

He was going to the top of that parade to see a man about a job. She was coming down it from the shops with a bag.

They met at about a quarter to twelve.

He said, "Morning."

And she said, "Morning."

And then they stood about a yard apart for about four seconds, both of them waiting for the other one to work out how long a conversation with a person like this can be, and neither of them could, and about a third of the way through the four seconds it stopped being a conversation and became a kind of stand, and it lasted about nine seconds in all.

And she said, "Well."

And he said, "Yes."

And she went on down.

That is the whole of it. There is nothing in it about 1994 and there never will be and they both know what that is.


And what he has been carrying is the wish to have not asked.

Not the wish that she had said something different. The wish that the question had not gone out of his mouth in a shop in May, because everything since that evening in May is a consequence of a sentence he put to a stranger in a place where he buys things, and before that sentence he had a comfortable arrangement, and it was a filthy arrangement and it was based on nothing at all, and he was not unhappy in it.

He has said nothing to a soul and has not gone near that shop again. He has twice started to write something down and has not finished either.

And what she has been carrying since May is a certainty she did not have in May, which is that a man has been walking about this borough for twenty-five years with an umbrella of hers in his own head, and that the reason she has never asked him for anything is not that she is careful.

It is that she is protecting him.

She has not been able to put that sentence into words in a way she can stand behind, and she has tried about four times, and the last of them was on about the fifth of August, in a kitchen, and she got as far as the end of it and then stopped.


At about four in the afternoon on the thirteenth he was on the top of that parade and the man he had come to see was not in, and he stood in the middle of that parade and thought about the front of a house he has not been inside since 1994, and about a corner where a blue umbrella was standing up on its end at half past seven on a wet evening in June 1994, and he could not remember anybody else in that house, and he could not remember any of their faces.

And what he had was the one thing he had been carrying since May, and it was this.

He has never been able to work out whether he is the man who took it or the man who lost it, and he has spent twenty-five years being careful with a woman because for twenty-five years he thought he knew which one he was.

**A MAN AND A WOMAN MET ON A PARADE ON THE TUESDAY OF THE THIRTEENTH OF AUGUST 2019 AT ABOUT A QUARTER TO TWELVE IN THE MORNING AND STOOD ABOUT A YARD APART FOR NINE SECONDS AND SAID GOOD MORNING TWICE, AND NEITHER OF THEM SAID ONE WORD ABOUT A BLUE UMBRELLA THAT WAS IN AN ENTRANCE ON THE FOURTH OF JUNE 1994.**
