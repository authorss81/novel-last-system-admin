# Chapter 0303 — Five Rooms

Friday the third of March 2017, from about twenty to eight in the morning, a kitchen at the back of a parade in Tideglass.

**HE WAS IN FIVE ROOMS BEFORE HALF PAST EIGHT IN THE EVENING, AND ONE PERSON IN A HALL OFF THAT PARADE PUT A QUESTION TO HIM THAT NOBODY HAD EVER PUT TO HIM BEFORE, AND HE ANSWERED IT, AND HE WENT HOME ON THE BUS, AND THE ONLY THING THAT CAME OF IT WAS THAT A WOMAN WITH A KEY SAW IT HAPPEN.**

There was a sinkful waiting in that kitchen and a pan in it that had been in since Wednesday, and the hot water on that side runs off a timer nobody has ever set properly, and the clock on the wall above it is wrong by a quarter of an hour, as it has been all winter.

He came in at half past seven. Washing up is what he does, and there is nothing else on his list of what he does. The woman whose sink he does had been away since the Friday before last and was not back until the Monday.

The rack was down across the sink and he turned it up and stood it on the edge of the sink, and he washed them, and when the water went cold at about nine he finished the last two in it. His hands were still split across the knuckles and they had got worse over the winter.

The fingerless woollen gloves were still in the left pocket of that cardigan where they had been since about the middle of December.

---

The floor of the shop that shuts early is his from about half past ten. That shop does the breakfast, the room at the back opens off the shop floor, and when the breakfast is finished the chairs come up and they are his to do.

That shop had five people in it at that hour. A man at the end of the counter asked him about Saturday night and he said he had nothing fixed, and the man said that was no trouble and went back to his paper, and that was the whole of it.

There was one more chair in that shop than there had been on the Tuesday.

It is the plain wooden one with the ply seat, the one with the ring on the top of the back where a hand goes to lift it, and it had been stood against the wall at the side of that room by the door and it was not in the row. The woman who runs that shop did not ask him anything about it and did not look at it twice.

He carried seven chairs through and set them against the wall by the door, and then he went back in and got the eighth one and put it on the top of that stack, the same way up and at the same angle as the others.

She looked at the stack for a second.

"Two hundred years," said the woman who runs that shop, and went back behind the counter.

Half past one took him to a kitchen in Corrance Lane with a stack of plates standing on the edge of that sink and a good deal more than plates. A man and a woman at that table wanted to know who he was, and the woman told them he was the one on the washing up, and after that nobody in that house spoke to him at all for as long as he was in it.

---

By ten past three he had a mop and a bucket and was working his way along the first floor of a building by the water, and the woman of about fifty-five was in the room at the end of that corridor with her bag on the bed and the light on.

There was a carrier bag on that landing that had not been on that landing the previous Friday. It was up against the wall opposite the room at the end, and there was a folded blanket in it and something heavy in the bottom of the blanket, and it was in the way.

"There's a woman comes on Wednesday," said the woman of about fifty-five, from inside the room, without turning round. "She does the empty one at the front. I'd want that off the landing before she comes."

"Aye," said the man of about eighteen.

He picked it up and it was heavier than a carrier bag with a blanket in it should be, and he carried it to the bottom of that stair and set it against the wall under the window, and he did not open it and he did not ask what was in it, and he came back up and cleaned that landing and the stairs, and the flickering bulb at the head of that stair did what it does, and he left it be, and he was back down that stair and out of the door by about half past four and pulled it to behind him.

The woman in the room next door along did not come out.

He was in that hall at about half past seven in the evening, nine of them or thereabouts inside, the meter on, and a lit porch outside the door.

He carried a tray round that room and everybody took a cup off it and a couple of them told him what they wanted in it.

The man in his forties had heard of that lad and had never been in a room with him, and the woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall had said in front of nine people at the end of January that there was a lad who does the dishes at the parade and comes in at about ten in the morning, and the man in his forties had said aye at the time and had gone back to the pipe under the floor.

The man in his forties put his cup down when the lad came past him with the tray for the third time.

"Which one do you do first," said the man in his forties. "This one, or the Corrance one."

"The Corrance one is half past one," said the man of about eighteen. "This shop's at ten. So this one."

"Right," said the man in his forties, and picked his cup up.

That took about eight seconds and it was the whole of it, and the man in his forties asked him nothing else that evening, and he did not ask him anything about the parade or the corridor or the stairs or the room at the end of that corridor, and the two words people use for somebody his age were not used at that table that evening.

He carried that tray round that room four times over. The man from the parade was on the far side of the table with his cup in both hands and did not say out loud the thing he has been saying since the summer. The woman of about fifty-five was in early with her coat still on. The man in his sixties was drinking a cup of tea and watching the parade through the glass. The young woman of about twenty sat at the far end and drank her tea and said nothing through any of it.

At about twenty to nine, going past the end of the table with the tray, he heard the woman of about forty-two with the key say to the man in his forties, quietly, without turning round, "You never asked me anything."

"I asked him which one he does first," said the man in his forties.

"Aye," said the woman of about forty-two. "You never asked me anything."

He did not set his cup down again and he did not turn round, and he carried the tray back to that kitchen at about ten past nine and filled it from the cold tap, washed it, and the clock on that wall was a quarter of an hour out, as usual.

**AND THE BAG WAS AT THE BOTTOM OF THAT STAIR ON THE WEDNESDAY AND THE LANDING WAS CLEAR, AND A BINDER LANDED ON A SHELF OVER BY THE WATER THAT MORNING WITH SOMEBODY'S SLEEVE CATCHING IT, AND HE WAS ON THE PARADE AT TEN WITH HIS CARDIGAN ON AND BOTH HANDS IN HIS POCKETS.**
