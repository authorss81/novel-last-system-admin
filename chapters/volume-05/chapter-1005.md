# Chapter 1005 — Four Feet Of Kerb

Undated in this draft.

**THE WEIGH PIT AT THE UNIT HAS ITS BRICK KERB GONE SOFT ALONG ONE SIDE.**

The weigh area is a pit about three foot by two foot cut into the floor of the unit's yard, and the four corners of it are covered at the edges by a chequer plate somebody laid the wrong way round, so that the rain coming off the yard roof goes into the middle of the pit instead of off the sides of it and has been doing since the plate went down. A post stands at the north end with a dial on the top of it the size of a dinner plate under a glass, and the needle on that dial has been sitting a hundredweight light since the spring, and everything weighed at it this year has gone out of that yard a hundredweight heavier than the yard actually sent it. The cause is a lump of lime gone hard in the joint of the platform plate underneath, and the lump has been in that joint since before anybody now at the unit could tell you who put it there. Round the whole of the pit there is a kerb of brick laid on edge in lime. The courses of it stand fair for about nine feet of the way round and are in line and plumb, except at the one foot of it beside the frame of the drain, which is the foot that still comes up square and which he has not touched.

Two things about the four feet on the south side are true and only one of them is his doing. The lime in the bed that kerb was laid in has gone out of it from the middle of the run outwards and has left a gap you could get a finger into along the whole of that side, and the brick in the course above the gap is sound from end to end, so what has washed out is the bed and not the brick, which is the whole difference between a job that wants a hod of cement and a job that wants a man with a bar and a week. The other thing is that he put four of those bricks out himself on a hot afternoon in the summer, for no reason anybody asked him for, and put them back badly, and has not come near it since.

There is one thing about that pit which nobody at the unit will say out loud. It was cut in the yard for the two trades that send lorries in twice a week and take their loads out again the same afternoon, and it was cut in a hurry, and the kerb round it was laid the same day by the two men who cut it. Neither of those men is here now and neither is coming back this side of the winter. That is no complaint and no threat either. It is only the shape of the thing: when a kerb wants doing, the one man on this yard who can do it is a man who does not want anything for it, and the arrangement this whole frontage has been standing in for as long as he has been on it is precisely that.

He has a lump bar and a cold chisel and a yard brush, and the yard has been his own since the others went, and he goes down into the pit on his knees with the chisel and starts at the north end of the soft run with the point in the joint, working it down and then along with his thumb behind the blade, feeling for the sound brick under the mortar before he breaks anything, because after twenty-five years a man can tell in his hand whether a joint will come out whole or come out in crumbs, and there is not much of the first sort in this run. What he finds along about the first two feet is that the mortar in the joint is not mortar at all, but a skin of lime that has gone to sand and let go of both sides of it, and it comes out under the chisel like something off a hearth. It comes out in a curve. He lays it along the yard wall in a row, because a man who throws lime dust about in his own yard is a man who comes back to it with a broom and finds the same mess plus more. Four bricks in, he stops to look at the plate over the corner, which has about half an inch of play in it now at the lipped edge and will take a heel through one wet winter. He has not told anybody this and he is not going to, and he is aware while deciding that not to tell anybody about a thing like this on his own ground is the same behaviour he has been sorry for in other people since the summer.

---

What he thought about down in that hole, with his hands in the cold dust and the yard going dark at the four corners and the rain starting again on the plate over his back, was not the kerb. It was a woman at number nine standing at her own front door in the last week before she went up the river, telling him that a man who offers is a man nobody in the world has to give an answer to, and that she had been entered on a book at that yard since she was seventeen years old and had not once in all that time been asked whether she wanted to be on it. He had gone up that frontage four times since then hoping to be asked for something. Nobody on that frontage had asked him for one thing at any point in it. What he decided down in that hole, with the point of the chisel going along the joint and his thumb telling him what was underneath it, was that he was going up to number twelve on the next fine day to ask the sister for the wall behind her mother's range. He has looked at that wall four times in a year and has never once offered it, and it is the only piece of work on that whole frontage that anybody with a book in front of them would have asked him for, and he has never offered it because offering is the easy half and he has spent twenty-five years on the easy half.

He got as far as the fourth brick and stopped, because a fourth brick out of four feet is not a thing, and he knows it better than anybody. He cannot lift four feet of kerb out in an afternoon and he will not put a stone in that bed tonight, and the unit has not a penny of what a foot of dressed kerb costs this month whatever anybody in the office believes about what the yard has spent, and the bed under what is left of the run has washed out and will not take another foot of anything until it is opened out to the bottom and filled and left to go off for a month before anybody tries to lay anything on it at all.

So the yard is open along four feet of its south side and the plate over the pit is lipping on nothing at that corner, and a man carrying a plank across that yard in the dark will put his boot through it, and nobody at the unit has any idea yet that there is anything to have an idea about, because he has told nobody and by the time there is somebody there to tell there will be nobody left in the building to hear it. He knows what he will be asked when it goes. He knows the two questions, and he has decided that the second one is the honest one and that he will answer the second one first, which is the only preparation he has made in four days and which is worth nothing at all.

He will not put anything back into that bed until somebody has shown him what a foot of stone costs, and the four feet of kerb he has taken out of the south side of his own yard is going to be lying there in the morning when he goes up the frontage to ask a woman for a wall.
