# Chapter 0429 — A Small White Saucer

Friday the twentieth of July 2018, and then every day of that week and the two weeks after it, a shop off that parade, and a van in a yard off a side street, in Tideglass.

**SHE PUT IT INTO HIS HAND AT TWENTY TO FIVE ON THAT FRIDAY AND SAID ONE WORD, HE ACCEPTED IT, AND NEITHER OF THEM SAID WHAT IT WAS OR WHY, AND ON THE FRIDAY NIGHT IT WAS IN HIS VAN AND BY THE END OF THAT WEEK HE STILL COULD NOT HAVE SAID WHY.**

The saucer is white and about five and a half inch across and has nothing on it.

It came in a tea chest out of a house in the town eight miles off in the first week of June with about ninety pieces in it, most of them a service for six that had been used every day for thirty years and were exactly what they said. Something over thirty pieces of that went in the first three days. The rest went over the following four weeks except this.

The chip is on the rim at about two o'clock and it is the size of a thumbnail and it goes right through the glaze and about a sixteenth of an inch into the body, so that it is a hole and not a stain. Inside there is crazing over about two-thirds of the well, which is what happens to a glaze that has been through a dishwasher twenty thousand times. In the middle of the well there is a brown ring about an inch and a half across which is a tea ring and not a mark of fire.

It is not valuable. It is not old. It is not worth anything to anybody in this world, and if a woman has said it is not, then it is not.

She had it on the sill behind that counter for six weeks. She moved it twice. In the first week she put it at the back of the sill out of the way of the light. In the third week she brought it forward because the back of that sill is where she puts the odds and ends that nobody has asked about, and she had begun to be aware of it every time she turned round, which is the ordinary way a small object becomes a thing in a room.

On the Friday she was not thinking about it. She was thinking about a man in her forties who had been in that shop at about ten in the morning and had asked her to hold a bundle of dowel until about half past four, because he had four things to do across two streets first and did not want it left against the front of the shop.

---

At about twenty to five on the Friday afternoon he came back for it.

He came in through the front and stood at the side of that counter with his left arm up across his chest holding a bundle of twelve lengths of dowel, planed, an inch and a quarter square, tied round twice with a piece of twine that he had twisted and doubled himself, which took up more room than the dowel did.

His right hand was free because he had put the money for it in the left pocket of his coat.

She went round the end of the counter, which she does not do, and she had that saucer in her right hand, and she said, "Here."

She put it into his right hand.

It went in palm down, the way a thing is handed over when the person handing it is not thinking about how it is handed over, and his hand was not there in any sense of the word — it was hanging at the side of him because the other one was full — and it closed round the saucer anyway, because a hand does that about four times in a minute without asking.

He said nothing at all. He did not look at it and he did not look at her and he did not say what it was, and she did not say what it was either, and his hand went down at his side and the saucer went on sitting in it as it walked away from that counter.

Then she said, "That's the lot for the week," meaning the dowel, and he said, "Right," and turned round and went out, and the whole of it took about four seconds.

She went back round the counter. She put her hand on the sill where the saucer had been and left it there for a second, and then she did a job on the counter that was already half done, and she did not think about it again until the following Tuesday, and by then it had become one of the things she has done in this borough and does not go into.

He went out to the van with about nine foot of dowel on his left arm and a small white saucer in his right hand.

He got into the driver's seat and put the bundle on the floor in front of the passenger seat, twisted round about four times until it fitted, and then he stood in the driver's doorway and looked at the saucer in his hand for about three or four seconds.

It is the first time anybody can say he looked at it. He turned it over once, so that he could see the back of it, which is plain, and then he put it down on the front of the passenger seat face down, which is what a man does with a saucer in a van, and then he shut the door and drove off to a house up past the last of the shops.

---

It was on the front of that passenger seat when he got back at about ten that night and it was on the front of that passenger seat when he started it again at about twenty to seven the next morning, and it was on the front of that passenger seat at about two o'clock that afternoon, and at about half past four that afternoon he moved it.

He moved it because he wanted the seat for a bag of sandpaper, and he put it in the door pocket on the offside, which had a folding rule in it and nothing else, face down, and it went in on top of the rule at a bit of an angle and stayed.

He shut the door pocket and went and did a job.

On the Monday morning he opened that door pocket at about a quarter to eight to get the rule out and it was in there, and he did not move it, and he shut the pocket again.

On the Wednesday afternoon he opened that door pocket for the rule and it was in there, and he shut the pocket again.

On the Friday he opened it to put a bit of offcut in that he had brought back off a site because it was a good bit of oak and he was keeping it for something, and he had to move the saucer about four inches to get his hand round the rule, and he moved it, and he put it back where it had been sitting, at the angle it had been sitting at, and he shut the pocket.

He has not decided anything about it.

He does not know why she gave it to him. He has not asked her, and he has not been asked, and he does not intend to ask her, and there is no version of the next month in which he says the word saucer to that woman.

He has not worked out what a person does with a thing like that. He has not thrown it out. He has not taken it home. He has not put it on the shelf in the kitchen of the house he rents on a side street, which is where a man puts a saucer if he decides to keep one. It is in a door pocket in a van in a yard off a side street, face down, at an angle, on top of a folding rule.

The yard is let to three men and the van is his and the pocket shuts and that is the whole of what exists between the man and the object.

What is not true, and what a man might expect to be true about a thing that has been put into his hands by somebody who works in a shop, is that she wanted him to have it. She did not want him to have it. She has wanted about nine people to have it. The first two were told the chip, the third was told the chip, the fourth took it away in a bag and put it in a cupboard in a house in the town eight miles off and has not mentioned it since, and the other five were not offered it at all because they came in for something else and left.

Being handed something does not put you on a list. There is nothing in his week about that saucer. There is no way of earning it and no way of spending it, no way of finding out whether it was a thing she had been meaning to give somebody for weeks or a thing she picked up off the sill with her other hand while she was coming round the counter. He is not going to carry it anywhere. It is not going into a house. He is not going to break it. He is going to leave it in a door pocket, and he is going to keep leaving it there, and the pocket shuts, and that is all that is going to happen to it as far as either of them is concerned.

**HE SHUT THAT POCKET AND WENT TO WORK, AND ON THE FRIDAY NIGHT OF THAT WEEK IT WAS STILL IN IT, AND HE COULD NOT HAVE TOLD YOU ONE SINGLE REASON WHY.**
