# Chapter 0355 — Half an Hour Each

Monday the twenty-fifth of September 2017, from about ten in the morning until about half past four in the afternoon, a shop off a parade and the back room of it, in Tideglass.

**A RACK AT THE BACK OF THAT SHOP WAS PUT OFF THE GOING BY A WOMAND ON A SUNDAY MORNING IN AUGUST AND A DOOR OFF THE SAME SHOP WAS DROPPED ON ITS HINGES BY A MAN ON A SUNDAY MORNING IN SEPTEMBER, AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS SAID ONE WORD ABOUT EITHER, AND THE TWO OF THEM ARE THE SAME SIZE.**

The shop has a counter with a card machine on it and a rack of work gloves behind the till, and a doorway at the back with a bead curtain on it, and behind that a room about twice the length of the counter with a table in it and a steel rack against the far wall.

The steel rack is a manufacturer's thing, four shelves, painted grey, and it stands on four feet and the front two of those feet have sunk into a floor that is not level, so the whole thing has been going towards the door since before the summer. There are about nine boxes on it and about four things that are not boxes.

The door at the far end of that room goes out to a yard. It is a fire door, painted, with a steel hinge on each side, and the bottom hinge has dropped about half an inch over a good many years and the door has been catching on the frame at the bottom corner ever since.

In the middle of August the man in his thirties was in that shop with the woman who works in a shop behind the counter, he holding a bag and she holding a pen, and she said the rack at the back was going over, in the way a person says a thing they have been meaning to say for some weeks.

He said he would see to it when he was next in.

Neither of them said anything else about it then. He has not been in that back room since the sixth of August and she has not mentioned the rack to him since the day she mentioned it.

He said he would see to it when he was next in.

He has not been in that back room since the sixth of August and she has not mentioned the rack to him since the day she mentioned it.

She did it herself on the Sunday morning of the fourteenth of August. The shop was shut and she had the key and she was in there at about half past seven with a folding step and a junior saw, and she cut two wedges off the end of a length of oak that was standing on end behind the table, and each wedge is about four inch long and about the thickness of two fingers, and she drove them under the front two feet and pushed the rack back until it was square with the wall, and it has not been on the go since.

That is about half an hour's work and she was in there for about half an hour.

She has not told anybody. There is nobody in that shop but the two of them, and the man behind the counter was not in that week, and there is no book in that shop and nobody has ever written one down.

In the third week of September he came in with his bag and was in that room for about ten minutes on his own business and she heard him at the door at the back and went in to see whether he wanted anything out of the rack.

The bottom of that door was off the floor by about half an inch and the hinge had been packed, and the door came to as he put his hand on it and it went the way a door goes when the thing it was catching on is no longer there.

That is about half an hour's work as well. It took him three screws and a packer and a screwdriver and it was done on a Sunday in the middle of September at about eight in the evening when the yard was empty.

He has not mentioned it to her. She has not asked him about it.

She has been through that door perhaps twice a day since the middle of September and she has not once said anything about how it goes.

---

He came into the shop at about ten past two with a bag and asked her for a length of three by two and she went to the rack behind the till for the tape and found that the box that wanted to be at the back had moved itself to the middle and had a tin of screws in it that had no business being there.

She moved the tin and came back round the counter with the tape and he took the length of board out of the shop and put it under his arm, and neither of them said anything at all.

The shop went on like that until about half past three and he was still there when a woman came in for a roll of brown paper and he went out to the front and read the top line of every card on that rack twice, and came back in and stood at the end of the counter with his bag on the boards.

The man in his forties came into that shop at about ten past four and asked for a bag of plugs, and the man in his thirties was still at the other end of the counter with his bag.

"Do you two ever shift that rack," said the woman who works in a shop, and she said it to the man in his forties, and she was pointing with her chin at the back room.

"It is not in my way," said the man in his forties.

"It is not in anybody's way," she said. "It has been going over since the summer."

The man in his forties said that was up to whoever had it up, and put the bag of plugs on the counter, and the man in his thirties said that a rack like that wants a foot under it and not a nail, and the woman who works in a shop said that a rack like that wanted doing in July, and the man in his forties said that it did, and the man in his thirties said that it did, and nobody said anything else about that rack at any time after that.

What neither of the three of them knew, and what the woman who works in a shop has not said out loud to anybody including herself, is that the rack has got two wedges under the front of it and that they are oak and about four inch long, and that she cut them off the end of a length at about half past seven on a Sunday morning when the shop was shut and there was nobody in the building.

The man in his forties went out of that shop a little before five. The man in his thirties left at about ten past four and went out into the parade and stood on the step for a second with the board under his arm and then went on down it.

At about a quarter to five the woman who works in a shop took a cardigan off the back of a chair and went into the back room and stood in the doorway for about half a minute.

The rack is square with the wall. There are two things on the bottom shelf that are not boxes and they are on the side nearest her and she put them there herself in June.

There are two things on the bottom shelf that are not boxes and they are on the side nearest her and she put them there herself in June, and they are a bag of cable ties and a tin of the size a tin of cream comes in, and they are on the side of that rack a person takes things off.

The door to the yard is shut and it came to when she put her hand on it and went the way it goes.

She stood in that doorway for about half a minute with the cardigan over her arm and then she went back out and shut the bead curtain and put the cardigan on the chair and went and did the money on the counter with the machine off.

She did not say anything about either of those two things to anybody, then or since, and she is not going to.

There is no version of that afternoon in which either of those two men walked out with anything, and no version in which the rack is leaning and the door is catching, and both of those are the case, and one of them was put right by a person who was in that building at half past seven on a Sunday morning in August on her own and one of them was put right by a person who was in that yard at eight in the evening on a Sunday in September on his own, and the two of them are the same size, and not one of the two is going to be the first of them to put that into words.

**THE CARDIGAN WENT BACK ONTO THE CHAIR AND THE BEAD CURTAIN CAME STILL AND THE MACHINE CAME ON, AND UNDER THE FRONT OF THAT RACK THERE ARE TWO OAK WEDGES THAT NOBODY IN THIS BOROUGH HAS EVER SEEN.**
