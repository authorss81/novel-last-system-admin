# Chapter 0826 — A Straight Edge With Nothing Behind It

Thursday the sixth of July 2023, at that roller door from the middle of the morning until the light has gone off the apron, and a broken edge to a yard that is going to be dug is the whole of that Thursday.

**THE APRON OUTSIDE THE ROLLER DOOR AT THE LOCK UP IS BROKEN FOR ABOUT A FOOT AND SHE MEANS TO CUT THE EDGE STRAIGHT AND TAKE THAT FOOT UP AND DIG OUT WHAT IS UNDER IT, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS IN THE OFFICE WITH THE DOOR SHUT ALL DAY AND IS NOT SEEN.**

That apron is a slab of old work in front of the shutter, about as wide as the door and about three feet deep, laid to fall away from the guides so that rain runs off it into the yard.

She has stood on that apron with the shutter half up a hundred times and never once thought about what it is made of, which is a foot and a half of hardcore and a slab over it laid in bays with a mesh in the middle of the bays and nothing else under it but the hardcore. It is not a floor anybody made much of. It is a place to stand while a shutter is up.

For about a foot at the near end it has broken. The top has crazed into small squares and the squares have lifted at the corners, and one corner has gone altogether, leaving a hole the size of a fist where the base shows through.

The crazing is not the fault. A slab laid in bays in a bad winter will craze in a grid like that whatever is under it, because the bays are a foot and a half across and the slab shrinks as it cures. The fault is that the squares have lifted at the corners, and a square that lifts at the corners has nothing under it any more, and a slab with nothing under it over a yard the size of this one is going to go on moving with every lorry that comes over the hard standing.

She starts with a chalk line and a straightedge, and a disc cutter hired for the day from the yard down the road, with a mask and gloves and a bucket of sand for the dust.

The line goes across the apron about a foot back from the broken end, square to the guides, and she marks it twice and checks it against both sides before she cuts. A cut edge wants to be straight because it will be seen every time the shutter goes up, and a crooked one shows for years.

Square to the guides is the only squareness she has. The guides are the two channels the shutter runs down, and they are set by the wall and not by her, and they are the one thing in that front that a hundred and forty pounds of curtain has kept in the same line for twenty years. If the cut is square to them it will look right from the road and it will look right from inside the bay on the door, and those are the two places anybody stands.

The cutter goes through in two passes, slow, with the dust coming up white and settling on her boots. She keeps the blade on the line and does not force it, and the second pass drops through into the base with a different note.

The first note is a scream and the second is a knock, and the difference between them is the slab going and the base going, and once she has heard it there is no going back to sanding the edge down and starting again at a different line.

Then the foot comes up in lumps with a bar and a sledge, into the barrow, and under it there is base.

A cut edge in a slab wants the material taken out from under it in pieces, not in one. She sets the bar in from the end of the cut and levers each lump up and tips it off the edge into the barrow, and the lumps are the size of a fist and a half, because the slab breaks where it is thin and she is not going to chase the pieces with a breaker.

Here is what she has to look at, and it is the middle of the day before she sees it right.

Under the apron the base is hard. It is broken brick and rubble packed tight, dry, with sand in the joints, and the bar will hardly go into it. She tries it in three places along the foot and in all three the bar goes in an inch and stops dead, with a ring off the steel that she feels in her shoulders.

A bar that goes into a base and stops and rings is a bar that has found something it cannot get past. Broken brick laid loose and pressed down goes into the bar the whole way and the bar comes out with brick dust on it and no noise at all. This has been packed, and it has been packed long enough that the pieces have bedded into one another and the sand in the joints has gone to glue.

Under the bay, inside, the base is nothing like it. She knows that ground from the winter, when the ply went over the dip and the dip was soft under it. That ground gives under a boot after rain and takes a footprint. This ground under the apron does not give at all.

She stands in the yard with the bar in her hand and looks from the hole to the shutter and back, because the two ought to be the same and they are not, and there is no reason on the face of it why outside work should be on harder ground than inside work that has had a roof over it for twenty years.

There is one answer that fits the day she has had and she does not believe it. The rain that comes down the slope of the hard standing goes out across the front of that apron and off the edge, and twenty years of it has washed the fines out of the top inch of that base and left the brick pieces sitting on the bottom with nothing between them, and a washed-out base dries as a solid-looking thing and gets hard under a boot. The base under the bay has had a roof over it and no water through it and it has stayed as it was put down, which was as a mess.

She could be right about that and she has no way of proving it without putting a hole in the apron and finding out, which is a different job on a different day and not this one. She does not pretend to know why. The ground is what it is, and the apron end was on the best bit of it and broke anyway, which tells her what she needs about tops and bottoms.

The other answer is that somebody made a good job of it, and she has no way of disproving that either, and a woman who has been in that yard a hundred times has no business deciding which of two stories about a foot of base is the true one on the strength of a bar going in an inch.

What is left of the morning goes on the hole. She digs the base out to the depth of a spade blade, square sided, and barrows it round the back, and sweeps the cut edge clean with a brush until the line shows white.

Square sided is the whole of it and it is what the bar will not do. The bar cannot pick out rubble a piece at a time, and a spade cut straight down four sides leaves a hole with four corners in it, and four corners is what a hole wants if it is going to sit open through a wet winter and not slump out into the yard one side of it.

The dust settles all afternoon and she keeps throwing the bucket of sand over the edge of the cut so that nobody walks on the line. The slab is three feet deep and a foot of it is gone, and what is left of it now ends in air over a hole a spade deep, and that is a thing that will be looked at by everybody who comes through that gate.

The office door stays shut the whole of it. The shutter is up over the roller door from first thing, and behind it the woman who books work is in there all day and is not seen, and no voice comes through except once when the kettle goes on, which is a thing that happens in an office and is not a talk.

“Put the dust sheet over the bench before you go,” she calls through the door, and the kettle clicks off behind it.

The shutter comes down when the light has gone off the apron, and it comes down with the heat still in the slab and the dust still on it, and the sound of it is the sound of that bay every evening and there is nothing in the sound she has not heard a thousand times.

---

By the time the light has gone off the apron the foot is up and the hole is square and the base out of it is in a heap round the back under a sheet.

She runs a finger along the cut edge from one guide to the other. It is straight enough to lay a rule against, with no tooth in it wider than a thumbnail, and the top of it is level with the rest of the slab.

The dust sheet is over the bench inside, and the sheet over the heap at the back is pegged down at the corners because there is a wind off the yard at the end of the day that will take a sheet full of rubble and put it across the hard standing.

And the hole is square and a spade deep with its four corners in and nothing in it but air, and the hard base is in a heap at the back of the yard, and the two of them are not going to be put back in the same order, and she has not found out why and is not going to on a Thursday.
