# Chapter 0795 — The Stile Out Of Square

Friday the twenty-fourth of February 2023, in this borough. He takes his near gate off its hinges at the end of the afternoon and it stands on two trestles in his own yard through the last of the light, and the whole of that day is the stile that has gone out of square.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES HAS A GATE ON TWO HINGES THAT HAS TO BE LIFTED TO BE SHUT AND MEANS TO PACK THE HINGE STILE WITH WEDGES AND SCREW IT TIGHT, THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN TAKES THE GATE OFF ITS HINGES AND GETS THE STILE BACK INTO SQUARE WITH A BRACE BOLTED ACROSS TWO CORNERS AND LET IN SO THAT IT IS PART OF THE FRAME AND NOT A STICK LAID ON THE FRONT OF IT, AND HANGS IT BACK ON NEW BUTTONS SO THAT IT SHUTS WITH A HAND'S WEIGHT AND NOT A SHOULDER.**

The place is his and he has been in his early forties for years. The woman from two doors down comes up the yard behind him in the last of the afternoon without knocking, because the gate in the gap stands open and is the only way in or out, and because he has been in that yard since the middle of the morning with a box of hardwood wedges under the bench and nothing done about anything.

---

There are two gates along the front of that yard and neither of them is a good gate.

The one in the gap is standing open and has been since the middle of February, because that one will not come home either, and when he shuts it he puts his whole arm into it and it swings over and stops a hand's width out from the frame and stays there. The latch is hooked back over that frame and there is nothing on that frame at all. The near gate at the near end is a different thing altogether and it is the one that has to be lifted.

The store door stands open on its new bottom rail with the brush along the head of it and a quarter of an inch of daylight behind a yard of woven brush. On the bench the length of oak he has been cutting to is lying with its shoulder out of the square and the shoulder will not come square with anything he owns. The shut drawer has the bagged brush down on the bottom of it and the bins along the front wall are full of offcuts and there is a length of scaffold batten in the second bin that has been in there since the autumn. The gate in the gap leans on its post where it has leaned since the spring, and the chalk on it has gone down to two marks and a clean square. The post that gate hangs on was dug out in the winter and packed and packed, and as far as he can tell it is exactly where it was put, and he has leaned on it hard enough twice since and it has not shifted.

He has been shutting that one hard every time he comes through it since the middle of February and kicking the bottom of it, and there is a fistful of cold air coming under it every time, and it has to be lifted as well as pushed.

His plan is the wedges.

He has a box of them under the bench. He means to take that gate off its hinges in the spring, pack the hinge stile tight all the way down with hardwood, screw the butt of each hinge to the frame again, and put the gate back so that it comes home on a packed joint and stays there. There is nothing wrong with that idea. Packing a stile is what packing is for.

---

"So you are packing the hinge stile."

"I am packing the hinge stile."

"With what."

"With wedges." She has come about as far as the trestles. "Hardwood. I have a box of them under the bench and half of them are the right taper."

"And what will you have done when you have done it."

"I will have had that gate packed and screwed and it will shut."

"It will shut." She is standing at the gate with her hand on the top rail of it and she is not looking at him. "And in August it will shut on the same packing, because that packing has not gone anywhere and nothing about it is any different. And in the August after that you will be back with a chisel."

---

He does not take that seriously at first, and then he looks at the gate properly for the first time since the summer.

The gate is four feet by three and it is a rectangle and it is not a rectangle. It has hung on those two hinges all winter with all of its own weight coming down on the far corner, and that corner has dropped about three inches and gone out from under the frame in the same twenty years, and the two sides of it that were cut to be the same length are a good deal out. Where the slats meet the rails the joints have opened on the diagonal and you can see daylight through the top joint on the latch side.

If he packs that stile he is packing a stile that is carrying a load across it. The load is not straight down the stile. It comes off the far end of the gate and across to the hinge side on the diagonal, and the packing is across that diagonal and does nothing about it.

---

Then they take it off its hinges, and it takes the two of them and it takes most of the afternoon.

He stands at the latch end and gets his shoulder under it and takes the weight. She knocks the top pin out of the top strap with a drift and a hammer, because you cannot lift a gate off a pin that is still in. Then they walk it forward off the hinges together and lay it face up on the two trestles with its stiles running along them and the bottom rail overhanging at one end, which is where she wants it.

He stands there with the whole weight of that gate coming down his shoulder while she gets the bottom pin out, and that is the whole of why it takes two.

---

Then she squares it, which is a different thing from packing it.

She does not do it by driving anything. She puts a long brace in first and lets the brace do the work.

She has a length of softwood with her, about five feet and square in section and about an inch and a half through, which is as near as a bit of a batten goes to the length of that corner. She lays it across the frame from the bottom of the hinge stile to the top of the latch stile and marks where it crosses each of them, and she takes a chisel and cuts a housing in each stile for it — not a slot in the face, but a shallow bed cut into the thickness of the wood on the inside of the frame, so that the brace goes down into the timber and part of it is inside the gate and part of it stands out on the inside, and you can see the joint at each end and it is not a stick laid on the front of a gate.

"You have taken the corner out to put it back."

"I have taken the corner out and I am going to put it back square, and the brace is going to be the thing that keeps it square once it is." She turns the brace over in her hands. "That gate hangs on two hinges and everything about it leans. It has leaned since it was put up and it leaned on the packing, and the packing has gone, and now it leans on nothing at all and you have to lift it."

"Where does it lean to."

"It leans across. The weight is off that far corner and it is pulling that corner down and away from the frame at the same time, and the two of those are the same movement." She puts the brace back into the housings and looks along the top rail. "A packing takes the two ends of a stile and does nothing about the ends of a gate. A brace takes the ends of a gate."

---

They bring the corners in and they bring them in on the brace.

He keeps a hand on the latch end while she draws that corner back up against its housings with a bar and a clamp, and puts one screw in at each end of the brace, one through it into the rail behind and one through it into the stile it crosses. The corner comes back up about three inches, the top joint closes over daylight, the bottom joint closes over daylight, and the two sides of that gate measure the same for the first time since it was built.

Then she gauges it, which she does twice.

She puts a straightedge down the two long sides and works it along until there is no daylight anywhere under it, and then she puts the other one across the two short ends and works that along too, and then she runs the flat of her hand down each joint in turn. She does not do it in a hurry and she does not do it twice the same way. Then she takes out the two wedges she drew that corner in with, and the frame does not move, because the brace is in it and bolted at both ends.

"You have taken the packing out of that gate and put it in the bin."

"That is what a brace is for. There is nothing in that frame now but the timber and the two bolts, and it does not want anything else in it."

---

Then she puts it back on its hinges, and the hinges are not the same either.

The buttons that gate hangs on are out on the pier behind it, a plate with a thread on it that the strap turns on, and there are two of them and they have had a gate swinging about on them for twenty years and the wood round each of them has gone soft and dark. She puts a fresh button in at each end, a little further apart than the old ones and a little lower, so that the gate hangs plumb against that pier instead of hanging out over the front of it.

He takes the weight again and she works the pins, and then she has him let it go without touching it, and it comes down on its own and hangs there with the far corner where it should be and not three inches below the hinge side.

"It comes home."

"It comes home because the frame is square and the buttons are right," she says. "Take a brace out of that gate and put it on another one and the other one will hang on its own buttons. It is not the hinges that make a gate swing. It is the far end of it."

---

Then she shuts it, to show him, and it shuts with one hand.

Not with her shoulder and not with her boot. She puts her palm flat on the boards and walks it and it comes across on its own and drops onto the stop.

He does it four times with the palm of his hand and every one of them it comes across on its own, and on the fourth of them the latch goes into nothing and comes back up again, because that frame has nothing on it at all.

The gate is square. The latch end is level with the hinge end. It comes across on one hand and drops onto the stop and sits there, and from the lane you would not know there was anything whatever done to it.

---

Then she goes out of the gate in the gap and up the lane, and there is one thing left on his bench that was not there when she came.

It is the only thing in that yard tonight he could not have done. It is lying on that bench with a housing cut in each end of it and both faces scuffed from being in a gate and taken out of one, off the short end of a batten that came out of his own second bin this afternoon. She cut it. She gauged it. She bored it and counterbored it.

He stands in the middle of his own yard with his hand flat on the boards and works out where the far corner was before it came back up — two fingers of a bar from the bottom rail and a foot and a half up the stile, and about an hour with the door open to the light.

Then he goes out to the gate in the gap, because it is standing open on its post and it is the only way in or out of that yard.

It comes across as far as it ever has and stands a hand's width short of the frame and stays there. He puts the flat of his hand on the boards and walks it twice and it does the same both times, and the latch is hooked back over a frame that has nothing on it at all, and that leaf has been leaning on that post since the spring and its far corner has come down about as far as this one's did.

He leaves the gate open. It is the only way in and out and it will be in the morning.
