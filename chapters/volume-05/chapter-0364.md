# Chapter 0364 — The Chair on the Other Side

Tuesday the thirty-first of October 2017, from about half past seven in the morning until about six in the evening, a parade, a hall on Carrow Lane, a building by the water, and the low end of a street, in Tideglass.

**A FORTNIGHT WENT THROUGH THIS BOROUGH AND NINE PEOPLE DID THEIR ROUNDS AND THE SAME THING HAPPENED AT THE SAME HOURS, AND A CHAIR IN THAT HALL ON CARROW LANE IS ON THE OTHER SIDE OF THAT ROOM TO WHERE IT WAS FOURTEEN DAYS AGO, AND THE MAN WHO PUTS THOSE CHAIRS OUT CANNOT SAY WHEN IT MOVED, AND A LINE UNDER THE LAST ENTRY OF A BOOK IN A BAY AT THE END OF A CORRIDOR IS STILL BLANK.**

The man who puts shutters up was on that parade at about half past seven with his ladder, worked his way along, and left the third shop until the end of the morning, which is the one that always takes the longest, and has for about nine years.

At about ten the woman who works in a shop was out on that parade with the pad in her hand, and a man came in and had four by two sawn to length at her counter, and she said a number to him while the saw was still going and he did not answer it. That is roughly the fifteenth time that number has come out of that mouth at that counter. It has not moved since the twelfth of January, and between the two of them they could not name two of the four.

The man in his forties was in that shop at about half past ten for a bag of plugs and gave the woman who works in a shop an account of about two thirds of a floor laid in a yard off the Cleeve road and coming up short, and she said short of what, and he said of nothing, and she looked at him, and he paid for the plugs and went out into the parade.

The man in his thirties went through that archway three times in the fortnight, early in the morning, in the middle of the afternoon and again towards the end of the day, and stopped in it none of the three times.

The man who works nights stood at that stop for the first bus on the Monday wearing his coat, and the bus that came was not the one he wanted. It stopped, it went on, and he stayed on the pavement for half an hour after it. Three Mondays in a fortnight have gone that way and not one person at that stop has said a word about it to him.

About nine people in that fortnight were carrying nothing at all, and not one of them was carrying what any of the others was carrying. A shopkeeper behind a counter. A tradesman with a bag. A man who does a hall. A man in his sixties with a cup on a Wednesday. A man who puts shutters up. A man who works nights. A man at the end of a street. A man in a doorway with a bag of chips and his back to the wall. A woman going up and down a concrete stair with a jug in each hand, who has not been near a bay at the end of anything since before the hot weather and is not going to.

---

The hall on Carrow Lane has about nine chairs with green seats and wooden frames, and it opens from ten until four, and the man who does that hall has every one of those chairs hanging on the wall by ten with the urn going, and works round the tables with a cloth over one shoulder.

He hangs them against the wall as he comes round from the back at that end, nine of them, always the same nine, always in the same order, and it has been like that for the best part of a year.

A fortnight ago the chair with the split across the back rail was the fourth one along on the left.

It is the second one along on the right.

He put the chairs out on the Monday, and the chairs were where they are now, and he has put that set of chairs out about a hundred and sixty times since the start of the year and he has never once had to think about a chair.

He looked at that chair when he was in that hall on the Wednesday and it was on the other side and he moved it about four inch to make a line of it with the other one and then took his hand off it and left it.

He has not thought about it since. If anybody had asked him about it he would have said that a chair is a chair and that somebody had it the wrong way round for a day, and he would have been wrong, and he would have said it the same way tomorrow.

A chair on the other side of a hall is the only thing in that fortnight that is different, and it is different now, and it is not possible to say when it became different, and there is no book in that hall and no drawing on the wall of that hall and no person in that hall who was watching a chair on a Wednesday.

The man in his sixties was in there on the Wednesday with his cup for about fifty minutes in the same place at the back and the chair that is on the right was on the right the whole time he was in that room and he did not notice, and the man who does that hall said good afternoon to him on the way out and he said good afternoon back.

---

Half past five was the time he let himself into that building with his own key. He went up the concrete stair outside it, went the length of the landing, and stood short of the far end of it with his hands in his pockets and his bag still on his shoulder.

The door of the bay at the end of that corridor stands open the way it stands open, on a hinge that has not shut over its catch since before the summer, and the book is on the second shelf up in there on that door with a lamp written against it and nothing under that line.

That register has not been added to since. It stands at nineteen, which is all of it, and the whole of what it still says at the end is a yard lamp, entered as lost a couple of Novembers back, against a heading somebody had held open for that class of loss. Not one further line has been written against that heading. Past that last entry the paper goes a little way and then gives out, well short of where the foot of that volume is, and nobody has ever tidied the gap and the book has gone on carrying it.

He did not go into the bay. He stood in the corridor with his back to the wall and counted to himself, the way a man counts a thing he has counted before, and the figure is one thousand and seventy days, and that is a line under a blank line in a book he has not opened and is not going to open, and it is not anybody's figure and it is not a figure anybody has asked him for.

He has done that count three times since the weather turned. He did it once on the sixteenth of August, on his own, in that bay, and wrote nothing down afterwards. Somewhere in the middle of the summer he was told that somebody else had worked the same sum out on a staircase and had said a number out loud in that hall in July. He did not ask who had said it, and he has not asked since.

The blank line under that last entry is the only thing in that building which has not shifted since the winter. It was empty at the start of this fortnight and it is empty now, nobody has put anything on it, and not one person in this borough would want to.

He went back down the concrete stair, drew that door to behind him without shutting it properly, and walked up the parade. Outside the hall on Carrow Lane the light put itself on somewhere around a quarter to six, and it was still burning at twenty past six the following morning.

A fortnight is a fortnight. There is no term running out anywhere in that parade, nobody has been asked anything, nothing has been minuted, and four answers to a question are still going round this borough and not one of them can be put beside any of the other three.

The woman of about fifty-five was on that outside stair at half past seven that evening with her two jugs hooked through each other, the way she has done twice a day since June, and drew the street door back to behind her down at the bottom, and nobody on that stair stood in her way and she put her hand out for nothing, and the bay at the end of that landing is still open and the book is still on the second shelf up in there with a lamp written against it and nothing under that line.

**A MAN CAME DOWN AN OUTSIDE STAIR AT SIX O'CLOCK WITH HIS HANDS IN HIS POCKETS AND WENT UP A PARADE PAST NINE SHOPS THAT WERE ALREADY SHUT, AND A CHAIR IN A HALL FOUR HUNDRED YARD OFF IS STILL ON THE OTHER SIDE OF THAT ROOM FROM WHERE IT WAS, AND THE ONLY PERSON WHO WOULD BE ABLE TO SAY WHEN IS THE MAN WHO PUTS THEM OUT AND HE SAYS A CHAIR IS A CHAIR.**
