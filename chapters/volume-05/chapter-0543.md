# Chapter 0543 — What the Man With the Firewood Said

The Friday of the twenty-fourth of January 2020, from about ten in the morning until about one in the afternoon, in a workshop off one street in this borough.

**A WORKSHOP OFF A STREET HERE BELONGS TO TWO MEN IN THEIR MID SIXTIES AND HAS DONE SINCE 1979 AND NEITHER OF THEM CAN NAME WHAT HE OWES THE OTHER HALF OF, AND IN JANUARY THE ELDER BROTHER SETTLED THE WHOLE THING IN ONE VISIT WITHOUT TELLING ANYBODY, AND THE YOUNGER LEARNED OF IT THIS MORNING FROM A THIRD MAN WHO HAD SOLD HIM KINDLING.**

They are both in their mid sixties and the older is three years the worse for it. Their uncle had the workshop from 1954 and left it to the two of them in the November of 1979, three weeks after he died, and neither of them wanted it and both of them could not afford the other one to have it.

So they shared it the way they have shared everything since. The workshop is worked in by one or the other or by both of them and it is not worked in by anybody else, and there is not a word of writing about any part of it anywhere, and there never has been.

Once a year there is a figure. It goes to the owner of the building at the back and it has to go in the January because that is when the owner says it goes in. They have never argued about it. They have never agreed a day. The older one has always paid first and the younger one has always paid the other half two or three days later out of the same money that is not there, and it has worked like that since 1979.

Neither of them would describe it as anything. It has never had a name and the naming has never mattered. If anybody asked them what the arrangement was they would both say there is no arrangement, and they would both be telling the truth, and they would both find it hard to explain how it is that it has not fallen apart.



What happened on the eighth of January is this.

The younger one went to see the owner on the Wednesday as he does, and the owner said that the figure for the year was more than last year's, and the younger one said right, and went to his bank, and drew out the whole of it, and paid the whole of it in one go, and said nothing to his brother.

He has said nothing since. Not to his brother and not to anybody else. He has not mentioned it in the car on a Sunday, which is when they talk, and he has not mentioned it to the man who does the roof over it, and he has not mentioned it to his wife, and she has not asked because he has not looked like a man with something.



The younger one found out at ten past ten on the Friday, in an open space at the end of a street, from a man in his early fifties who sells firewood and coal and has sold both to that workshop every year since about 1998.

The younger one goes there every year about the middle of January. It is the one thing in the calendar that is in his own name, and it has been there since 2001 and he does it in a van in his own clothes and takes about twenty minutes.

The man had the last of the coal in and they were talking about whether there was any left for a house at the top of that street, and then the man said, in the middle of it, without any particular weight on it at all:

"Your brother was in here on the eighth."

And the younger one said, "Was he."

And the man said, "He wasn't after wood. He came for a sack of kindling and he was not in a hurry, which for him is a thing."

And the younger one said, "Right."

And the man said, "He came to tell me the yearly figure's been seen to."

And then the man stopped, because he had worked out about a second late that there was something in the sentence he had not been meant to be the one to hand over, and he put his palm flat on the sacking and looked across at the younger one and said, "Right," in exactly the same tone.



The younger one put the notes down on the wooden thing by the office door and came away with the kindling stacked in the back of the van.

He drove to the top of that street and parked and sat in the van for about ten minutes. He did not ring his brother. He sat there and looked at nothing and worked out what he was going to do about it and the answer was that he was not going to do anything about it.

Then he drove to the workshop and put the kindling inside the door and went out again and got back in the van.

It is about a mile from the workshop to his own house and it is a road he has driven on and off every Friday morning since 2001 and he has not been able to say what was different about it until he had gone a quarter of the way.

What was different is that he had gone out.

He has gone into the workshop on the Friday of every week since 1979, every Friday of his working life and then some. He has gone in and stood there and the other one has been at the far end working, and they have got on with what they were doing and he has stayed a bit and gone.

He did not go in on the twenty-fourth of January. He put the kindling inside the door and he left and he drove away and he has not been back.

There is one more thing about the twenty-fourth of January and it happened at about one o'clock and it was not deliberate.

The older one finished what he was doing at the far end of that workshop at about ten to one and went out to get the back of the van and found a sack of kindling standing inside the door with about two pounds of it spilled out of the top onto the floor.

He stood looking at that sack for about a minute and a half.

Then he picked up the spilled wood and put it back in, and he took the sack and put it against the side where the wood goes, which is where kindling goes, and he went out and got into the van and went home.

He does not know why it was there. He has a fair idea that it was left by somebody who buys wood in that open space at the end of that street, because that is where the wood comes from, and because the two brothers have bought from the same man since about 1998 without ever once having spoken to each other in the place.

What he does not know, and has not worked out, is that it was not dropped by accident by the other one's hands, because the other one carried it in with his own two hands and set it down and walked out.

So the older one has put that sack against the side where the wood goes, in a workshop, on the twenty-fourth of January. It is there now. It will be used. Neither brother will ever know whether it was used.

He is at home now. He has told his wife that the workshop was shut, which was not true, and she said, "Is it?" and he said, "The stove's out," which was also not true.



The man in his early fifties has not thought about it again since about the middle of the morning and he will not. What he thought at the time was that two men who come into the same open space for the same reason on the same morning in the same week are two men who should not be talking to each other about the other one, and that is the whole of what he thought.

The older brother paid all of it on the eighth of January and will say so to nobody for as long as he is asked, and the younger brother now knows, and will not put it in a call, and will not put it down on a sheet and send it, and will not put it into the conversation that happens in a car on a Sunday.

He will pay his half in three or four days, out of money that is not there, out of the same account, in the same way he has done since 1979, and neither of them will say one word about the eighth of January to the other for as long as either of them lives.

He will pay his half in three or four days, out of money that is not there, in the same way he has since 1979. Neither of the two brothers will ever mention the eighth of January to the other.
