# Chapter 0936 — Down The Standing Bore

Saturday the thirty-first of August 2024, at the length of tube standing on end in the yard of the unit with the flags already warm. Down a standing bore.

**HE HAS A LENGTH OF SCAFFOLD TUBE STANDING ON END IN THE CORNER OF THE UNIT YARD WITH SOMETHING WEDGED ACROSS ITS BORE, AND HE MEANS TO PRESS THE FOUR FINGERS OF ONE HAND DOWN INTO THAT BORE WITH THE THUMB LAID ACROSS THE RIM OUTSIDE, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE TUBE IS HOLLOW AND WHAT IS ACROSS THE FAR END OF IT.**

The tube came down off a run of scaffold that came down in the spring, and it has stood in that corner since the day it came down, on end, with the open end up. Nobody has had a reason to move it and nobody has had a reason to leave it anywhere else.

It is a length of galvanised steel tube about eight feet long and an inch and three-quarters through, of the kind that is bought by the six-foot length and cut off in whatever length the job wants. The bore is the inside of the tube, and there is something pushed down into the top of it.

---

A tube on end with its open end up is a hole that points at the sky. So the four fingers go down together and the thumb goes across the rim outside and the whole hand bears on the metal. The rim is blunt all the way round where the tube was cut off, and blunt in a different way from the arris of a stone, because a cut steel tube leaves an edge that has never been anywhere near a sharpening stone and never will be.

The tube is hollow for eleven inches from the rim. Then there is a face across it, and the face is not steel.

It is a piece of softwood about two inches by one, sawn square at both ends and pushed into the bore and cut off flush with nothing above it. The saw marks run across it and not along it, and the offcut was taken off a length of something else and used because it was to hand.

The four fingers stop against that sawn face and cannot go past it, and what is on the face and around it is the inside of a steel tube that has stood open to the weather since the spring. There is a scale of rust up the wall of it, thin and even and about the thickness of a leaf, and the rust comes off a finger brown.

---

A scaffold tube is made in a way that decides everything about the inside of it. A pipe has a hole in a solid and a hollow section has a corner in it, and a tube of this kind has neither. It is a strip of steel about four inches wide rolled into a long curl and welded along one long edge, and that welded line is the reason a length of it can be stood on end in a yard for a whole summer and not be asked a single question about it.

What the weld does to the bore is put a lump in it about a foot from the end of the tube, running the length of the tube's inside on one side. A hand going down a tube bare meets that lump before it meets anything else, and the lump is the width of a finger and as deep as the thickness of a coin, and it is blunt along its length where the weld has been ground back flush with the roll.

The welded line is the strongest line in the tube and it is the only line in the tube a hand can find without looking. Steel that has been rolled and welded goes out of true in the length when it is cut, because the cut releases the tension the rolling put into it, and a cut tube is never quite as round as the tube it was cut out of.
This one is out of true by about the thickness of a sheet of paper at one side and not at the other, which is why the four fingers go down easier on one side of the bore than on the other.

The offcut was put in for one reason and it is the ordinary one. A tube standing open collects rain, and a tube standing open in a yard collects everything the yard has, and a tube of steel that has stood open over a wet spring has water standing in the bottom of it by the end of April.
Anything pushed into the top of a tube stops that, and a piece of softwood cut to length is what most yards use, because it is in the yard and because it does not rot in three weeks.

The sawn face at the top of this one has been dry-grey for about a month, which puts the cutting at the back of August and not at the front of the wet. Rain that gets past a wedge does not go far, because the tube's own bore is out of true and the low side of it is the high side a hand's width further down, and a tube out of true keeps its water in a line along itself.

The rust inside is a scale and not a crust. A tube left lying down goes orange inside within a season and the rust comes off in flakes the size of a five-pence piece. A tube left standing on end goes grey and the rust comes off in dust, and the dust lies in the bottom of the bore in a ring a couple of inches across, carried by nothing at all, exactly as deep as the tube is.

That ring of dust is the only thing in this tube that has moved, and it has moved because the tube was moved once, at the beginning of June, by whoever put it in this corner instead of the other one.

The corner is where a tube goes when a yard wants a place for it. The rest of that yard is stacked to the roof in places and swept in others, and a corner is neither: it is out of the line of anything anybody carries across, it is not on the way to the office, and it is dry enough in a wet month because the wall behind it takes the weather.
A length of tube eight feet long goes over more easily than anything else kept on a yard, and a man walking with his hands full goes into it more easily still, and a corner is the one place on a yard where neither of those can happen by accident.

A tube stood on end also shows its own condition better than any other way of putting it down. Laid flat, a tube shows rust along whichever side was uppermost and nothing of the rest of it. Stood on end, it shows the depth it has been standing in exactly, because the dust in it takes the level of its own rim and goes neither higher nor lower, and a man can read that level off from the yard in a second if he thinks to look.

He takes the fingers out of the tube and puts that hand in his pocket, because the woman from two doors down has come out at the open end of the covered way along the side of the building and is crossing the yard without turning in.

She goes on up the yard empty-handed and takes that corner without a glance at it, and she does not slow for the step at the office end, and by the time he takes that hand back out of his pocket she has gone out at the gate.

The bore keeps its eleven inches and its ring of dust at the bottom of it, and the offcut keeps its sawn face across the top with the two weatherings on it, and nothing in that corner was touched except by the four fingers that went in and came out again.

A hand that goes down there to-day comes back out with the saw marks on it and the colour of new pine under the dust, and what the wedge in that bore is for is to keep a yard's worth of rain out of eight feet of steel.
