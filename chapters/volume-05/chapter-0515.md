# Chapter 0515 — Six Minutes

The Tuesday of the twenty-seventh of August 2019, and the part of it that was worth anything ran from about twenty past seven in the morning until about half past eight. One street in this borough, in Tideglass.

**EVERY MORNING SINCE 2003 A WOMAN HAS WALKED TO THE MOUTH OF THAT STREET AND BACK AGAIN INSIDE SIX MINUTES, AND ON ONE MORNING IN JUNE SHE WENT FURTHER AND WAS GONE LONGER, AND NOBODY KNOWS. ACROSS FROM HER A WOMAN WHO HAS BEEN STANDING IN HER OWN DOOR AT THE SAME HOUR FOR FIVE YEARS GOT AT THE FACT THIS MORNING THAT SOMETHING IN IT IS DIFFERENT, AND ASKING WOULD BE TO ADMIT THE COUNTING.**

Nobody on that street has ever asked her where she goes. She has lived at the terrace end for twenty-nine years and she is not a private person and there is nothing to ask.

The six minutes is out of her own front, round to the mouth of the street, a stop with the water in front of it, and back again. She does not look at the water. She has never once looked at the water. She looks at the wall of the building on the far side of the junction, at the part above the pipe, and then she comes home.

For sixteen years the six minutes was exact enough that her husband was out of the house by the back of it. He died in 2016 and there was no reason left for her to be exact and she kept going out at twenty past seven, and there is nobody left in that house who would notice if she stopped.

Across the street, at number twenty-two, a woman comes out of her own door every morning at the same hour. She opens a shop in the town and the shop opens at half past eight and she is not a morning person, and she stands in her own door with her coat half on, looking down at the pavement, waiting for herself to arrive.

For five years she has looked up and down that street and she has seen the woman from the far end come round the junction and go back again. She would tell anybody she has never counted. She would tell anybody she has never once wondered.

She counted this morning.

---

She stood in her own door and the woman went out at twenty past seven and went to the junction and turned and came back, and it was the same as it has been every morning since April, and something in it was not the same.

It took her the best part of the following week to get at what the thing was, and she did not get at it by thinking. What happened was that on the fourth of July it rained hard from half past six and she was in her own door with her bag over her shoulder waiting for it to stop, and the woman went out at twenty past seven in the rain and went round the junction and did not come back, and stood on the far side for a while with her back to the water, and then came back, and the whole thing had taken a good deal longer than six minutes, and the woman had not run and had not hurried and had come back at exactly the pace she came back at every other morning.

And that was the fourth of July. And the woman across the street has told nobody, and has not said a word to herself either, and has carried on standing in her own door at twenty past seven every morning, and the reason she has carried on is that she has worked out that she cannot ask.

She has gone over it, and what she has worked out is this.

If she goes across there and says, did you go past the end on the fourth of July, then she has said three things at once. She has said that she was in her own door at twenty past seven. She has said that she was watching the far end of the street. And she has said that she has been watching the far end of the street long enough to have noticed a morning that ran a full two minutes over.

And the woman at the far end has not said one word about any of it in sixteen years, and has never once mentioned the six minutes, and has never once asked, and there is a great deal in that, and it is not the same as not noticing.

So she cannot ask. And having worked that out on the sixth of July, she did the only other thing available to her, which was to stop.

She stopped standing in her own door. She did not stop at twenty past seven. She had it in her head, all through the second week of July, that the sensible thing was to stand in her own door some other time instead, so that the two mornings were not the same morning. She worked out that if she went out at five to seven, the woman would be on her way back and would go past her, and there would be nothing in that, and it would be over.

And then she did not do it. She told herself she had not noticed the time, which was a thing she had noticed very well.

What happened on the twenty-seventh of August was that she was out at ten past seven.

She had got up early and she had gone out at ten past seven without deciding to, and she was standing at the end of her own garden with her coat on and no bag, and she looked up the street, and the woman came out of her own front at twenty past seven exactly and went down towards the junction, and the woman saw her.

That is the whole of it. The woman saw her standing there at ten past seven and she saw her, and she did not stop, and she did not call out, and she went on to the junction and turned and came back, and the whole outward half of the six minutes was exactly the six minutes it has always been, and then she was within thirty foot of the woman at number twenty-two, and she stopped.

The woman at number twenty-two said, "Morning."

And the other one said, "Morning."

And then the other one stood where she was for a moment and then she said, "They've had the trees in."

And the woman at number twenty-two said, "They have, mind."

And the other one went on home and shut her own front, and the woman at number twenty-two went on home and shut her own front, and the whole of it from the woman at the far end going out to her own front shutting was eight minutes and some seconds.

She had never once before in five years got a sight of that woman at close to, with daylight on her face, from a distance of thirty foot.

Her face was not a face anybody had written anything about. She had got a new coat since April. And what the woman at number twenty-two found, standing in her own door afterwards with her bag still not on her shoulder, was that the whole of the difference on the fourth of July, the part she had not been able to put her finger on for the best part of two months, was that the woman had looked at the far side.

She had looked at the far side. She had stood with her back to the water and looked at the far side for a while, and then she had come back, and whatever she had been doing over there she had done it looking at the buildings.

And then the woman at number twenty-two understood the second half of it, which was that this morning, at twenty-five minutes past seven, at thirty foot, the woman had looked at her.

And she understood the other half of it standing there, and the other half was that she had been going back over the mornings since the fourth of July, one at a time, and that the earliest one she could place was the twenty-third of June, and that she had not been able to place a single one after the fourth of July at all, and that whatever the woman at the far end had done on the twenty-third of June she had not seen her do.

Not at the buildings. At her.

---

She did not go out early the next day. She went out at twenty past seven on the twenty-eighth and the twenty-ninth and the thirtieth, and the woman came back at twenty-six minutes past, and there was nothing in it, and the woman at number twenty-two went to work.

On the thirty-first she was out at ten past seven again.

There was no decision in it. She told herself on the Sunday that she would not do it again, and on the Monday morning she was out at ten past seven before she had thought about it. She has been out four mornings since the twenty-seventh and she has never once been in the street when the woman is going out.

She has only ever been in the street when the woman is coming back.

And on the Wednesday the woman at the far end stopped at the junction for longer than she ever has, and looked across at the water for a while, and then came back, and when she got to thirty foot she said, "You were out yesterday."

And the woman at number twenty-two said, "I was."

And the woman at the far end said, "I saw you when I came round."

And the woman at number twenty-two said, "I was going in."

And the woman at the far end said, "Right," and went on, and shut her own front.

And that is all there has been. It is the first time in sixteen years that either of them has said a whole sentence to the other, and it was about the day before.

**SHE WENT OUT AT TEN PAST SEVEN ON THE TWENTY-SEVENTH OF AUGUST AND STOOD AT THE END OF HER OWN GARDEN WHILE SOMEBODY MOVED ABOUT IN A HOUSE ACROSS THE JUNCTION, AND WHEN THE HOUSE WENT QUIET SHE STILL DID NOT GO UP THE STREET, AND SHE COULD NOT HAVE GIVEN A REASON FOR ANY PART OF IT.**
