# Chapter 0927 — Between Finger And Wall

Monday the twenty-second of July 2024, at the lead collar on the back wall of number twelve with the render dry to the touch. Behind a collar.

**SHE HAS A LEAD COLLAR ON THE BACK WALL AT NUMBER TWELVE WHERE AN OLD FLUE CAME OUT THROUGH THE RENDER AND SHE MEANS TO PUSH TWO FINGERS OF ONE HAND BEHIND THAT COLLAR WITH THE PALM FLAT TO THE WALL AND THEN OPEN THEM APART, TO FIND OUT HOW WIDE THE SPACE BEHIND IT IS AND WHAT IS IN IT.**

The flue came out of that wall in a fortnight in the winter the house was first piped, and it was bricked up at its mouth some thirty years later, with the collar left where it was because nobody thought of it again.

It is a collar of lead about five inches round the pipe it once carried, flanged back against the render and bedded in a ring of old lime that has gone hard as a stone. She wants the space behind it, and the whole of what is under the flange is in that space.

A flange bedded in lime stands off the wall everywhere the lime has not crushed. So the two fingers go behind the collar. The lead is cool and slack, and the lime behind it is gritty and has begun to open away from the metal in a curve about the width of a thumb.

The fingers open apart and find both edges. The gap is a finger wide at the top of the flange and closes to a paper's thickness at the bottom, and the two edges are not parallel to each other. Pressed between them the hand finds a wedge of render standing proud in a slow wave, which is the swell of the wall behind the flue and not a crack in it, and the wave itself is slick along its length where the lime has been drawn out of the face.

---

Lead is soft and it moves, which is why it was used to collar a pipe and not to collar anything else. A lead flange bedded in lime has nowhere to go when the wall moves, and the wall always moves, so the collar stands off first at its top and last at its foot.

A flange standing off at the top is a funnel. Rain that reaches the wall above it runs down into the mouth of the gap and stands there, because a lead lip a quarter of an inch proud keeps whatever gets into it. What gets into it here is four summers of render dust washed off the wall by that rain, and it has packed into a bed a third of an inch deep along the top of the flange.

Underneath the bed the lime is intact. It has gone hard and crumbly at the same time, hard enough to ring under a knuckle and soft enough to come away in a thumbnail's worth. The wave of proud render behind the flange follows the line of the pipe that went through the wall thirty years ago, and it stands a sixteenth of an inch proud of the render either side of it.

A collar is made in three pieces and dressed once. The first is a sheet let round the pipe and hammered into a collar; the second is a flange beaten out flat from the same sheet; the third is a tail cut on the slant. The three are dressed together and bedded in lime in the same morning, and a collar bedded in two goes round the pipe twice.

The flange here is beaten flat and not dressed round a roll, which is the older way and the way a tinner in a hurry does it. A flat flange has nothing in it to stand against the wind getting under it and everything in it to keep the water out, which is a good trade until somebody points the render round it in a later decade. The flue was a common one and it went straight up into the roof space, and the render was renewed over the whole back wall in the spring, which is why the colour of the render round the collar is a shade paler than the colour along the plinth.

A renewed render lays on in days of fine weather, and a render laid in June has set hard by July while a render laid in October has not. The pale ring round that collar is a June render, and it is the newest thing on that wall apart from the paint on the window sill. The lime in a June render is still soft in August at its centre, and soft lime is what takes a nail and lets it go again, which is why shelf brackets on that wall sit further out from the render than they did before the spring. The paint on the sill is three coats of a colour no house round there has used since, and it has flaked on the sunny side and gone matt on the shaded one, which is the ordinary record of a sill that faces south-east.

Where the wall takes the weather it goes pale and dusty; where the wall keeps its shade it keeps the green of a growing thing. The growing thing on this wall is a film, and a film on a dry render rubs off on a sleeve. The plaster inside the flue hole is the last of the lime put in thirty years ago, and it is now the driest plaster in the house, which is the whole of what a cold flue gives back once it has been closed at the top.

Closing a flue is cheap, and closing it badly is common, because the usual way is to brick the mouth and leave the pipe standing in the wall. The pipe then sweats for a year and stains the plaster round it and dries again.

What a sealed pipe gives up in a year is about a bucket of water. What it keeps in the wall round it is the same amount of dampness that would otherwise have gone out through the render, and neither figure is written down anywhere. The bed of dust under the flange is packed hard enough now to ring when a knuckle goes on the lead above it, and it has been packed hard enough since the second spring after the collar was made.

Packed dust under a flange is not a fault in the collar. It is the collar doing the one thing a collar is for, which is to keep the water on the face of the wall out of the wall. What it keeps out is what it also keeps in, and the lime behind it stays dry while the render round it stays soaked, which is the trade every flashing on every building makes and none of them makes well.

A dry lime behind a flange crumbles from the bottom up, because the damp gets into it from the wall end and works against the cold of the back wall. It is about halfway through, and the bottom inch is the colour of a biscuit. The bricked-up mouth of that flue is on the inside of the wall and it is four courses deep, and the inside of it is the driest part of the whole structure, which is why nobody found the wall smelled before the collar was looked at.

The yard door is where her sister stands when she comes up from the beds, a pair of gloves in one hand and no sign of putting them on.

Gloves in one hand, she stood in the yard door and looked up at the collar, then down at the beds, then up at the collar again, and then she put the gloves into her apron pocket and went on towards the beds with the yard door left open behind her.

What passed between them there was the wasps in the hedge and whether the far end of the hedge wanted cutting back before the fruit set. Neither of those touched the collar, and neither of them named the four summers of dust packed under its top edge.

The dust goes on packing itself under the top of the flange and the lime goes on crumbling from the bottom of it up, and the two have not met yet.

The lead collar keeps its bed of grit where the rain drove it, a finger wide at the top and a paper at the bottom, and in the sound lime between the top and the bottom of the gap nothing has moved.
