# Chapter 0951 — Between The Two Chords

Monday the fourth of November 2024, at the end of the beam in the bay of the lock-up with the roof lights off. Between two chords.

**AN OPEN-WEB STEEL BEAM STANDS IN THE BAY AT THE LOCK-UP WITH ITS TOP AND BOTTOM CHORDS AND THE ZIG-ZAG OF DIAGONALS BETWEEN THEM, AND ITS END OPEN, AND THE HAND GOES INTO THE SPACE BETWEEN THE TWO CHORDS AND COMES OUT AND GOES IN AGAIN WITH THE WRIST TURNED A QUARTER, TO FIND OUT WHAT THE WEB GIVES AT ONE ANGLE AGAINST WHAT IT GIVES AT ANOTHER.**

The beam is eighteen feet long and was part of a floor at a works that closed. And it came here in three loads on a flat-bed with a man and a boy and a hired lorry, and it has stood on end in the corner of that bay since the week it arrived with its lower end on the flags.

There is nowhere in that bay to put a beam of that length except on end or leaning. And it is on end because a leaning beam wants a wall to lean on and the corner has two and the open floor has none.

It is not propped and it is not tied. A thing of that weight stood on end at that height would go over in a night if it were anything but a beam, and a beam is the one shape of eighteen feet of steel that stands up on its own end without anything being done to it.

The hand goes into the space between the two chords and comes out again, and then goes in a second time with the wrist turned a quarter, so that what the web gives is taken off the difference between the two goes and not off the angle of the wrist.

The space between the chords is fourteen inches at the middle of the depth and a foot at the end where the diagonals are flat bars instead of angles. Going in flat, the hand meets the first diagonal three inches from the end and cannot go past it.

Coming out and going in again with the wrist turned, the hand meets a vertical member instead, which is four inches further along. There is a clear two inches between that vertical and the chord above, and a hand goes into that space and stops in it and gets no further.

Behind the web the bay is dark and then the far wall is not, and there is nothing in between them but air and the dust that is always in a bay. The lower flange of the bottom chord is pitted rather than scaled, and the pits are shallow and round and about the size of a pinhead.

---

An open-web beam is two chords and a set of diagonals, and it is stronger than a solid bar of the same depth by a very long way. All the steel in it is on the outside of the shape, the top one in tension and the bottom one in compression, and the diagonals between them stop the thing folding into a parallelogram.

That is the whole reason a floor of that period was built out of these and not out of solid rolled sections. A solid beam of the same strength would weigh four times as much, and a floor does not care what a beam weighs so long as the beams can be got up to the height of a ceiling by two men and a crane rope.

Above the diagonals, between them and the top chord, there is a line of daylight about an inch high along the whole eighteen feet of that beam, because the top chord sits on the gussets and nothing closes the gap over them. That line is why the bay has a stripe of dust down its length on the flag side of the corner and nowhere else.

The diagonals here are flat bars, not rolled angles, which means this is an old beam, because flat-bar webbing went out of use when rolled angle became cheap. Where each diagonal meets a chord there is a gusset plate of about four inches square, and each of those plates is riveted on with four rivets, and every one of the rivets is still where it was put.

Nothing about that web is symmetrical, and the asymmetry is how it works. The diagonals all lean the same way, so every one of them is in tension at one end and compression at the other, and a load that comes down anywhere along the floor above finds at least two diagonals that are taking it as a pull.

The rivets are the oldest thing in that bay, and the paint on both chords is brittle over the whole eighteen feet of them and has crazed where the damp gets at the underside of the top one. The paint on the rivet heads is brittle too, and the rust under it on each head has gone as far as the steel below and no further, because a rivet head that was not struck properly never gets scraped.

---

It stands on the flags on a bed of old mortar, and between the mortar and the steel there is a piece of sawn packing crushed to half the thickness it started at, which is as far as that sort of packing goes before it stops behaving at all.

The end of the beam is the end that was bolted to a column at the works, and the bolt holes in its end plate are still round and still in the plate. There are four of them in a pattern that does not match the gussets because the end plate went on before the gussets did.

What rests on that beam at this moment is nothing. It is a beam taken out of a floor and stood in a corner, and it is resting on the packing and the packing on the mortar and the mortar on the flags, and none of those three has shifted since the day it arrived.

There is a cast-iron padstone let into the wall six feet above it that used to carry the end of something and now carries nothing, and the rust from it has run down the wall behind the beam in one stripe a yard long.

The woman who books work is at the office window of the lock-up with the sash down and her elbows on the sill, and she stays out there. She is on that side of the yard because the office is warmer than anywhere else in it, and that comes out before anything else does.

They get as far as whether the man who cut hay has been, and whether that hay was cut at the weekend or during the week, and whether the weather was good enough on the Saturday for it. Not one of those three questions concerns the beam in that corner, and the fingers were back out of that web and hanging at her side before she was at the sill.

When she goes she goes back the way she came and the sash comes down to the same inch it was at, and the bay goes back to being a bay with a strip light along one wall and an open door and a beam standing in the corner of it.

The beam keeps its eighteen feet and its open end and its four rivets a plate, and the two inches between that vertical and the chord above are the two inches they were, and the pits in the lower flange are not through it.

Not one thing comes down through the rooflights over that corner, because the glass in them is opal and forty years of the smoke off the bay has been laid on the outside of it, and the whole of that bay is lit from a strip light along the other wall and from the open door.

Between those two chords the first diagonal stands three inches inside the end and the vertical member stands four inches further along, with two inches of clear air between that vertical and the chord above, and behind both of them is the same bay with its strip light along one wall and its open door, and the end of that beam stays as open as it was cut, even if the bay is shut up round it and nobody looks at that corner again.
