# Chapter 0441 — Ten Minutes Before

Monday the seventeenth of September 2018, six days beginning about ten past eight in the morning, a shop off that parade, and two streets off it, in Tideglass.

**A MAN TOLD A WOMAN SOMETHING THAT WAS ENTIRELY TRUE AND MEANT NOTHING BY IT, AND SHE BUILT SIX DAYS OUT OF IT, AND BY THE SUNDAY NIGHT SHE KNEW EXACTLY HOW MUCH OF IT WAS THERE, AND HE NEVER LEARNED THAT SHE HAD BUILT ANYTHING.**

He comes in most weeks. That Monday he came in at about ten past eight in the morning, which is early for him, and he was in a hurry in the way a man is in a hurry when he has been up since before half past five and has not eaten.

She asked him about the man at the top of Ashlay Street.

She asked it the way she asks things, which is straight out and with no lead-in, and what she said was that her cousin was after a bed up there and did not want to knock on a door and find nobody in, and could she say whether there was anybody living at the top of that street.

What he said was: "I saw him about ten minutes before I came in."

That is the whole of it. He said it flat, the way a man mentions something that had been in front of him that morning and that he had not gone on thinking about afterwards, and he went on, and put his money down, and said, "I couldn't tell you if he lives there or not."

She said, "Right," and gave him his change, and he went out and got into his van and drove up to a job off Corrance Lane.

What he had actually done, at about half past eight that morning, was reverse up Ashlay Street in the van to turn round, and a man he has never spoken to had come out of a gate at number twenty-two with a black bag in each hand and walked to the corner bin and put them in and gone back in. He looked at that man for about two seconds and thought nothing whatever about him, and would not have been able to tell you the colour of the gate.

She had asked about a man living at the top of that street. What she had been given was a man at the top of that street, at half past eight in the morning, on one Monday in September, with two bags.

---

She did six things.

On the Tuesday she had her own delivery brought to the top end instead of the bottom, which is a good bit further up and puts the van across a bus stop, and the man who brings it stood there about ten minutes with the tailgate up waiting for her to come and get it. She came and got it and did not say why it had gone to the other end.

On the Wednesday she asked the woman at the next counter whether that man had anybody with him. The woman said she did not know the man and had never been up there, and then said, in the way people do, that there is one at the top for the water and that it may be the same one, and the woman at the next counter does not know that either and has never wanted to.

On the Thursday she moved the day she does her own big shop from the Friday to the Tuesday, which takes a day out of the week and puts her own list a day out of order, and she worked the new day out on the back of a sheet of wrapping paper and put it under the till where she keeps things she will not lose.

On the Friday she went up to the top of Ashlay Street at about half past eight in the morning and stood there about ten minutes with a carrier bag and her coat buttoned to the neck, in the wind, doing the thing a woman does on a pavement when she is waiting for somebody she has arranged to meet and has not been told is not coming.

There was nothing to see. One car went past. A man came out of number fourteen with a dog and went down the street. Nothing at all happened at number twenty-two, and there was no sign of anybody having been there that morning or the morning before or the morning after.

On the Saturday, at about ten in the morning, she put it to a man in his sixties, up front near the door, that the man at the top of that street was keeping an eye on things and had been at it a while.

The man in his sixties looked at her for slightly longer than he usually does, said, "Aye," and went back to the shelf he was on.

That was the one that cost her. She saw it cost her. She saw a man of that age decide, in about a second, that she had made that up, take the whole of it back off her in his own head, put it down, and carry on.

On the Sunday she did nothing at all, deliberately, and it was the hardest day of the six. She washed the window inside and out, which she had been meaning to do since July, and she did it badly and did it twice, and she did not go up that street, and she thought about the man in his sixties saying *Aye*, and about the way he had said it, which was not unkind, which was the thing that made it expensive.

---

On the Sunday night she sat down and went over it properly and found out what she had.

He had not said the man lived there. He had said he had seen a man about ten minutes before he came in, and he had said, before he left, that he could not tell her if he lived there or not, and she had heard both halves of that and had taken one.

She had a man in a street who was at his bin at half past eight in the morning. Out of that she had made somebody keeping an eye on things. She had moved a delivery and moved a shop day and lost a day out of the week, and she had said one sentence to a man in his sixties that had to be taken back by both of them before the morning was over.

He did not know any of that had happened. He came in on the Monday the twenty-fourth, in the middle of the morning, and bought a bag of something, and she was exactly as she was and he was exactly as he was, and not one syllable of any of it got said between them, then or since, and it is not going to.

**A DELIVERY WENT TO THE WRONG END OF THAT PARADE ON THAT TUESDAY AND A MAN OF THAT AGE SAID *AYE* ON THE SATURDAY, AND SHE HAS NOT SAID ONE WORD ABOUT EITHER.**
