# Chapter 0725 — The Dry Pen

Sunday the fifteenth of May 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number twelve from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a pen that will not write and a gate lamp that burns on into the morning.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT TRIES HER BALLPOINT PEN ON THE EDGE OF THE GAS ENVELOPE IN HER OWN KITCHEN, FINDS IT DRY, PUTS THE PEN BACK IN THE DRAWER, SPEAKS TO HER SISTER AT THE GATE ABOUT THE LAMP, AND PULLS HER FRONT DOOR TO FOR THE NIGHT.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight at number twelve on Sunday. Her sister stops at the path near dark and she speaks to her out there.

---

She empties the bowl at first light and again at dark, and the tap does nothing else all day.

Then she sits in the kitchen under the window with the cushion under her. Under its cloth on the left of the table rests the box with tape round it. The gas envelope is on the right face up with six things on the back of it in pencil, nothing crossed off.

Four of six.

She looks at it while the cup warms her hands and she does not touch it yet.

---

At the middle of the morning she opens the kitchen drawer and takes the ballpoint pen out.

It is the only pen in the house. It has lived in the drawer since the winter, beside the string and the spare bulb for the passage. She has used it twice, once for a note to the meter man and once for a line under a date on a leaflet, and both times it wrote pale but it wrote.

She squares the gas envelope with her left hand and she tries the pen on the white edge where there is nothing written, a short stroke down and then another across.

Nothing comes. She shakes it and tries again and the ball leaves a dent and no ink at all.

She tries it a third time on the back of a leaflet from March and it leaves the same dent, grey and dry.

---

She sits with the pen in her fist and the envelope in front of her and she works out what she has got.

If the pen will not write then the envelope stays as it is, six things in pencil and nothing crossed off, and that is not a new trouble so much as the old one with its coat off. There is no pencil in the house and there has been none since before Christmas, and now there is no pen either that will make a mark.

She puts the pen down on the envelope and looks at it, and then she picks it up again and puts it back in the drawer and pushes the drawer shut.

She does not put it away in anger. She puts it back where it lives, because a drawer with a pen in it is still a drawer with a pen in it, even when the pen is dry.

---

She spends the middle of the day in the kitchen and the passage.

She wipes the sill with a damp cloth in one pass. She squares the cloth over the taped box and smooths it flat. She steps over the brush in the passage on her way to the back door and back again, the brush with its handle across the runner, unwashed, where it has lain since the autumn.

She does not pick it up. Nobody picks it up. It is part of the floor now, the way the shim under the table leg is part of the table.

At the back door she looks out at the yard and the coal shed with its padlock open in the hasp, and the gate propped back flat against the brick where it has stood since the first of September, and the two bolts in her apron pocket knocking soft against her hip.

She leaves all of it as it is.

---

Her sister comes off down the road at the end of the afternoon with a bag over her arm and stops at the gate.

"You've got the lamp on," her sister says, looking up at the bracket over the gate. "In daylight."

"I know," she says. "It burns all summer in the daylight and all winter in the dark. It has burned since June."

"You never got it looked at."

"No."

Her sister looks at the lamp and then at her, and then at the front door standing open behind them onto the passage.

"You want me to ask up the road for a man?" her sister says.

"No," she says. "Leave it. It burns and nobody has come about it, and I would rather have it burn than have a stranger up a ladder at my gate."

Her sister nods, and the bag slides down her arm and she hitches it back up.

"You tried the pen, then," her sister says, seeing the inkless dents on the leaflet where she left it on the table.

"I did. It is dry."

"What will you do?"

"Nothing," she says. "There is nothing to write that cannot wait for a pencil."

---

She sees her sister off at dark and then she goes back through the house from the back door to the front.

The passage light is on. It has been on since June. She leaves it on. The front door is not on the latch, as it has not been all spring, and the night air comes in round it with the smell of cut grass from two gardens along.

She stands with her hand on the edge of the door and looks out at the road with nothing on it, and the lamp at the end of the road burning amber over a gate and steady through the dusk.

Then she pulls the front door to until the gap is the width of her hand, and she leaves it there, not shut and not wide, the way she leaves it when she is in for the night and her sister is off down the road.

She does not turn the latch. She has not turned it all spring and she does not turn it now.

She goes back to the kitchen and sits under the window with her hands round a cold cup, and the drawer stays shut with the dry pen inside it, and the envelope stays on the right of the table with nothing new on it.
