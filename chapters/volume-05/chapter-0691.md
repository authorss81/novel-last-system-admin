# Chapter 0691 — Two Ladders And Neither One Mentioned

Friday the twenty-fourth of December 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from first light until dark with a man at her gate in the evening, and the whole of the day is a chair under a hatch and a thing she works out that is not worth anything.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT STANDS ON A KITCHEN CHAIR AND LOOKS AT A HATCH IN THE CEILING OF HER TOP LANDING AND FINDS THAT A FOLDING STEP LADDER IN HER FRONT ROOM WOULD NOT HAVE REACHED IT BY A LONG WAY, AND ON THE SAME EVENING A MAN IN A FLEECE PUTS A FIBREGLASS STEP LADDER UP AGAINST THE LAMP ON THE BRACKET OVER HER GATE AND SHE GOES OUT TO THE GATE AND NOT INTO THE YARD AND STANDS IN HER OWN DOORWAY WHILE HE IS ON THE FIBREGLASS LADDER AND DOES NOT TELL HIM THERE IS ANOTHER ONE IN THE HOUSE.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight. She is on her own all day. One man comes to her gate in the evening with a step ladder off the back of his van.

---

She gets the kitchen chair out in the middle of the afternoon and puts it under the hatch on the top landing.

It is the same hatch. It is shut. It has been shut since the spring and the paint round it shows a faint line where the damp has got to the edge nearest the front of the house, and that edge is over the front room.

She stands on the chair.

What she can do from the kitchen chair is put her hand flat on the hatch door about a foot from the frame, and she can see the four rungs going up beside it and she can see them go out of sight about two feet above her head, and she can see the dark where the rest of them are.

The folding step ladder in her front room is folded.

She has not opened it out and she is not going to. She has worked out from the doorway, on Friday and again on Saturday and again this morning, that four foot is the most of it, and that four foot is a thing you can see from a doorway without touching anything, and that is the whole of what she knows about it.

The hatch is at the top of a stairwell and the stairwell has a ceiling, and the ceiling over a stairwell on a top landing in a house this old comes down lower than a landing does, and she has known that since the spring when she got a foot and a half higher on a stool out of the front room and heard a high level cistern weeping at the far side of a joist.

Four foot from the floor of a front room is not the same as four foot from the floor of a top landing with a stairwell ceiling over your head, and even with the stairwell she is not going to get her head above a hatch that is in the top of a ceiling.

That is the whole of what the week has produced.

She stands on the chair and looks at the hatch and works out that a step ladder in her front room would not have got her an inch closer to that ceiling than the chair has, and that the chair is doing it for nothing, and that the step ladder is standing folded against a wall under a window in a room she has not sat in since July, and that it is a good ladder, and that it is a household one with a tray on the top for paint cans.

She gets down off the chair and puts it back under the worktop and does not think about it again for the rest of the day, and what she has found out is a thing about a step ladder and a stairwell and nothing whatsoever else.

---

She has never been afraid of a ladder and she is not going to start on a Friday in December.

What she cannot do is the bottom of it. Not the top and not the dark. The bottom. Somebody has to stand on the top tread of that fixed ladder in the dark at the top of a cold tank and keep the frame while a woman of seventy-eight gets a hand round a ballcock, and the reason she went up there alone in the spring is that there was nobody in that house to keep it steady, and there is nobody in that house now either, and the difference between the spring and today is that since the autumn she has been told, twice, by two different people, that a free call will come and put a camera on a pole, and that it is for the freeholder and not for her.

And a free call does not come with a second pair of hands.

What is new is not the roof space. What is new is that since Friday there has been a piece of aluminium in her front room, put down and squared by a woman of thirty who said *I need a wall*, and she has had it long enough now to know exactly what it is worth to her, and what it is worth to her is nothing at all, and she worked that out standing on a kitchen chair.

---

The bath tap is not done and has not been done since before Christmas, and there is a bowl under it with a folded cloth in it, and she empties it in the morning and in the evening and puts the cloth back in it.

The front room is not done. The gate is not done. Four of six is done and there is no pencil in the house.

There are two printed cards in the pocket of the apron hanging on the back of her own front door and they have the same free number on them and the two drop bolts are in there with them, and one of those cards came off the bottom of a council man's form in October and one came out of a surveyor's hand on the twenty-sixth of November, and a report has gone to the freeholder of this house with a line in it about a first floor ceiling, and the freeholder has her address and the number of the company that sent the woman and will do whatever it is that freeholders do in the winter.

Nothing has come of that and nothing is going to come of it in this house, and she has not rung the number, and there is no version of today on which she rings it, and it is Christmas Eve and the man who would know what that patch is has a bit of card in his shirt pocket with twelve on it in his own writing and has not been up this road since the seventeenth of June.

---

The lorry comes up the road when there is hardly any light left in it.

She is at the sink when it stops and she watches out of the kitchen window, which is the window that looks across her own yard to the side wall of the coal shed and to the gateway and the lamp on the bracket above it, which is on, and has been on since the summer.

The man gets out of the cab and takes a fibreglass step ladder off the back of the van and stands it up in the gateway against the bracket, and he is in a fleece and he is about fifty and he has a brush and a coil of rope and a roll of lamps in a box on the back seat.

---

He knocks on the gate frame.

She goes out to the gate and not into the yard, and he is standing in the gateway on the other side of it, and there is a fibreglass step ladder up under the lamp about six feet away from her.

"Lamp's out."

"It isn't."

"Light's not making." He is looking up at the bulb in the fitting, not at her. "That's the old type. That's got a filament in it and it's had a go at the holder. Right."

"You want a new one."

"I want a new one. Have you had this one on all day?"

"All day."

"All winter?"

"Since the summer."

---

He gets his hand on the ladder and looks up at it and then looks along the side of the house at the side of the coal shed with the padlock hanging open in the hasp, and the gate standing back against the wall, and then he looks back up at the lamp.

"Whoever did this did it well," he says. "That's a good bracket. That's been put up proper."

"My neighbour asked the council for it."

"Right." He goes up two rungs. "Was it the council that put it up?"

"I don't know. I was never asked."

---

And that is the one.

It comes out of her before she has got the sentence, standing in her own gateway on Christmas Eve, to a man on a fibreglass ladder, and she hears it leave her and she stands there.

The light is on all day and has been since June. She has never found the switch that turns it off without going under the stairs, and she has been under the stairs twice this year and there is nothing up there. She eats at a table under a window on a green chair with a cushion on it. The tap drips. A step ladder is standing in a room she will not sit in. There is a woman in this borough who could not ask anybody to be in this house with her, and this lamp is the one thing in this borough that was ever put on for her, and her neighbour asked the council for it and she was never asked and nobody has ever come back and found out whether the switch is under the stairs.

"That'll be a switch under the stairs," says the man.

"It might."

"You'd want to get somebody to have a look at that."

"Yes."

---

He does not ask her why nobody has looked. He does not ask her why the gate is off the wall. He does not ask her what the coal shed is or why there is a padlock hanging open on it, and he is not avoiding anything, and he is not being kind to her, and she can tell the difference between those two things and it has taken her most of a year to be able to.

He puts the new lamp in. It takes a while and he talks to himself about the holder. The ladder goes back in the van and the van door shuts.

Then he stands at the gate frame with the box of lamps under his arm and looks up at it again and says: "There you are."

---

The light is on.

That is the whole of the outcome and it is worth having. The man from a firm with a free number to give and a report to write has been up a ladder in her gateway and gone down it again, and the lamp over her gate is on, and it is a new lamp in the same fitting or the same lamp back in it, and from where she was standing at her own door she could not tell which, and it makes exactly the same amount of light.

---

She goes back in and stands in her own passage with the apron pocket against her hand and the four things in it, the two drop bolts and the two cards with the same number on them, and the brush is in the middle of the runner at her feet and the passage light is on the whole length of it.

There are three step ladders in this house and that gateway and all three are on the page at the same time, and she can name two of them and has never seen the third.

The four rungs beside that hatch are part of the house and have been since before she can remember and she has stood under them and has not been up them. The folding one is in her front room, folded, with a strip of dark tape round the second rail. The fibreglass one is in the back of a van that has just driven off the end of this road, and the man with it did not come down the path and did not see the gate open and did not see the shed and did not see inside her house, and she did not tell him.

And that is not an oversight and it is not a missed opportunity and it is worth being exact about why, because she has had a week to think about it and she has not thought about it once.

There is no version of it that is a question. You do not stop a man on a ladder in a gateway and say *I have got a step ladder in my front room and I do not know where it came from and I cannot get rid of it.* A woman of seventy-eight who cannot ask a lad's company for its name cannot ask a man in a fleece standing on his own ladder whether she might keep hers.

So it is two ladders in a gateway in December and neither person says the other one is there, and that is the whole of what happened, and it will not be the whole of what happened.

---

She eats her tea at the table under the window on the green chair with the cushion on it, and the cushion is a thing that is there, and the plate is quite clean and she leaves it there, and the envelope is face up square with the edge of the table, pencil side up, and the cloth is over the box on the left of that table, and there is a ladder in the front room and a lamp on the gate and a tap in the bathroom and a dark hatch at the top of the stairs.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS STOOD ON A KITCHEN CHAIR AND FOUND THAT THE ONLY THING HER HOUSE HAS BEEN GIVEN THIS YEAR IS THE WRONG HEIGHT FOR THE ONLY PLACE SHE WOULD WANT TO USE IT, HAS TOLD A MAN ON A LADDER IN HER OWN GATEWAY THAT NOBODY EVER ASKED HER ABOUT A LIGHT AND HE DID NOT ASK HER WHY, HAS GONE BACK IN AND SAID NOTHING TO HIM ABOUT THE OTHER LADDER, AND THE LAMP IS ON AND THE TAP DRIPS AND FOUR OF SIX IS DONE AND NOTHING HAS BEEN CROSSED OFF.**
