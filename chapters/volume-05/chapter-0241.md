# Chapter 0241 — First Out

Friday the twentieth of May 2016, from about half past seven in the evening, a Methodist hall on Carrow Lane in Tideglass, and the corridor outside it.

**THIS BOROUGH GAVE UP ITS FIRST CALL AND SAID SO OUT LOUD IN A ROOM, AND THE ONLY REASON IT COULD BE DONE IS THAT NOBODY IN EITHER CITY CAN REQUIRE ANYBODY TO DO ANYTHING, AND THAT IS THE FIRST TIME THAT ABSENCE HAS BEEN ANY USE, AND IT IS ALSO THE FIRST TIME IT HAS COST SOMEBODY SOMETHING, AND A CARD ON THE WALL IN THAT ROOM SAYS BOTH HALVES OF THAT AND NEITHER OF THEM WAS ARGUIED OUT OF THE OTHER.**

About nine people came. It was a Friday and the hall is on a meter and somebody had put a pound in it.

Mrs Obi, who is about thirty-six, had the pad shut on her knee the whole night.

She put it in about ten past eight and she did not build up to it, and she had it in three sentences, and Jonas Mercer was in the corridor with a radiator that has been out since about 1998 and a laminated notice about a lost cat from 2014, and he heard the whole of it through the door that was open four inches.

"Here's what we're doing tonight. We're giving up our first call. If there's a regional emergency, our crews go out of this borough before anything inside this borough gets done, and that includes the low end, and that includes the boat and the engine, and it starts now, and there is no end date on it, because there is nobody who could set one."

Nobody spoke for about four seconds.

"Is that the thing that went out of force in November," said the man from the works on the far side of the river. "Because if that's it then—"

"It's not that," said Mrs Obi. "That thing went out of force at about nine in the morning on the eleventh of November and all seven of its clauses stopped at once and nobody has renewed it and nobody is going to. This is not that and it is not standing on that. That thing is a column in a book in a building by the water with the last entry in it in September. Nothing tonight goes near it, and nothing tonight needs it, and if anybody in here thinks this is that thing being done again, they should get up and leave, and I'm telling you that seriously."

Somebody at the back of the room spoke up, and nobody else in that room did.

"Does it go on paper?"

"No."

"Somebody ought to write it down."

"No," said Mrs Obi, and did not open the pad, and the young woman of about twenty, who is at a college four days a week, said, "let it not," and nobody wrote it down.

The man from number forty-one was at the back on a chair. He is not on anything. He put a pump in his own doorway in the November before last, ran everybody below his landing off it for two days, shut the stopcock off when the water went down, and has not mentioned it since.

He stood up when she had finished and he did not sound angry, and that made it worse.

"You're not asking me," he said.

"No."

"You're telling me you've done it."

"Neither of those," said Mrs Obi, and she said it carefully, and she looked at the wall behind the man in his sixties while she said it, at the piece of card the size of a postcard that has been on that wall since about the September, with two printed sentences on it and nothing else.

"A body that cannot require anybody to do anything cannot be told what it will do," read the man in his sixties, out loud, from about four feet away. "And a body that cannot require anybody to do anything is not asked what it will do. Both of those. Not one of them without the other one and I'm not going to sit here and let anybody use half of it."

"So which is it," said the man at number forty-one. "Am I allowed to walk out, or are you all just going to talk about the fact that you can't make me stay?"

"You can walk out," said the woman of about forty-two with the key, from the side wall. "That's the other sentence. That's what it means. You can walk out and nobody in this room will say a word, and there isn't anybody in this room who could stop you and there isn't anybody in this room who could tell you that you won't."

He sat down. Then he stood up again, which is not the same thing as leaving.

"Then it's worse, isn't it," he said. "You'd rather I went and you'd rather I stayed and there's no way of telling which."

"Yes," said Mrs Obi.

"Right." He looked at a man from the boat and a man from the engine, who had come because it was Friday and it is what they do on a Friday, and who had not opened a box. "I'll tell you what it is, and then I'm going home, and nobody is to write that down either. It isn't the crews. The crews are fine, a crew's a crew, they'll go wherever there's work and there'll be work in three weeks when the paving season starts. It's that nobody's coming down here now. That's it. There's not a body in this borough that had the low end first at eight o'clock tonight. There's a card in a lock-up with a gap in it and two crews and a man who doesn't have his name on anything, and that's what was holding that street, and tonight it's a street that somebody might turn up at. And I'm still going to be in it, because I live there, and I'd be there if there was nothing on the board at all, and I'd be there if all of you had to move away tomorrow."

He sat down.

A man from the engine crew said, "Do you want the key to the lock-up."

"No," said the man at number forty-one. "I had a look at the board and there's a gap in it, and in about a year somebody's going to write my name in it, and I'll not have handed them the padlock to do it with."

Nobody is required to say yes to any of this, and nobody said anything at all for about half a minute, and that hall has never been good at that part.

"Then let's get it said properly," said the man in his sixties, "so it isn't a favour. It's a right, from tonight, going both ways in the sense that this borough can send a crew out of itself and be told nothing about it, and any crew that comes back into this borough from anywhere has a right to be sent to whatever needs sending first, and if that cannot be written down in this borough then it is not because of a form. It's because the only two people who could write it are not in this room and one of them is eighty-nine and keeps a book."

**AND HE DID NOT TAKE THE LOCK-UP KEY, AND HE WENT OUT AT TEN O'CLOCK WITH THE REST OF THEM, AND JONAS MERCER STOOD IN THAT CORRIDOR AND LET ALL OF THEM GO PAST HIM.**
