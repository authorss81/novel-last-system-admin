# Chapter 0250 — Leave It

Monday the fourth of July 2016, from about half past seven in the evening, a hall off a parade of shops in Tideglass, a parade, and the Methodist hall on Carrow Lane.

**THE SIX WERE PUT IN A ROOM AND HANDED TO PEOPLE WHO HAD TO ACT ON THEM, AND NOT ONE OF THEM WAS ANSWERED, AND ONE OF THEM WAS ANSWERED WRONG ON PURPOSE BY A MAN WHO SAID SO BEFORE HE SAID IT, AND THE ROOM LEFT IT WHERE IT WAS.**

The hall off the parade is free on a Monday because nobody rents it and the woman who has had the key to it for about nine years is in it, and the gas is on a meter, and she had put a pound in before anybody came because she had been doing that since the February and there was no reason on earth to stop now there were nine of them.

About nine people came. Mrs Obi, who is about thirty-six, was at the front with the pad shut on her knee. The man in his sixties, who is a building estimator and who is very good in that room, was standing up before he had his coat off, which is what he does. The young woman of about twenty, who is at a college four days a week, was in. The woman of about fifty-five, who has the room at the end of the first-floor corridor in a building by the water, was in, and had been twice now. A man from the parade. Two others. And a man in his forties who had come for the last part of it and had been in since eight.

What the man in his sixties did with the six was not the thing he did in April, which was to take the best part of an hour and put them in order and read them out so that a room could hold them. This was the other thing. This was handing them round.

"I'm not going to read them," he said. "Everybody in this room has read them or been read them. What I want to do is change the verb. Nobody in this room is going to find out what the answer to any of them is tonight, because there isn't one, and we have proved that twice. What I want to do is go round and say what you would do about it. On a Monday. In your actual week. And I want the honest answer and I'm going to say before we start that most of them are going to be the same answer, which is nothing, and that nothing is not the same as a failure because there is a difference between a thing nobody can do and a thing nobody has thought of."

He went round.

One. Which of the dated things this borough has done once is the first one somebody will be able to prove, and whether it is proved by a person standing there or by a person who reads about it afterwards. What would you do about it on Monday.

And the man in his sixties, who had not been asked by anybody and had been waiting, said:

"The register."

He said it before he put his hand up.

"That's the answer," he said. "It's the oldest dated thing this borough keeps. There's a copy of the earlier one in a room off a corridor and anybody can be carried to it. It's the one that will be proved, and it'll be proved by a person who reads about it afterwards, and that's the one I'd put money on."

Mrs Obi said, "You've just said a wrong thing on purpose."

"I've said a wrong thing on purpose," said the man in his sixties, "and I said so before I said it, and I'd like the room to notice that I said so before, because that is the only reason this evening is going to be worth anything. That is wrong. The register is at nineteen and the last thing anybody wrote in it was a bulb in a maintenance column, and the line under that goes down the page and stops about two inches short of the bottom, and everything this borough has done since is not in it, because it was not written down, deliberately, by about nine people in this room over about seven weeks. Nothing in that book will ever prove a compact. A book with a blank line under the last entry proves that a room of people could see a blank line and leave it."

"Then why say it," said the man in his forties.

"Because somebody will," said the man in his sixties. "And if somebody says it and it's wrong, a room will take it apart. That is the normal way. That is what rooms are for. And if nobody in the room can take it apart, then the room is going to go home with a wrong thing in it that everybody in it can repeat, and four of them are going to be in other rooms this week, and the wrong thing is going to travel further and get said more confidently, and by August it will be the sort of thing a man from the works repeats at a kerb." He put his hands flat on the table. "I am not allowed to correct it. If I correct it, I have to say the right one. I don't have the right one. If somebody in this room stood up and gave me the right one it would be a guess, and a guess said out loud in a room on a Monday night is in nine heads by Wednesday and in every other room in this borough by the end of the month. So it is going to be the wrong thing, and it is going to be the wrong thing with a person's mouth on it, and mine is the mouth I'd rather it came out of than anybody else's in this room."

Nobody in that hall corrected it.

There was no motion about it and there was no vote about it, and after about a minute and a half somebody said it should have gone.

"It should have gone," said the young woman of about twenty, after a bit. "That's what I keep coming back to. You're allowed to leave a wrong thing in a room if you can put the right one where it was. He can't. None of us can. So it stays and it goes out of here. And that is not the same as the room believing it and I want somebody to be clear that those are two different things, because I've been in rooms where they were the same thing and everybody came out agreeing and nothing had happened."

Two. Which of the eight dated things is the first somebody decides to keep, and whether it is kept because it was good or because it was expensive. The man from the parade said, without any thinking about it at all, that it would be kept because it was expensive, and then stopped, and then said, "That's me, not the answer. Put that down as me."

Three. Whether the page or the chair is the first thing a person is asked to agree to. The young woman of about twenty said it was a pad on a knee with a date written at the top of a page and nothing else, and the man in his sixties said he would not count that, and she said she would not count it either and it was still what had happened, and neither of them had anywhere to go with it.

Four. Which of two opposite shapes this city is going to have to stop, and what a person has to be holding to stop each. Somebody asked the second half out loud, in the plainest words anybody had used in that hall, and about nine people looked at a table, and the man in his sixties said, "Nothing. Still nothing," and did not expand on it.

Five. A permission and an obligation and a right of return. He said he was not going to lay the three of them out on a table in a room again and that he had already told the room why in June and that the reason had not gone anywhere. Mrs Obi asked what any of the three had actually got anybody this month, and the room said nothing, and she said, "Right, so we are not doing that again this term."

Six. A claim in a drawer at the end of a corridor, and a schedule of eleven names in the same room, and a sheet of eight towns in a locked drawer under a trade counter. The woman of about fifty-five said that the first two of those had not been opened since the fourth of June and were not going to be opened by anybody in this borough, and that the third had been out of its drawer once and had gone back in with the lock turned twice, and that all three of them were correct, and that correct had turned out to be the least useful property a document can have.

Then Mrs Obi shut the pad on her knee without opening it and said the two sentences that closed the evening, and they were that the argument that would settle three of the six at once was not going to be made in that room again, and that the man in his sixties had made it on the eleventh of April in a different room and had told them himself in May that he could not unmake it, and that the reason he was not making it again was not tact and was not modesty, it was that it rested on a piece of paper that nobody in this borough could put on a table, and that everybody in that room had heard him say so twice and was not going to hear it a third time from anybody, and if it were raised again the person who raised it would be asked to leave, and she said that last part in exactly the same voice as the rest of it.

---

The rain came in about half past nine and Jonas Mercer was under the awning of the shop at the end of that parade with his coat over his arm, because he had said he would come along and had not said he would go in, and there is a difference between those two things that about four people in this borough could explain.

He was not asked anything that evening by anybody, which was now a habit, and he had brought nothing with him, and he did the sum in the end against the inside of the awning with a biro on the back of his hand out of pure habit and it came to five hundred and eighty-six, and there was nowhere to put it.

---

The Methodist hall on Carrow Lane is the other one and the argument on the Monday got to it by itself, because a man from the parade who had been in the first room said to somebody in the corridor outside the second, on the Wednesday, that the register was the thing that was going to be proved.

"It'll be the register," he said. "He had it in a good way, that. You can be carried to the old one."

Which is exactly what the man in his sixties had said on the Monday, word for word in the part that mattered, by a mouth that had never heard him say it and would never know that he had arranged it.

**AND THE SIX WERE NOT ANSWERED, AND ONE OF THEM WAS WRONG, AND THE MAN WHO PUT IT THERE SAID SO FIRST, AND THE ROOM LEFT IT, AND BY THE WEDNESDAY IT HAD GOT ITSELF INTO A CORRIDOR BY ITSELF, AND NINE PEOPLE WENT HOME WITH IT.**
