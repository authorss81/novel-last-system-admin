# Chapter 1020 — A Stair With One Person On It

Undated in this draft.

**THE STAIR AT NUMBER NINE IS NARROWER THAN A PERSON AND TURNS AT THE BOTTOM.**

The stair at number nine is one stair from the front room up to the room over it, and it goes up along the left-hand wall of the passage with a quarter turn at the bottom and about two feet eight of going, so that a man in a coat cannot get up it without turning sideways and a woman with a cup in her hand cannot get up it at all without going round. The boards under it are the passage floor and they have been lifted twice in forty years and put back with the boards nearest the wall about half an inch higher than the ones out in the middle, so the floor under that stair is level along the outer edge and has gone down about an inch along the wall, and there is a place where the nails stand up out of them. The wall on the turn carries three coats of paint in three colours and the fourth coat was never finished.

He was on the bottom step with his hand flat on the wall beside him and he was not coming out of the way. She was on the step above him with the door of the room over the front room open behind her and a cup in her hand that she had brought down out of there, and she had to stop where she was.

"You have put three things to me in a fortnight and two of them you could have worked out yourself."

"I have put one thing to you and I have been let off the other two."

"You came to my house on Thursday and gave me what you were going to do at number twelve in the voice of a man reading out a delivery note, and that was the one." She set the cup on the step above his knee. "And now you are standing on my stair in the way. So out with it."

"What happens to a person who asks a woman for money."

She did not answer that at once, and he could see that she was not refusing to answer it. She was deciding how much of it he could carry.

"Worse than asking for work," she told him. "Much worse, and I will give you the true thing instead of a comfortable one. A woman can turn down work. She can put it that the room is not right, or the day will not do, or the figure is too much, and a man goes out and everybody in the room is exactly where they were. You cannot turn down a man asking you for money without making him small in your own front room, and you have to do it in front of the person he is asking, and you cannot go back afterwards and put it right."

"You would have agreed."

"I would have agreed, and here is why, and you had better have the other half of it as well as the first. My mother was on that book before me and I have had two men come into this house in my life and neither of them would take a shilling off me, and I am sick of the pair of them and sick of what it has made me, having to be grateful for it." She moved the cup along against the wall of the stair so that it was not on the nosing. "A woman who has gone a whole life without asking anybody for anything agrees to a man asking her for money, because she cannot stand being the one who turns a person down in a room of her own. And then he has money she wanted to keep, and she has gone back into her pantry and felt it, and neither of them ever puts the word about it again for as long as they keep working."

He stood on that step and understood that the woman at nine had given him the price of asking, and that the price is not money, and that every woman in that house who had ever agreed to a man like him had paid it quietly and gone away, and that not one of them had ever been thanked and had never wanted to be.

"And I have been doing it for nothing for ten years," he told her, "and nobody ever made me ask for that either."

"No. Because a man who works for nothing is not refused by anybody. A man who asks to be paid for work he has already done is a different animal, and you have spent ten years being the first one and you did it on purpose." She picked the cup back up. "Go and ask her for a figure. And when you ask her, do not tell her what you think it is worth, because you are wrong. And do not tell her what a man in your trade would want, because nothing of that sort is standing in this borough with anything behind it at all. Ask her what it costs her, and let her tell you, and take it out of her hand in front of her, and do not go out into that yard afterwards and do it in a pocket."

"You have thought about this more than a person thinks about a thing that is only going to happen once."

"I have thought about nothing else since the season shut, because I am the only person on that frontage who has done the thing you are about to do and had nobody standing next to me while I did it." She went up past him with her shoulder turned, which is the only way a person gets up that stair with a cup in a hand. "So I have had a fortnight with nothing in it and you have had a fortnight with a wall in it, and mine came to this: me standing in my own kitchen on the fourth night with the fire in, working out what I would do if anybody ever asked me for anything at all, and the answer is that I would have had to go and find out first what I wanted."


He had a thing to work out between that front door and the bottom of the yard, and he worked it out on the flags. A man who asks a woman for money is doing two things at once and only one of them is the money. He is finding out what a thing costs her, which is the easy half and takes a minute. And he is putting himself where a woman can turn him down, in a house, with nobody standing between them, which is the half that has cost him the whole ten years and that he has never once put himself in.

The woman at nine had been in that position at the end of the season with the woman who keeps the book, and she had got herself out of it by making him carry it up there and stand in a room where nobody put a word in, and it had cost her the six weeks of her own work that she did up the river. He was going up to that house to put himself in the same position and he was going to do it without a basket and without an errand and with nothing at all in his mouth but the words.

There was no other way of doing it, which was the thing he understood on the flags. The whole of the arrangement he has lived in for ten years is a man who cannot be refused, and nothing on this earth will get a name onto a piece of paper except by making him somebody who can be refused, and he had worked that out in a shut bay the day before with a hand flat on a rack of boards and had not liked it and had not been able to put it down since.

She had done one more thing at that stair that he did not get past until he was in the street, and it was that she had not asked him a single question about the wall. An hour ago he had wanted a woman at number nine to be on his side. She had given him the price, the method and the reason a woman agrees to a thing she cannot afford, and she had kept herself out of it entirely, and he understood on the way down that a man who has arranged his whole life against being asked for anything cannot tell the difference between a woman helping him and a woman standing well back.

He went down the turn and out of that front door with the whole of it in his mouth and with the only two things in his head that had ever been any use to him, which were a woman's six weeks up a river and a tin on a deal table being carried back to a dresser.

---

He has decided to ask a woman for money and he has never done it in his life. Put a question to a woman about what money is and you are a man in the trade, and he has arranged for ten years not to be that.

The cup went down on the top of that stair against the wall where nobody could knock it, and the light off the front room door went out of it while a man who had not got out of her way stood on the step below and told a woman in her own front room what he had come for.
