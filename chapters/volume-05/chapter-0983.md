# Chapter 0983 — Behind The Cleats

Undated in this draft.

**A SMALL SHELF ON TWO CLEATS FIXED TO THE SIDE WALL AT NUMBER NINE STANDS OFF THE BRICK BEHIND IT WITH DUST PACKED IN THE SLOT.**

The shelf is on the side wall of number nine, four feet above the ground, under the overhang of the roof where the wall turns along the side path. It is an ordinary board about a foot and a half long and eight inches deep with the arris taken off, and it is fixed to the wall on two cleats, one at each end, and it has stood there long enough for two coats of paint to come off it in flakes.

The near cleat has come away from the brick and the board has followed it, so that the shelf now stands clear of the wall by a quarter of an inch along its whole length. Underneath it, between the top face of the board and the brick, there is a slot about a quarter of an inch deep and the width of the board, and it is closed at either end by the two cleats and shut along the front by the board itself.

Into that slot the dust of the roof has been coming down the wall since the board came away from the brick, and the dust has packed the slot full from end to end. It has packed it as a dry grey mat about an eighth of an inch deep, which is loose on the surface and hard underneath, because the mat has been damp and dried more than once and has set into itself. The far cleat is still on the wall and the board is screwed to it, and the two holes of that screw show in the brick at the back of the slot as two dark circles half an inch apart, with the heads of the screw against the front of the board.

The side wall of number nine is of old brick under a coat of paint that has crazed on the sunward side and gone grey where the paint has stopped and let the weather at it. The overhang above the shelf throws the roof water clear of the wall, so nothing ever runs down this face of the building, and what comes off the roof goes out into the path and along it.

The paint on the shelf itself is a green that has gone chalky on the top face and is still bright on the front edge, and the flakes that come off it lie in the grass along the base of the wall and go to dust there in a season. The shelf carries no pot and nothing has been stood on it since the honeysuckle that used to grow out of the soil behind it was taken out. The side path runs from the side gate past the base of that wall and on towards the front path, and it is laid in the same cast slabs as the front with the same joints open between them, and the grass at the foot of the wall is about four inches high and grows out of soil that is damp at its top inch for most of the year. The brick of the wall is damp along its bottom for a foot and along the line where the path's edge meets it, and dry in the middle of the day, and the slab next to the wall keeps it in a dark line along its edge that dries from the top of the line downward.

Between the shelf and the wall the air does not move at all. It is the same air for a foot either side along the slot and there is a faint smell of old paint in it that a hand coming out of the slot carries away on the skin.
---

The pad of one thumb is pressed up into the dust packed behind the shelf, from below, and is pushed a long way in, and is kept there long enough to take the shape of what is packed there.

The mat of dust takes a print of the pad at once and the pad takes a print of the mat, and they are the same shape, which is how the packing can be read off without any tool at all. What the pad comes back shaped like is a flattish cushion with a groove along the middle of it, and the groove is where the dew that comes off the brick has run down it and laid a channel in the dust, and the dew has done that every night the shelf has stood open.

The depth of the cushion is about an eighth of an inch over most of the slot and rather more at the far end, where the mat has built up against the far cleat and nowhere to go. At the near end the packing is shallow and the pad can feel the corner of the near cleat through it, and the cleat is still on the wall at its top end and away from it at its bottom end.

Underneath the dust, in the two dark circles at the back of the slot, the pad finds the two screw holes in the brick. They are half an inch apart, they go in about an inch and a half, and their bottoms are filled with grit out of the mortar which has gone soft in them, because the board has moved at the head of the screw and ground the hole round.

The shelf board is softwood of the kind that comes out of a joinery shop as an offcut, with the grain running its length and a knot near the middle of it that has been filled and faced. It is about three-quarters of an inch thick and it is not warped, and the whole of the quarter of an inch that it stands off is the thickness of the render that used to stand proud of the brick behind it, which is the way the space got there.

Where the cleat pulled out of the plaster there is a rough place on the wall about two inches by one, and it is not the plaster that has let go but the render that was skimmed over it, which has come away with the cleat and taken a skin of old lime with it. The old lime is gone hard on the face of the break and powdery at its edge, and the near cleat, which was bedded in that render and never reached the brick, has let the near end of the board swing back against the brick again, which is why the near end of the slot is a quarter of an inch shallower than the rest of it.

The two screws of the far cleat are of two different sorts, which is common and means the shelf has been re-fixed once without the first pair being taken out. The left head is bright where a spanner has been across it and the right one is rusted in the slot and would want a different blade.

The dirt in the bottom of the slot has the grain of the wall in it. Every time the render has come away it has dropped down behind the shelf and made the packing thicker, and the packing is now hard enough at the back that a fingernail does no more to it than a thumbnail would.

The woman who does her shopping is standing in the gateway of the side gate with the gate leaf open back against the wall behind her and the side path in front of her and nothing in her hands at all.

She does not come up the path to the shelf, and the shelf is over her head and behind her and she does not turn round to look at it.

The shelf gets no mention. After a while she asks whether the jumble table is still on at the church hall on a Saturday, and the reply is that it is, and that it has been in the side room since the spring, and that the side room is the one that gets warm last.

Her hand stays out beside her, palm down and empty, until the other one has gone through the gateway and along the path, and drops after that.

The channel that the dew has worn down the dust runs the length of the slot and dies away at the far cleat, and the far cleat is still on the wall with the board screwed to it.

By the end of another winter the shelf will stand a little further off the brick than it does now, and the packing behind it will be a little deeper along its whole length.
