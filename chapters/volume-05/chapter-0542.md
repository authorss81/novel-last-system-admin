# Chapter 0542 — The Barrow at the Turn

The Saturday of the eighteenth of January 2020, and two hours of it, at a first-floor window in this borough and at the turn of the street underneath it.

**FOR NINE YEARS A MAN IN HIS LATE FIFTIES WENT PAST A BARROW AT THE TURN TWICE A DAY AND IN DECEMBER 2019 HE STARTED GOING THE OTHER WAY AND HE HAS TOLD NOBODY AND BEEN TOLD NOTHING, AND A WOMAN IN HER EARLY EIGHTIES OPPOSITE HIM HAS SEEN EVERY MORNING OF IT SINCE THE FOURTEENTH OF DECEMBER AND HAS NOT GONE DOWNSTAIRS.**

The barrow is a builder's barrow standing on its face at the turn where Vine Street comes off the main street, with the two handles up in the air and the back of it against the face of the building that has a door in it that nobody uses.

It has been there since the March of 2010. It went there one Monday in bad weather with the hands of two men up to the elbows in it, and the two men were working on something behind the face of that building for about three weeks and then they stopped, and the barrow did not.

Nobody has taken it. Not the man whose door that is, and not the four or five people who own bits of the front of that building, and not the people who do the collections on that street, who have been putting a wheel on it and taking the wheel off again since 2010 as part of the round and have never once been asked by anybody what it is doing there.

It is not beautiful. It is a red thing with a rust bloom on the handles and about nine inches of standing water in the bottom of it that has not been wet for a while.

---

The man is in his late fifties and he lives at the top of Vine Street and he goes out at about a quarter to eight every morning of the week and comes back at about half past five.

He is not a man whose going out would be a thing anybody in that street would describe.

For nine years he came down Vine Street and turned left at the turn by the barrow and went up the main street to the place where he works, and in the evening he came back down the main street and turned right at the turn by the barrow and up Vine Street to his own door.

Since the eighth of December he has come down Vine Street and turned right at the turn, which is the other way, and gone along the terrace past the shop that shuts at one, and come out on the far side about two hundred yards further on.

It is about a quarter of an hour longer each way. He has done it for six weeks. He has a back that is not good and he has been telling himself since the second week that the quarter of an hour is not what it is.

Nobody in Vine Street has noticed. There are nine houses in that street and a dozen or so people living in them, and one of them is her, and not one of the rest of them knows which way he goes.



She is a woman in her early eighties and she is on the first floor of the building with the door that nobody uses, on the other side of the turn from the barrow, and her window looks straight at it.

She has been in that flat since 1974 and the window has looked at that turn since 1974. She does not sit at that window to watch anything. She sits there because that is where the sitting is done and the window is in front of it, and what is in front of it, across the width of that street, is the turn, and the barrow is at the turn, and the barrow has been at the turn for nine years.

She has seen that man go past it four or five mornings a week for nine years. She has never had a thought about it. That is not a figure of speech. She means that there was nothing in it at all, that he was a man who went past a barrow, and that she could not have told you his name if it had been written on him.

What happened on the eighth of December is that he came down the street and turned the other way.

She saw him turn. She was at the window with a dish in her lap and she watched a man in his late fifties come down the length of Vine Street, slow, and go past where she could see to, and then carry on past where she could not see to, and then not come out of the front of the butcher's on the main street the way he did every day, and she sat there for a while with the dish still in her lap.

She did not think it meant anything on the eighth. The next day she thought it meant his hours had changed. On the two days after that she thought it meant she had been asleep, or had simply not looked.

On the fourteenth of December she saw him come the other way, and she saw the two directions from the same window on the same morning, and that was the day she knew.



She has not gone down.

She has had five weeks of not going down, she has not gone down once in any of them, and she is not going to now either. It is worth being plain about that, because there are two versions of why and she has been through both of them.

The first version is that a man who has stopped walking past a barrow for reasons that are his own is not a person anybody comes down about. The second version is that if she comes down, then he knows that a woman on the first floor has been keeping count of his mornings, and she has not been keeping count of his mornings, and that is the difference between the two of them that she would be giving away.

So she watches. She is a woman in her early eighties and she watches a turn from where she sits, and she has not told anybody in that building about it, and there is nobody in that building she would tell.



On the eighteenth of January he came past at about ten to eight and she watched him come the whole length of Vine Street and turn right at the turn, as he has done every morning for six weeks.

Then she got up off the seat, because her knees want a minute, and she stood at the window for a quarter of an hour, and then she went and found something to do about the middle of the day, and came back at about half past twelve.

He came past at about half past twelve going the other way, which means he has come home early, which she has not seen him do before.

She watched him come up the street and this time he did not turn right at the turn.

He slowed down. He stopped about four feet short of the barrow and stood there for what she would call fifty seconds and no more, and he looked at it, and then he went on up the street to his own door.

And then he came back down about ten minutes later with a jacket over his arm and went off the other way, and did not look at it, and that was the whole of what happened on the eighteenth of January.



She has not written anything down and she has not told anybody. She has not gone down to that turn at any time in the five weeks since the fourteenth of December, and the way up to that flat is the one she has to climb.

What she has done about it is that she has moved her seat about two feet to the left along the front of the place, away from the window, so that she cannot see the turn from it without standing up, and she did that on the twenty-second of December, and she has had to get up about twenty times since then, and she is going to put it back where it was.

There is a woman who does her hair at the top of Vine Street who comes in twice a week and who sat with her in that flat for a while in the early part of 2019 while something was dealt with at the back of the building. She has not been in since the summer. She would not ask about a piece of furniture either, and if she did, the answer would be that the place needed the space.

**HE HAS WALKED PAST THAT BARROW EVERY MORNING FOR NINE YEARS AND ON THE EIGHTH OF DECEMBER HE TURNED THE OTHER WAY, AND SHE HAS WATCHED HIM DO IT SIX MORNINGS A WEEK FOR SIX WEEKS, AND ON THE EIGHTEENTH OF JANUARY HE STOOD BESIDE IT FOR FIFTY SECONDS AND LOOKED AT IT AND WENT ON, AND SHE HAS NOT GONE DOWNSTAIRS, AND SHE HAS MOVED HER SEAT.**
