# Chapter 0846 — The Grass Off The Kerb

Thursday the twenty-first of September 2023, along the front at number nine from the middle of the day until the shade has crossed the gutter, and the grass cut back off the kerb is the whole of that Thursday.

**THE GRASS HAS GROWN OVER THE KERB AND DOWN THE FRONT OF IT INTO THE GUTTER ALL ALONG THE FRONT AT NUMBER NINE AND SHE MEANS TO CUT IT BACK OFF THE STONE WITHOUT MOVING THE KERB, AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING COMES IN AT THE FRONT DOOR WITH HER BAGS ONE AT A TIME.**

That kerb is the row of stones at the edge of the footpath all along the front, each about the length of two flag widths, with a channel of gutter between the kerb and the road edge.

Since June the grass has come out over the footpath, down the face of the kerb and into the channel, and where it stands in the channel it makes a lip of dead thatch and silt that keeps the water in and sets it back up against the face of the stone.

She does not move the kerb. A kerb is set in the road and comes up with a breaker and a bar and two men and half a day, and it comes up to be put back, and a kerb that has been up once is never the one that was there before.

Cutting back is a hook and a lot of bending.

She starts at the gully end with a hook on a short handle, and a rake, and puts the spoil on a sheet on the footpath so it does not go back into the channel while she works.

The blade goes low and comes back toward her along the face of the stone, edge down, drawing rather than chopping. A hook pushed down into the base of the grass takes the turf with it and leaves a torn brown line along the top of the kerb, and a hook drawn along the stone cuts the grass off at the stone and leaves the stone.

She takes the first foot at knee height and lays it on the sheet, and then drops to the next foot and takes that, and works down the face of the kerb the whole length of the run in a band about the width of her two hands.

---

The green comes off in a long mat where it has knitted together, and under it the face of the kerb is pale and clean and true, with the top arris running straight along it as if it had been dressed last week.

She thinks about the kerb while she works, how a stone is set with its face to the road and its arris to the air and neither of them is anything to do with the kerb keeping anything back. What it does is take the footpath's edge and show it to the street. A kerb face covered in grass is a kerb face nobody can see and nobody can read the dip in, and the dip is the whole of what a kerb face is for.

The woman who does her shopping comes in at the front door with three bags and goes through them one at a time, the door open behind her and her feet clear of the channel the whole while, and shuts the door when the last is in.

Three doors on three bag trips inside forty minutes, and not one step of hers in the gutter, and the band person does not turn round.

Near the step end the arris is gone. Two feet of it is knocked back to a slope where a barrow wheel has caught it and taken the corner off, and the slope runs down from the sound stone into the knocked place and back up again.

She takes the grass off the slope the same as the rest, all the way down to the stone and into the dip of the knocked corner, and leaves the corner.

When the face is bare all the length she starts on the channel, and the channel is the part nobody has looked at since the spring.

She rakes the lip out first. It is thatch and silt and fine soil knitted into a mat about the thickness of two fingers at the deepest, and it comes away in slabs where she puts the rake under it, and under the slabs there is the top of the kerb where it has never been in daylight.

She rakes from the step end towards the gully so the spoil goes down the channel and off at the low point, and then she goes back along and rakes out whatever the first pass pushed ahead of it.

The channel comes up out of the muck and there it is, about the width of a hand, cut in the old stone and worn smooth, and it is not straight. It falls in two places and it gathers at the second fall and then it runs on and finds the gully, which is what it was cut to do.

She rakes the fall at the step end out until it is a fall and not a puddle, and where the mat came off in slabs she takes the loose with the rake and leaves the roots, because roots in a channel are the only thing that will keep the silt off it once the winter is in.

There is one place where the grass is not in the channel at all.

Where two kerb stones meet, about a third of the way along, a fine grass has rooted in the joint between them and come up through the silt and made a small green tuft standing straight up in the middle of the gutter.

She cuts it level with the joint and leaves the root where it is. Grass rooting in a kerb joint is keeping the two stones to one another, and that joint is the only thing between them and a wheel, and the stones of a kerb are not tied together by anything else there is. Pull it out and the joint opens the width of a finger, and the stones walk on the first hard frost, and the fall she has just cleared goes with them.

The stop is the whole length of the channel and not the one tuft. She stands on the footpath at the step end and looks along it, and from there the whole fall can be seen in one go, gully to kerb to step, and there is nowhere in it where the water would stop.

She takes the sheet of spoil off the footpath with the corners and tips it in the heap at the side of the road where the barrow goes, and the green on it is drying already in the sun that has come round to the front by then.

---

The hook goes in the bucket and the bucket goes by the door, and the rake stands against the wall where it lives.

She stands at the step end in the last of the light and looks along what is there. The kerb face showing the width of a hand the whole length of the run, the channel open and falling to the gully, and one small tuft of grass left standing in the joint between two stones.
