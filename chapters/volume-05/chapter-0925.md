# Chapter 0925 — Down Behind The Trough

Monday the fifteenth of July 2024, at the stone trough in the side yard at number twelve while the shed roof keeps the sun off the wall. Down the gap at a trough's rest.

**SHE HAS A STONE TROUGH IN THE SIDE YARD AT NUMBER TWELVE STANDING ON TWO LOW BLOCKS WITH A GAP WHERE THE TROUGH'S OWN STONE CLEARS THE BLOCK IT RESTS ON AND SHE MEANS TO LOWER THE HAND DOWN THE OUTSIDE OF THAT GAP WITH THE FINGERS GOING FIRST AND HER WRIST BENDING AFTER THEM.**

The trough was set up on its two blocks in a morning in the spring of the year the wall behind it was repointed, and the wall has been pointed once since while the trough has kept its blocks. Both blocks were cut for this trough and neither of them has been moved since it was put down.

It is a trough cut out of one block of sandstone about four feet long, and the two blocks under it are off-cuts of the same stone, laid flat, one at each end. She wants the gap, which is the space the trough's own stone leaves between itself and the block where the two do not meet.

A trough tells you its inside and its rest are two different questions. So the hand goes down the trough rest. The stone feels cool across the pads, and the underside of it drags where the mason ran a chisel across the bed and never came back to it.

The fingers go down about four inches and stop against packed grit, pale and loose, with a root from the block's corner come up through it and turned along the joint. The hand bears on the grit, and it gives a quarter of an inch and closes again the moment the weight comes off.

Behind that grit the underside of the trough runs on. It is slick where the mason polished the bed flat and coarse where the chisel was left standing, and the hand can tell which of the two it has passed without any light at all.

At the far end the trough stone comes down onto the block and the gap shuts itself in the last inch. What is in the shut inch is grit, packed by the weight above it into something closer to a sand than to a gravel.

---

Water standing in a trough finds the low end and stays there. This one was laid with a fall in it, a finger from end to end, and the fall was in the stone before the mason ever saw the piece.

A fall like that is not carelessness. A trough laid dead level keeps nothing at its top end, because everything that goes in has nowhere to lie, and a trough laid with a fall empties itself in a day and a half instead of standing a week with the water off the back of the animal drinking out of it.

The blocks under it were cut to take the fall in their own tops. Each was dressed across, and the chisel was run along the fall so that a straight edge laid on the block tells you the way the water will go before any water is put in. Between the two blocks the trough bridges eight inches with nothing under it, and a bridge like that sags in the middle by the weight of a full trough, though it has not sagged enough to show.

The stone of the trough came out of a pit where the beds run true, and it carries a curl across its length that no mason chose. The curl runs with the hollow, so the hollow is a shade deeper on the high side than on the low one, and the difference is about half an inch from end to end.

A hollow that curls gathers a skin of grit along the curl, and the skin is slick where the finger rides it and coarse at the ends where it stops. Any trough collects this in a season, and the skin only shows once the trough has been emptied and let dry.

The block at the far end has a chipped arris where the mason split the off-cut and it never stopped running true after. That arris faces out at the yard and catches the hem of everything that goes past it, which is why the soil in the yard at that corner stands a little higher than the soil a foot away.

The two blocks are the same stone as the trough and were broken off the same piece before the trough was hollowed, which is why the colour matches across the joint and the tool marks do not. The trough was worked with a point and a chisel from the inside out; the blocks were snapped and then dressed across, which leaves a grain that runs at right angles to the trough's own.

Where the block grain crosses the trough grain the joint under the trough cannot bed, because one side wants to swell and the other does not. That difference is a sixteenth of an inch across eight inches of bearing, and it is the whole of why the grit under the trough matters more than the grit in it.

---

The trough stone was cut green and set out before it was dressed, and a green stone lays itself flat and then goes out of flat as it dries, so the two blocks were left a sixteenth proud at the head end where the trough would take its fall. The grit under the head end sits on the high side of the block top and spreads outward in a fan as it dries, and a fan like that is how you find the fall without looking for it. A trough let a sixteenth high at one end keeps its water where it was put, and a trough dressed after it is set loses that sixteenth in the first wet winter and is dry at both ends by the second.

No mortar went under this trough. The two blocks sit on the earth of the yard, and the earth has closed round the foot of each one, so the whole of it stands on the ground the way a wall stands before anybody has pointed it.

Earth that closes round a foot keeps that foot where it was set, and while it does the weight above is carried on the ground and not on the joint. Where earth has scoured away under a foot the joint takes the load instead, and a joint taking a load is where a trough opens first.

Slick stone takes a wet hand's palm and hands it back; the same stone takes a dry hand and puts it away. The faces round the inside of this trough have gone one and then the other across three years, and the outside of the trough has gone slick along the underside of the foot where nothing ever touches it. The trough was filled from the shed tap for as many years as the house kept that tap, and the tap then stood where the builder left it against the trough's back wall, its mouth a hand's width above the fall and its thread packed with lime gone hard.

A stone that has stood water for years keeps it in the pores, and a dry summer takes it out again a season at a time rather than all at once. So the stone at the foot of that trough sweats on a still night in the third week of July, with no water anywhere above it and none due for a fortnight.

Her sister comes to the side gate from the lane with a folded horse blanket over her forearm and does not shake it out. Blanket on her forearm, she stood at the gate and looked at the trough, then at the wall, then at the trough again, and then she shifted the blanket up to her elbow and went on through the gate and up the side yard with it. She got as far as the second block and no further.

Nothing of that morning's business went into words. What went into words was the price of hay, and whether the far field had been cut or not, and what the far field would yield if it was cut late, and neither answer touched the trough.

The joint under the trough goes on packing itself tight with what the yard washes down beside it, and the block at the head end stands a little higher of itself than it did when the mason's straight edge first lay across it.

The trough stone stays on the two blocks, the grit packing hard in the joint between them, the block having taken the load the earth under it was set there to take.
