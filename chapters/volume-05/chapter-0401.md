# Chapter 0401 — Under the Iron

Sunday the first of April 2018, from about half past nine in the morning until about half past ten in the morning, a lane off a side street, in Tideglass.

**A MAN IN HIS THIRTIES TOOK TWO BANDED BUNDLES OF SLATES HALF A MILE DOWN A LANE FOR A MAN IN HIS FORTIES AND FOUND THE GATE OF THAT YARD OPEN AND NOBODY IN IT, AND HE WAITED ABOUT TWENTY MINUTES AND PUT THEM ON A LEDGE UNDER A LENGTH OF CORRUGATED IRON THAT WAS LEANING AGAINST THE WALL ABOVE IT, AND HE TOLD NOBODY, AND THE OTHER MAN FOUND THEM THREE DAYS LATER AND DID NOT ASK WHERE THEY HAD BEEN.**

The merchant is on the Cleeve road and it opens at half past eight on a Sunday because it opens at half past eight every day of the week, which is not normal and is because the man behind that counter does not do Sundays.

The man in his forties bought about two dozen slates on the Friday and they are a bundle of about thirty pound, and he put them in two bands of twelve and tied each one with a length of strapping, and the merchant put them on a pallet at the front of his own yard by the door with a bit of sacking on the pallet so the bottom band would not slide off it.

That yard does not go anywhere a van can turn in.

There is a one-way street for about thirty yard and then a loading bay that a van can back into and no more, and the man in his forties could not get in there on the Friday without blocking the whole street for the length of the delivery, so he said he would come back on the Sunday.

The man in his thirties offered to go and get them.

They said that on the Friday, in a shop off the parade, in about half a minute, and the man in his forties said the gate at the end of that lane is open at eight and shut at four and on a Sunday it is on the latch, and the man in his thirties said he would be all right.

---

He went on the Sunday at about ten, and the merchant had them out on the pallet, and he put the two bands in the back of his own van and drove up to the top of the town.

That lane, off that side street, is about three hundred yard long and was built for carts. It is nine foot wide between the walls and there is a bend at the top of it and it is not a road a van goes down.

He stopped at the top of it at about half past nine and put the bands on the pavement and carried them.

Half a mile, down the lane and along the last hundred yard of that side street, because the last hundred yard is too narrow as well and there are four bollards on the corner at the bottom of it.

That is two bands of twelve in about five minutes a band, over a pavement, with his hands, on his own, and the strapping had cut a stripe across the back of his right hand by the second band and he put a bit of a sacking scrap round it at the corner.

He came out at the gate of that yard at about ten o'clock.

The gate is a wide one on a hinge that has dropped and gone off square over fifty years, and it stands open against the inside of the brick, and it is open at eight and shut at four, and on a Sunday it is on the latch, and latched means a bar across the mouth of it.

The bar was up. It was leaning against the brick beside that gate about three foot to the left, where it lives, and that yard was open.

---

There was nobody in it.

There is a workshop building along one side of that yard, about thirty foot by sixteen, with a felted roof and a roller door, and there is hardstanding, and a stack of blocks under a polythene sheet against the far wall, and about six feet of ledge along the bottom of the wall of that building, the width of a boot and about nine inch off the ground, which is where a van backs up to when it is loading.

The man in his thirties stood in that yard for about twenty minutes with the two bands at his feet.

He has a phone on him. He rang the man in his forties at about ten past ten and it rang out. He has never rung that man twice in a row about anything in his life and he did not ring him a second time.

He stood there a while longer and then he went out to the gate and looked up the side street both ways, and there is a bus stop at the top of it and nothing came along it, and he came back in and stood on the hardstanding with his back to the workshop door.

He put his phone away and stood there. He went out to the gate twice and looked up and down the side street both times and there was nothing on it.

He waited until about a quarter past ten and then he put them where they could be got.

He thought about it for about a minute, standing there.

There were four things he could have done. He could have carried them back to the van and left them in it. He could have rung the man again. He could have gone and found out who else had a key. He could have put them down by the gate, which is where anybody coming in would walk over them.

He did none of those and he did not take long deciding. He put the first band flat on that ledge, long side along the wall, the strapped side up. He put the second on top of it the same way, so the two of them are about a foot four in all and about two foot long, and the ledge is six foot, so they are not near either end of it and not near the ground.

There is a length of corrugated iron leaning against the wall of that building above that ledge. It is about six foot by two foot, and it leans the other way, on a stack of three pallets, and the bottom edge of it is about five foot up, and it has been there since before Christmas, and there is a lump of mortar on the underside of it about halfway along, and it is not going to go anywhere by itself.

He got hold of the bottom edge of that iron and pulled it down and over the two bands, and it came down and rested on the slates and on the concrete at the same time, and it covers them completely.

He put it back up against that wall, leaning the way it had been leaning, so that from the gate it looked the way it had looked before.

Then he went out and got the van and drove home.

The man who works nights came up that lane at about half past six that morning.

He has walked up that lane and down it four or five mornings a week for two years, because it is the short way from the parade to the street his car is parked on and it is quicker than the parade at that hour.

He came round the corner at the top of that lane at about half past six and there was a man in that yard with a length of iron in his hands, pulling it off a stack.

The man in his thirties was in the middle of pulling that corrugated iron down. He had a band of slates on the ground at his feet and another one beside it, and there was a piece of sacking round the back of his right hand.

The man who works nights came level with the gate, about fifteen foot off, and he saw him.

He did not stop. He did not say anything. He went down that lane at the pace he goes down it, and he did not look back, and that yard and that gate have not been spoken of by him to anybody since, and are not going to be.

That yard stayed empty for the rest of that Sunday, and on the Monday, and on the Tuesday, and the man in his forties was not in it on any of the three.

He went along there on the Wednesday of the fourth, about ten in the morning, on foot, because his van was in the next street with a job in it.

He came into that yard through a gate that was standing open as it always stands.

The corrugated iron was down.

That is what he saw first, from about twenty foot, because it was leaning the other way and there was a lump of mortar on it that he has looked at from that gate a dozen times.

He came across and pulled it off, and the two bands were underneath it, flat, on that ledge, dry, the strapped side up, the two of them the way a man lays them down.

He stood there for about ten seconds.

Nobody was asked where they had been. The man in his thirties was not asked whether he had been in that yard, or on what day, or why he had not brought them in on the Sunday. He has not asked since and is not going to.

He put the bands on the pallet, one on top of the other, and went back up that lane and the half of that side street, half a mile, the two bands in two loads, on his own, and put them on the pallets himself when he got to the workshop, and he was on that roof on the Friday.

**HE PUT THE IRON BACK WHERE HE FOUND IT, LEANING THE OTHER WAY, AND THREE DAYS LATER THE OTHER MAN CAME THROUGH THAT GATE AND FOUND WHAT WAS UNDER IT AND SAID NOTHING TO ANYBODY.**
