# Chapter 1031 — Four Inches At A Time Round A Corner

Undated in this draft.

**THE FLOOR UNDER THE LADDER HAS A RACK ON IT STANDING TRUE ON TWO FEET.**

The store at that unit is a low room the length of that yard, with a roof light over the middle of the floor and its only door on the yard side, and the ladder up to the drying floor goes up out of the middle of that floor with its foot wedged against a rack that is his own and that he put up himself out of angle iron on three uprights of four by three. That rack is out of true, and the third upright is the one that did it, set about half an inch proud of the other two over a folded piece of paper somebody put there before him. Whatever goes on that rack slides down the slope of it and comes to rest against the wall at the foot of that third upright, and everything that has been on it has done the same since the day he put it up.

Above his head, up that ladder, is the floor his timber used to stand on, and the last nine foot of the slope on it used to have his own stack stood on edge against a brick chimney breast, and four feet of that is gone, cut off the bottom a fortnight ago with a saw and his own arm for a rule, because the bottom two lengths of a dry stack are the pieces that have never moved since before the war and a tray wants the best timber in the building.

He came down that ladder with the four foot of deal under his arm and put it on the floor at the foot of the rack, where the earth is hard in the middle and soft at the edges where men have stood about and turned since before he came to that yard, and he put the board down on the hard part on purpose, because a board that has to come over an edge wants a floor that has not moved underneath it. And while he was putting it down he looked up through the opening and could see, from where he was standing, that the boards above the cut had already begun to come across into the gap. They have moved about half an inch over a fortnight and the stack has taken up the shape of the hole under it, which is what a stack does in a wet winter and which cannot be put back, because timber that has stood on that rack since before the war will not lie down on it true again for as long as he is building.

---

A tray is not a hard thing, and everything about what is wrong with it is in that. It is the least difficult piece of work in the whole of that fortnight and it is also the first one, because there is not one hour of it that wants a second pair of hands, and there is not one hour of the rest of that fortnight that does not.

The tray he described to himself in a shut bay four streets away was galvanised. This one is not, and it is better, because a galvanised tray under a fire for as long as anybody remembers goes at the four corners where the water stands on it, and a tray out of a dry stack does not. What is wanted is a board about two foot by three with a lip turned up round the whole of it an inch and a half deep, so that whatever runs off the floor behind a range has a channel to go down and away instead of standing in a puddle and finding its way back into brick. The lip comes off the edge of the board and what is left flat under it is what the range stands on.

To turn a lip over an edge you want three things, and this yard has two of them. You want the board to come down over something straight, and you want something flat and hard to come down onto, and you want to put the board over the edge and walk it round the corner with whatever is in your hand, four inches at a time, until it is over and stays over. The fourth thing is a free hand at the corner and he does not have that, and he has not had that for most of his working life, and it is the same missing fourth thing as the two men under the range.

The rack upright is four feet of angle iron, and the lip he wants is three foot along the long side of a tray two foot by three, and the upright is a foot longer than that run needs, which means that for three foot of every run he has the straightest edge in this building to work over. A rack that is out of true on its third foot is crooked as a rack and no use. It is a piece of angle iron with four feet of one edge running dead straight inside it, and he put it up himself and never once looked at it that way.

He put the deal on the floor with the wide face down and the edge of it under the angle, and he got his weight on it with his hip until the board had gone over the corner, and then he worked the bend along in four-inch runs with the flat of a bar, going down the length and coming back up the length, and the lip came up off the edge of that board about an inch and a half in the first foot and then went on coming, and by the end of the first three foot it was up the whole way and had not cracked anywhere.

The corners are the whole of it and he knew that before he started and it took him most of the middle of the day. A lip turned round a square corner has to be cut into the corner and the two ends brought round onto each other, and the inside of that corner has to end up mitred instead of lapped over itself, and nowhere in this yard is there an edge you could bring a corner round on. He worked each of the four of them with the edge of the bar and his thumb and the end of a cold chisel used as a hammer, a quarter of an inch at a time, with the bar stood on its end and the weight of both arms on it, and the first one took him the best part of two hours and the fourth one took him twenty minutes.

Nobody in that yard knows a foot of timber came off that stack. He told nobody when he cut it and he has told nobody since, and it is the second thing he is carrying that he has not put in front of anybody, and the two of them are a hole in a kerb and a gap in a stack in a loft over a store that nobody in this borough can be shown anything about.

He did the two short sides the same way afterwards, two foot runs on the same edge of the same upright, and when the fourth was over he stood the tray on its edge against the wall of that store with its face turned in and the two and a half inches left under the lip out of the damp, and that lip went round the whole of it, and it came true to the corner on every one of the four. There is a mark on the edge of the angle iron where the bar slipped in the second corner, and he has left it there, because there is nothing to be done with a mark on a man's own rack.

What he has finished in that fortnight is a tray with its lip an inch and a half all round, and what the job at the back of that house still wants is two men to lift a range onto it. The tray is standing on its edge against that wall where he left it, and it can stand there till a range comes off a wall somewhere, and the two men are not going to arrive because a tray is done.
