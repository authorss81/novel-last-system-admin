# Chapter 0665 — The Brush On The Floor

Saturday the fourth of September 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from first light until midday, and a man from the gas board is in her passage for a while in the middle of it, and the whole of the morning is a brush she does not move and a gate she does not shut.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT LETS A STRANGER INTO HER PASSAGE TO READ A METER OVER A BRUSH ON THE FLOOR, LEAVES A GATE STANDING OPEN AGAINST A COAL SHED WALL, AND SEES DAMP HAS GOT TO THE EDGE ABOVE THE FRONT ROOM, AND COMES DOWN WITHOUT TOUCHING ANYTHING.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is on her own all morning, and for a while in the middle of it she is not.

---

She wakes with the passage light on, as it has been since June, and she leaves it burning while she gets her dressing gown from the back of the front door. The light falls the length of about four feet of runner to the middle, where the brush lies with the handle across the walking line.

She looks at it from the doorway of the back room and does not bend for it, because bending for it would make it a thing she had picked up, and she has decided it is a thing that gets stepped over.

She lifts her right foot over the handle and then her left, and the sole of her slipper brushes the runner and not the wood, and she is on the tiles of the kitchen before she has had a thought she can put words to.

Behind her the passage light goes on burning down the runner onto a brush nobody is going to move today either.

She puts the kettle on and stands at the sink while it heats. The sill over the sink is empty, and she is glad of it in a way she does not examine. She eats toast at the table on the green chair with the cushion on it, which has not been in the front room since the thirty-first of May, and the table does not rock, because the leg is back in dry with a card on the joint and an ash shim planed and sawn flush under the foot on the window side.

The gas envelope is face up square with the edge of the table, pencil side up, six things on the back of it in pencil and nothing crossed off. The gate. The front room. The window in the back room. The hotplate on the left. The bath tap. The passage light. Four of six are done and she knows which four without counting, and there is no pencil in that house and there has not been since before Christmas.

The front door is not on the latch and never has been since before Christmas, and she hears it move a little in the wind while she drinks, and she does not go to it, because a door that is not on the latch is not a thing she can shut by going to it.

---

It comes again. Three knocks, and then a pause, and then the door moved on the wind and came back and moved again, and she has not got off the table.

"All right," she says. "I'm coming."

She takes her cardigan off the hook and goes down the passage, and there is a man on the step in a grey jacket with a bag in one hand and a book in the other, and he takes his hat off.

"Gas," he says. "Reads it twice a year and it's my round this morning."

"All right," she says, and steps back, and he comes in.

And that is the moment the morning goes. She had the kitchen and the passage and the runner and the brush arranged in her head, and there is a man in the passage with a book, and the arrangement is a thing he can see.

---

He gets as far as the cupboard and then stops, because the brush is in the middle of the runner with the handle across, and he looks down at it, and she watches him decide what it is.

"That's a heavy brush."

"It's for the paint."

"Aye, well." He steps over it, and he does it in one movement without looking at his own foot, and she is not sure what she expected and is not going to find out. "Old job, that paint."

"It was," she says.

He opens the cupboard and gets the meter out on its chain and turns the little glass so he can read it, and she stands in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded while he does it, which is as much as she can bring herself to.

"Right," he says. "Now, was one of these here before? Previous reading."

And she does have it, which surprises him a little and annoys him a little, because a person with a previous reading written down is a person who has to be shown something.

"I have it," she says. "It's in the front room."

"Do you want to fetch it?"

"No. I can tell you what it says."

So she tells him, from memory, standing in the doorway, and he writes it in the book and shuts the book, and at no point does he ask her how a person keeps a meter reading in her head, and she is grateful to him for that in a way she has not been grateful to anybody for a long time.

---

He is at the step when he turns round.

"That door's off the latch, that."

"I'll see to it."

"You'll see to it," he says, and writes something in the book, and she does not ask him what.

Then he looks up past her, up the stairs, at the turn of the rail.

"You all right up there?"

"Yes."

He waits, the way people wait. She stands still and lets him, and the passage light is on over both of them, and then he says, "Right, I'll leave you to it," which is a phrase she has heard all her life and has never once had to answer, and he goes down the step and off up the path.

She shuts the front door behind him. It is shut when she shuts it, and it does not latch, and she stands in her own passage with her hand flat on it and listens to him go out past the coal shed, because the grass on the walked-flat line has a sound to it and that is the way he will go.

Then she stands there and looks at the brush.

Nobody has ever stepped over it before. It has been on that runner since the first with nobody in the house, and now a man who has been in her passage and gone again has gone over the top of the thing she made, and he stepped over it the way a person steps over a cable in a doorway, and he did not ask what it was for, and she is not going to think about it now.

She steps over it herself, out towards the front door and back, to make sure the step can be done without turning her foot too far. It can.

---

She gets her coat from the back of the front door. The reason for the coat is the gate, and the reason for the gate is that she wants to see it in daylight with nobody about, and it is Saturday morning, and the road has nobody on it and has had nobody on it at this hour since before Christmas.

Both drop bolts are in the pocket of the apron she has on under the coat, and she puts her hand in and feels the cold of them through the cloth, and she does not take them out, because taking them out would be a step towards putting them back in their sockets.

She stands on the inside of her own gate with the path behind her and the road in front of her, and looks up and down without moving her head much. There is no van and no figure and no bus. It is not a road anybody has a reason to be on.

She did not open that gate so that somebody could come up the path. She opened it because it had not been left open, and she wants it to stay open.

---

In the bathroom under the landing she empties the bowl, because the bath tap has been dripping since before Christmas and there is a folded cloth in the bowl under it, and she empties it morning and night. She lifts the cloth out and wrings it into the bowl and carries the bowl to the bath and tips it away and puts the folded cloth back in the empty bowl under the drip.

While the next drip gathers she looks up without meaning to, and the brown mark on the bathroom ceiling is about a foot across with a hard edge, and the edge looks darker than it was in August.

She puts her thumb to the edge of the mark and takes it away, and there is grit on her skin, and she wipes it on the cloth in the bowl and the grit stays in the weave.

The landing is over the front room. She worked that out on a Friday night in August with a torch, standing on a stool in the front room to hear a cistern weeping at the far side of a joist, and she has not been up the stairs since.

She goes up on the half landing with her hand on the rail, and she has come up without deciding to, in her slippers, with the bowl still in the bathroom behind her. The stairs are colder than the kitchen. She is aware of a man having been in the passage below her and aware of the house being a different house for it, and the house being a different house for it is what takes her up.

From the half landing she can see the ceiling of the landing where it meets the hatch with the fixed ladder over it, and she can see a faint darkening at the edge nearest the front of the house, which is the direction water has to travel to get from the bathroom side to above the front room.

She does not go up the ladder. She has never been up it and she is not going to start, and she cannot turn the supply off from downstairs because she does not know where the pipe comes up and never has.

She listens for the weeping she heard in August. The house is quiet except for the tap dripping through two floors below, and she cannot hear it from here, and she does not expect to. She listens longer than she has any need to.

---

She comes down and goes back to the bathroom and sets the bowl under the drip and washes her hands at the basin and dries them on the towel on the back of the door.

In the kitchen she sits at the table under the window on the green chair with the cushion on it. The gas envelope is face up square with the edge. The second list is on the front of that same piece of paper in her own handwriting, eight boxes put back and four to the loft and two and the curtains and one and the green tiles and four and the stair, and the top corner of it is gone, taken by tape, and neither woman can write on that paper again, and she does not turn it over.

She sits until the light goes off the kitchen window, and she does not go into the front room, and she has not sat in there since the sixteenth of July, because there is now a question in that room and she has never been able to be a person being asked about a thing.

The rush-seated chair is against the wall by the window in the front room and nobody sits on it. The sideboard has nothing on it. The ceiling in that room is white and it will not be white by the winter.

She gets up and puts the kettle on again and stands at the sink, and the passage light is still on, and the brush is still in the middle of the runner where everybody walks.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS LET A MAN SHE HAS NEVER SPOKEN TO INTO HER OWN PASSAGE AND HE HAS STEPPED OVER HER BRUSH AND ASKED HER NOTHING, AND HAS LEFT A GATE STANDING OPEN SO THAT IT STAYS OPEN, AND DAMP FROM A ROOF SPACE HAS GOT TO THE EDGE ABOVE THE FRONT ROOM SHE WILL NOT SIT IN, AND FOUR OF SIX IS DONE AND NOTHING HAS BEEN CROSSED OFF.**
