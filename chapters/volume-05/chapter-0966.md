# Chapter 0966 — Between The Standing Boards

Undated in this draft.

**TWO BOARDS ON THE RACK AT NUMBER TWELVE STAND APART BY LESS THAN A THUMB AND THE DARK BETWEEN THEM RUNS THEIR LENGTH.**

The rack at number twelve stands along the yard wall under the eaves, made of two uprights and three crossbars, put together years back out of timber offcuts and left to weather.

Two boards stand on edge on the lower crossbar with their faces almost together, both of them softwood about eight feet long, nine inches wide and an inch thick. They were put there after a repair to the fence and have stood through two winters, and the rack uprights at either end shut the slot between them so it runs only the length of the boards.

The slot between the boards is less than a thumb wide at the top and narrower below where the boards belly toward each other. Midday gloom fills it, a spider slinging a grey dusty web athwart one mouth.

Each board has a face that was planed when new and a back that was left rough from the saw, and the two rough backs face each other across the slot. The planed faces look outward, one toward the yard and one toward the wall, and both have gone grey with weather except where the crossbar has kept a pale band across them.

Laid edge-up, the board kisses cold along the cheek of the palm, dry softwood sipping heat. The crossbar beneath draws more where the iron bolt goes through it. The bolt through the bar stings cold after one heartbeat, bright iron fashion. The uprights are of harder timber and draw more, and the bolt heads draw most of all, bright where hands have touched them.

The boards have bowed by different amounts, because one stood nearer the wall and stayed damper. The near board bellies toward the yard by about half an inch in the middle, and the far board stays almost straight, so the slot is narrowest where the bow is greatest and wider toward either end.

Dust has settled on the top edges of both boards and fallen into the slot, and the bottom of the slot where the boards meet the crossbar is lined with a soft drift of it. A wood shaving from the planing still clings to one board near the top, curled and pale, and a nail hole in the other board shows where a nail was drawn and the hole left empty.


The bunker beside the rack keeps coal under a timber lid, the lid warped by rain and sun into a shallow bow. The coal beneath lies in lumps of different sizes, bright on fresh faces and dull where dust coats them. One lump near the top shows the print of a leaf from the pit forest, delicate and black, preserved in the shine. The bunker walls are brick with old paint gone hard, flaking in scales that reveal earlier colours beneath, green under black under grey.

The yard channel beyond the rack runs to a gully where leaves gather in autumn and rot to a dark mat. The mat smells of earth and rain and breaks underfoot into flakes. A frog lives beneath that mat through summer, emerging at dusk to cross the flags. Its track shows in dust as two dotted lines with a drag between, gone by morning.
---

The flat of the hand goes down into that slot edge first, with the thumb along one board and the fingers along the other, and it slides along the length from one upright to the other.

The hand goes in sideways because the slot will not take it flat, and the edge of it reads the width while the flat reads the depth. Advance comes inchmeal, halting to board pressure and gliding past slack spots.

First discovery is the depth it allows. At either end the hand reaches the crossbar, which is about nine inches below the top edges, and in the middle it stops short by about the width of two fingers, where the bow of the near board closes the slot onto the back of the hand.

Second discovery is what waits at the far end. At the far upright the slot ends against timber, with a wedge of dust packed into the corner and a dead fly folded into the web. Along the middle the slot ends below against the crossbar, which is rough and ridged where the saw left marks, and the drift of dust there is deep enough to cover the skin of the knuckles.

Boards squeeze the hand yet stir for nobody, each ballasted by self-weight into perpendicular. The near board gives by a hair when pushed and comes back when let go, while the far board does not give at all. The difference between the two is the bow, and the bow is greatest where the hand stops.

The hand comes out of the slot at the far upright with dust along its edge and a grey line across the wrist where the top edges rubbed. The slot keeps its former width, the end web torn solely along the hand track and left unmended.

Softwood seasons by losing water through its faces, and a board left standing on edge seasons unevenly because one face looks to the sun and the other to the wall. The face to the yard has checked along two grain lines for about a foot, fine splits no deeper than paper thickness, while the face to the wall shows none. The checks are dry inside and full of dust, and a fingernail run across them catches only at the deeper of the two.

The rack itself was framed with housed joints and a single bolt through each, and the bolts have rusted into the timber so the crossbar cannot be shifted without splitting it. The lower crossbar carries four boards besides these two, and it sags by a hair between the uprights. The sag throws rain toward the middle, which is why the dust there lies deeper and darker than at either end.

The jambs closing the slot join themselves to brickwork via aged limework turned rock-firm, with the jointwork fissured down one bond and crumbled in granules across the transom. Granules rest where dropped amid powder, wan on umber, no broom having visited. The yard around the rack is swept clean to the channel each week, but the rack itself is left alone because boards standing on edge do not like to be disturbed. The broom passes by it and leaves the drift beneath to deepen through the year.

A nail hole tells its own history when read from the side. The hole in the far board is ringed by a dark stain where water once stood round the shank, and the timber inside the hole is darker than the face by a shade. The nail was drawn straight, because the hole is round throughout and not torn toward either face. What is left is a bore no wider than a pencil that goes clean through the inch of the board and shows daylight at the far end when sighted truly.

Dust in a slot sorts itself by weight when left through seasons. The pale soft drift on top is mostly wood fibre from the boards themselves, while the darker grit below has blown in from the yard and settled where rain cannot reach it. A fingertip pressed into the drift leaves a print that stays, because the dust beneath has taken damp once and set into a soft cake. The cake breaks under the nail into flakes that smell faintly of timber and rain.

Roof water drips from the eaves onto the top edges in rain and runs down the outer faces, which is why the outer faces have gone grey and the inner faces have stayed pale. Night dew beads the top edges and lifts by late morning, the slot dust staying arid below. By afternoon the sun reaches the outer face of the near board for about an hour and warms the dust at the top of the slot, whilst the depths keep chill gloom till dusk.

The bunker-side sibling loiters yardward at number twelve, fuel store interposed twixt her and timber, circling neither flank, palms open and empty.

The palm has slipped free of the boards and rests against the thigh as the sister glances past the bunker. The bunker lid beside her is shut and the coal beneath it is dark and unmoved, and the yard smells faintly of rain on dust. Planks, rack and cargo pass without comment. The sister looks at the clouds for a moment and then the wall, and then goes back toward the house without coming nearer.

The length of board spans its uprights and carries its own bow without complaint, until the dust settles again in the dark between them.
