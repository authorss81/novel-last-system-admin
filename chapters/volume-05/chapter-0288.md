# Chapter 0288 — A Woman With a Clipboard

Thursday the twelfth of January 2017, from about half past ten in the morning, a pitch on the hard at the top of Marsh Lane in Tideglass.

**A QUESTION THAT HAD ONLY EVER BEEN PUT IN A ROOM WENT OUT INTO A STREET ON A THURSDAY MORNING, AND A MAN SAID AN ANSWER BACK THAT NOBODY WHO HAS HEARD THE REAL ONE WOULD HAVE KNOWN, AND THE WOMAN WITH THE CLIPBOARD CARRIED ON COUNTING.**

There is a bit of hard standing at the top of Marsh Lane between the parade and the shelter that is about thirty foot long and gets used on a Thursday, and on that Thursday there were two stalls on it and a barrow of potatoes against the kerb, and it was four degrees and about ten people were on the whole of it.

The man from the parade was there because he does his own shop and it is down the parade, and he had his hands in his pockets and he was not in a queue for anything.

The woman with the clipboard was at the end of it, on her own, with a hard hat and a pair of gloves in her pocket, and she had a sheet on the board and she was writing on it as people went past her, and she had been doing it since about half past nine.

She is doing a course. There is a college that runs a certificate and the certificate wants a tally of footfall and she had asked the man in the office of that college whether she could do it at the top of Marsh Lane and he had said yes, and nobody in this borough has asked her what she is doing and nobody has offered to help.

There was a woman in a green coat at the front of the plants and a man of about sixty behind the potatoes and a bus came at about half past ten and went off up the Cleeve road with nine people on it.

The man from the parade stood about four foot from the woman with the clipboard and looked at the people on the hard for a while, and then he said, out loud, in the open air, to nobody in particular, which was the first time in about four months he had said it anywhere but a room.

"Right," he said. "Somebody's going to be able to stand up in about a year's time and tell you which one of these it is. Out of the four or five of them. And I'm not after an argument, and I'm not after a show of hands. Does anybody on this pitch know?"

Nobody said anything for a second, because a man with his hands in his pockets asking a question at the top of a street in four degrees is not a thing people answer quickly.

A man in a wax jacket at the potatoes said, "Which ones."

"What we did," said the man from the parade. "The dated things. There's more than one going. That's all I want. If somebody in this street knows which of them it is, I'd rather have it here in the cold than have it come back round a table every Friday and change shape on the way."

It was the woman in the green coat at the plants who said it, and she said it to the man in the wax jacket and not to the man from the parade.

"It's the one they pull out," she said. "That's what it is. You know when a house is sold and they come with a folder? Same thing. Somebody over the water, in a big office, they open a drawer and there's a bit of paper in it and the paper's got our name at the top of it, and that's the one they use, and it'll be about ten years' time and the lot of us'll be dead or gone and none of us will have been told what it was."

The man in the wax jacket said, "How would you even know that."

"I don't," said the woman in the green coat. "My mother was in that sort of office. Not for us. For a firm over there. She used to say you've never got to be in the room, the room's got the paper."

"That's the one," said the man in the wax jacket. "That's exactly it. I've been saying that for years."

The man from the parade stood there with his hands still in his pockets, and after a moment he said, "Right," and that was all he said about it.

Nobody in that street had ever heard the third of those three answers said out loud in a room. It had come out of a woman in a hall off a parade on the sixth of December and it had come back round that same hall three weeks later with a shape on it that nobody at that table could account for, and it had got out of that hall by word of mouth down a corridor and through a doorway and into a kitchen in Corrance Lane, and every road it had travelled since had been that one. A man selling potatoes at the top of Marsh Lane had not been in any of them.

---

He did not know that about nine people had heard the same shape of that sentence in a hall off a parade in the first week of December and in the same hall three weeks before that, and that one of the women who had heard it there had said that it wanted nothing off anybody, and that the woman who had given it round in the first place had not been told where it had come from and had not asked.

The woman in the green coat went back to her table and started putting paper round a tray of bulbs, and the man in the wax jacket went back to his potatoes, and the man from the parade went off down the parade to open his shop, and the question went down the lane with him and did not come back out into the street.

At about a quarter to twelve the woman with the clipboard got a sheet off her board, and the bus came in and went out, and she wrote a one against a column headed for a quarter of an hour without looking up, and then she wrote something else underneath it in the box where the notes went, and then she took her gloves out of her pocket and put them on and packed the board under her arm and carried it back down the lane to a car park, and she did not speak to the man in the wax jacket or the woman in the green coat or anybody else, and the pitch was bare by about half past one, and the man of about sixty took his barrow up the parade singing.

The man in his forties was at the bus shelter at about a quarter to twelve with a folded piece of paper in his pocket and he had heard the whole of it from about twenty foot away, and the man from the parade came past him going down and stopped.

"What was that," said the man in his forties.

"That," said the man from the parade, "is now four."

"It'll be five by the middle of February," said the man in his forties, and went and got on the bus, and the man from the parade stood at that shelter on his own for about a minute and then went down and opened his shop.

**AND THE WOMAN WITH THE CLIPBOARD PUT HER BOARD UNDER HER ARM AND WALKED DOWN THAT LANE PAST THE BARROW OF POTATOES WITH THE PAPER OFF IT AND SPOKE TO NOBODY, AND SHE WAS NOT IN THAT HALL ON THE FRIDAY NIGHT, AND THE MAN FROM THE PARADE CAME IN AT HALF PAST SEVEN AND HEARD A THING HE HAD HEARD ON A BIT OF HARD TWO DAYS EARLIER AND HEARD IT AGAIN IN A ROOM, AND HE PUT HIS CUP DOWN ON THE TABLE AND SAID NOTHING AT ALL FOR ABOUT A MINUTE.**
