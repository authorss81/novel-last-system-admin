# Chapter 0200 — The Boxes

Saturday the twenty-fourth of October 2015, from about ten in the morning, a first-floor room over a shop in Tideglass.

**A MAN OF ABOUT SEVENTY-SIX CAME WITH A QUESTION THAT HAD A PERSON'S NAME IN IT, AND THE PERSON WHO ANSWERED HIM WAS RIGHT, AND THERE IS NOTHING ANYBODY CAN DO WITH THE ANSWER.**

The open covenants legal clinic has two rooms and it opens six days. It is run by a woman of thirty-nine called Nell Ardent, who founded it, and it has been open for about three years, and it is free, and nobody has ever been able to pay for anything in it, and there is no arrangement by which anybody could.

On the table under the window there are four cardboard boxes with the word PARTY written on them in marker pen. They have been empty since the clinic opened. A party in a thing like this is a person or a body that can be named as one side of it, and there is no money in this city for a box and no standing for a person in this city that would let anybody put a name in one, and the boxes are for cases that need one, and the cases that need one do not come.

The man of about seventy-six came in at about ten past ten and stood in the doorway for a moment and then came the rest of the way in, and he had a folded piece of A4 in his hand, which he put on the table and did not open, and then he opened.

"There's a name in this," he said. "Ashgrove. My name is not on it. He's my tenant's. He's my tenant's was."

"Sit down," said Nell Ardent.

"I let him a room in a house in Kilburn Street in 2011 and he was in it until April and he walked out in the second week of April owing me fourteen days, and I let the room again in June, and there's about ninety pound of his in a tin in the drawer and it has been there since April and I have not touched it and I am not going to, because I have no way of handing it to him and I am not going to sit in a room and wait for a man to walk in and be handed money that he does not know I have."

"That is right," said Nell Ardent. "Keep not touching it."

"He cannot be found."

"No."

"So this." He put his finger on the paper. "On the Wednesday a woman came down from Alderwick and read that thing out in a room over a shop, and somebody told me about it on the Thursday, and I have been three weeks getting somebody to ask me about it. She's got a schedule and his name's on it and the schedule says he's at Alderwick in a rented room in a work contract since the eleventh of August, and I was in that room on the Wednesday and read it and I thought: right. So he's got a landlord, and a landlord's money, and a name on a piece of paper in two cities, and somebody has to do something about that."

"You have."

"So do I go over there?"

"You could."

"With what. A tin."

"With whatever you have and a great deal more than you think, and it would be the most expensive thing anybody in this building has ever done, and it would be a person walking into a city across an estuary to find a man who has not asked to be found, which is the thing that has already happened to him once, on Wednesday, in a schedule, and he did not have to be in a room for it."

That is the sentence the room needed and it took the better part of an hour getting there.

A man of about seventy-one who was in the room with him, and who volunteers at the clinic on Saturdays and is not a lawyer, said that there were solicitors in Alderwick, and that his sister knew somebody, and that a person could get a power of attorney across. All of that was true. Nell Ardent agreed with all of it and did not answer it.

"I am not going to tell you that you cannot hand the money over," she said. "You can hand the money over. It is yours, and there is nothing in this city or the other one that says you may not, and no document in either of them mentions a landlord, a room, a deposit, an arrears or a sum of money, and the clause about claims is about claims arising out of work, and your money is not that. If you hand it over it is a gift and it is legal and nobody can stop you and nobody will know."

"Then I can."

"You can. And if you hand it over, two things happen. He becomes a man with money sent to him, and a man with money sent to him is a man with a bank, and a bank is a thing that can be looked at, and you are about to make a man findable who is currently only findable because a duty clerk put a line next to his name in a list, and if you were not careful that is how it would come to feel, to him, from a distance, that the people in the city he left have started keeping track of him." She put her hands flat on the table. "And the second thing is that you will have done it, and there is no place in this city to write down that a landlord in Kilburn Street offered a man ninety pounds in October and was refused, or accepted, or ignored, or never got an answer. You will have done it and it will not exist."

"So what do I do."

"Nothing today," said Nell Ardent. "That is the answer. Nobody in this room can act on any part of it, including me, and I have given you the whole of what I have, and it is the truth, and it is the only answer anybody in this room gave this morning, and I have said a version of it to about nine people in three years and about five of them were not glad to hear it."

The man of about seventy-six sat with his hands on his knees. He said, "I have had it in a tin since April."

"Yes."

"I could have posted it."

"Yes," she said. "You could have posted it in April and you did not, and there is nothing in this city that could have made you, and the reason you did not is that you are a careful man, and I have not been able to get a careful man in a room with a piece of paper with a person's name on it in three years."

He left at about half past eleven with the A4 folded again in his pocket. He said thank you to her on the stairs, and she said it was all right, and neither of those things was about the case. The man of about seventy-one left with him and said on the landing that the power of attorney could be got, and she said yes it could, and let him say it twice.

**AND BEING RIGHT IS NOT A FINDING, AND IT IS NOT A VICTORY, AND IT IS NOT A LOSS, AND ON THE TABLE BY THE WINDOW THE FOUR BOXES ARE AS EMPTY AS THEY WERE AT TEN PAST TEN.**

Jonas Mercer was in the corridor and not in the room and she came out and found him there.

"You were in the building."

"I was in the corridor."

"You could have come in."

"I could have. I would not have done anything with it."

"No," said Nell Ardent. "You would not, and that is the difference between you and the other nine people who were in there this morning, and I could have had any of the nine. I am not going to tell you that either, because the day I start telling a man what he is in a corridor is the day this room turns into something else, and it would not be a good something."

She went down the stairs. The room had four boxes under the window and about four chairs and a woman in it who was right about the whole of it, and the only record of the morning anywhere is a line in an appointment book with a day on it and a dot against it, and the book is a hardback one and it lives on a shelf behind her and nobody has ever been asked to read it.
