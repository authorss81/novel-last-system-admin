# Chapter 0972 — Under The Flag Arris

Undated in this draft.

**THE PATH AT NUMBER TEN HAS OPENED ALONG ONE JOINT WHERE THE BEDDING SAND HAS WASHED AWAY.**

The path at number ten runs from the lane to the door in flags of cast stone, each a square of two feet, sand-bedded twenty years gone.

One flag near the middle has settled toward the garden by half an inch, opening a joint along its house side that runs the full two feet of its edge. The bedding sand beneath that edge has washed away into the soil, leaving a shallow run under the arris about two fingers deep and as wide as a hand.

The run lies pitchy at noon, joint grit sifting down in a pallid streak. At the lane end of the run a dry leaf has folded itself in, stalk and all, while at the door end a snail shell lies empty with its mouth toward the dark.

The flag above the run is of cast stone with a moulded face full of small pits, and its arris is square and sharp except where feet have rounded it at the middle. The underside of the flag is smooth from the mould and pale where the sand has left it bare.

Cast flagstone deadens the finger cold, dense and deliberate. The grit in the run draws less and feels sharp. The snail shell draws nothing and is smooth as glass inside.

The flags around the open joint sit full on their beds and do not rock, while the settled flag rocks by a hair when stepped on at its garden corner. The rocking is slight and even, and the flag settles back onto its remaining sand as the foot leaves it.

The joints between the other flags are full of sand that stands flush with the tops, pale and loose on the surface and packed below. Joint sand of this kind is washed river sand with grains of different sizes, and the larger grains lock together while the smaller wash out first. The sand that remains in the full joints is of the larger sort, sharp and pale, while that in the open joint was of the smaller and has gone into the soil. A fingertip pressed into a full joint leaves no print, the grains wedged adamant. Ants mine those joints for fallen crumbs, disappearing into darkness shouldering loads overhead twice their size. Grass has grown over the outer edges of the path by an inch, binding the soil and keeping the sand from washing sideways. A lobworm casting rises at the lane mouth of the walk, newborn overnight.

The house flank by the walk rises in brick with veteran lime joints turned adamant, and the pointing has cracked along one joint above the open flag. Brick of this kind was fired in a kiln and carries a dark skin on its face with a softer heart within. The skin draws more heat than the heart, and a broken brick shows the difference in colour from edge to middle. Veteran lime sips scant warmth and flours beneath fingernails, pallid on dark brick. The crack there runs full of dust that spills when touched, and the brick around it is dark with rain that runs off the eaves. The eaves above throw clear of the path in light rain and spill onto it in heavy, which is why the sand washed where it did.

Overnight dew silvers the flags and burns away an hour after sunrise, the under-run keeping its aridity. By afternoon the sun reaches the garden corner for an hour and warms the upper flags, whilst the under-run keeps sunless chill till evening. Eaves spill drops through a downpipe at the lane end, then sheets across the walk, gouging a gutter through sand by the nether flags. That gutter runs nail-shallow for six feet ere it fans.

Dwarf box hems the garden verge, shorn to the knee, crowded with wee leaves. Spring shears shaped the box; clippings carted, a handful of blanched leaves snagged at the roots. Lignified stalks below run rigid, sipping scant warmth, foliage granting nil.

The lane beyond the path runs between hedges with grass down its middle, and carts have worn two ruts that shine after rain. The ruts slice through turf to subsoil, baring pebbles where runoff scoured, each pebble polished crown-up by iron tyres. Each stone there sits fast in the earth and does not move underfoot, worn smooth on top by wheels and rough at its sides. The ruts run full in heavy weather and dry to pale lines in sun. A gate at the lane end stands open against the hedge on its hook, and the hook there is bright where hands have touched it. The gate itself hangs of ledged boards with three horizontal ledges gardenward, each fastened with roseheads rusted solid into timber, the wood deaf to their grip after decades of rain. The boards have shrunk across their width through summers, opening slits between them no wider than paper thickness. Through those slits the lane beyond shows in thin lines of light. At dusk those lines redden as the sun drops behind the church tower, then gutter out one by one.
Two fingers lie flat into the run under the slab edge, with the backs against the sand and the tips toward the dark.

Prone ingress fits the meagre runnel; flag belly interpreted digitally. They move from the lane end toward the door, stopping at the leaf and easing past the shell.

Reach under answers first. At the lane end the fingers reach under by about three inches before they meet the remaining sand, and in the middle by about four, where the wash has gone deepest. Toward the door the run shallows again to two, where the flag still bears on its bed.

What lies beneath answers next. Under the greater span only air lingers, flag gliding overhead and sand pillowing below. Mid-run rests a coin-sized pebble ferried in with sand, keen on one rim, stranded on the bed. It feigns death to a top prod, pivoting to a flank prod till the sandbank arrests it.

The stone draws little heat and feels rough on one face and smooth on the other. Kiln brick in the flank wall prickles cold against the knuckles, fired-clay keen. The sand below draws nothing and gives under pressure.

Grit-mantled digits vacate the runnel plumb-stained down one cheek, else pristine. The run abides, the coin stone halted where the shove halted it, a nail-breadth off its first seat.

The slab abides fingertip pressure, tonnage grounding it with three corners bedded. The fourth angle bridges its void unprotesting, uncracked. Palm load tips it a whisker; bedding receives it anew.

Cross-path runoff rarely creeps beneath the lip save in lashing storms; thus the run bides arid and its leaf entire. Neighbouring box flings droplets past the cleft; the shell bides desiccated save post-deluge.


Ledged-board gates shrink across their width because tangential grain moves most. The gate at the lane end shows this in slits between boards that widen in March and narrow in September. Through the March slits the lane grass shows green; through September slits only dust shows. The ledges behind prevent those slits widening to gaps, screwed with rosehead nails that rust in rings. Each ring bleeds brown down the ledge in rain, paling to orange in sun.

Lane ruts keep water longest where clay lies nearest the surface. One such pool by the gate mirrors clouds for an hour post-rain, then drains through a wormhole with a soft gurgle. The gurgle marks the moment children love, when the mirror breaks into rings. Those rings widen to the rut margins and dissolve into turf. Youngsters time leaps by those rings, clearing the waterline with whoops scattering woodpigeons from the boxwood crowns skyward.
---

The lane-end sibling pauses where footway meets highway at number ten, thoroughfare dorsal, footway ventral, utterly empty-handed.

Palms turn upward emptied of grit beside the settled flag while the sister halts at the lane end. Flags and under-flag secrets draw no remark. The sister studies the open joint, then the door, advancing no closer, lane at her spine and sunlight on the neighbouring hedge.

Under-flag hollow yields its sand grain by patient grain, before noon sun crowns the garden wall and dusk takes the lane for night.
