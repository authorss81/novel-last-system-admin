# Chapter 1004 — His Name At The Bottom Of A List

Undated in this draft.

**THE COAL SHED AT NUMBER TWELVE HAS ITS INNER WALL OPEN TO THE AIR ALONG THE COURSES.**

The coal shed at number twelve is a brick shed about five feet by seven against the back of the yard with the door on the front and one high vent in the back wall and nothing in it at all except a shovel, a bar of iron, and about four inches of slack along the bottom, and he has not gone in there since the spring. The wall inside it is the same wall as the outside of it and it is solid old brick that was limewashed on the face about the time the house was built and has been at the weather on one side of itself ever since. Where the lime is still on it has gone to a grey and has gone hard as a stone and you could knock it off in flakes with the back of a hand. Where it has come off, the brick under it is about the colour of a biscuit and is soft to a depth of half an inch in patches, and along the bottom course, where the wall meets the floor of flags, the lime the brick was set in has gone out of the bed of it from the middle of the shed outwards and there is a gap you could get a finger into along about four feet of it, and the courses themselves run even for that four feet and are a finger's width out of line on either side of the gap. She is standing in the doorway with a sack on her arm and she puts the sack down on the flags before she has put a word in, which is the way of a woman who is not going to be interrupted, and she keeps the doorway of her mother's coal shed, in her work clothes, with the sack at her feet and she looks at him for about as long as it takes him to stop pretending he has come about the shed.

"I want you to listen to the whole of it before you answer, because you have a way of answering the first half of a thing and my mother has spent a week on the river learning how you do it."

"I have not come about the shed."

"I know why you have come. I have had four days to think about why you have come and I am not going to pretend I do not know it, and I am not going to thank you for it either." She put her hand flat on the door frame, which she has no need to do. "So I am going first, and you are going to hear me out, and then you can put into words whatever it is you have come to put into words."

"What I want is for you to come back when the season closes and put down the six houses."

"I have been putting them down all week."

"Not the six houses. The six houses and your name at the bottom of them." She put it in the voice she uses on the afternoon shift when a thing has to go right the first time. "House, date, what was done. And at the bottom of the last one, in your own hand, your name. And I will keep it, because there is nowhere else in this house anybody would keep it, and when anybody on that frontage asks me who did my mother's range wall and my sister's bed frames and the step outside number two, I am going to give a name instead of giving nothing, which is what I have given everybody about all of it for six months."

"That is a list of six houses."

"That is a page." She did not raise her voice and she did not need to in a shed five feet by seven. "And I have been on this frontage thirty-one years and I know exactly what a page is and I know exactly what it is not, and it is not that book and it is not your trade and it is not going to get you into anything, and I am asking you for it anyway, because it is the only thing I can think of to give a man who has done six jobs for nothing and told nobody." He stood in that shed and let it go all the way in, because it does go all the way in, and the reason it does is that she has asked, out loud, for him, and not for a job, and it is the first time anybody on this frontage has asked him for a thing that was not work, and he is fifty minutes late for that and he knows it, and the reason he is late is that he has spent a fortnight working out that being asked was the whole of what he had come up this frontage to get, and she got there first and got there honestly and got there by putting coins into his hand off a piece of folded paper.

"I will do it."

"Good." She picked the sack up off the flags and put it through into the house and came back, which took about forty seconds. "Now go on. You have been standing in my mother's coal shed for ten minutes waiting for me to stop talking."

"I came to ask you something and I am not going to tell you what it is, which is the whole of what I have learned in the last four days, and I am going to do it anyway, and afterwards you are going to be able to say that a man asked you for a thing and not a woman who wanted something for herself."

He has never once in his life walked into a room and not put into words what he had come to put into words. That is the entire difference between him and the women on this frontage and he has never had to think about it until this week, and what he has learned in four days is that the reason he can put anything to anybody is that he has never wanted anything badly enough to have to be careful, and a woman can be careful, and she can be careful in a way he has no idea how to copy, and the copy is the whole of the ask. She did not answer him at once, which is the first thing about her that is exactly like her mother, and she turned and looked at the inner wall of the shed for a while, at the lime gone off the face of the courses along the whole length of it, and at the place at the bottom where the bed of it has washed out.

"You are not going to be able to tell the difference either," she said at last, "between the woman who wants something for herself and the one who is doing it to get at the man, and if you ask me for a thing in a fortnight's time to get a line in a book, I am going to have to agree to it, and then you will have it, and I will be the woman who agreed."

"Yes."

"So do it and put it into words now, while we are both standing in here." She put her hand on the chalk that is on a nail by the door and took it down and gave it to him, and he stood back from the wall because she told him to. She stood against the end wall with her arms folded while he did it and did not help and did not go away, and the shed is five feet by seven, and there is a depth of slack on the floor and two tools standing against the wall, and the light in there is the light off the yard coming through the door at his back, and it takes the chalk at an angle off the brick because the brick is not flat where the lime has come off. He put four words on the coal shed wall in chalk, low down, under the courses where the lime has come off and gone hard and where that has gone from between them along four feet of the bottom course, and what the four words set down is that she asked him there, and by the spring the lime and a bit of the chalk will have gone off that wall together and there will be nothing on it to read at all.
