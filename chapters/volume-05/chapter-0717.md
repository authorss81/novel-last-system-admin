# Chapter 0717 — The Receiver Lifted To Stop The Noise

Thursday the fourteenth of April 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number twelve from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a machine in the front room that has taken a call meant for a house that is not this one.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT GOES INTO HER OWN FRONT ROOM OVER HER OWN BRUSH BECAUSE THE MACHINE IN THERE HAS RUNG SINCE BEFORE CHRISTMAS, LIFTS THE RECEIVER OFF IT TO STOP THE NOISE, HEARS A STRANGER ASK FOR WHOEVER IS DEALING WITH THE UPSTAIRS, SAYS THERE IS NOBODY UPSTAIRS INSTEAD OF SAYING THE NUMBER IS WRONG, AND PUTS IT DOWN.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight. She is in her own house all day, and her sister is on her own step at the end of it. She speaks to her there.

---

She starts with the bowl because it is first light and it has to be done at first light.

The bathroom is under the landing. The bowl is half full by then and the water in it is cold enough to feel through the china, so she carries it down in two hands and tips it into the bath and the whole of it goes down the side of the tub in one long sheet and takes the ring with it. Then she wipes the inside of the bowl round the top with the cloth, dries it on the cloth, sets it back under the tap dry side up, and puts the cloth flat on the tiles beside it rather than folded, because she has folded it every morning since Christmas and there is nothing in the world that fold is for. The tap goes bead and bead. She counts it twice and then stops counting, because counting it was a thing she did once and there was nothing at the end of it.

That is the whole of the bathroom and she does not spend any longer in it.

Then the kitchen, under the window, on the green chair with the cushion on it. The table stands dead flat on a shim under the window-side foot with a card on the joint and no glue. The taped box is where it has been since before Christmas, on the left of the table under a cloth, with a second label gummed over the first and neither one lifted. The gas envelope is on the right face up with six things on the back of it in pencil and nothing crossed off and no pencil in this house.

Four of six.

---

The machine goes at about half past the middle of the morning.

It is on a low table in the front room against the far wall under the window, and the telephone is on top of it, and the flex goes up the wall to a socket by the door frame, and the room has a cold fire that has not been lit since Easter and a sixty-watt bulb that was put in on the seventh of December and a rush chair against the front wall that nobody sits in.

And under that window, folded against the wall, is the step ladder she carried up this road on the seventeenth of December, four treads shut true, tray down, a strip of tape round the second rail and a chalk mark on the third tread.

She is in the kitchen when it goes.

She hears it as a sound with nothing under it, the way a machine sounds in another room, and she stands at the sink with the cloth in her hand and counts the rings from there, and then she puts the cloth down and goes.

---

She goes over the brush.

It is on the middle runner about two feet inside the front door, handle across the walking line, unwashed since the first of September, and she steps over it the way she has stepped over it since June. She does not bend for it. She has never bent for it and she is not going to start.

Then she is in the front room doorway, and then she is in the front room, which she has not done for a while.

The ladder is where it is. She comes round the foot of it with her shoulder in and the room is cold and the window is shut and the sixty-watt bulb is on out of the daylight, and the machine is going.

She lifts the receiver off the top of the machine.

That is the whole reason she lifts it. She does not lift it to speak to anybody. She lifts it off the cradle because a machine going off in an empty room is a noise in her own front room and she has been in this house since before Christmas and it is hers to stop.

The noise stops.

And a voice says, "Hello?"

---

"There is nobody upstairs," she says.

There is a pause of about the length of a breath and then the voice says, "Oh. Right. I'm sorry, wrong house."

"It's all right."

And then she puts the receiver back on the machine, and it does not go dead at once, because there is a click after you put it down that does not come from the machine, and she stands there while the line goes.

That is the whole of the call. She has been over the words of hers twice since and there were only a handful of them, and the whole thing took about the length of it takes to wipe a sill.

---

She stands in the middle of her own front room with her arms down and works out what she has done.

Somebody has rung this number wanting to speak to whoever is dealing with the upstairs, and she has told them there is nobody upstairs, and they have said sorry, wrong house.

And the wrong house is what they will now think it is.

If she had said, on the first ring, *this is not that number*, then that woman would have rung the office back and given them the number she actually wanted and this house would have been finished with before the kettle boiled.

Instead she has told a stranger that there is nobody upstairs, and a stranger has believed her, and the number is now a number that somebody has rung and got an answer to, and somebody will ring it again. Friday, most likely. She heard her say Friday.

She names the day out loud in her own front room and it is the first day anybody has named in this house since before Christmas.

"Friday," she says.

And then she stands there, because the next part of it is that Friday is now a day, and she has not got anything to put in it, and naming a day is the one thing she has never done for herself because every day she has got is a day she does the same thing in.

---

She does the same thing in it, then, and that is how the day gets through.

She takes the cloth off the taped box and squares it and puts her palm flat across the top and does not lift it. She wipes the kitchen sill with a damp cloth in one pass, which she does not need to do, and the damp is at the edge where the water sits and wiping it did not dry it.

There are four cups on the goings and she picks up the one she had this morning and washes that one and tips it and sets it upside down on the drainer, and the other three stay on the goings with their rims stained. She warms her hands on it and the stove warms and does not boil, as it has done since the element was put in, and she has a cup of tea and half a cup of tea and a cup she will not drink and she does not pour any of them out.

At the middle of the day she goes out to the gate with her apron on.

The gate stands back against the coal shed wall with the two drop bolts in her pocket and the padlock in the hasp open where it has been since the first of September. She does not go near the shed door. She looks down the road and there is nothing on it.

Her own path and her own gate are the only parts of this she can be responsible for, and they are twenty foot of paving and a lamp on a bracket.

---

Her sister comes at the end of the day, off the front path, with her bag over her arm, and stands on the step.

"You've had the front door shut all afternoon," her sister says. "I could hear the machine."

"I heard it."

"Did you answer it?"

"No," she says.

Her sister looks at her for a moment, and then says, "It could have been anybody."

"It could have been."

That is where it stops. Her sister does not ask her what it said, and she does not say, and they stand on the step with the door wide behind them and the cold coming off the paving.

Then her sister says, "There's soup in the pan if you want it put on."

"No," she says. "I'll warm what I've got."

And her sister goes off down the path without going up the road, as she has never gone up it.

---

She does not sleep on it and she does not lie in bed thinking about it. She gets up at the usual hour and does the bowl, because it has to be done at first light.

And then she stands in the kitchen with her hand round the cold cup and thinks, not about the call, but about her own front room.

She has been in it three times since before Christmas. Once for the man with the parcel, who came down her own passage over her own brush and put a thing on her table. Once at the doorway with the cloth. And once today.

And today she was in it for the length of a call about somebody else's upstairs.

She thinks for a moment that the room is not a room she will not sit in any more. She thinks that and then she puts it down, because it is not true and she has not tested it, and a thing nobody has tested is still the thing it was.

---

At dark she goes in to the machine and puts her thumb on the record button and does not press it.

There is a button on that machine for keeping a thing, and she used it once, in the summer, and she kept her finger on it until the thing said the message had been deleted, and she has not touched it since.

She has got her thumb on it now and she can feel where it is without looking, and whatever that button kept would be a stranger's number sitting in a machine in a house with no pencil in it, and she would not be one bit better off with it than she is now.

She takes her thumb off and puts the receiver back square on the cradle, which does not need doing.

And then she stands in her own front room and works out what Friday is, because the woman said Friday and she heard her say it.

There is no number of hers that anybody could ring. The line in this house comes in on a name that is not hers, and there is nobody in it she could ask to answer a telephone on her behalf, and the front door of this house has not been on the latch since before Christmas.

So Friday is a day she will be in this house for, and she has not got anything at all to put in it.
