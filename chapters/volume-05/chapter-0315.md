# Chapter 0315 — A Foot of Scaffold

Tuesday the eighteenth of April 2017, from about a quarter past seven in the morning, at the low end of Peverell Street in Tideglass.

**THE OFFSIDE MIRROR OF A VAN WENT ACROSS A DOWNPIPE ON THE FRONT OF A HOUSE IN THAT STREET AT TWENTY PAST EIGHT AND TOOK TWO BRACKETS AND ABOUT TWO FOOT OF PIPE WITH IT, AND THE MORNING OF A MAN WHO GOES TO THE WORKS WENT ON FROM THERE, AND THE ONLY PERSON WHO EVER HEARD ABOUT THE PIECE OF SCAFFOLD SOMEONE PUT BEHIND IT WAS A WOMAN AT A COUNTER WHO WAS NOT IN THAT STREET THAT MORNING.**

The man in a fleece is in that street on the mornings he is not at the works, and on those mornings he has about nine houses, a van, a bag of tools, and nothing anywhere with his name on it.

He has done the same work for years: brackets for guttering, downpipes, hoppers on back extensions, a length of pipe off a gable where a builder has cut the render off and gone, a plate for a meter box. It is not urgent work and it is not anything anybody rings anybody about, and it comes round about every eight or nine years, and he does a house in about twenty minutes and takes his time over it.

The house is the fourth one in from the corner, two storey, a bay on the ground floor and a rendered return down the side of it, and the downpipe runs down the right-hand side of that bay with the brackets about four foot apart and the pipe painted the same colour as the wall, which is a mistake anybody makes once.

He had the back of the van open in the road with a step under the tailgate, and he was up on that step with a bucket of screws in one hand, and the van is on the offside against that kerb, and he reversed it about a foot to get more light on the wall.

The mirror took the pipe off at both brackets. It came away off the render in a long scrape, split about two foot up from the bottom where it had gone soft, swung out across the bay, and hit the glass about five foot up, and stopped there.

It made a noise like a ladder going over in somebody's yard, and it was not a small one.

---

He stood up on that step and looked at it.

About two foot of it was in the channel against the kerb and the rest of it was up in the air off the front of that house at about the height of a car's roof, and the top bracket had come with it, and the plaster behind that bracket had blown out about the size of a closed fist into a crumbly ring, which meant the water had been getting behind the paint on that wall for a good many years and nobody had ever had a look at it because nobody ever does.

He was on that road for about ten minutes.

He had a hopper to fit on a back extension up at the far end of that street at half past eight and then the rest of that round, and the last house of the lot is on a corner and wants a scaffold tower, which is a morning on its own and is booked for the week after. Nine houses is three hours and the morning is four, and he worked out that he could not do the fourth house at all. Not that morning and not that week. The bracket in his bag is a fascia bracket, and a fascia bracket wants a timber fascia to screw to, and that house has a rendered return and no fascia and never had one. The screws in that bag are four-inch screws for a nine-inch hole and a plug, and behind that render is solid brick with a course of engineering brick where the wall was built up, and he does not put screws into that and call it a week of work.

What he did was lift the pipe off the bay window and lay the whole of it along the channel against the kerb, so that nothing was hanging over a footway, and he put the two brackets in the bag and got down off the step.

Then he went up the street and did the other eight.

He came back at ten past twelve.

The man in his thirties was coming along that pavement at about a quarter past twelve with his bag in one hand, going nowhere in particular, because he is in that part of this town more days than he is not and he has never once had a reason to say so.

He stopped when he saw the pipe on the kerb and the ring of blown plaster, and stood about three foot off the wall and looked up at it for about ten seconds, the way anybody looks at a thing of that sort in a street.

"A plate's come out of that," he said. "Not a bracket. They've put a bracket on a wall with no fascia on it and it's been hanging there in the wet for years."

"I know what it is," said the man in a fleece. "It's not doing it today."

"What've you got."

"A length of pipe, a hopper for the back of the other one and the rest of the round, and it's quarter past twelve."

The man in his thirties went about four inches further into the render and came back out again.

"Aye," he said. "You'd want a back plate and two bolts and a length of treated timber packed into the wall behind it, and that wall's engineering brick at the bottom, so you'd have to cut a hole. That's a different day with a different man on it."

"I know that as well," said the man in a fleece.

What he did next took about five minutes, and he did not ask, and he was not asked.

That van has carried a length of scaffold in the back of it for longer than anybody in that street has been trading, and he took his out and knocked the head off about a foot down with a hammer out of the side pocket of the bag, and split that foot of it with the claw, and it came into three, and he took the piece that was about two inches by four and stood it on end in the channel where the render returns and leaned the pipe back onto it, so that the weight of nine foot of pipe came down onto a foot of scaffold instead of onto a blown plate.

Then he unwound about two feet of wire off a coil, because wire is in every van in this borough, and went round the pipe and the top bracket and the render with it, and twisted it off short with a bar, and cut the tail off and put the coil back in the bag.

The pipe came back to the wall and sat there on a prop about three foot off the ground, not touching the glass, looking much the way it had looked for however long it had been on there.

"That's not a repair," said the man in his thirties. "That's a prop. It'll do you till the autumn, and in the autumn the wall wants opening up, and that's a length of pipe and a plate and a bit of timber packed in behind it."

"Aye."

"Not by me," he said. "I haven't the gear for a hole in a bay wall and I'd be doing it off a ladder, and that's how people end up on the pavement."

"You've a scaffold in the back of that."

"I've a length of it. A tower's different to a length."

They stood there for another half minute, and then the man in a fleece got his hand in, pressed the pipe about an inch in and let it go, and it came back and stayed.

He said nothing about it and the other one said nothing either. Neither of them would have thought of the other one doing that, and having watched him do it, neither of them had anything to put on it.

Then the man in his thirties put his bag on his other shoulder and went up the street.

The man in a fleece finished that round at about ten past six, and he had been on that road since a quarter past seven, and nobody rang him about any of it, because there is nobody here he is on a list with and nobody in that street knows he was in it at all.

---

The woman who works in a shop is at that counter four days a week and this was not one of the four.

She was in on the Thursday, and the woman who runs that shop had a length of floral paper open out along the counter and was cutting into it on a board, and the woman who works in a shop asked her about a delivery coming the next day and got that in about four sentences.

Then she said, "What was all that noise at the bottom end yesterday."

The woman who runs that shop did not look up.

"You'll be talking about the pipe."

"Aye. And the man went off up the street and came back at ten past twelve, and then a man with a bag came along and did something to it, and I could see him doing it from up here through the glass on that door, and he wasn't in long."

"What'd he do."

"Put a piece of wood under it," said the woman who runs that shop. "Off a length. And wired it to the wall. There was a bit of a thing like a length of scaffold propped against my side wall and I was going to come out and say something, and by the time I'd got my coat on he'd gone."

"Who was he."

"He's nobody," said the woman who runs that shop. "He's a man with a bag. He comes down that parade two or three times a week and I've never known him to stop in here. He's not doing anything for me and I don't want him doing anything for me, because the second a man like that starts doing things for a shop he's been in, he thinks he's been asked."

The woman who works in a shop looked at the floral paper.

"You didn't ask him."

"I did not ask him and I would not have asked him and I'm not going to go out and ask him now," said the woman who runs that shop. "I'll sell the man a roll of paper like anybody else that walks in that door and that's the end of it."

"That pipe's not going to hold that wall up in the winter."

"No," said the woman who runs that shop, and cut another length off the roll. "It'll be somebody's in the autumn, with a proper trade, and it'll cost what it costs, and the house is going to have a hole in it where he's been in. You can put a bit of scaffold under a pipe any morning you like and it changes none of that, and none of it this morning. He came off that road at ten to four. He's not on a list of anything, and he wasn't on one this morning before he was on that road either."

The man in a fleece came in on the Saturday for a roll of paper, and she waited on him the way she waits on everybody, and he paid and went, and nothing was said to him about a pipe, and he has not been in that shop since, and he does not know that either woman said any of it.

**AND A PIPE IS STANDING ON A FOOT OF SCAFFOLD AND A PIECE OF WIRE ON THE FRONT OF A HOUSE AT THE LOW END OF PEVERELL STREET, AND A MAN IN A FLEECE PRESSED IT AN INCH IN WITH ONE HAND AND LET GO OF IT AND IT CAME BACK AND STAYED, AND NEITHER OF THE TWO OF THEM SAID ANYTHING ABOUT IT, AND THE ONLY PERSON WHO HAS DESCRIBED THAT PIPE TO ANYBODY WAS BEHIND A COUNTER UP THE PARADE AND WAS NOT IN THAT STREET THAT MORNING.**
