# Chapter 0334 — A Saturday in July

Saturday the eighth of July 2017, from about half past eight in the morning until about ten o'clock at night, a hall on Carrow Lane in Tideglass.

**THE HOTTEST EVENING OF THAT YEAR SO FAR CAME DOWN TO A STREET WITH A SHUTTER GOING UP AT THE FAR END OF IT, A VAN THAT STOOD AND WENT, A MAN WHO WENT UP A STREET WITH A BAG AND CAME BACK DOWN WITHOUT ONE, NINE PEOPLE IN A ROOM, A HARD-BACKED BOOK ON A SHELF IN A BUILDING BY THE WATER WITH NOTHING WRITTEN ON A LINE UNDER THE LAST THING IN IT, FOUR SENTENCES STILL GOING ROUND THAT TABLE, AND ONE QUESTION THAT NOBODY ANSWERED.**

It was the hottest day that year had managed. The tar outside was soft at the kerb by one in the afternoon and the front of that hall has a window that looks out over a parade of shops, and at about a quarter to nine that evening a man was on a ladder at the shutter of the shop at the far end of it and he was doing it on his own, and he came down the ladder and folded it and carried it twenty yard and put it up again at the next one.

Nobody was helping him and he was not asking anybody and there is nobody on that parade to ask.

At about ten past nine a van came down that parade at about ten miles an hour and stopped outside the fish shop, and there was no lettering on either side of it and nothing in the back that anybody could see from the road, and it stood there for about ten minutes, and then it went on and did not come back.

At about half past nine a man went up the low end of a street with a bag over his shoulder and there was nobody on that pavement, and at about a quarter to ten he came back down that street without it, and nobody in that hall could have said what he had gone up there for or what he had brought back down, and there were nine people in that hall and not one of them went to the window.

At about ten o'clock there was a shower. It came over the roofs from the west and went through, and the tar on that parade went from soft to hard in about four minutes, and it did not reach that hall.

---

There were nine of them in that hall at half past eight, and it was still too warm in there, and the top of that window had been open since about six and the fan on the wall had been on low since about half past six.

Eight of them were in that room at the end of May, and there is a set of those eight that anybody in that room could write out in about a minute, and nobody in that room has ever written it out. The man who does that hall was standing up with his cloth over his shoulder going round the edges of the table. The man in his forties was nearest the door with his forearms down either side of a cup and his cup about three inches from the edge. The man in his sixties was in the split seat. The man in his thirties was in late with his bag on the floor. The man who works nights had his coat still on and was leaning on the frame of the door. The man from the parade was early again with both hands round his cup. The woman who works in a shop had turned her chair round and was looking at the wall. The woman of about fifty-five had hung her coat over the back of her chair and had brought nothing with her.

The last of them was the man in a fleece, and he had not been in that room before, and he came in at about ten past eight and put his coat over the one empty chair without asking anybody whether he could.

The man who does that hall looked at him.

"You'll be all right," said the man who does that hall.

"I'm all right," said the man in a fleece, and nobody asked him anything else, and that is the whole of what that room ever did with him.

The man in his thirties said good evening to her and she said good evening back and that was all.

She said it about halfway through, out of nothing, with her hands round her cup.

"That book is still on that shelf."

Nobody asked her which book and nobody needed to, and she said it again anyway.

"That book on the second shelf up in the bay at the end of that landing. It's a hard-backed one with the edges rubbed and it is open at nineteen of them and the last one of the nineteen is a lamp, and somebody put a lamp in there about two years ago under a heading for that sort of thing, and there's not one thing under that heading since. I've counted it. It's nine hundred and fifty-five days off that line and I counted it myself off a calendar in the kitchen and I've counted it twice, and I go past that bay about twice a day and nobody has ever written a thing on that line since that lamp, and there's a gap at the foot of that page about two inches deep that nobody's ever left and the book's got it."

The man in his forties did not look up.

"Nobody's going to check that."

"No," said the woman of about fifty-five. "And I'm not asking anybody to."

"It's a lamp," said the man in his sixties.

"It is," said the woman of about fifty-five, and put her cup down, and nobody took it any further, and nobody in that room asked her how long she had been counting, and nobody asked her to stop.

---

They were the same four the room had been given since the twelfth of January, and the woman of about fifty-five had sat through all of them being said out loud at that table, and there was still no way of setting one of them beside another to see what the pair came to, and she had stopped saying so out loud, because saying it had not moved any of the four.

"Four," said the woman who works in a shop, to nobody in particular.

"Still four," said the man in his forties.

"None of us has put a fifth one on and none of us has taken a fourth one off, and I have not the least idea where the four came from, and I am not going to sit in this room and add up four things none of us has ever seen the ends of."

The man in his forties said that if those four were going to turn into something else it would not be because somebody at that table counted them again over a cup.

"That is not the first time anybody has said that," said the man from the parade, to the man in his forties, and not unkindly.

"And it will not be the last time somebody says it," said the man in his forties, "and there is no writing it down anywhere, and if those four ever do turn into some fifth thing it will not be because nine people in a room on a hot night decided to take a look at them."

Nobody at that table took that further either.

At about a quarter to nine the man in a fleece set his cup down nearer the front edge of that table than anybody else had put one all evening, and the man in his forties looked at it.

"There's a thing I've been asked twice about," he said, "and I'll say it once and then I'll never say it again. I was asked, in one place, to do something for nothing, and I said yes. I was asked, in another place about a month later, to do another thing for nothing, and I said yes again, and I did both. And what I want to know is whether anybody in this room has ever been asked to do something they were not going to be paid for and said no."

Nobody at that table said anything to that for about half a minute.

The man in his forties put both hands flat on the boards of that table.

"And it stays a question," said the man in his forties.

"That's right," said the man in a fleece, and not unkindly, and that was the end of it. He lifted his cup and drank, and the man in his forties turned his cup round on the boards, and nobody at that table carried it any further.

Then the man in his thirties said that the two slates on that corner gable are still on that gable with the nail heads a good deal further out of them than they were in May, and the man in his forties said he had it from a man in a shop and he was not going to build on a man in a shop, and the man in his thirties said aye.

Nobody turned the corner lamp off and nobody had to, because that one comes on by itself at that time in the year and stays on until about half past six in the morning, and the case was shut and locked at about twenty past nine, and the chairs went up on the wall afterwards one at a time by whoever was nearest, and the man who does that hall did the last two without asking anybody for help with either of them, and the porch light on that side of the door was on the whole of the time and nobody in that hall knows which of the two of them turned it on.

A man came past that porch light at about ten o'clock with a bag over his shoulder, and nobody inside that hall could have told anybody what was in it.

At about a quarter past ten somebody in that hall asked whether anybody knew what the woman of about fifty-five had come in with, and nobody answered that either, and she said she had not come in with anything, and then she said that there was a bowl under a wet strip at the end of that corridor which nobody had asked her to put there and which she had emptied four or five times a day since the Tuesday of that week without being told to, and nobody at that table said a word about the bowl or about the carpet on its side against a wall three streets away, and those two things have never had anything to do with one another, and she is not going to be told otherwise by anybody who was not in that corridor.

**NINE PEOPLE WENT OUT OF THAT HALL AT TEN O'CLOCK INTO A STREET THAT HAD BEEN SOFT AND THEN HARD SINCE ABOUT ONE O'CLOCK IN THE AFTERNOON, AND A PADLOCK WENT ON THE CASE BEHIND THEM, AND A MAN IN A FLEECE WALKED DOWN THAT STREET TOWARD A PARADE WITH HIS HANDS IN HIS POCKETS.**
