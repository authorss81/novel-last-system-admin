# Chapter 0228 — Two Sums

Friday the eighteenth of March 2016, from about ten in the morning, a shop on a parade in Tideglass, behind the counter.

**ONE SHEET OF PAPER, THE SAME SHEET FOR EVERY JOB, NOT ADDRESSED TO ANYBODY, NOT A BILL, SIGNED AT THE BOTTOM BY A CREW THAT WAS NEVER ASKED WHAT THE SUM ON THE TOP OF IT WAS, BECAUSE THE SUM ON THE TOP OF IT WAS ALREADY PRINTED.**

He brought it out in an envelope, which he had not had when he signed it.

Nell Ardent took it out of the envelope at ten past ten and read it the way she reads everything, once, straight through, and then read it again with her finger on the line under the figure, and she had her own ruler in her bag and did not use it because there was nothing to compare.

"A claim form," she said.

"That's what I said."

"No, you said you didn't know what it was." She put it flat on the counter and squared it to the edge, which is a thing about her. "This is a claim form. It's a claim, and a claim is not a bill, and it is not addressed to anybody, and it is the same sheet for every job there is in that region, and it is correct in every part of it, and I can tell you that without going near a single thing anybody has said to me in a year."

She read it out. Job at the top in a box, and a rule, and then the figure, and then the line under the figure, and then a long blank, and then at the bottom, above another rule, the printed words.

*The sum for the work, as agreed: £163. — the rate for that work as agreed, 1987.*

"Signed at the bottom," said Nell Ardent, "by a man of thirty-two in a good coat who does not live in that county and was not on a crew."

"He thought I was going out."

"He thought you were going out because nobody in that yard had met you, and you were standing in ten feet of concrete in a good coat at half past eleven in the morning, and the man who runs it is eighty-nine." She turned the sheet round. "And he did not ask you what the job was, and you did not tell him, and you signed it anyway."

---

Behind the counter there is a rack of keys, a till, a spike for orders, and a book that has been open on the same page since February. Tomas Renn is a steward here and is on the counter that morning because the man who is on the counter that morning had a hospital appointment.

"He'll not be missed," Renn said. "Where's the sheet."

Nell Ardent put it down.

Renn looked at it for about a second and a half.

"That's a claim form," he said. "That's what they give you at the yard. Everybody signs it."

"Everybody signs it," said Nell Ardent.

"Aye."

"Does anybody know what's on the top of it?"

Renn picked it up again and looked at the top of it, at the box, and at the figure, and at the line under the figure, and then he put it down and squared it up to the edge of the counter, which he has never done in his life and did not notice he had done.

"There's the sum," he said.

"That's the sum," said Nell Ardent. "What is the sum."

"It's what the job comes to."

"What did that job come to?"

"That's the sum," said Renn.

**AND THAT IS THE WHOLE OF IT, AND THE MAN BEHIND THE COUNTER WAS RIGHT AND WAS BEING HELPFUL AND WAS ANSWERING A QUESTION, AND THERE IS NO WAY TO ASK THAT QUESTION SO THAT A PERSON HEARS IT.**

---

The woman who keeps the counter most weeks had come in on her own account at about a quarter to eleven and was standing at the end of it with a carrier bag, and she had been in that shop about six years and had worked in two others, and nobody in that shop knew how old she is, including her, in the way that nobody does.

Hanna Wray. Her name was on the invoice spike going back about a year and a half and that is how Nell Ardent had it.

"I did the Nettlebed," Hanna Wray said. "Last year. In the August, and I did the two at Brackmoor before that, and I've got a bit of paper at home for each of them because the money's posted and you keep it, don't you."

"You've got eleven," said Jonas Mercer.

"I've got nine of mine and I've got two off the van that aren't mine and I've never asked for those." She put the bag down. "And I'll tell you the thing about it, and then I'll go and do my Friday, because I am not stood here making a speech. The thing about it is that the sum on that paper is not what the job cost. Everybody in that shop has known that for about two years. We asked, in the August, and the man in the office there said no, he said there's no charge for anything, and then he showed us the book, and the book's got two columns on it, and one's the parts and the fuel and the hire, and one's the sum."

"And the first one is bigger."

"The first one is about four times bigger," said Hanna Wray. "Every time. And everybody has known, and nobody's done anything, because there's nobody to do it to. That's not an accusation against anybody. There's nobody in that yard to be an accusation against. And they don't cheat, that's the thing everybody gets wrong when they hear about it. The sum's the rate and the rate's old and the rate's the rate and it's the same as it was and nobody's robbed anybody, because the work got done."

"Then it's the same sentence twice," said Nell Ardent.

"It's the same sentence three times if you count the year," said Hanna Wray.

"The first one of mine was the fifth of May," said Nell Ardent. "The week the water went down. What was the year on that one?"

"Same year as all of them. Every one of mine's got the same year printed under the figure and it isn't changed and it isn't going to be." Hanna Wray had her hand on the bag and had not taken it up yet. "I'll tell you what nobody's said about it. In the May of that year there was one of those, and it was the first, and the year underneath it was old then as well, and I read it and I thought: that's a lot for a fortnight, that's not going to be worth the petrol. And I did it anyway and I was right about the petrol and I was wrong about the figure, and I've had a year and a half of them and it's never once been different, and the reason nobody's ever said anything about the year is that the year doesn't seem to do anything. It sits there. It's the same on the May one as it is on the one I did in March." She picked the bag up. "That's the whole of what I know about it and I've had it in a bag on a shelf for a year and a half and I've never once needed to open it."

Nell Ardent did not ask her the next question. Jonas Mercer wanted to and did not, and Hanna Wray went and did her Friday.

Nell Ardent picked the sheet up off the counter and put it back in the envelope and put the envelope in her bag, and it went in her bag and not into his coat, and he did not ask her to.

"You had that in a coat pocket for three weeks," she said.

"Nineteen days."

"You could have taken it in on the Monday."

"I could have."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because on the Monday I didn't know what it was," said Jonas Mercer, "and on the eighteenth of March I do, and the difference between those two is one sheet of paper with a year printed under a figure on it, and I signed it in a good coat standing in ten feet of concrete with a man of eighty-nine who thought I was on a crew, and if I had handed it in on the Monday it would have gone into a bag in this shop and come out as a thing somebody had brought."

**AND A SHEET OF PAPER THAT HAD BEEN IN AN INSIDE POCKET FOR NINETEEN DAYS WENT INTO A HANDBAG ON A COUNTER IN A SHOP, AND THE FIGURE ON IT DID NOT CHANGE, AND THE YEAR UNDER IT DID NOT CHANGE, AND A WOMAN WHO KEEPS THE COUNTER MOST WEEKS WENT AND DID HER FRIDAY.**
