# Chapter 0786 — The Four Feet Of Wall

Tuesday the seventeenth of January 2023, in this borough. A woman of seventy-eight is out in her own yard at number twelve from first light to the end of the light, and four feet of wall that has stood bare since the summer is what she is out there for.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS FOUR FEET OF FROST WALL WITH NOTHING ON TOP OF IT AND A HEAP OF LOOSE BRICK IN THE GRASS AND MEANS TO LAY FOUR OF THEM ALONG IT AND BE DONE, HER SISTER TAKES THAT FOUR FEET DOWN TO THE BOTTOM OF THE FOOTING AND PACKS IT AND BUILDS IT BACK UP DRY WITH EVERY PERPEND PACKED AND NO MORTAR IN IT ANYWHERE, AND THREE WEEKS LATER THE OLD WOMAN TAKES FOUR BRICKS OFF THE TOP COURSE AND PUTS THEM BACK HER OWN WAY.**

She has been seventy-eight since before she came to number twelve, and it is a Tuesday, and her sister walks in through the yard gate before the middle of the day, and nobody has to let her in, because that gate has stood propped back against the wall since September.

---

The coal shed is shut and its padlock is hanging open in the hasp. There is a brush lying across the way out to the yard and she goes over it. On the table in the front room the gas envelope is face up and square with the edge and the taped box is under its cloth at the far end of it, and she does not go in there. The kitchen window has been down in its own frame since before she came and the sheet on the kitchen floor is where it was put down in the autumn. Down at the near end of the yard wall the new kerb stands along the front of the flower bed with the wall dark from the foot up behind it, and out in the side passage four stones of a bay stand a hand's width proud of the rest of them. Neither of those is this morning.

The wall is along the yard wall at the coal shed end and it is a frost wall.

It is about two feet high and it runs the width of the coal shed, and it carries nothing at all. It is there because the ground behind it is clay and the fence it stands behind wants the clay a foot below where it is. It is older than the house and it was built of brick in a lime putty that has gone to sand.

Three feet of the coping on it was rebuilt in the autumn and is good. The other four feet has nothing on it at all, and its top course has been open to the weather since before she came. Water goes into the joints of it and stands there, and when it freezes it goes off in the night with a sound in that yard like a plate put down hard, and the four feet lifts about the thickness of a thumbnail and comes back down on itself in three or four places at once, and the top course ends up proud at one end and low at the other.

Her plan is four bricks.

There is a heap of loose brick in the grass at the foot of the wall, put there in the summer when a man took the front off a house at the top of the lane and left the skip, and she walked out into it and took what she wanted. About forty bricks are in that heap. Four of them are sound and whole and hard, and the rest are broken in half or have a piece out of one end.

She means to lay the four sound ones along the top of those four feet and be done with the wall for the winter, because that is what the wall is short of, and it is at the top of the wall that the water is getting in.

---

Her sister is in the yard before her, walking straight down it, because there is a light on in the window and the old woman is not in the front room.

"There is a woman in that yard in a coat in January," her sister says, "and there is a heap of brick at the foot of her wall with four of them taken out of it."

"They are good bricks."

"They are good bricks of the wrong thickness." Her sister crouches and picks one up and turns it edge on between her fingers. "That is out of a different house and a different kiln. Those four will stand up above the course they are on like a row of teeth, and the water will go along the top of them and behind them and straight back into the same joints."

---

Then she looks at the wall.

She does not touch it for a while. She runs the side of her hand along the coping that was rebuilt in the autumn and then along the bare four feet at the same height, and her hand comes away with grit on it.

"Put your hand under the top course."

She puts her fingers under the top course where it overhangs and lifts. It lifts about the thickness of a thumbnail at the near end and nothing at all at the coal shed end.

"Now the ground at the foot of it."

"It is ground."

"It is the last of the clay coming up out of that bed every winter." Her sister goes down on her knees and puts the back of her thumbnail into a joint near the bottom. "That clay comes up under four feet of wall that has nothing over it, and it comes up unevenly, because it never comes up evenly, and where it comes up high it lifts the course above it and leaves that proud at one end."

"Then it wants weight on it."

"It wants putting back down where it was, and something on it that keeps the water out of the top of it. Four bricks out of a heap is not that. That is four lumps laid on a wall that moves."

---

So they take the four feet down, and it takes the whole of the day, and the first of it is going down.

Her sister knocks the courses out from the top of the wall down, one at a time, working along from the coal shed end, and lays the bricks out on the grass in the order they came out. Every brick in that four feet comes out whole or with no more than a nibble off one end, because the lime putty between them has gone to sand and the whole of it comes apart in the hand.

At the bottom of it they come to the bottom of the footing. That is where the ground stops being soft under your thumb and starts being hard stuff, about ten inches below where the wall is standing now, and they can see it because the old footing was dug as a trench and its sides are still there in the ground, in the same place, as straight as a cut.

"There," her sister says. "That is the bottom of it. That is where somebody stood when they dug it."

"And the ground has come up from there."

"The ground has come up from there into the hole you can see, and it has been coming up into it every winter since, and it has nowhere to go, so it goes up."

---

Then she packs it.

She packs the bottom of the footing first, and she packs it the whole way, not the top of it. It goes in as broken wall, and there is broken wall in that yard in a heap by the shed — lumps of old brick and stone about the size of two fists and bigger, out of the foot of that wall and off a boundary post that came out in the summer. She stands those lumps in on end and close together, the way you stand stones in a footing, so that there is no hole anywhere down in it. Then hardcore over that, in layers, packed down with the back of the handle until the handle rings. No water goes into any of it. She says so twice, and the second time she says it to herself.

The other half of it takes most of the afternoon, and it is the ground. Both sides of the wall, back from where the bricks were to the untouched ground behind and in front of them, are taken out to the depth of the footing in a spade's width, packed with the same stuff in layers going back up, and trodden underfoot as they go. Nothing goes in there loose. She puts a layer in and stamps it all over with her own weight and the weight of the bar, and then puts another layer in on top of that.

---

Then the wall goes back up, and it goes back up dry.

There is no mortar anywhere in that four feet of wall and no cement and nothing wet at all in it. She lays every brick on its bed of chippings and fills every perpend with the same chippings, driven in with the point of her own chisel and packed tight enough that you cannot put a thumbnail in any joint of it. The bricks go back in the order they came out in, which is the whole of why the wall fits, because a brick that came out of a course goes back into that course, and every course of them goes back exactly where it was, eight courses in a wall about two feet high, and the eighth one is the one the coping bedded on.

The coping goes on the top. The four stones for those four feet came off in a frost two winters back and have been lying face up in the grass ever since, and she has walked round them since the summer without turning one over. Her sister gets each of them up with a wedge driven under the low end and the end of the bar, lays them across the top on a bed of chippings, and packs the same chippings into the joints of that course too.

---

It is better than that wall has been since it was built.

Her sister stands in the grass and pushes the heel of her hand down on the coping, and it does not move. She runs the flat of her hand the length of the four feet and down the face of it, and there is nowhere along it she can put a thumbnail.

"It is a good wall."

"It is a good wall," her sister says. "And there is no mortar in it anywhere. It is brick and broken stone and it is packed the whole way up, so it will not lift, because there is nowhere in it for anything to come up into, and the clay coming out of that bed will come up against the outside of it and go over the top and away."

Her sister goes out through the yard gate in the last of the light with her chisel and her cold chisel in her bag and the old woman walks her as far as the gate and stands against the wall beside it and watches her go down the yard and out, and then goes back up the yard on her own and stands in front of four feet of wall that is not going anywhere.

---

Then three weeks go by and the old woman takes four bricks off the top course and puts them back her own way.

She does it on her own in a dry spell in the middle of the morning, and she does it because she cannot stand looking at it.

From the yard the top course of that wall shows its bedding. That is what a wall packed with chippings looks like from four feet away. Every perpend between the two top courses is a dark line the thickness of a finger with pale chippings standing in it and catching the light, and a pale line runs the whole length of the coping above them, and from where she stands at the kitchen window it looks like the wall has been built of rubble with the brick face laid on the front of it for show.

It does not leak. She has looked at it every day since it was done, and there is nothing coming out of the top of it and nothing standing on the coping.

So she takes four bricks off the top course at the yard end and out on the grass, and beds them back on a scrape of the fine stuff she has swept off the yard over the winter, the dust and the grit that comes off the wall itself. She does not pack a single perpend, because packing is the thing she cannot stand the look of.

---

Then the wall begins to go again at that end.

The top course at the yard end is not proud the whole way. It is proud at the coal shed end where it has always been proud and low at the yard end where those four bricks are, and one of the four goes under her thumb and rocks about the width of a thumbnail.

She stands in the yard in the first of the light with her thumb under it. The four joints of that course are open the whole length of them and she can see daylight through two of them, and that four feet of wall is the best thing that has been done to this yard in the time she has been in it, and the four feet at the yard end is going again.
