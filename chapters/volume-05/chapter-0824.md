# Chapter 0824 — The Sight Down The Bed

Friday the thirtieth of June 2023, in her own back garden at number twelve from the middle of the afternoon until there is no light on the top of that wall, and a raised bed of broken brick that is leaning out of line at one end is the whole of that Friday.

**THERE IS A RAISED BED OF BROKEN BRICK ABOUT NINE FEET LONG IN HER OWN BACK GARDEN AT NUMBER TWELVE THAT HAS BEGUN TO LEAN OUT OF LINE AT THE NEAR END, AND SHE MEANS TO TAKE THE LEANING END OUT AND PUT IT BACK, AND HER SISTER COMES INTO THAT GARDEN ABOUT HALF WAY THROUGH WITH A BARROW SHE IS TOLD SHE CAN HAVE, AND TAKES IT AWAY FULL.**

That bed is about nine feet long and two feet wide and two courses high, laid dry, no mortar in a single joint, out of broken brick and whatever came out of the old kitchen floor when the kitchen was done.

She built it herself and it is the oldest thing in that garden that she has made.

It is built along the side of that garden where the soil is worst, because the bed was never in the good ground. The good ground in that garden is at the front of the house and that is where the beds are and where the beans go. This one is at the bottom of the slope, where the ground has been a path once and then a scrap place, and the ground there is made of what was tipped on it.

---

It has begun to lean out at the near end.

Not falling. Leaning.

The near three feet of that bed have pushed out towards her by about two inches at the top course and not at all at the bottom, which means the bottom of it is still where it was and the top has walked out, and two inches at the top of a two-course wall is a great deal of lean.

The reason is underneath and it is not a mystery.

That end of the bed is on ground that has gone down. She put a fork into it in the spring when the beans went in at the other end and the fork went into that ground about as deep as the spade would go and then into something soft underneath.

A dry wall of broken brick has no mortar in it and gets its strength from two things only: the weight of itself and the bond. Every brick breaks the joint of the one under it, and a bond is what stops a wall opening up and leaning.

Two courses of broken brick on ground that is going down will lean, because there is nothing under it to be pushed against and nothing to tie the top course back to the bottom one except the bed of rubble and the ground it is laid on.

And what is under the bottom course at that end is not a bed of rubble at all any more. It is the tipped ground, and the tipped ground has been going down for about six years, one wet winter at a time.

---

She starts at about the middle of the afternoon.

What she does first is take the top course off the leaning three feet, because that is the only way to get at the bottom course and because the bottom course is not moving and is not the fault.

The bricks come off into a barrow, in the order she lays them down. She takes them off from the far end of the three feet backwards and puts each one down in the barrow and keeps the two courses separate so she knows which row was the top.

Broken brick comes off a dry wall with two hands and a bit of care and no tools at all, because there is nothing to undo. She turns each one in its bed first, the way you turn a stone in a wall, and then lifts it out flat and sets it in the barrow with the rest.

It takes about forty minutes and about a hundred and forty bricks come off that bed in the two courses and she does not drop one.

Her sister comes into that garden about half way through the afternoon with the barrow.

"You want this," her sister tells her.

She wants it. Her sister takes the one with the bricks in it out through the side gate and comes back a minute later and takes the second one too, and the wheelbarrow she has brought from the shed as well.

"You'll want to bring all that back," her sister tells her.

"You can have it," she tells her. "Bring it back in the morning."

And her sister goes out through the side gate and does not come back into that garden.

Under the bottom course at that end there is no bed and no rubble.

There is about two inches of soil and then broken brick and the odd lump of the old concrete floor, and that is what that end of the bed has been standing on since she made it, and about a foot of that end of the bed has now sunk into it.

She digs it out.

The ground comes up with a trowel and a fork in lumps, and under the lumps the ground is grey and it is the old tipped stuff, and there is about a foot of that to come out before she gets to something that has not moved, and a foot of digging in a garden that size is a day if you are careful and it is an hour if you are seventy-eight and know where to stand.

Then she puts it back in.

Broken brick out of that bed, broken down, and the lumps of old concrete, in layers, packed with the end of a length of pipe — the same thing she did with the wall at the back of a unit about a year ago, and the same reason, which is that the end of a length of pipe puts a person about the weight of a kettle into a place they cannot get a boot into.

Three layers, and each one packed until the pipe will not go into it, and then the top of that footing levelled with a straightedge and the high places taken down and the low places built up until the straightedge has nothing to find.

No mortar. There is none in that bed and there was never any in it and there is not going to be any in it now, and that is not a principle, it is what that bed is. A dry bed of broken brick can take up the ground going underneath it a foot at a time, a bit at a time, for about as long as the wall is going to be there, and a bed with mortar in it does about half of that and then splits down the line of the mortar and looks like nothing that grew.

The brick goes back the same afternoon and it takes another hour.

The first course goes back with the bond the way it was, every brick breaking the joint of the one under it, and each one set flat in the bed of broken brick below it and packed down with the heel of her hand so that it does not rock.

She has a spirit level she trusts. She puts it on the top of the first course at the near end and works along, and the first course of nine feet of bed goes in and it comes up to about the same height it was all the way along.

The top course goes back over it.

And here is where that Friday stops being a Friday about a bed, and it is the last half hour of the light and she is on her knees on the grass with her hands in the broken brick.

There is not enough brick in that bucket.

That is the whole of it. There is a bucket of leftovers by the wall and there is the barrow behind her with a hundred and forty bricks out of a bed that has been down for about twenty minutes, and there is about nine inches of that top course at the near end that cannot be filled with what is left.

She has used the wrong bricks.

Every body she used in that first course was picked because it was flat and because it was the right depth, and a top course is not picked that way. She went round that bucket in the spring and put aside about thirty bricks for the top, and the thirty bricks in the bucket are thirty bricks with one good face and nothing else whatever: too thick on one side, too thin on the other, cracked in two, and one of them with a bit of old concrete stuck to one end.

They go in. It is a top course, it is nine inches of it at the near end, and there is nothing else available and she is not starting the bed again on Monday because the bucket was wrong.

She gets the high ones off the top with the back of the bar so that the face of the course is even with the rest of it, and that is the best that nine inches can be done, and she packs the joints full because a full joint is what keeps water out of a dry wall and an empty one is a crack with a year in it.

Then she sights down it.

She gets down on the grass at the near end with her eye about a foot above the top course and she looks along the length of that bed to the far end, which is nine feet away and eleven inches higher than where her eye is.

Broken brick cannot make a straight line and neither can this. There is no straight line on the top of that bed from end to end and there is not going to be, and anybody who sights down it expecting one has come to the wrong place.

What a broken brick top course does give you is a wave, and a wave you can read.

She goes along it twice. The wave comes up and goes down along nine feet the way a wave does, about the same every time, and about two thirds of the way along there is a place where the eye stops.

The whole of that bed comes to a point there.

The course underneath it is about half an inch low. Not an inch and not a quarter, which is what she would have seen if a whole course had been wrong. Half an inch, over about four inches of it, which is one brick.

That one brick is out of the bucket. It is the thickest thing in that bucket with a bit of concrete on it, and it is in the wall, and it is half an inch proud of the course above it at one end and flush at the other because she got the high off it with the bar and could not get at the low end of it in the time there was.

And that is where that bed bends. Nine feet of broken brick on a footing she dug and packed and levelled with a straightedge, and the whole of the line of it comes to a point at the fourth inch of one brick out of a bucket that had thirty in it in the spring.

Her sister has not come back into that garden and the barrow is behind the shed and the front step and the drain at the back of that house have not been touched in any of it.

She stands at that end of that bed in what is left of the afternoon and sights nine feet of it again, and the line comes to a point at the same brick the second time, and it will be there next spring and the one after that, and there is nothing in that garden or in this borough that is going to move it.
