# Chapter 0682 — Twelve

Tuesday the sixteenth of November 2021, in this borough. He is in his unit until midday and on a tip at the far side of the ring road in the afternoon, and the whole of the afternoon is a taped parcel on the top of a heap that he is not going to open and is not going to put in his van.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES FINDS A TAPED PARCEL WITH A NUMBER ON THE LABEL AND A STREET HE HAS GONE PAST ON THE TOP OF A HEAP AT A TIP, DOES NOT OPEN IT AND DOES NOT PUT IT IN HIS VAN, WRITES TWELVE ON A BIT OF CARD AND PUTS THE CARD IN THE SHIRT POCKET, AND THE CARD IN THE SHIRT POCKET IS THE FIRST NUMBER HE HAS WRITTEN DOWN FOR ANYBODY SINCE JULY.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is on his own all day except for a man at the bench opposite, a woman at the till of the shop where he buys timber, and a merchant who lets him into a tip.

---

The morning is at the unit and the shoebox is on the top of his own bench where he cannot come in without seeing it, and the element is in it, and the lid is on it.

He has been in that unit since before the others came in and has not gone near the box, and that has been the arrangement since the third of August, and he has got quite good at it, and being good at it is not the same as it being settled.

He makes a set of brackets for a job on the ring road and machines them on the bandsaw and files the ends and does not use the bench the box is on, because the other bench is two feet away and takes the same setting up with a walk to it, and he has made that trade once already and has not gone back on it.

---

The man at the bench opposite is in early and there is tea.

"Who was that on the telephone yesterday?"

"That cupboard door off the corner."

"Ten minutes?"

"About ten minutes."

"Nothing to do with me," the man says, and goes back to his own work, which is the arrangement they have without ever having made it.

Then, a bit later, without looking up:

"There's a skip out there with a load of nothing in it."

"There's always a skip with a load of nothing in it."

"That one's got boxes in it."

"Boxes are always the last thing in."

The man does not take it further and neither does he, and the radio goes on low at the bench with the talk on it that neither of them follows.

---

Later he goes to the shop on the ring road for a length of oak that has come in, because he would rather buy oak than use anything else for a sill and he has a list in his head of what he wants.

There is one person in front of him and the woman at the till is about thirty-five.

"You after the oak?"

"Aye."

"There's some behind you and some on the wall. The wall's dearer because it's the wrong length for about half the jobs people want."

"The wall's fine."

"You're not fussed."

"I'm not fussed."

She writes it on the ticket and looks at him for a second.

"You're that joiner that came round my sister's," she says. "Number whatever, off the corner."

He does the thing he does with that, which is a small pause, and then he nods.

"Aye."

"Tell her the shelf is coming Friday and I haven't got a date for the beading yet."

"Right."

"She'll want a date."

"Friday's a date."

"It's not a date, it's a day of the week."

"Then she'll have Friday," he says, and pays for the oak, and goes out with it on the shoulder, and the woman says *no, I said tell her* to his back.

---

He unloads the oak at the unit and stacks it and puts the offcut in the rack, and then he goes to take the afternoon job off the man who subbed it for him, which is rubbish off a bathroom on the far side of the ring road, and he takes the van round because the tip is on the way.

The van goes with the drawer shut and the shoebox on the bench at the unit and the canvas bag in the footwell with the stranger's brush in the side pocket and the stone in the leather bottom of it, and the drawer is not open because he shut it this morning, and he had never once shut it in all the time it had stood open, and he shut it before anybody else was in without any ceremony at all and then stood looking at the front of it.

He does not think about that while he is shutting it. He thinks about it later, at the lights.

---

The tip is a builders' merchant on the far side and he has a permit and a key for it and the merchant lets him in in the early afternoon.

There is about four foot of rubbish in it and most of it is plasterboard and loft insulation with the odd bag of household stuff on top, and the middle of it is a mattress and a wardrobe door and a bag of slates.

The parcel is on the top, near the far corner, half in the wet.

It is a box taped in a cross like the ones that come off the same sort of job, and it has the same kind of label on it, and this label has not gone the way those go.

The brown tape has gone hard and dark at the edges. The bottom corner has a grey mark on it.

The label is on the top of it, half of it. A printed label with a number on it in the middle, and a street above the number.

The street is not gone.

He reads it twice, and the street is there, and he knows the road, and he has gone past the end of it once, in the last week of August, without slowing down, and once on the sixth of October when he stopped with the engine running just past the turning, where he can see down the short road to the side of number twelve.

Number twelve.

---

He stands in the middle of the tip with a bag of plasterboard over one shoulder and looks at it.

The next part is the whole of the afternoon.

What he has is a street, a number, a parcel, and a van with a shoebox on the bench and a drawer shut for the first time this morning and nine pounds' worth of a brush he bought with his own money in a plastic bag in that drawer, because the woman who clears houses put her hand over her mouth in a kitchen on the sixteenth of July and said she would do the gate, and if he goes first he takes that off her and there is no way of putting it back.

He has never been up that road since the seventeenth of June.

If he puts this parcel on the passenger seat of that van he has a decision in it. Not a big one. He has to get to number twelve, and to get to number twelve he has to go up the road, and going up the road is a visit, and a visit is the one thing he has arranged with two women that he will not do.

If he leaves it on the tip it goes over the fence along with everything else, and it will be in a county bin and that will be that, and he will have lost a parcel that is not his, and there is no law about that and no rule and nobody would ever know.

---

He puts the plasterboard down.

He takes his hand off it twice before it is on the ground properly, because it is heavy and the wet has gone through the bag on one corner.

Then he goes and gets a bit of card out of the van off the roll he uses for scribing, and he tears a corner off it about the size of a playing card, and he writes on it.

T W E L V E

That is all it says. He does not write the street and he does not write a name and he does not write what it is for, because it is one of those and he has to be able to remember which in a month's time without the card saying so.

He puts it in the shirt pocket of the coat on the back of the cab door.

Then he stands there again.

Then he turns the parcel over, and there is a name on the other half of the label, and he reads it, and he puts it back down the way it was, and he does not write that down either.

---

He stands looking at it for a while and what he is doing is this: he is trying out the sentence.

Not saying it. Trying it. He has been in this borough eleven years and he has said *nothing* to a woman in a kitchen in July instead of a figure and he has driven past the end of that road in August and stopped at the end of it in October and let a woman at a kitchen window stand with her back to the glass, and every one of those was a sentence he tried out first and did not use.

There is a version of this afternoon where he puts a card on the seat and a parcel in the back and drives up there and puts it on the step and knocks, and the knock is the sentence, and the knock is the end of every arrangement he has.

He does not do it.

He does the other thing instead, which is that he looks at the parcel for a while longer and then he does the only part of it he is prepared to do, and the only part of it he is prepared to do is make sure that when it goes over the fence it is not just loose rubbish.

So he folds a bit of the bag of plasterboard over the corner of it and pulls a length of his own tape round it and tears it off, and he writes nothing else, and he puts the parcel back on the heap with the folded bit of bag over the top corner.

That is all he has done about it and it is not nothing and it is not enough and he knows exactly which of those two it is.

---

He shovelled plasterboard until the tip was down to about a foot and it was nearly done.

There is a thing about a merchant's tip and a rubbish man and that is that they are the same trade with different gates, and he has said that to two different people this year and got nowhere with it, and he does not say it to the merchant because the merchant is not asking.

The parcel is still up there. He did not put it under. It came off a van at the start of the week and it will be at the top of that tip for as long as the tip is there, and there is a bit of plasterboard bag over the top corner of it with his own tape round it, and no one in this borough will ever know that.

On the way out he locks the gate and puts the key in the box and the merchant says nothing about him coming back on the same day, and he gets in the van and the drawer is shut and it stays shut all the way home.

The card in the shirt pocket has TWELVE on it in his own writing with a scribing pencil, and that pencil does not come out of him for anything else all afternoon, and there is no other number on it and no name and no street.

He has not written a number down for anybody since the sixteenth of July, when a woman in a kitchen asked him how much a gate was and he said nothing and decided it in about four seconds while he was still looking at the drops.

---

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES HAS READ A NAME OFF A LABEL AT A TIP AND PUT IT BACK DOWN THE WAY IT WAS, HAS WRITTEN TWELVE ON A BIT OF CARD AND PUT IT IN THE POCKET THAT HAS HAD A BOY ON IT SINCE OCTOBER, HAS TURNED DOWN THE VERSION OF THE AFTERNOON WHERE HE PUTS A CARD ON THE PASSENGER SEAT AND A BOX IN THE BACK AND DRIVES UP THAT ROAD, HAS TAPPED A FOLDED BAG OVER THE CORNER OF A PARCEL THAT WILL GO OVER THE FENCE WITH EVERYTHING ELSE, AND HAS SHUT A DRAWER THAT HAD STOOD OPEN SINCE THE THIRD OF AUGUST.**
