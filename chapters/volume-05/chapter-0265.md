# Chapter 0265 — A Second Day

Saturday the seventeenth of September 2016, from about half past seven in the evening, the kitchen at the back of a parade of shops in Tideglass, and the top of that parade.

**THE BOY WAS ON THE DISHES AND WAS NOT RUNNING ANYTHING, AND NOBODY HAD ASKED HIM TO, AND TWO PEOPLE WENT OUT ON THE SAME EVENING WITHOUT HAVING TOLD EACH OTHER, AND TOLD NOBODY AT ALL.**

There had been a breakfast on at the shop that shuts early and the kitchen at the back of the parade had done the food for it, and the washing-up had gone on past the middle of the afternoon and into the evening, and the woman he works for had gone home at six and left him the rest of it.

There were about six things left in the sink at half past seven. There were two boxes by the door with the week's cardboard in them and one of them was leaking a bit of grey water onto the step. The boy had a cardigan over the back of a chair and a clock on the wall that was nine minutes fast and had been nine minutes fast since the summer and that nobody in that building had ever fixed.

He washed the six, and the hot water went off at about eight because the machine that heats it has a timer on it and nobody has ever reset it, and he finished in cold.

He was not on anything. He had not asked anybody for a day and he had not offered anybody one, and nobody in that kitchen knew where he would be at half past seven on a Saturday in September and there was nobody in it he would have told.

The bus from the college comes in on the lane at about a quarter past seven on a Saturday, and the young woman of about twenty got off it with a bag of books she did not need to have brought, and walked up the parade past a shuttered shop and a bank that shut at half past four.

The woman of about fifty-five was coming out of the door of a room over a shop at the end of the parade at about the same time, with a bag of her own and her key in her hand. She had been in that room since the Saturday morning and would be in it until Monday, and there was nothing in the week in front of her to be anywhere else with, and she had said none of that out loud to anybody and was not going to start.

They did not see each other on the way, because one of them had come off the bus at the top of the lane and the other had come out of a door at the bottom end of the parade, and they met at the top of it, and neither of them said a word to anybody at all.

---

They met at the top of it, outside the shop whose shutter goes up at about eight in the morning and will go up at about half past eight in the morning tomorrow.

"You're walking," said the young woman of about twenty.

"So are you."

"There's nobody in it."

"There's nobody in it," said the woman of about fifty-five.

They went down the middle of the parade. The shutter on the next shop along was half up and there was a light in the back of it, and there was a bus at the end by the bank with its interior light on and nobody on it but the driver, and a man came past with a dog and a plastic bag of something and did not stop.

"On the seventh," said the young woman of about twenty, "there was five of us stood in the rain in the middle of this and you didn't know four of them, and I didn't know the other three, and it wasn't even his idea, he'd copied it off somebody."

"I was there."

"You were there and you didn't know who was going to turn up, and neither did he," said the young woman of about twenty. "That's the bit I've been thinking about all week. It wasn't a big thing. Five, in the wet, doing nothing, for an hour and a half, and then the rain came on proper and everybody went in different directions like a lot of policemen."

"Five."

"Five," she agreed. "And it was better than nothing, and it was a good deal worse than this."

"How's it a good deal worse than this?"

"It's got nothing in it," said the young woman of about twenty. "That's all. There's nobody in this at all. There's you, and there's me, and there's a bus, and the whole of the rest of that week happened in rooms to a handful of people and none of it came out on a street."

"That's what I said."

"I know what you said," said the woman of about fifty-five.

They got to the end of the parade by the bank, where the shutters were down and had been down since about half past four, and there was nothing to do at the end of it, and so they turned round and came back up the other side.

"You'll have to tell somebody, won't you," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"No."

"Because otherwise it's just two people going for a walk."

"There's a difference," said the young woman of about twenty, "between two people going for a walk and a thing that happened. That's all it is, and I know how it sounds. If we tell anybody then it's a thing, and then somebody's done it twice, and two times is a habit, and then somebody asks how many of us there were, and there isn't a number. There is nobody's list and no form off anybody's counter and no number anywhere, and there is nobody who has to do one single thing about it, and that is the entire reason that it is better."

"I thought the reason was that nobody asked us to come."

"That's the same reason," said the young woman of about twenty. "Nobody said we can. That's it, that's all of it, that's the whole thing I had."

They came back up the parade to the shop with the shutter, and the young woman of about twenty went left towards the bus and the woman of about fifty-five went right towards the room over the shop, and neither of them said anything else and neither of them looked back, and there was a bus coming and it was empty.

---

The boy had the last of the cardboard boxes tied by about nine. The clock on the wall said five to ten when it was about quarter to ten, and he had not fixed it in the summer and was not going to.

There was nothing on the parade to see. He put the key in the door of the kitchen, and the door at the top of the steps, and came down into the dark, and there was nobody on it and nobody had told him about anything.

**AND THE SIX THINGS WERE DONE AND THE TWO BOXES WERE OUTSIDE THE DOOR FOR THE BIN MEN, AND THE SHUTTER NEXT DOOR ALONG FROM THE ONE WITH THE TRADE COUNTER WAS DOWN AND UP AGAIN BY HALF PAST SIX AND HAD BEEN SINCE THE SUMMER, AND THE BOY WENT DOWN THE PARADE WITH HIS CARDIGAN OVER HIS ARM AND DID NOT PASS ANYBODY.**
