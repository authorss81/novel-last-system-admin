# Chapter 0949 — Up The Head Gap

Friday the twenty-fifth of October 2024, at the head of the store door at the unit with the sun in the yard behind it. Up a head gap.

**THE STORE DOOR AT THE UNIT HAS DROPPED ON ITS TWO BUTTS AND STANDS THREE INCHES CLEAR OF THE HEAD OF ITS FRAME AT THE HINGE CORNER AND NOTHING AT ALL AT THE OTHER, AND THE HAND GOES FLAT AND UP INTO THAT GAP AND THEN TURNS END OVER END INSIDE IT, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE GAP GOES AND WHAT IS BEHIND THE HEAD OF THE DOOR.**

The store door is a boarded door in a timber frame and it was hung thirty years ago on two butts of the ordinary pattern. And it has done what a door on ordinary butts does over thirty years of being shut and opened in this weather, which is to come down on its hinge at the hinge corner and open at the head.

At the hinge corner the gap is three inches.

Along the top of the door it narrows as the leaf leans, and by the latch corner there is nothing at all between the top edge of the door and the frame, and a folded strip of paper has to be worked in there with a fingernail to find out which way round it went. At the foot the leaf has closed on the same hinge and the bottom edge is now down on the flags, which is why the door shuts hard and why nobody in this yard has swept under it in a while.

The door itself is four boards with two ledges across it and a total thickness of two inches. And the paint on it is the green paint of thirty years ago over an older white, and the paint is brittle everywhere it is not rubbed, and along the top edge it has lifted off the third board in flakes the size of a thumbnail and left the bare wood underneath a shade paler than the rest of the leaf.

The hand is put flat and up into the gap at the hinge corner and then turned end over end inside it, so that the knuckle comes out where the palm went in and the width of the gap comes off that and not off the eye looking up into it.

The gap is as deep as the frame is thick, which is three inches of sawn oak, and it is a clean rectangular hole with the head of the frame running along the top of it. In the corners of that hole the old paint has crazed off in scales and the scales have gone soft and grey, and a good deal of the dust in the frame head has gone down with them.

Above the head of the frame is the lintel, another beam of the same oak let into the wall. And between the head of the frame and the soffit of the lintel there is a run of old lime and rubble about four inches deep that nobody has looked at since the wall was put up.

The head of the frame is a single timber through-tenoned into the jambs at each end, and the tenon has withdrawn from the head by about a quarter of an inch as the oak dried. That withdrawal is why the gap is a hole at all instead of a line. A head like that is not fixed to anything above it. It sits on the lintel and its own tenons sit in the jambs, and the jambs are what carry the weight, so the head is free to move in every direction except down, and down is the one direction it has taken.

It is a heavier way of making a frame than a wedged lintel, and it was the ordinary way in a building of this age, and there are two such heads in this yard and both of them have gone the same way. A joiner who wanted a frame to stay square for forty years tenoned it, and a joiner who wanted a frame to come apart again wedged it, and this one was tenoned and has come as far as the oak has let it.

A butt hinge is two leaves and a knuckle and a pin, and the pin sits in a hole bored through both halves of the knuckle, and the hole is bored a sixteenth of an inch under the size of the pin, because a pin that is a tight fit in cold weather will not swing in a cold month.

So the pin has always had a little play in it, and thirty years of that play has taken the inside of the knuckle into a crescent. A crescent of clearance at a knuckle is a door that comes down about a sixteenth of an inch every ten years without anything having visibly happened to it at all.

Both leaves of each butt are still screwed to their own timber and the screws are still in. The screws are brass and turned into oak and they have rusted at their own shoulders into the paint, and none of them is loose, so all of the drop in that door is in the two knuckles and nowhere else.

The drop is the reason the door is heavy to lift and light to shut. Weight that used to be shared between the top hinge and the bottom one is now all of it on the bottom one, and the top hinge has a knuckle on it that a knife blade would pass through.

The door does not move under the hand at the head of it, and not one thing of the play between that leaf and that frame can be felt for. The frame itself is oak bedded into a wall of rubble that has not shifted, and the soffit above the frame head is old lime and rubble that came back hard in the year it was built and is brittle at the face of it.

---

The woman from two doors down is standing in the office doorway with the door standing open against the wall behind her. And she is not coming out of it and she is not going in.

She stays where she is for a while with her arms down and looks at the flags. And once she turns her head about ninety degrees without moving anything else about herself, and nothing else in the yard moves either.

The office is open along that side for twenty feet and the light comes in across the yard and lays a rectangle of it on the flags inside. And the rectangle does not move except when she moves, and the store door four feet away stands square across its own shade with its head three inches clear of the frame.

The office is a long low building with a corrugated roof and its own door off the same yard, and the store door is four feet along from it, and both are hung on butts and both have come down at the head over the years. Only one of them has a gap wide enough to get a hand into, and the difference between the two is four years of the same weather.

The hand had been out of the gap and down at his side before she was at the door. The gap at the hinge corner of that door is three inches and it is three inches deep, and nowhere else along the head is it a hand's width, and the dust and the old paint in the top corners of it are where they have always been.

The office door four feet away has half an inch at its head on the same corner and a knife blade's worth of play in its top knuckle, and the two doors in this yard will go on in one another's company doing the same thing at different rates.

The gap at that hinge corner stays three inches by three inches and the office door four feet away will have two inches of its own half inch in another twenty years. The head of that door is where the door is, rather than where it was.
