# Chapter 0884 — A Hand's Width Of Hard Ground

Tuesday the thirteenth of February 2024, along the front at number nine from the time the light has come onto the flags until the street has gone quiet. The strip of ground where the turf came up.

**THERE IS TWELVE FEET OF BARE GROUND ALONG HER FRONT WALL WHERE A STRIP OF TURF CAME UP IN THE SUMMER AND IS NOW STACKED AGAINST THE SAME WALL, AND SHE MEANS TO LAY A HAND FLAT ON THAT GROUND AND PUSH IT ALONG AND THEN TURN THE HAND ON TO ITS EDGE AND RUN IT ALONG THE BOTTOM OF THAT WALL, AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING STANDS ON THE PORCH STEP WITH HER BAG AT HER FEET.**

Twelve feet of turf came up off the front of that house in the summer and it is lying against the front wall now with its roots together and its grass against the bricks, and it has been there four months and it is not going anywhere.

Which leaves a strip of bare ground about two feet wide the whole length of the front, between the edge of that stack and the bed that goes down to the lawn.

That strip is the only part of the front of that house that has not been touched since the summer. Everything else along it has been dug over, and manured, and had the winter on it.

A strip of ground that has not been touched for four months is not hard.

She can put her palm flat anywhere on it and go in to the first joint without any trouble at all, and what is under her hand is about two inches of dark stuff and then the beginning of something firmer, which is the ordinary arrangement in ground that is dug every year and has never been made good.

She starts at the gate end with the fingers leading and pushes along the strip, away from her, a hand's width at a time, all the way down its length to the other end.

The whole twelve feet is the same.

Then she turns the hand on to its edge and runs it along the bottom of the wall.

That is a different job and it is the one that finds it. You cannot find what is under a wall-end with the palm flat, because the thing that is under the bottom of a wall is directly underneath the wall and a flat hand cannot get under a wall.

A hand on its edge can. The knuckles go along the ground at the foot of the bricks and the tips of the fingers go in behind them, and the hand can tell the difference between ground and the bottom of a wall by the temperature of it and by whether anything pushes back.

The first three feet are the same as the rest of the strip. Soft, two inches, no trouble.

Then the hand comes on something that is not the same at all.

It is about a hand's width wide.

She finds where it begins and measures along it and finds it runs the whole twelve feet, without a break, right up to where the stack of turf stops it.

The ground there does not give.

Not a small amount. It does not give at all. The hand with the fingers behind the knuckles pushes down as hard as is worth pushing down with a hand at that age and the ground is there and comes back up the same, and the knuckles run along it and the bricks run along above it and between them there is a strip about a hand wide that is doing something completely different from the two feet of soft ground either side of it.

It is not a wall footing and it is not a drain.

A footing would be wider than that and would be at the foot of the wall and would have a drain or a pipe or a chamber on it somewhere. There is nothing on this strip and nothing comes out of it and nothing has ever been fixed to it.

What it is, is a made ground. A strip of ground that has been made good by somebody, once, for a purpose, and then had a building put beside it and a foot and a half of soil over the top of it.

And there is only one thing a house gets made good for like this.

That is a path. An old path, running along the front of that house, that somebody walked on and that was made good because people walk on it and a footpath gets ruts and a rut needs mending. And then the path was taken up and the front of that house was dug over from end to end, and ground that is dug over gets dug over, and nobody digs over a strip that does not give.

Because a path is not a road and not a yard. A path takes the feet of one person going to one door and coming back, twice a day, for fifty years, and that is enough to make good a strip about a hand wide and not enough to make good a strip a foot wide. Whoever mended it mended exactly what the feet had done to it and stopped there, and they stopped because it stopped.

And it was not mended more than once.

That is what her fingers tell her going back along it. There is no second course under it and no different stuff under it and no place in the whole twelve feet where it is thicker than it is anywhere else. It is one course of made ground, the same depth, the same stuff, laid along the front of that house by somebody working along a line and stopping when the line stopped.

And then the path was taken up. The flags came off it, or whatever it was made of, and they went somewhere else in the house or somewhere else in the borough, and the ground that was under them was made good and covered with a foot and a half of soil by somebody who was in a hurry and did not know what was underneath, because nobody looks under the soil they have only that minute put down.

Which means it is still there.

Everything in that front for as long as there has been a front there has been dug over two inches at the top of it, and manured, and turned, and nothing has ever gone deeper than the bottom of the spade because there has been nothing down there to stop it. And along the whole twelve feet of the foot of that wall, the digging has stopped at two inches because the ground there is a made path bed that was put there by somebody who knew what they were doing, and there is no way of telling that from a wall by looking at it.

Which is not a fault.

That is the part worth saying, because everything above it sounds like a discovery and it is not one. A hard strip under soft ground that does not show on the surface above it is a perfectly ordinary thing and half the ground in this borough is like it, and it has cost nothing and it is going nowhere.

What it means is narrower than it sounds.

Anything anybody wants to put into that front in the next twenty years and wants to put it in a hand's depth, a spade's depth, a bar's depth or a post hole, will go in along that front wall without any trouble whatever until it comes on the hand's width. And the hand's width runs the whole twelve feet of it, and it will be there whichever year anybody decides to start, and it will be found by whoever puts the bar in rather than by her, on the afternoon, in a hole.

---

The woman who does her shopping comes to the porch step in the middle of the afternoon and stands on it with her bag at her feet and does not knock.

"That'll be the last of the light."

"It'll be the last of it."

"Ours went in October."

"Ours went in the September."

She stood there a little longer and looked up and down the front and then went down the path, and the door shut, and the front of that house was the front of that house.

---

Nothing is wrong with a hand's width of hard ground under the foot of a wall, and it is a good deal harder to find than it would have been if a person had put it there on purpose.
