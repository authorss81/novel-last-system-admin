# Chapter 0319 — Three Days With No Water in It

Thursday the fourth of May 2017, from about half past seven on the last Saturday in April until about ten to ten that morning, a building by the water in Tideglass.

**THE STOP TAP IN THAT BUILDING WAS PUT BACK ON LATE ON THE LAST NIGHT OF THAT WEEK AND STAYED ON THROUGH THE WHOLE WEEKEND, AND A WOMAN WENT DOWN THAT PASSAGE THREE MORNINGS WITH TWO EMPTY JUGS AND A STAIR TREAD ON THE STAIR OUTSIDE HER DOOR CAME UP LOOSE AND A MAN SCREWED IT DOWN, AND NEITHER OF THOSE THINGS WAS TOLD TO THE OTHER.**

Down at the bottom of that building, in that passage, the stop tap sits behind a board that lifts out inside a wooden case with a little door on a hinge, and it is shut from about half past seven on the last evening of the week until somebody puts it back on the first working morning, and whoever gets down there first on the Monday morning opens it, and after that there is water in that building again.

It has been that since before anybody now living in it came to live in it.

On the Saturday morning she went down at about half past seven with a two-gallon jug in each hand, and she got to the case and put her hand up to the flap of it, and the case was shut, which is how it is over a weekend.

She opened it and the board was off, and the handle of the stop tap was across, and it was on.

She stood in that passage. The strip fitting over her head came on off the switch by that meter, and there was water at the sink at the back of the ground floor flat, in about the way it runs.

Nobody in that building had been down that passage at half past seven on a weekend morning for three days, and whoever had gone down there late on the last night of that week had put the tap on and had gone back up that outside stair, and it was about half past ten at night, and the landing light had been on until about ten past ten, and the woman of about fifty-five was where she always is at that hour and had heard somebody on the landing and had not opened her door and had not gone out and had not looked, and that is the whole of what she knew about it.

She turned the cold tap over the sink in the ground floor flat a quarter of a turn and it ran for about two seconds and stopped.

There is a meter on the wall of that passage with a glass in the front of it and a row of figures behind the glass, and it was going while she stood there, and it is the only thing in that building that got what it wanted in that week, and there is no number on it that anybody in that building has ever written down, and nobody in that borough could tell you who reads it or where what it says goes.

She put the board back and shut the case over it and carried two empty jugs up that outside stair and in at her own door and put them on the floor by the sink.

---

She did it again the next morning at about half past eight, and again on the first working morning at about half past seven, and the tap was across both times, and the jugs came up empty both times.

In the middle of that day the woman in the room next door along came out onto that landing about midday and stood at the end of it, and the corridor of that landing is short enough that a person at one end can see a tea towel hanging over a chair through an open door at the other.

The woman of about fifty-five had her door about two inches open and the woman in the room next door along could see the two jugs standing on the floor by the sink and the kettle not plugged in.

"There is not a drop of water in the place," said the woman in the room next door along.

"I know there's no water in it," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"I'd not say that loud."

"I'm not saying it loud, I'm saying it to you."

"I've got a bucket of washing-up water in my bath," said the woman in the room next door along. "About half of it, and it's clean, and you could have it for the kettle and you could have it for washing if you wanted it, and I would not want anything said about it and I would not want it fetched."

"No," said the woman of about fifty-five, and shut her door, and that was the whole of it.

The woman in the room next door along went back inside and left her own door as she always leaves it, which is open the width of a hand, and she sat down and did not come out onto that landing again that day, and she has not said a word to anybody about the three days.

On the Monday night somebody put that stop tap off again, and on the Tuesday morning somebody put it on, and by the middle of the week there was water in that building for the first time in four days, and the woman of about fifty-five filled both jugs at about half past seven and carried them up one at a time and did not find out who had done either.

---

The stair outside that street door is a concrete stair with a steel handrail on the wall side and nothing on the open side, and there are nine treads, and the second one up from the bottom lifts at the front about half an inch and has done since before Christmas.

It lifts because the nosing on it has rusted through at one end and the fixing has worked loose in the concrete, and it is not a tread anybody has replaced, and it is a tread that everybody who uses that stair uses, and everybody puts a hand on the wall side as they go up it and steps over that one with the foot they have already got down.

On the Thursday morning at about ten to ten, the man in his forties came along that street with a bit of kit in a bag, and he went up that outside stair, and the second tread came up about half an inch under his weight and made a noise like somebody knocking once.

He came back down for the spanner, the length of wire and the driver with its own battery on the end of it, and went back up.

He took the front left corner of that nosing up off its fixings with the driver and found that one of the two screws had sheared about an inch and a half up the shaft and the other one had pulled out of the concrete with a plug about the size of a finger, and he cleared the hole out with the bit, put a longer screw through the bracket that came with the tread, and drove it about two inches into the concrete, and put the corner back down and put the other screw in about half a turn, and stood on it twice.

It did not move and it made no noise at all, and he went back down that stair, and he did not knock on a single door on that landing, and nobody in that building and nobody on that street has any idea that he was ever on it.

Three feet above his head on that stair the woman of about fifty-five came past with a full jug in each hand and a full kettle hooked over one arm, and she said nothing to him, and he said nothing to her, and he had his back to the top of that stair when she went up past him, and neither of them was looking at the other.

The second tread of that outside stair is screwed down and does not knock, and the man who put two screws into the front corner of it has gone back down the street with his bag, and nobody in that building is ever going to know that it was ever loose.

Nobody in that building knows why that tread is down and nobody on that street knows it was ever up, and none of it was done for anybody, and none of it is going to be spoken about in there.

**AND SHE WENT UP PAST HIM ON THAT STAIR WITH A JUG IN EACH HAND AND NEITHER OF THEM SAID ONE WORD, AND HE WENT BACK DOWN A STAIR THAT DID NOT KNOCK UNDER HIM, AND SHE SHUT HER OWN DOOR ON THE LANDING AT THE END OF THAT CORRIDOR.**
