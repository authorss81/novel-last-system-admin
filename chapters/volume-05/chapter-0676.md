# Chapter 0676 — A Label With The Street Washed Off It

Friday the twenty-second of October 2021, in this borough. She is in her van and on a piece of waste ground off the ring road from first light until dark, and the whole of the day is a skip and a man who charges by the skip and a parcel on top of the pile that she is not going to open.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES TAKES A TAPED PARCEL OFF THE TOP OF A FLY-TIPPING PILE INTO HER OWN VAN AND PUTS IT BEHIND THE BENCH WITH THE DRAWER SHUT, TELLS NOBODY SHE HAS IT, AND DRIVES HOME THE LONG WAY ROUND SO SHE DOES NOT PASS THE END OF A ROAD.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is on her own all day apart from a skip man of about sixty and a woman who cannot get to her bin. The parcel is never described to anybody.

---

The ground is off the ring road past the garden place and it is the fourth job she has had on it this month, and the parish pays for the skips and she gets paid by the skip and the two numbers have never once been the same, which is a thing she has stopped finding interesting.

It is a piece of ground with a chain round it and a hole in the chain at the corner by the gate, and the tip has been tipping since somebody started it in the summer, and by October it is four foot deep in places and the rain has got into it.

She parks the van at the top because the ground goes down and she has never trusted the bottom with a loaded van.

The skip man is called by his trade and he is about sixty and he has a way of walking round a skip that tells her what is in it before she has looked at it.

"Morning."

"Morning. It's tipped."

"Course it's tipped." He looks at the gate. "You've not got a lock on that."

"No."

"Somebody's had a bonfire out of this, mind. Look at that glass."

She has already seen the glass.

---

She works the pile from the top down, which is the only way to work it, and she is two hours in before she gets to the far end of the tip where the water gathers, and that is where the parcel is.

It is on top of a black bag that is on top of a mattress that is on top of a broken wardrobe, and it is about the size of two loaves, and it is taped round with brown tape in a cross and the tape has gone hard and gone dark at the edges where the rain has been on it.

She looks at it from where she is standing for a bit before she touches it, which is a thing she does with everything on a tip.

The label is on the top, half of it, and it is a printed label with a number on it in the middle of it, and a street, and the street has gone. Not torn. Gone, the way ink goes off a wet label — the number is there and it is legible and the street above the number is a grey smear with the top of a couple of letters left in it.

She reads the number twice and does not have anything to read it against.

---

Her rule, and she has had it since the first year, is that she does not open other people's things, and there is a version of that rule for tips and it is this: on a tip, everything is already somebody's, because the person who put it here put it here deliberately and threw it away, and if she opens anything on a tip she has made herself a person who looks.

So she does not open it.

What she does do is put her hand flat on the top of it and push, to see if it is empty, and it is not empty, and there is a weight in it that slides about about an inch and then stops, which is the weight of things in a tin.

That is a thing she knows. That is a thing she has known since she was twenty-two. Tinned things in a box on a wet tip on a Monday morning is a box somebody did not want at the end of a week, or a box that came by mistake, or a box a firm posted to an address that was never occupied because the person had moved and never told anybody.

And she has got four hours of gate work in a summer behind her with no invoice anywhere in the world for it, so she knows exactly what a box that came by mistake looks like when it is still shut.

She picks it up.

It is heavier than she expected, and the bottom corner is wet through, and there is a mark on her glove where the label is.

---

She puts it on the tailgate and then thinks about it.

Not for long. She is standing on a wet piece of waste ground on the ring road with a skip man watching her from the cab of his own vehicle, and the thinking takes about a second and a half, and what it consists of is: it is going in the skip and the skip man will put it in the skip and it will go off with three tonnes of somebody else's life, and she has a tape measure and a rate card and no way of charging anybody for a box.

She carries it round and puts it on the passenger seat of the van.

That is not a decision about the woman who sent it. It is a decision about the skip.

---

She works until about eleven and then goes to the other side of the ring road for her lunch, and the parcel goes with her, and she takes it out of the van with her because she does not leave that on a seat in October.

It goes in a bag with her flask in it and stands against the wall of a shopfront while she eats a cheese sandwich she can grip in one hand, because the other palm has a plaster on it and the one without the plaster is open to the air and pink along the crease where the skin pulls.

She moves the parcel out of the way twice when the wind comes round the corner.

Then she goes and does a house on the far side where a woman wants a garage emptied and has moved out and cannot get to her bin, and the woman stands in the doorway of the garage the whole time and talks at her about the bin and about the council.

"I've had three men come."

"Right."

"They're all the same. You ring and they say Thursday and Thursday comes and there's nothing."

"They'll come."

"You'll come?" The woman is about sixty and she has both hands in her own sleeves.

"I'll come if I'm booked."

"You can come and look, now, if you want. There's nothing in it."

"You don't need me to look at an empty garage."

"I do, because they don't come and I want to see a face."

She does not look at the garage.

She does the garage in about forty minutes because there is nothing in it, and she does it with a broom and not with her hands where she can manage it, and she puts what she cannot lift at the bottom and the rest on top, and she leaves the two lengths of shelving on the wall because the woman says she might want them.

At the gate she says the figure and the woman says that is more than she expected, and the woman does not say it is more than another fella said, which is what the woman in October said, and the difference between those two women is the difference between a bad afternoon and a good one and it has nothing to do with the figure.

"You'll send it?"

"You'll get a text with a card on it."

"I don't do cards."

"You'll get a text."

The woman goes back in and shuts her own door, and the door shuts true, and the woman who made it that way is at number twelve and has a door that has not shut since before Christmas.

---

In the afternoon the parcel goes behind the bench in the van.

The drawer under the bench on the passenger side is shut, and on top of two extension leads and a tin of screws inside it is a bolster off her own board, and the parcel does not go near the drawer. It goes in the space behind the bench, on the floor of it, against the bulkhead, where the light does not get.

It fits. That is the thing about it. It goes in behind the bench with the tailgate up and the door shut and it is not visible from the side of the van, and it is not visible from the front either, and there is a bag of rags on top of it before she has shut the door.

She shuts the door.

The drawer is shut, and it is the first thing in that van that has ever been shut, and she shut it herself in August.

---

She does not open it again that day.

The van is warm when it is shut and the box is behind the bench and she is driving a road she knows with a box of tinned fish behind the seat, and she has no paper on her knee with the number on it, because she did not write the number down.

That is the part she will turn over on the drive home. She read a number twice off a grey smear on a wet label on a piece of waste ground and she did not write it, and she has a pad in the glove box with a band round it and her own handwriting on it and the whole of the rest of the week on it, and the number is not on it anywhere.

She drives home the long way round so she does not pass the end of that road.

The shop with the galvanised bucket by the door goes by on her right, and she does not stop at it, and there is a rack by the window where a man bought nine pounds' worth of brush with his own money in the summer, and she does not know that and has never known it.

---

She parks and carries her bag in and washes at the sink.

The plaster on the palm with the cuts is soft at the edges from washing and she leaves it, and the palm without a plaster is open to the air and stings when the water goes over it.

She puts the flask on the table with the pad on it, and the pad has the band round it and the band is tight and the pad is shut, and on the page inside there are two houses and a garage and a figure against each in her own writing, and no number from a parcel.

Four hours of gate work in August is still unpaid with no invoice, and she still thinks it was the right thing, and she has still not thought of asking for one.

She eats standing up because both of her hands are a problem for a knife.

And a parcel that is not hers, with a number on it and no street, is standing behind the bench in a white van on a road off the ring road with a bag of rags on top of it, and she has not put it in the drawer with her own tools, and she is not going to.

The one thing she would be able to do about it, if it were anybody else's, is ring the number, and she has not written the number down and is not going to stop and write it down now with the van warm and the door shut, and that is the arrangement she has made with herself and it will be in place tomorrow.

She has got a text to send before midnight and she sends it from the kitchen table with the pad propped on a tin, and the pad has the band round it and the band is tight and the pad is shut when she has done it.

And in the van, behind the bench, under a bag of rags, a box with a number on it and no street is going nowhere in particular until somebody makes it go somewhere.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES HAS PUT A BOX THAT IS NOT HERS BEHIND THE BENCH IN HER OWN VAN WHERE THE DRAWER WITH HER OWN BOLSTER IN IT IS SHUT, HAS READ A NUMBER OFF A WASHED LABEL TWICE AND WRITTEN IT NOWHERE, AND HAS DRIVEN HOME THE LONG WAY ROUND SO AS NOT TO PASS THE END OF A ROAD SHE HAS NO WORK ON.**
