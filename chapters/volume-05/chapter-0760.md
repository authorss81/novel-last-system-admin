# Chapter 0760 — The Kitchen Window Cord

Sunday the twenty-fifth of September 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number twelve from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a kitchen window that will not stay up and will not go up either.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS THE SASH IN HER KITCHEN WINDOW COMING DOWN IN HER HAND, A MAN FROM THE LANE RE-CORDS IT AND HANGS THE WEIGHTS ON THE WRONG EYES, AND THE WINDOW IS LEFT SITTING A HAND'S WIDTH LOW FOR GOOD.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight at number twelve on Sunday. A man from the lane comes to the back door, takes the second half of the job at her sink, and re-cords the sash.

---

She is in the kitchen from morning with the stove warm and the green chair drawn up to the table. Nothing on the table has been moved. The envelope is face up where it lies on the right and the taped box is under its cloth on the other side, and neither has been looked at since she was up. The passage light burns above the runner and the brush lies across it where it has lain since the spring. The ladder is folded under the front window in the front room and is not in this room and does not come out of it.

The kitchen window will not stay up. It has been going down for a fortnight and lately it has begun to come down while she has it in her hand, so that she has both hands on the sash and one foot on the sill and it still goes, and it comes to rest with the top edge of the glass down past the frame and the gap letting the cold in along the top.

She has a screwdriver, a pair of pliers with a bright scratch in the chrome where they slipped once before, and a reel of sash cord in the drawer under the worktop.

The man from the lane is at the back door in the middle of the morning with a chisel and a hammer in his hand.

---

"You are doing that on your own."

"That is what I am doing." She has one foot on the sill and both hands on the sash. "It comes down. I have got it up twice and it has come down twice and I am not going to be here at seventy-eight wrestling a sash."

"Move your foot." He comes in past her with his head under the lintel. "I have done this in your street for years. How long has it been doing that."

"A fortnight. It was an inch and now it is more."

"More." He looks up at the sash and then at the frame. "Then the cords have gone slack and the weights have come down off their hooks, or one of them has, and that is the fortnight."

"Will it be the hooks."

"It will be the hooks if the weights have come off them, and it will be a rotten cord if the top half is going cold in along the frame." He looks at the reveal. "We will find out in a minute and it will not cost you anything either way."

---

He takes the bottom rail off and the sash comes down and away onto two stacks of bricks he has carried in from the side of the shed, and the two cords come up out of the bottom of it. One of them is grey and furry and goes to pieces between his fingers. The other is sound and has more slack in it than it ought to have.

"That is your fortnight." He lifts the sound one up. "The weights have come down off their eyes and the cord has slack in it because of it, and that is why it drops further every week instead of staying where you put it."

"And the other one."

"The other one is dead." He lays it on the sink top. "That is why it has been letting cold in along the top, because the top half of the gap is where this one runs. It has not been in a pulley since spring."

"You will want more cord than what is on that reel."

"I will want about four times what is on that reel." He looks at her. "You have not got four times what is on that reel in this house."

"Then you will not do it."

"Then I will do it with what you have, and you will get a window that goes up and stays up until somebody bangs the front door." He turns the sound cord over in his hands. "Or you leave it shut and I put the glass back where the sash is and you have a window that opens in the summer and shuts in the winter. That is the other answer and it is not a bad one for a kitchen."

He does it with what she has, and the cord is the right cord. He runs it over the pulley on the head of the sash and down the pulley in the stile, and down to the weight in the box, and hooks the weight on, and it is right, and when he puts the sash back in the frame and pushes it up it goes up the way a sash is supposed to go.

Then he stops.

He goes down off the sill and looks into the box at the two weights and puts his hand in after them, and comes back up and puts his palm flat on the glass and looks at the frame.

"How far up does it go."

"It goes as far as it goes."

"Look at that." He puts his thumb on the top rail of the sash where it is sitting against the head of the frame. "That ought to be up against the head and it is a hand's width down, and that is not the cord. The cord is right. That is where I have hung them."

"Where you have hung them."

"The eyes at the end of the chain." He takes his hand out of the box. "There are three pairs of eyes in there and the weights were on the middle pair and they belong on the low pair, and the high pair is a hand's width above the low one, so a window hung on the high pair sits a hand's width lower in its frame than a window that is right."

"You have put them on the wrong ones."

"I have put them on the ones I could reach without taking the box apart, and the box is not coming apart today." He looks at the weights. "Now. I can put them on the low pair in about ten minutes. That is the whole of what is left. I cannot do it for nothing and I will not do it after I have gone."

"Then do it now."

"I will do it now." He puts his hand into the box again and stops with it in there. "Except that when I shift these off the high pair the weights will be on nothing for as long as it takes me to get to the low pair, and if I drop one of them down that box it goes to the bottom and I am on my knees with a lamp in the box for twenty minutes and you are standing in a cold kitchen watching me do it."

"Then do not drop them."

"I am not going to promise a thing I cannot see." He takes his hand out and closes the box and stands up. "And I will not open that box up again. That is a box a man opens when he means to take a whole sash out and do it properly with the head out, and I am not doing that on a Sunday in a kitchen with a reel of cord in a drawer."

---

He goes out through the back door. She stands in the kitchen and looks at the window for a while before she touches it.

The sash sits where he left it, a hand's width below the head of the frame, and the gap along the top is deeper than the gap was on Friday by the width of her own hand. She puts her fingers under the bottom rail and brings the sash down, and it goes down easy and true and comes to rest on the rails, and the frame comes over it and the catch goes across and the window shuts from the inside with the same noise it has made for years.

It will open when she wants it open. It will come up and stop a hand's width short and sit there and stay there, and the gap will be along the top of the frame from the day she is in it.

She tries the catch once to be sure, and then again with her shoulder against it, and then she stands at the sink with the dead grey rope lying on the drainer where she will find it in the evening and put it in the bin without thinking about it, looking at a window that will not stay open and will not shut up.
