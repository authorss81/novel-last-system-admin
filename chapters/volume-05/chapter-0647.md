# Chapter 0647 — The Back Room And The Blanket

Monday the fourteenth of June 2021, in this borough. She is in a kitchen off the parade and then in a back room off that kitchen for about an hour and a half, and the whole of it is a window that has not shut since before Christmas and a gate nobody has offered to touch.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES FROM TWO DOORS DOWN OFFERS TO LOOK AT A WINDOW AND IS LET IN IN ABOUT A SECOND, AND THE FIRST THING THE WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT DOES WITH A PERSON SHE HAS NEVER HAD IN THAT HOUSE IS PUT A PIECE OF PAPER INTO A DRAWER WITH THE STRING AND THE FUSES, WHICH IS THE FIRST THING SHE HAS HIDDEN IN THAT KITCHEN, AND THE YOUNG WOMAN ASKS HER ABOUT THE GATE, WHICH IS THE FIRST LINE ON THE ENVELOPE, AND THE OLD WOMAN CANNOT EXPLAIN WHY IT IS FIRST.**

She is a woman in her early thirties. She is a woman in her late seventies. There is a third person in this borough who has a bin and a hedge in common with both of them and who is not in that kitchen on that Monday, and who works lates and who would have said something.

---

Here is how it starts, and it starts with a woman in her early thirties at a kitchen table in number ten with a cup of tea she has not drunk.

She has a roll of brown paper tape in her coat pocket and it has been in her coat pocket since the spring. She has a screwdriver she does not need. She borrowed a mastic gun off a man at work in April and returned it in May and has been thinking about it since April.

And what she has been thinking about since April is a blanket.

She has seen it from her own kitchen. She saw it from the top of the parade.

That is where she saw it and it is not a good place to have seen it from. You can see about nine feet up the side of the last house in that row from the middle of the parade, past the parade's own wall, over a roofline, and not one inch of the rest of it. She was standing outside the shop on a Saturday in May waiting for somebody who did not come, and she looked down the side road because there was nothing else to look at, and there was a small window at the top of the far wall with a blanket folded across it.

And she has not been able to unsee it since.

Not a sheet over it. A blanket. One of the old woollen ones, folded once, and jammed in at the bottom, and pushed at the sides, and not fixed at the top at all.

---

And she has not mentioned it to her sister.

That is the first thing about her and it is worth setting down, because her sister works lates and does not come in until the small hours and would have said something. She would have said *is that a blanket* and she would have said it on the doorstep on her way past, and that would have been the whole of it.

And this woman has not said anything, and she cannot give a straight reason for it in her own head, and the best she can do is this.

You do not go and say to a person of seventy-eight that you can see their blanket.

---

She goes at about ten in the morning on the Monday with the tape in her pocket and the screwdriver.

She knocks at number twelve at about ten past ten with four knocks, which is too many, and she knows it is too many while she is doing it.

And the door opens in about three seconds, which it always does.

---

The woman in her late seventies looks at her for about two seconds.

And she works out in the two seconds who she is. She has been at that door about four times since before Christmas and she can tell a person by the knock: the woman in her late forties knocks two and pauses and knocks one, and the man knocks two quick and a slow one. This is four, and nobody she knows knocks four.

And the woman in her early thirties says, "I'm from number ten. I've come about your back window."

And there is a pause of about a second and a half in that doorway.

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Come in."

---

It comes out of her in about a second and she has thought about it for a whole morning.

That is the fact. Not that she is polite, and not that she is lonely, and not any of the things that people say about a woman of seventy-eight with a house that nobody comes to. It is that the answer came out in a second, before she had any idea what she was going to say, and that is because she does not know how to refuse a person who has knocked.

She has never in this borough said *no* to anybody who has come to her door. She said *no* to nothing. A woman in her late forties said *I don't know* in her own passage on the Monday and then said *well, neither do I*, and she stood in that passage and listened to it, and it took her about a week.

So a young woman comes to her door with a screwdriver and an offer, and the only answer available to a woman of seventy-eight inside about two seconds is *come in*.

And she hears herself say it, and she hears what it has cost the young woman standing on her step, and she cannot undo it.

---

Inside, the kitchen.

The woman in her early thirties comes in out of the light and her eyes go round the room in about a second and a half, the way eyes do, and she sees four things and does not ask about one of them.

A green folding chair at the table under the window with a cushion on it. A folding chair with a cushion on it in the kitchen of a woman who lives by herself, and no other chair in that room at all.

The corner by the sink, which is empty, and which is not the corner you would put a corner in.

The passage light on, which is a five watt bulb in a passage at half past ten on a Monday morning.

And the table itself, under the window, with a cloth on it that is not quite square, laid down twice this morning by somebody who has had nothing else to do with her hands.

---

And here is what the woman of seventy-eight is doing while that is happening, and it takes her about four seconds, and she will be able to say afterwards exactly how long it took her.

She is standing in the doorway of her own kitchen watching a stranger look at her kitchen and she has seen the envelope.

It is on the table. Face up. In the middle, square with the edge of the table, because a man put it back that way on the Wednesday and she has not moved it since and she has not looked at it either.

And it is six lines of pencil in her own hand, and the first one of the six is about the gate the young woman is about to ask her about, and the woman in her early thirties is standing about eight feet away with her coat still on.

---

She crosses the kitchen.

She does it in four seconds. She goes past the table and she does not pick the envelope up where the young woman can see her do it, because she cannot think of a way to do it that does not look like something, so she waits until the young woman has her back turned, which takes about a second and a half, because the young woman has gone to the window end of the kitchen to look out.

And she puts it in the drawer under the telephone.

It goes in under the string and in on top of a folded instruction leaflet and it goes in on top of the two screws and the wall plug and the battery that is not the right battery, and she pushes it to the back of the drawer with the heel of her hand and she shuts the drawer.

And she stands there with her hand on the drawer for about two seconds.

And what she has done is hide something.

That is new. In a week in this borough a woman of seventy-eight has made three stories for one house and told a man he did a kitchen for a daughter she has not got and told a woman she was the council about the damp, and not one of those was hiding anything, because every one of them was said out loud to somebody's face.

This is the first thing she has put out of sight where it cannot be seen.

---

And here is what the young woman does next, and she is very good at it.

She looks at the window for about four minutes and she says nothing about the chair and nothing about the corner and nothing about the envelope and nothing about the marks on the floor, and she has taken all four of those in and she will not mention any of them for about a month.

And then she says, "There's a gap you could get a sheet of card through."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "I know."

And the woman in her early thirties says, "The catch is off. That's not the catch, that's the frame. Somebody's pushed that frame."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "It was out at the top when I put the blanket on."

And the woman in her early thirties says, "It's worse than that now."

---

And then she tells her the truth, which is the thing about her, and it takes her about a minute.

And she says, "I'm not going to fix that. That's a joiner job and it's got to be taken out and put back and it'll cost. All I can do is take the glass out and put a sheet in, and that'll keep the draught out till the winter and you'll be able to open it in the summer."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Will it stand."

And the woman in her early thirties says, "It'll stand."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Can you be certain."

And the woman in her early thirties says, "No. I can't be certain of that. But it'll stand."

---

And it takes them from about half past ten until about a quarter to twelve, and here is what it actually is.

The glass comes out. It comes out on the sill in the back room onto a folded blanket that is not the blanket on the window and that has been in this room since before Christmas. There is a mattress on the floor of that room with nothing on it and it has not been a room anybody slept in for some time, and the woman of seventy-eight has not been in there at all.

And she has to get down on the floor with her knees.

And she cannot do it.

And she tries, and it takes her a hand on the bed frame that is not there because there is no bed, so it takes her a hand on the wall, and the woman in her early thirties says, "No, sit down. I'll do this bit."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "I've managed."

And the woman in her early thirties says, "You've managed on your own. Sit down."

---

And that is the sentence the whole afternoon is actually about, and neither of them ever refers to it.

*You've managed on your own.*

Nobody has said a thing like that in that house since before Christmas. A woman of seventy-eight who has been managing has been told by a doctor once, in a letter, in about March, and has not been told it by a person standing in the room.

And a young woman who has known her for four minutes says it as a way of telling her to sit down on a mattress in a room she does not use.

And the woman in her late seventies sits down on the mattress, in her own back room, and does not say anything for about a minute.

---

The sheet goes in at about ten to twelve and the young woman tapes it round the rebate with tape off a roll she has brought, and the blanket comes off, and the blanket ends up folded on the mattress because there is nowhere else for it.

And the room goes from four degrees colder to not quite four degrees colder, which is a small thing, and both of them can feel it and neither of them says it.

---

And here is the last of it, and it is the whole of why the young woman comes back at all.

They go back through the kitchen to the front door and on the way past the sink the woman in her early thirties stops, because the gate is under the window at the front and the front door is open and there is a draught coming in off the step.

And she says, "Your gate's off the latch."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "I know."

And the woman in her early thirties says, "That'll be swinging in the wind."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "It swings."

And the woman in her early thirties says, "I can do that in about two minutes. I've got a screwdriver in me pocket."

---

And the woman in her late seventies says, "No."

And there is about three seconds of that kitchen in which a young woman of thirty is being told no by a woman of seventy-eight and does not know why, and the not-knowing shows on her face, and it is the first thing that morning that is not about the window.

And the woman in her early thirties says, "Right."

And she does not ask why.

And that is the most useful thing she has ever done in this borough and she will not find out until a great deal later, if she finds out at all.

---

And here is what she is going to have when the young woman has gone, and she has not thought about any of it and all of it comes at her at about ten past twelve.

There is a woman who has now been in that house, and who is not one of the two.

And there is a piece of paper in the drawer under the telephone that she hid from her, and she did not have to, and nobody in that house has ever had to hide anything from anybody.

And there is a line at the top of that paper about a gate, and a woman in her early thirties has just offered to fix that gate in two minutes with a screwdriver, and she has said no, and she cannot say why, and there is nothing on earth she can put in place of the reason.

---

She waits until the gate has gone.

And then she goes to the drawer under the telephone and opens it and takes the envelope out and puts it down on the table in front of her, face up, where it was on the Thursday.

And she does not read it.

That is the thing she will be able to say afterwards, and it is the whole of what she did with that morning. She wrote six things on Thursday and she read them back four times on Thursday, and on the Monday a young woman of thirty stood in her kitchen for an hour and a half and the woman of seventy-eight hid that envelope off the table with her own hands four seconds after she saw it, and there has not been one single reason since to read it again.

---

And instead she does the other thing.

She reads it once, deliberately, standing up, the way you read a thing you are about to be asked about.

*the gate*

*the front room*

*the window in the back room*

*the hotplate on the left*

*the bath tap*

*the passage light*

And she puts her finger on the first one and says, out loud, in her own kitchen, to nobody:

"You'll have to not be daft about that one."

---

And then she puts the envelope back in the drawer, on top of the two screws and the wall plug and the battery that is not the right battery, and she shuts the drawer, and that is the end of the Monday.

And the difference between Thursday and Monday, and she has worked it out standing at the sink, is this.

On Thursday she left it face up because she had not thought about it.

On Monday she puts it in a drawer because she has thought about it.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES CAME ABOUT A WINDOW AND WAS LET IN IN A SECOND, AND THE FIRST THING THE WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT DID WAS PUT AN ENVELOPE IN A DRAWER, AND THE YOUNG WOMAN ASKED HER ABOUT THE GATE AND WAS TOLD NO IN THREE SECONDS WITHOUT BEING TOLD WHY, AND THE OLDER WOMAN THEN READ HER OWN SIX LINES ONCE, ALONE, STOOD THERE WITH A FINGER ON THE FIRST ONE AND SAID OUT LOUD THAT SHE WAS NOT TO BE DAFT ABOUT THAT ONE, AND PUT IT BACK IN A DRAWER BECAUSE SHE HAD NOW THOUGHT ABOUT IT.**

(End of chapter 0647)
