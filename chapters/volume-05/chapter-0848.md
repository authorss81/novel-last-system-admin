# Chapter 0848 — Dust Down The Guides

Thursday the twenty-eighth of September 2023, in that bay at the lock-up from the time the yard has dried until the light has gone off the shutter, and both guides brushed to the floor is the whole of that Thursday.

**THE TWO GUIDES THE ROLLER SHUTTER RUNS IN ARE PACKED WITH DUST AND GROUND GRIT AT THE LOCK UP AND SHE MEANS TO BRUSH BOTH CHANNELS DOWN FROM THE TOP OF THE OPENING WITHOUT TAKING THE SHUTTER OUT OF THEM, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK COMES OUT OF THE OFFICE DOOR AND GOES ACROSS THE YARD TO THE GATE WITH HER BAG.**

That shutter is the roller over the bay, a steel curtain on a barrel, running in two channels one each side of the opening about the width of a hand.

The dust of this yard goes everywhere there is a hole to go into and it has been packing in both channels since the spring. What has made it hard is the grit in it, ground in and polished in by the curtain coming down and going up, four times a day and every day since it was first hung.

On the near side the curtain has got slow. It comes down the last foot and drops onto the floor instead of running into it, and the sound it makes is the sound of a hundred and forty pounds of steel landing on concrete, and it has been making it every working day since the spring.

She does not take the shutter out. A curtain out of its guides is a curtain on the floor of a working bay, and it wants two people and a winding bar and most of a morning, and while it is out that opening is a hole in the front of the place.

She works from the top of the opening down, which is as high as she can get without going up on something.

She starts with a sheet on the floor under each guide, the two of them the width of the opening and back a foot so it catches what comes off the flags.

The brush is a soft one on an ash handle long enough for the opening. Down the outside face of the guide first, from the top, so the loose dust on the steel goes off it and not into the bay. It comes away in a rope that falls a long way and then breaks up on the sheet.

Then she turns the brush and puts the bristles into the channel and brings it down the inside, the full height, with the handle kept to the outside so the brush is pulled and not pushed.

The grit that has been ground in comes out with the dust. Not as a cake but as a run of small stones and hard bits of old bedding, one after another out of the channel, and each one that lands on the sheet is the size of a pea and is grey and hard as a nail and has been in there since June.

By the time the first guide is done to the floor the sheet under it is grey across the whole width and the channel is visible from the doorway, and the steel in it has a shine in it the width of the curtain where four months of grit has been polished into the paint by nothing but weight going up and down.

She does the second guide the same way and stops at the foot of it, and stops on purpose.

Where the curtain lands on the near side there is a band of grease off the barrel across the whole width of the channel and out onto the concrete, black and shining, and it has come out of the bearing and run down the guide on the first hot day since July and set on the steel.

She does not take it with the brush. Grease does not come off steel with a brush, it comes off with a cloth and warmth and time, and a cloth at that foot of that guide means lifting the curtain up out of its own head, and lifting that curtain up out of its own head is the one thing she is not doing on a Thursday morning in a yard where the bays are being let.

What she can do is the paint.

The guides are painted and the brush has caught the paint over the years wherever she has gone into the channel, and along the outer lip of the near one there is a ledge of built paint about the width of a thumb standing proud of the steel the whole height of the opening. That ledge is what the curtain has been riding on all this year and it is half of why the curtain bangs.

She goes at it with the flat end of the handle, little taps up the height, and it comes away in flakes about the size of a flake of paint, and under it the steel is bright and bare where the paint has been since before the shutter went on.

She clears the lip the full height of both guides. The flakes go on the sheet with the grit and the sheet gets folded over the four corners when it is done.

The office door stands open on the latch where it stood in the summer and the woman who books work comes out of it in the middle of the afternoon with her bag over her arm, and goes across the yard in a straight line to the gate, and does not slow at the guide or look down at the sheet.

The stop is the noise.

She puts her palm flat on the curtain at about the height of her shoulder and brings it down. It runs the whole way without a sound and goes into the floor instead of dropping onto it, and she puts it up again and lets it go and it comes down the same way.

Twice more, because one is a good day and two is a habit.

She thinks about the shutter while she works, how a curtain in a guide is a steel thing running in a steel groove on a steel edge with nothing between them at all, and everything that gets into the groove is now a bearing surface. Grit in a guide does not wear the curtain out in a year. It polishes the paint off the lip and lets the water in behind the curtain where the roll is, and the roll is the one part of that shutter that cannot be reached without taking the whole thing out of its frame.

She sweeps the flags under the guides where the sheet was, and there is grit in the joints of them that came off the curtain in the rope and has found a home.

---

The brush goes on its nail by the bench, and the folded sheet goes into the bin, and the shutter stands down and shut.

She stands in the bay doorway when the light has gone off the shutter and looks at what is there. Two channels brushed clean the full height of the opening and a band of old grease left lying black across the foot of the near one.
