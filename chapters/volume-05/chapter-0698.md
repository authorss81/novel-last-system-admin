# Chapter 0698 — Six Pads On The Fridge

Monday the twenty-fourth of January 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number nine from first light until dark and the whole of the day is a fridge with six pads on it and a front room with a stool in it and nothing under it.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES WIPES THE TOP OF A FRIDGE WITH SIX PADS AND CROSSES THE FIRST LINE TWICE, LOOKS AT AN ASH STOOL IN HER FRONT ROOM WITH NOTHING UNDER IT, SPEAKS TO HER SISTER AT NUMBER TEN AT HER OWN DOOR ABOUT A SHED ROOF AND FELT AND SAYS IN THE SPRING AND PUTS NO DATE ON IT, AND DOES NOT ASK THE MAN NEXT DOOR WHO BORROWS.**

She is a woman in her late fifties. She is on her own all day except for her sister at number ten at her own door in the afternoon. She speaks to her there.

---

The fridge is in the kitchen against the wall by the back door.

It has six pads on the front of it, put there since March, each with a line on it in her own hand. The first is crossed twice. The second is crossed once. The rest are not crossed. She fixed the back door herself in March with a card at the hinge and it shuts true.

She takes a damp cloth from the sink and wrings it out and wipes the top of the fridge, which is where the dust sits in a grey film, and then the front where the pads are, round the edges of each pad without lifting any of them. The pads curl at the corners where the heat gets to them. She smooths the first one flat with her thumb. It was crossed twice, once in a hurry and once properly, and the two lines cross each other like a letter.

She puts the cloth back in the sink and rinses her hands. The window over the sink looks into the yard. The yard gate wants a bolt. She does not write that down, because it is not one of the six and because six is enough for a fridge.

The kitchen is warm. The fire in the front room has not been lit since Easter. She never goes up the road. She has never gone up the road.

---

The front room is opposite the window.

There is a chair on one side and a settee on the other, and an ash stool in the middle of the room opposite the window, three rails oak and one ash, and the space under it has been empty since the fifth of November, and there is a sixty-watt bulb in the ceiling of that room since the seventh of December.

She goes in with the cloth and wipes the sill under the window. The room is cold. The bulb is on. She took a step ladder out of an open porch two doors down in December and used it on that bulb, and a man from that house asked at her own door whether she took the ladder out of his porch and then whether she knew whose it was, and she said she did not know, and she did not ask the man next door that she was told borrows, and she told her sister she cannot put back what she cannot give, and her sister carried it away on a Wednesday in the dark.

The ladder went up a road on the thirteenth of December on her sister's shoulder and into the front room of a house at number twelve on the seventeenth, folded, against the wall under the window, and it has not been opened out and it has not been moved. Her sister carried that whole leg herself, on her own shoulder, in the dark, and she has not walked a yard of it, and she is not going to ask her about that either.

She looks at the space under the stool. It is empty. It has been empty since the fifth of November. She does not put anything under it.

---

At the middle of the day she eats in the kitchen.

She eats standing up at the worktop with the plate in her hand, looking out at the yard. The yard is small and paved and the fence at the end of it wants a board. The shed at number ten has a draught off the top of it and the ply in the drawer will not do it and the felt is gone at the front edge, and she knows that because her sister told her, and she has never seen the shed.

She cuts a bit of cheese and puts it on a cracker and eats it over the plate so the crumbs fall straight. The clock on the wall has no hands on it, because they came off it in the summer and she has never put them back and has never sent for a man to. She knows the middle of the day by the light on the yard wall. When the light leaves the top course of bricks it is past the middle, and when it is gone off the wall it is afternoon.

She washes up and puts the plate away. She wipes the worktop from the back edge to the front, corner to corner, and rinses the cloth. She goes back to the front room and sits on the chair for a bit without lighting the fire, because sitting in a cold room for a bit is what she does on a Monday, and the settee is for other people.

The stool is opposite the window. The chair is on one side. The settee is on the other. The bulb hangs from the ceiling. None of it wants anything. That is the thing about a room that is right. It does not ask.

---

The knock is at her own door in the afternoon.

It is her sister from number ten, with a bag of shopping in one hand and her coat done up wrong. She lets her in off the front path and shuts the door behind her, which shuts true on the card at the hinge.

"You look cold," she says.

"I'm cold," her sister says. "Shed's doing my roof in."

"Come in the kitchen. It's warm. I've just wiped the fridge and put the cloth back."

They go through to the kitchen, which is the warm room, and her sister puts the shopping on the worktop and stands with her hands round a cup.

"Did you get up on it," she says.

"I got up on it before Christmas. It's no use. The felt's gone at the front edge. Ply will do nothing."

"Then you'll want to get some felt."

"Aye."

"You could get some off the man at number eleven. He had it on his bench all summer, I saw it through the window."

"I know."

"You could ask him."

"I know."

---

Her sister drinks the tea and looks at the fridge with the six pads on it.

"You've crossed the first one twice," her sister says.

"Aye."

"Is that allowed."

"It's my fridge."

Her sister laughs, which she does not mind. They have been sisters since before either of them had a house, and a fridge with six pads on it is not a thing to fall out over.

"What does the spring look like for you," her sister says.

"In the spring," she says, and puts no date on it, because she has no date to put on it and because the spring is where the felt goes and the shopping goes and the fire goes. "I'll come over and we'll look at it. We'll get the felt and we'll do the edge."

"Right."

"Have you got a date."

"In the spring," she says again, to nobody in particular, and washes the cups. "When it is dry enough to stand on it without going through it."

Her sister nods. She does not put any date on it. She does not open a calendar, because she has never in her life had a list on a wall and she is not starting one in January with a bit of ply in a drawer at number ten.

---

"How is the woman at the end of the road," she says, because she has not asked since December and because she carried a ladder round to her sister's in the dark and her sister carried it up a road and she has never walked up that road in her life.

"She's all right," her sister says. "She's got a bowl under a tap and a box under a cloth and a ladder in a front room. Four of six is still four of six."

"Did she ask after it."

"No. She didn't ask me a single thing. Not who I was, not where it was from, not what I wanted with it. She just stood there and let me put it in the room."

"Wouldn't you think she'd want to know."

"I'd have thought so."

They stand for a bit without talking. The kitchen is warm and smells of tea and damp cloth. The front room is cold beyond the shut door. The stool has nothing under it. The bulb is on. The street outside goes quiet as the light goes.

---

Her sister goes at the end of the afternoon with the empty bag folded under her arm.

She sees her off at her own door and stands on the step and looks down the street. Two doors down the porch stands open with the door off its hinges and no note on it. The paint on the step is worn where the ladder stood in December. She can still see the two marks its feet left in the dust, though the rain has taken most of it. The house was emptied and the ladder stood in it and she took it in because it was going to be rained on, and now the porch is empty again and the rain goes in as it likes.

A woman goes past with a pushchair. A van goes past with ladders on the roof, tied fore and aft with orange straps. She watches it out of sight, because a van with ladders on it is a rare thing in this street and because the man driving it does not know how rare it is.

The man next door's yard is quiet. He is the one she was told borrows. She has never asked him. She does not ask him now. She goes back in and shuts her own door, which shuts true.

She goes to the front room and looks in. The ash stool is opposite the window. The space under it is empty. The sixty-watt bulb hangs from the ceiling. The fire has not been lit since Easter.

She goes in and dusts the stool, rail by rail, three oak and one ash, with the damp cloth, and sets it square to the window again. The dust has sat since November and it comes off dark. She wipes the sill and the top of the settee arm and the seat of the chair on one side, and the room smells of cold dust and old ash. She does not light the fire. Lighting it for nothing would warm nothing, and the coal in the shed at the end of the road is not hers to think about.

She stands in the middle of the cold room for a bit with the cloth in her hand and looks at the bulb. It throws a small round of light on the ceiling and leaves the corners dark. On the seventh of December she stood on a borrowed ladder under it and fitted it with both hands while her sister talked from the doorway, and the ladder shook once and then sat true. She has not been up a ladder since. She has no ladder to be up. The one she borrowed went round to her sister's and up a road and into a front room at number twelve where a woman of seventy-eight does not go in after it.

She shuts the front room door and goes back to the kitchen.

At dark she wipes the top of the fridge again, though it does not want it, and looks at the six pads. The first is crossed twice. She does not cross anything else. A list with nothing crossed off it is not a list that moves. A repair with no date on it is not a repair that starts. She has known that since December, when she stood in an open porch in the rain with aluminium in both hands, and she puts the cloth back in the sink and turns the light off over the worktop.

---
