# Chapter 0669 — What Is Hers To Mend

Wednesday the twenty-second of September 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house at number ten from morning until dark with tools out on the kitchen table, and the whole of the day is a curtain rail she can mend without knocking anywhere else.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES MENDS A CURTAIN RAIL IN HER OWN HOUSE ON A WEDNESDAY, TELLS A GIRL AT HER DOOR THE TRUTH ABOUT A CAT SHE HAS NOT SEEN, DOES NOT GO UP THE ROAD WHEN HER SISTER SAYS SHE COULD, AND PUTS THE TOOLS AWAY.**

She is a woman in her early thirties. She is at home all day. Three people speak to her and not one of them is the one she has been waiting for.

---

The rail in the back bedroom has been loose since the summer, with one bracket pulling out of the plaster and the curtain hanging low on one side, and she has put off doing it because the drill is loud and the wall is thin.

This morning she gets the drill and the plugs and the screws out on the kitchen table, and she clears the sill so nothing falls behind the settee, and she puts a cloth under the drill because the table is the one she eats off and she does not want to find swarf in a sandwich.

She wants to get it fixed before dark without borrowing anything and without asking anything up the road, because asking up the road would make it a thing she had carried there.

She moves the bed out from the wall a foot and covers it with a sheet, and she drills the new hole slow with her hand steady on the grip, and the dust falls down onto the sheet in a pale fan. She puts the vacuum nozzle on the end of the hose for the second hole because the first one has settled her opinion about the wall.

The plug taps in tight with the end of the screwdriver, and the bracket goes back true, and the rail sits level when she lifts it on. She tightens both brackets and hangs the curtain and draws it across and back, and it runs free.

She stands in the doorway and looks at it. A curtain rail is a thing a person can be wrong about for months, and it took twenty minutes, and nobody in that house has to know anything about it.

She puts the drill and the bits back in their case and wipes the dust off the sill with a damp cloth, and she shakes the sheet out of the back door into the yard.

---

She eats at midday at her own table with the tools put away, and she looks out of her back window across the gardens towards number twelve, where the grass on the walked-flat line shows a little in the afternoon light.

She cannot see the gate from here. She can see the roof and the chimney and the top of the coal shed wall.

She does not go to the front to look. Looking from the front would make it a thing she was watching.

In the afternoon she tapes the back room window of her own house where the edge has lifted, because she taped a back room window in May up the road and she knows brown tape wants pressing down twice to stay.

She presses it with her thumb along the whole length and then again, and it stays. Her thumb comes away with a grey line on it, and she wipes that on the cloth too, without thinking about it, and the cloth goes in the bin and the grey is gone.

The roll came out of that same drawer in May, off a shelf, and she took what she thought she would want and she took the whole roll, and there is about four inches of tape on it now in a drawer in her own kitchen, and if the window on the back room here ever comes away again, it can be done in a minute, and the roll will be the right roll, and it is the same roll.

That is the whole of what she has taken from that house and she has never told anybody she has taken it.

Let in once when she was nineteen and stayed too long, and she has worked out since that the reason she was never sent away is that nobody in that house can send anybody away. She thinks about that while her thumb moves, and then she stops thinking about it.

---

The knocker goes in the early afternoon and it goes twice.

She stands in the back room with the tape roll still in her hand and does not move for a moment, because the last two people who ever knocked on that door were somebody selling something and the man who built the stool, and she knows which of the two this is going to be from the shape of it, and she would rather it was the first.

"Who's that?"

"It's the girl from number eight. Have you seen our cat?"

"I've not seen your cat."

"He's not been in since yesterday. He's got a white bib and one ear."

"I've not seen him."

"You've not seen him at all?"

"Not today. I was in all morning and I've not seen a cat in the garden or the road." She leans on the door with it still shut behind her hip. "Did you try the sheds at the end?"

"He won't go near the sheds. He went up on my car bonnet once and scratched all down one side of it."

"He'll be under something."

"He'll be under something and the something'll be a car." The girl sounds about eleven and not about nine. "Mam says if he's hurt he'll hide, and if he's not hurt he's in a shed."

"That's about right."

"Are you sure you haven't? Because my mam said ask the houses with gardens, and you have got a garden."

"I've had one and it's not much of a garden. I've not seen a cat in it."

"Right." The girl waits, which is the hardest part, and the woman at number ten stands in her own front passage with the tape roll going soft in her hand and lets her wait. "Right. Thanks."

"You could try the allotment gates on a Sunday. He's an outdoor one, isn't he? Outdoor cats go a long way."

"He won't go on a lead."

"No. He won't."

The girl goes back down the path. The woman shuts the door and stands in her own passage for a moment, and what she is thinking is that she has not seen a cat, and that there is a woman up this road she has spoken to twice, and that the one thing she could have said to a child on her step was *I have been up there and I know what is going on there*, and she did not say it, and the child did not need her to.

---

Her sister comes round at dark on her way back from a late, and comes in the back way because the back door shuts true now, and they drink tea standing up in the kitchen with the curtain rail mended above them in the back bedroom.

"How's the rail?"

"Done."

"That quick?"

"It wasn't doing anything dramatic. One bracket." She hooks her thumb at the ceiling. "Come and see it."

"After I've had this tea. If I come up there I'll want to sit on the bed and I'll never get off it."

The sister is at number nine and has been at number nine all her life, and she says that, and the woman at number ten says it, and neither of them says the other thing, which is that the sister is the only person in the world who does not have to be asked twice to come for tea.

Her sister does not mention number twelve. The talk is of the council form and of a back door that shuts true and of a woman at number eight whose cat has gone missing and whose car has a scratch down one side of it.

Then her sister says, "So the light's real, then. The one up there."

"Lamp on a bracket by the gate."

"Mains, or one of the little ones on a post."

"She didn't say. I asked."

"You asked."

"I asked what kind it was. I'm not going up there to read a letter about it."

Her sister looks at her over the mug for a second. "You could, though. It's a lamp. You could walk up and read the letter off the door and you'd have done something."

"I could."

"Everybody else in that street has. There's only you and her left who haven't."

"That's not a reason."

"It's a reason. It's the same reason as the form, and the form got your back door shut." Her sister puts the mug down. "You went up there in the spring and put a tape sheet on her window and she never said a word to you about it either, did she. That's two of you not saying anything."

"She said thank you."

"She said thank you for a sheet of tape. That's not the same as saying thank you for coming in the house, and you know it isn't."

The woman at number ten puts her own mug in the sink and turns the tap off hard enough to make the pipes knock in the wall.

"You could go yourself, then. You've got my form. You were the one filled it in."

"I did not fill it in, I helped you with the bit about the property, and the property is number four, not number twelve."

"Number twelve's got a lamp on it now, hasn't it. You could write to the council about number twelve."

"Because I know her. In what capacity do I know her."

"You've got no capacity at all. That's the point of you." Her sister picks up her bag. "I'm not saying go up there. I'm saying you could."

"You've said it four times."

"I've said it three times. I counted." She is at the back door and she has her hand on it. "Put a bit of something on that rail so it doesn't look like a job a woman did on her own. A bit of wood, a filler block, anything. It's not the doing of it, it's the looking of it."

"It looks fine."

"I know it looks fine. That's what I mean, and you're not listening to me." And she goes down the path, and the back door shuts true behind her, and it makes a sound the woman at number ten has not got used to.

---

After her sister goes she goes up and checks the rail once more and draws the curtain across for the night, and the room looks right with the curtain hanging straight.

She stands in the doorway of the back bedroom for a moment and looks at the two brackets and the four screws, and it is the best thing she has done in that house in a year, and there is nobody in the world she can tell that to.

The way you do that, she has found out, is not to tell anybody. It is to have a second look at it later, on your own, with the light off and the door nearly shut, and to be slightly disappointed in yourself both times, and to do it again in a month whether or not the curtain has moved.

The gate up the road is standing open against a coal shed wall with both bolts in an apron pocket. The brush is on a passage floor in the middle of a runner with the handle across the walking line. A woman up there is at a table under a window with six things on a piece of paper and no pencil in the house.

Neither fact needs her. That is the whole of what she has learned since July.

She bolts the front door and the back door and turns the kitchen light off, leaving the landing light on the way up.

She goes to bed with the tools away and the house straight, and the tape on her own window pressed down twice, and the memory of about a minute on a doorstep in the summer with an open door, and all of it used on a roll of tape in an older woman's hand while the chair stood in the room behind her the whole time and she looked at nothing else.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES HAS GOT THROUGH A DAY WITHOUT CARRYING ANYTHING UP THE ROAD SHE ONCE KNOCKED ON, AND HAS TOLD A GIRL AT HER DOOR THE PLAIN TRUTH ABOUT A CAT SHE HAS NOT SEEN, AND HAS NOT ASKED WHY A GATE WAS REFUSED IN THREE SECONDS IN MAY, AND HAS MENDED WHAT IS HERS WITH WHAT IS HERS.**
