# Chapter 0724 — The Back Of The Card

Thursday the twelfth of May 2022, in this borough. He is at his own unit from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a cover off the wall and two pairs of wire and one number written on the back of a scrap of card for the first time since before Christmas.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES TAKES THE COVER OFF A TELEPHONE ON THE WALL OF HIS OWN UNIT AND LOOKS AT TWO PAIRS OF WIRE JOINED TO EACH OTHER AT THE PLATE, WRITES A TELEPHONE NUMBER ON THE BACK OF A SCRAP OF SCRIBING CARD THAT HAS TWELVE ON THE OTHER SIDE, RINGS A WOMAN WHO TAKES TELEPHONES OFF WALLS FOR A LIVING AND ASKS HER WHETHER SHE HAS SEEN ONE, AND PUTS THE COVER BACK ON THE WALL BEFORE HE LEAVES.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is at his own unit and nowhere else, from the first hour to the last. A woman he has had a number for since the spring is at the other end of a line in the afternoon. He speaks to her on it.

---

He starts on the run at the front of the unit and it goes the same as it went on Monday, which is not at all.

The top run is proud of the three below it by about a quarter of an inch over its whole six foot. He has been at it since March. He has shimmed it, he has planed it, he has taken a shaving off the top of it and put it back in March and taken another off in April, and it is proud now by exactly what it was proud by in March.

He stands out at the far end of it and looks along the frontage with his hands down, and it is a quarter of an inch, and you cannot see it, and anybody who stands where he is standing and looks along that wall can see it.

He puts the plane down and does not pick it up again.

---

Then he is in the unit at about the middle of the morning and he does a thing he has been putting off since he got here.

The telephone is on the wall by the door of the office part, cream, on a bit of board, and it has not rung in his hearing in three months.

He has a screwdriver on the bench and he puts the blade in the slot of the cover and turns it and the cover comes down into his hand.

And on the back of the plate there are two pairs of wire going up into the plaster, and they are joined to each other.

Two of the screws in the box are bright and two are gone brown with dust round them, and the join in the middle of the plate has the dust of somebody else's ceiling in it, and it is the same join he has had in front of him twice in his working life and both times it was in a hall and both times there was no light on.

He stands with the cover in his hand and looks at it for about as long as it takes him to walk from the office door to the bench and back.

---

What he does next is not a decision and he knows that afterwards.

He walks to the bench and picks the mobile up off it and goes into the one entry he has had in there since the spring, and the entry is a unit number off the industrial estate and a road and four digits after that, and there is no name on it because he put it in from a card somebody left on the front of his bench and he never put a name to it.

He has never rung it. That is the whole of his relationship with it.

He stands with his thumb over the button, and the thing that has been sitting in him since the middle of the morning is not a question about this unit.

It is that if there is one of these in a wall in this town then there will be more than one, and that every one of them is in a wall in a house that belongs to somebody, and that he is a man who goes into houses.

---

He puts the cover down on the bench.

He does not put it back for a long time.

What he does instead is get his tape measure out of the case and hooks the end of it round the pair at the plate, and he pulls the measure out about eight inches and reads it and writes nothing down, because he has nothing to write it on, and then he puts the tape measure back in its case, and that is the first thing today that is a habit and not a job.

Two pairs joined at the plate is not a fault anybody can see. It is not a fault anybody would ever be told about. It is the sort of thing a house has, and a street has, and a town has, and nobody rings anybody about it, because there is nothing to ring about. A woman can put a receiver to her ear and hear four words from a stranger and put it down again, and a man in a different town can find a bell under his own back-room floor and hear it in a passage he cannot get to, and not one person at either end of that knows the other end exists.

And there is no way to have that conversation.

There is no version of it. He is a man who does doors and gates and floors and he has never wired anything in his life and he would not know where to start, and if he did start he would be a man who has been into this house on a Tuesday and then come back on a Thursday to take a telephone apart, and by Friday the whole of this row would know why.

So the cover lies on the bench with the bell wires showing, and at the end of the day it goes back on, because a unit with a cover off is a unit that looks like it is being worked on.

---

At about the middle of the afternoon he gets the card out.

It is in his shirt pocket and it has been in his shirt pocket since before Christmas. It is a piece of scribing card about the size of a postcard, torn off square, and it has twelve written on one side in his own pencil, and the other side is blank, and it has been blank for as long as he has been carrying it.

He has been carrying a thing that could be looked at since before Christmas. He has done that on purpose. He knows exactly what he has been doing and he has never once put it the other way up in public, and he has not needed to.

He takes the pencil out from behind his ear and looks at the blank side.

And he writes the number of a woman in her late forties on it.

He writes it out in four goes, the road, the unit, the two pairs of digits, and he gets every one of them right, and the reason it is in that telephone is that a woman who clears houses dropped a door frame at the shop in the spring and wrote the number out on the back of his own card for him and left it on the bench, and he put the entry in that night.

He writes it slowly and he gets all of it right and he looks at it and it is the first thing that has been on the back of that card since he started carrying it.

Then he puts the card back in his shirt pocket with twelve on the other side, and the pencil goes back behind his ear, and he does not do anything else about it all day.

---

He rings her in the afternoon.

He takes the mobile off the bench and he has the number in there as well and he could have used that, and he uses the card instead, and he has to look at the back of it to be sure.

She answers inside of about four rings and says her own word for the shop and then hears that it is not the shop.

"Hello."

"It's the joiner," he says. "Off the industrial estate."

"Right."

There's a pause of about the length of a door coming off a frame.

"You never ring," she says.

"No."

"About what?"

And this is where he could have said any of four things.

He could have said it was about the gates. He could have said he was after a price for a pair of stiles. He could have said he had a floor to give away. All three of them would have been a way of ringing her without a reason, and all three of them would have been about a bench.

What he says is: "Have you ever taken a telephone off a wall."

---

"Yes," she says.

"Did you see anything at the back of it."

"Yes."

"Two pairs."

"Yes. Blue to brown, and a bit of tape round the twist."

He stands at the bench in his own unit with the cover lying on it and the wires showing and he does not say anything for long enough that she says:

"Well?"

"Nothing," he says. "That's all. That's what I rang about."

"About nothing?"

"There's nobody to tell," he says. "That's it. That's why I rang."

And he rings off before she can put the next thing to him, which he knows she is going to do, because the next thing is *well, who's the somebody*, and there is no answer to that at all.

---

He stands there with the mobile in his hand and looks at the four digits on the card in his pocket and works out what he has just done.

He has found out that somebody else in this borough has put their hand on the same two pairs of wire and has not told anybody either, and that between them there are now two people who know and about four hundred people in this part of the borough who do not, and no sentence between the two of them that would be worth making.

And he has rung a woman for the first time since the spring to find that out.

That is what a telephone is for. That is the whole of what he got out of it, and it took the length of a door coming off a frame, and she has not rung back, and she will not, because the last thing he did was hang up on her.

---

He puts the cover back on the wall in the middle of the afternoon, and it goes with the first turn of the screwdriver.

The gates stay leaning against the back wall on their batten where he put them before the winter. The numbers are still in the chalk on the top rails and he does not re-chalk them, because re-chalking a gate that is paid for and installed is the act of a man who thinks it is going somewhere.

He has not been that way since the seventeenth of June and nothing on any calendar puts it back in front of him. It is four streets. There is a gate and a window and a hotplate and a front room, and there is a ladder folded under a window that he has never seen, and he does not do roofs, and the work is paid, and the latch stile is right.

He shuts the unit gate and it latches, and there is nothing in his pocket on either side except a number and a number, and both of them are his, and neither of them is anybody else's.

---

The telephone on the wall by the office door goes when he is down to the last of the light and the gate bars are across and the van is outside with the doors shut.

He has the screwdriver in his hand and he is four foot from it and it is the shop's own number, and it has not made a sound in this unit in three months.

It goes again.

The cover is back on. Two turns of the blade, tight, the dust of somebody else's ceiling still caught in the join at the plate, and behind that cover there are two pairs of wire joined to each other, and that is three times in his working life, and all three of them were in a room with a floor in or a floor with a room under it.

He stands there with the screwdriver and does not move.

It goes about eleven times. He counts them, standing in the middle of his own unit, and there is nobody in the building and no bell on the floor and no second bell in the passage, and it is a telephone on a wall with a cover on it ringing because a line somewhere in this town has got a room on it.

Then it stops, and the unit is quiet, and the radio is off and the van is outside with the doors shut.

He puts the screwdriver down on the bench, and he does not put a note in the book, and he does not get the mobile out.

There is a thing he could do about that telephone in about four minutes. It would cost him nothing. He has a card in his pocket with a number on it that he wrote this afternoon, and a woman at the other end of it who has taken these off walls and described one of them to him down a line, and he has had this join in his hand three times, and there is a bell under a floor in a house off the ring road under two nails he put in himself.

And there is no version of that conversation in which he says the word bell out loud to anybody who could then be asked about it by somebody else.
