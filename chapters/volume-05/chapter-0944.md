# Chapter 0944 — Behind The Lifted Sheet

Friday the fourth of October 2024, at the sheet on the office wall of the lock-up with the first rain on it since the spring. Behind a lifted sheet.

**CORRUGATED IRON STANDS AGAINST THE SIDE OF THE OFFICE AT THE LOCK-UP AND ITS BOTTOM EDGE HAS COME AWAY FROM THE WALL ALONG ONE LINE, AND SHE MEANS TO PUT THE SHUT FINGERS OF ONE HAND INTO THAT OPENING AND ROCK THE HAND TWICE UP AND DOWN, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE LINE RUNS AND WHAT STANDS BEHIND IT.**

The sheet was hung on that north side in eight days of March and finished before the April weather, and it has been fixed along the top and along the two sides and along the bottom at one end only, because the other end of that wall is a corner that a man could not get a drill into. It has been like that since the week it went up.

It is a sheet of corrugated iron about eight feet by three, set on edge against the rendered wall and fixed with four screws along the top and two down the sides, and the bottom edge at the flag end stands away from the render by about the width of a hand at the head of the sheet and closes to a paper at the corner. The gap is the length of the bottom edge where the sheet is not touching anything at all.

---

A sheet that stands off a wall is a line and not a face, so the closed fingers of one hand go into it and the hand rocks twice against the wall behind, so that the depth comes off the wall rather than off the arm. The arris of the bottom flange is blunt along its whole length where the corrugation has been opened out, and the inside of it is rusty in a way the outside never is.

The gap runs the whole eight feet and is widest at the upper end and closes to nothing in the last foot at the corner. Against the wall behind it, along the whole of that line, stands a length of batten about two inches by one, fixed to the render at three points, and the wall between the battens is render and dry and about as far off the sheet as the fingers say.

---

A sheet fixed at the top and at two sides and at one end only is a cantilever, and a cantilever is exactly what the man who fixed it put up. The top of an eight-foot sheet is reachable from a ladder and the sides are reachable from the flags, and the bottom edge at the far end was down in the angle of the wall where a ladder does not go and a drill does not reach on a Sunday.

So the sheet is carried at the top and at three corners and left to hang at the fourth, which is what a wall of that height does to sheet iron in a wind. The wind gets under the bottom flange and lifts, and the sheet rises about the thickness of a coin and comes back, and it has been doing that since April without any visible sign of it to anybody standing in the yard.

What a sheet does when it moves is not move far. It moves at the free corner and hardly at all at the corner it is screwed to, so the bottom edge stays straight along most of its length and lifts only where it has room to, and the free end is the only part of that sheet that has ever been anywhere.

The flange is where all of that shows. The bottom flange of a corrugated sheet is a turn of the metal about three-quarters of an inch deep, blunt inside from the moment it is rolled and blunter outside after one winter, because the outside is what the water sits in and the inside is what dries last.

Under that flange there is a line of grit about a finger wide the whole length of the sheet, and the grit came in when the sheet was put up and has stayed because the flange turns up and not down. A bead of this morning's rain is sitting in the curl at the head of the sheet and has not reached the grit yet, and it will get there within a week.

The batten behind is sawn softwood fixed to render with plugs, and the plugs are the older kind, cut from a length of rod and set into the wall rather than driven into it, and they have gone hard, and not one of them has anything loose about it.

A length of corrugated iron against a wall is carried on the battens and takes nothing from the render, because iron moves at a rate render cannot follow. Render takes up water in a wet month and gives it back in a dry one, and a sheet of iron does the same thing about four hundred times less, and so the sheet is given a batten to sit on with the render free behind it.

The battens are there for that reason, and the reason is also why the sheet stands off where it stands off instead of lying flat on the wall. The fixings at the top are through the batten and through the sheet and into a plug in the render. Not one screw on that wall goes straight from sheet to render.

Corrugation is the oldest cheap answer to the same problem and it works the same way. The flutes of a corrugated sheet are what make it stiff across its width, and the whole reason a sheet can stand eight feet up on three corners is that it is not flat. Flat sheet of that thickness would buckle in a moderate wind and come off the wall; corrugated sheet does the opposite and carries the wind into the battens behind it.

A sheet fixed like that one has a life measured in decades and not in years, because the rust starts inside the bottom flange where the rain cannot dry it and it goes round the sheet from there at about the rate a nail rusts in a wet cellar. On this one it has gone about as far as that goes in a first wet season after two summers, and there is plenty of the sheet left.

The bottom flange is also the reason nothing has ever grown in that line, which a person who has not looked at it would assume is the opposite of a gap along the bottom of a sheet of iron. The flange turns up and the grit under it is dry and coarse, and nothing settles in it.

The sheet has not rusted through anywhere. The rust inside the flange is bright and it is only skin deep, and the outside of the sheet is streaked where the rain runs off it in four lines, and the lines go down to the bottom flange and stop.

The woman who books work sits down on the office step with her back to the rail beside it and her feet on the flags, and she stays there.

"There were three of them under the bridge this morning."

"There were two, and the third was further up."

"Then they have come up the cut after the rain."

"They come up after the rain or they do not come up at all."

She sits there with the rain going past the edge of the step and does not look at the sheet, and the flags in front of the step go from damp to dry under her while she is sitting there, and the sheet stands its eight feet above her head with its bottom edge off the wall exactly as it was before she arrived.

There was a bridge, and a cut, and the rain, in that order and at that length, and not one of the three had anything to do with the gap under the bottom flange.

The sheet keeps its bottom edge and its line of grit and its four screws and its batten, and a hand put into the gap along that edge this afternoon comes out at the corner with the render dry on the pads of the fingers, provided the corner by the drain is left exactly where it is.
