# Chapter 0348 — A Week in the Middle

Sunday the twenty-seventh of August 2017, from about nine in the morning until about half past five in the afternoon, a parade, a shop with a shutter, and a house in a street off a lane, in Tideglass.

**ON THE WEDNESDAY A SHOPTER ON THAT PARADE CAME DOWN AT HALF PAST TEN IN THE MORNING IN FRONT OF A FULL SHOP AND WENT UP AGAIN AT ONE, AND ON THE FRIDAY A MAN WAITED IN A HALLWAY IN A STREET OFF A LANE FROM NINE IN THE MORNING UNTIL QUARTER PAST TWO FOR A JOB THAT TOOK HIM TWENTY MINUTES, AND THE ONLY PERSON IN THIS BOROUGH WHO WAS TOLD BOTH OF THOSE THINGS HAS NEVER SPOKEN TO EITHER OF THE MEN INVOLVED.**

The shop is the second from the top of that parade and it sells cards and papers and a bit of stationery and it has a shutter of the pressed kind with a slot in the top of it, and the slot has been out of order since before the summer.

The woman behind that counter on the Wednesday had about nine people in and the queue at the card machine was three deep, and at about half past ten she said she was going out for her lunch and went through into the back, and the man behind the counter went to the front door and put the bar across and pulled the shutter down with the rope on the inside of it, and the rope jammed about halfway for about a minute and then went, and the shop was shut.

It was not a closure. The light was on. You could see the top of the card machine through the slot if you stood on the kerb and looked up, and the man behind the counter was in there the whole time with the door bolted, and at about one the bar came off and the shutter went up and the woman came back through with a bag from the corner shop and said nothing about it and went on serving the queue.

About thirty people went past that shop between ten and one. Nine of them looked at it. Not one of them said one word about it to anybody, then or afterwards, and the man who puts shutters up was not near that parade that day and the man behind that counter has not mentioned it to anybody either.

The man in a fleece was going by at about ten past ten with a paper under his arm and he saw the whole of it, including the rope jamming.

---

The man in a fleece came into the shop off that parade at about half past three on the Thursday and stood at the counter with his paper and asked her for a pad of graph paper, which he did not need, and while she was at the rack he told her about the shopter on the Wednesday.

"I was stood there," said the man in a fleece. "They shut it at half past ten in the morning with about nine in and a queue, and the light was on, and you could see the top of the machine through the slot."

"Yes," said the woman who works in a shop. "That is her dinner, that, and no more than it is."

"That's what I thought. It's not a thing, is it."

"It's not a thing," said the woman who works in a shop, and put the pad on the counter, and he paid for it, and he said the weather was talking and she said it was, and he went out into the parade.

That is all that happened on that parade that week that anybody put into words, and the man in a fleece has not mentioned it since, and it is not a thing that has been anywhere.

The house is the third on the left, going up that comes off a lane going up past the ironmonger's, and it is a terrace of six, and there is a low wall and two iron gates and about nine foot of front garden on every house, and the gate on the third one does not latch and has not latched since June.

The woman who lives in that house is about thirty and she has a mother who is not well and lives at the other end of that lane, and the man in his forties went there on the Friday morning at about nine to take a length of four by two out of a cupboard in the back kitchen and put a new catch on the gate.

She told him at the door. She said it in the ordinary way, the way a person says a thing they have said to a tradesman before, which is that she was going to the clinic with her mother and would be back about two and that the buses were on the hour and it was as well to come before, and she was sorry.

He said it was no trouble and that he would come at two, and he did not come at two.

He waited. There is a hard standing at the side of the front room of that house, about four foot by six, between the side wall and a wooden fence about five foot high, and it gets the sun about half an hour before four o'clock, and he stood on it and then he did not stand on it, and after about ten minutes he went and sat in his van in a car park about a quarter of a mile away with the windows up.

The job was twenty minutes. He did it at about ten past two, and he came down the side of that house at about half past two with his bag, and the woman who lives there said he had better not bother about it and he said it was nothing, and he went out through that gate onto the pavement.

He did not go home. He has a workshop with his own name over the door and there was a job in it he had promised for the Monday, and it is not a job you can do properly in four hours in the last of the light, and he did not want to be in that workshop in the middle of the afternoon that day with nothing in his hands and the door open.

So he sat in the car park with the windows up. The car park belongs to a building with a shutter on it and it empties at about three and there is one man in it who is doing a stocktake on a van, and they nodded at each other about half past two and neither of them said anything, and about twenty past three the man in the van finished and drove out and the car park was empty and the man in his forties was still in it.

He read the paper he had brought with him. He has read it in a car park about four hundred times this year and it takes about an hour and a half. He drank a bottle of water he had brought for that. At about half past three a woman walked a dog through the far end of it and the dog stopped to do what dogs do at the kerb and neither of them looked.

At about ten to four he turned the engine over and sat with it running, and at about five past four he put it into gear and went out and did the last thing of the day, which was a lock on a side gate in a street four streets off, and it took him about fifteen minutes, and he got home at about half past five.

That is the whole of what happened to that man on the Friday. He had come out at about ten to nine and he was not home again until about half past five, and there are about six hours of that day that belong to nobody, and he has not mentioned the day to anybody.

---

The woman who works in a shop heard the first of those two things on the Thursday afternoon from a man who goes to the works and does not know her. The man in his forties came by her shop at about ten past four on the Friday with his bag and told her the second of them, standing on her step, in about four sentences.

"Lost you a day."

"Did you."

"Well, I did." He set the bag against the step with his boot. "House at the top off the lane, woman away, told me at the door about ten, and I went at quarter past two, and the whole of the day on that, and it was twenty minutes of work."

"You could have said no."

"I could have walked," said the man in his forties.

She said that was more or less the shape of a lost day, and lifted the bag off the step and gave it to him, and he went out into the parade with it.

She did not say to him that a shop on that parade had been shut with the light on at half past ten on the Wednesday, and he did not know that she had been told that at all, and the two of them did not arrive beside each other anywhere in her head.

Two things happening in a week in a borough this size is not a thing. The man in his forties loses about a day a month to somebody being out and he has never once made anything of it. The man in a fleece goes past that parade four times a day and has nothing to say to anybody in it most weeks and he said the thing about that shutter because it was in his face at ten past ten and he was on his way to a bus.

The one person who was given both is standing behind a counter in a shop a third of a mile from both of them, and she has never spoken to the man in a fleece in her life and does not know his name and would not have recognised him, and he has not set foot in that lane since that Friday morning, because there is nothing else at the top of it he wants.

**A MAN STOOD ON A HARD STANDING FOR FIVE HOURS IN A STREET OFF THAT LANE BECAUSE A WOMAN WAS AT THE CLINIC WITH HER MOTHER, AND A SHOPTER ON A PARADE CAME DOWN AT HALF PAST TEN IN THE MORNING WITH NINE PEOPLE INSIDE HER, AND NEITHER OF THOSE THINGS WAS EVER MENTIONED AGAIN.**
