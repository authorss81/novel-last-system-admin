# Chapter 0269 — The Day

Thursday the thirteenth of October 2016, from about half past seven in the evening, a first-floor room over a shop at the end of a parade in Tideglass.

**A QUESTION WENT TO A ROOM THAT HAD NEVER HAD THE RIGHT ANSWER IN IT, AND THE ROOM GOT A DIFFERENT ONE, AND THE DIFFERENT ONE IS BETTER, AND THE ROOM TOOK IT WITHOUT ASKING WHO PUT IT THERE.**

There are no chairs in that room. There is a table with a kettle on it and a gas ring pushed under the table and a second table against the wall with about nine cups on it, and a man in his thirties who has it leaves the door open on a Thursday evening from about seven and does not put anybody's name anywhere and does not tell anybody else when it is.

About six of them come. It is not called anything.

The young woman of about twenty is one of them. The man from the parade is one of them and has been coming since the summer. The woman of about fifty-five came at about ten past eight with her bag and a raincoat over it. The man in his forties came for the last hour and brought nothing, as he always does, and said so when he came in. A woman who works in a shop in a place with a name off Corrance Lane came off a late. And a man who is not in any of this came in out of the rain with a folded newspaper over his head at about half past seven, and did not come back to that room again after that night.

Nobody in that room has ever been in a room with the man in his sixties, and nobody in that room has heard him explain anything, and two of them have never spoken to him at all.

---

The man from the parade had it by about half past eight, standing up, because there is nowhere in that room to sit down.

"Same thing I asked in the hall," he said. "Which of the dated things. Somebody's going to be able to prove one of them first, isn't that what it is. That's what the six of you have been going at since the spring, one after another, in about four rooms. So which one."

Somebody said the shop's own, and somebody else said something one of us wrote down, and the man in his forties said not that one.

"Go on," said the man from the parade. "The young woman. You were in the room in April. You were in it."

They all looked at her, and the young woman of about twenty put her cup down on the table.

"It won't be a paper," she said.

"A paper."

"It'll be the day," she said. "The twenty-eighth of June."

The room did not say anything for a second, and the man from the parade said, "The day."

"It's the only thing we've ever agreed on," said the young woman of about twenty. "That's all it is. There's a shop with a shutter that goes up the same minute every morning, and a hall on a meter, and a pump in a doorway, and a register in a building by the water, and a bank that shuts at half past four, and not one of them's ever been agreed on by anybody in this borough, not once, not by anybody. The day has. One man in a street said it, and five people turned up, and none of us had been asked, and we all came, and it wasn't organised, and that day happened, and you can't lose a day. That's the difference. You can lose a piece of paper. You can lose a docket. Somebody's wallet goes in the river in October and a docket's gone and there's nothing left of it anywhere at all."

"You'd prove a date," said the man from the parade.

"You'd prove a date, and a date is the easiest thing in the world to prove, because everybody's got one and everybody remembers where they were when it was, and there is not one piece of paper in this borough that anybody is going to be able to lay a hand on in ten years' time, and there is a room full of people who were stood in the same rain on the same afternoon in the same place."

"That is a great deal better than the register," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"It is better than the register," said the young woman of about twenty. "The register's a book in a building by the water and anybody can shut that book. The day's us. That's all. It's not better because it's more true. It's better because it's ours."

The woman who works in a shop said, "It sounds right, that." The man in his forties said he thought it did, and that he had thought the register one was right in the summer and had gone off it since. The man from the parade put his thumb up on the table and said he would put money on it, which he would not, and a man who is not in any of this said that he had a mate over at Marrowgate who had been asking that exact question in a pub for a year, and nobody asked him about the mate and he did not say what the mate's answer was.

It went in about as quick as that. Nobody in that room had ever heard the answer that is not there, and the woman of about fifty-five, who has the two pieces of A4 in a bag under a chair and has said since April that they are copies, heard a room agree with something as quick as you can put a cup down and did not say one word about it.

---

At about twenty to nine there was a knock at the door and a man standing in it with a clipboard and a high-visibility waistcoat over a good coat, and he had a small job for somebody and a price for it, and he had it written down on the clipboard and the price was on a separate card.

The young woman of about twenty went down to the door because he was standing in it.

"I can't say yes to you," she said. "There's six of us in here and not one of us can say yes to anything, and it isn't me being careful with you. If I said yes to you I'd be saying it for a man who isn't here and a woman who isn't here, and they'd not know afterwards that I'd said it."

"That's all right," he said. "I'll come back."

"I don't think you should bother, honestly."

"I'll come back anyway," he said, and wrote something on the clipboard, and put the clipboard under his arm, and went off down the parade in the rain with his hood down.

He did not come back. There was nothing to come back to and nobody had asked him to ask, and about three weeks later he was doing a job in another part of the borough and mentioned to a man he was working for that he'd tried a room at the end of a parade and been turned away, and the man said, "Aye, they won't," and neither of them said another word about it.

About a quarter of an hour after that door had shut, the woman of about fifty-five was at the window with her bag on the sill and the rest of them had already gone back to the price of glass.

"He could tell us," she said, not turning round. "That's what I keep coming back to. He could tell us in about four seconds, that man, and none of you have ever heard him, and it isn't because he isn't in this borough."

"He couldn't tell us anything," said the young woman of about twenty. "He tried. He told a shopkeeper on the thirtieth of July that he could not get the two halves of his own answer apart, and he was stood in the street when he said it, and it took him about a minute, and not one of us was standing in that street. He's not got the other half either. He never had it. He said the one in a room in July and he can't get it back and he isn't going to, and that is the whole of what he knows about it, and that is not a fault in him. That is a man who has been honest in a room full of people who weren't listening."

"So he's no use to anybody."

"He'd never told anybody," said the young woman of about twenty. "And he hasn't been told about this either, and look what it's done. It is the best thing anybody has said in this borough in months and he hasn't heard a word of it and he's not going to, and that's the correct outcome and I'd fight anybody in that room who tried to make it a different one."

**AND THE KETTLE WENT ON AGAIN AT ABOUT HALF PAST NINE AND CAME TO THE BOIL AND WENT OFF AGAIN, AND THE RAIN KEPT ON AGAINST THE SHOP ROOF ABOVE THAT ROOM FOR TWO HOURS, AND THE MAN WHO HAD COMED IN WITH A NEWSPAPER OVER HIS HEAD WENT OFF DOWN THE PARADE AT ABOUT HALF PAST TEN STILL CARRYING IT AND DID NOT PUT IT IN A BIN.**
