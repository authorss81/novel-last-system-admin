# Chapter 0292 — A Hundred and Fifty-Five Pounds

Tuesday the twenty-fourth of January 2017, from about half past eight in the evening, a hall off a parade in Tideglass.

**A THIRD PIECE OF PAPER WENT ON THAT TABLE WITH A FIGURE ON IT AND THE WOMAN WITH THE KEY USED THE SENTENCE SHE HAD USED TWICE BEFORE, AND IT DID NOT WORK THE SECOND TIME, AND A MAN TOOK A CARD OUT OF A PLASTIC WALLET AND CARRIED IT OUT INTO THE PORCH AND STOOD IT AGAINST THE WALL.**

There are about ten people in that hall on a weeknight now and the man from the parade comes early and the man in his forties comes about half way through and brings nothing, and the woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall is in early with her bag still in her hand and her coat across the back of a chair, and the man in his sixties has a cup of tea and spends the first part of the evening looking out of the window at the parade.

The meter is on the wall by the door and the woman of about forty-two pays for that gas out of her own money and has done since about the summer, and there is a pound in it most weeks, and if it runs out at about ten on a Friday night the hall is dark by twelve and everybody goes home.

On that table there is still the card a letting agent put down before Christmas, with his firm's name on one side of it and a mobile number on the other, and it has been on that table since the Wednesday before Christmas, and the sheet with two hundred and sixty pounds at the top of it is gone off that table and is in the bin, and the room knows about the bin and has not raised it.

---

The heating engineer came in at about ten past nine and he had not been asked to and the man in his forties had rung him in the afternoon about the money for the boiler.

He had a bag and a folder and he put the folder on the table and did not open it, and he said that he had come because a man had rung him about a figure in this address that had gone on a piece of paper in November for taking a boiler out, and that he had not been the one who put that figure there and that he had come to look at what was left.

"That'll be nothing," said the man in his forties. "We had a boiler out of it in November. It's gone down a lane on a trolley."

"Right," said the heating engineer. "So what have you got."

"There's a gas," said the man in his forties. "And a meter. And two battens over the foot of that stair, one of which has been out since the summer, and a pipe of some description that comes from under the boards, and about nine electric sockets and an emergency light and an outside light that has not worked since about 2011, and that lot's the state of it."

"Where does the gas come in?"

"Under the floor off the shop next door."

"Then that's your answer," said the heating engineer. "Because the run's the shop's, and the meter on your wall is fed off a length that's practically under this floor, and if that shop ever goes and does anything to that run, whatever's on your meter is on their supply, and there'll be nobody in this room who can say a word about it because it's nobody's wall."

He opened the folder and took a sheet out of it and put it on the table face up, the way things get put down in that hall.

The top of it had his firm's name, a reference and a date, and against the words service and record there was a figure of a hundred and fifty-five pounds, and the bottom half of it was empty except for a printed line along the foot for a name and an account.

The first line on the top said that a service could not be booked until the account was opened, and that there was no such thing in that room as an account, and that nobody in that room had asked anybody for a service.

"That's the same as the other one," said the man in his forties. "That's word for word what the last one said. You're all on the same sheet."

"No, I'm not," said the heating engineer. "The last one wanted you to prove something. This one wants me to sign something, and if I sign it, it's me that's on it, and not the man down the road. That's the difference and it's the only difference and I'll not dress it up."

"Who'd open the account."

"I'd have to open one with a name on it," said the heating engineer. "The hall's got no name on anything. The hall's got a meter in a different company's name — I looked at it when I was in — and the shop next door's got a name, and if you put the shop's name on my sheet I have no idea what I'm agreeing to for them, and I'd have to ask their landlord, and their landlord's in a town I couldn't get to on a Wednesday."

He shut the folder and stood with his hand on it.

"There's no boiler in here," he said, "and that's what's made this hard. You could have had a hundred and fifty-five pound's worth of heating off me in September, on a boiler, in a room, with a gas, and you'd have walked away with a piece of paper with a date and a number on it. Instead there's a set of stairs, two battens, one of them out, and a length of pipe under a floor that belongs to a shop that's stood empty since the summer, and there's not one thing in that list I can put my name next to."

---

The woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall had been at the door through all of that and had come in about half way through it and stood at the side of the table with her hand on the back of a chair.

She had said a sentence in that room in November and she had said it again in December, and she had a month between the two of them to think of better words, and she came back with the same ones, in the same sequence, at the same speed, and every person at that table knew it.

"I am not having that done," said the woman of about forty-two, "and I am not refusing it, and I want the room to hear the two of those."

And then she said, because the sheet was on the table in front of her and because she was looking at it, "and the shop next door is not having it either."

"Which shop next door," said the man in his forties.

"The one with your back to me."

"That is not yours to be speaking for," said the man in his forties. "That's got a company on the deeds of it and a name over the door and about four people who work in it, and none of them in this room, and you cannot put a not-having and a not-refusing on a thing that is not in the room."

"I know that," said the woman of about forty-two.

"Then where's the other half of it."

"Which other half."

"You've said the two halves three times in this room," said the man in his forties, "and every other time it worked, because there was one thing on the table and your two halves went either side of it. There's three things on that table now. There's that sheet, and there's that card with the number on it that's been sitting there since Christmas, and there's whatever you were about to say about the shop next door. Your two halves don't go either side of three things. They go either side of nothing, and I'm not being clever, I'm saying you've learned the sentence and not the room, and it's the same mistake you'd have made in November and it's the one you'll make again in the summer."

Nobody said anything about it after that.

The heating engineer said that the sheet could go on the table or could go in the bin and that he was not going to say which, and that he would not leave a card and had not brought one, and that his number was in the book at that shop on the parade, and he left at about twenty to ten.

Nobody opened an account. Nobody rang the mobile on the card that had been on that table since before Christmas, and nobody rang the office either, and the sheet with a hundred and fifty-five pounds at the top of it went on that table face up next to the meter on the wall and it was on that table at midnight.

At about ten past ten the man in his sixties got up and went behind the meter on the wall and came back with a plastic wallet and put it on the table and opened it, and out of it came a card about the size of a playing card in a frame of stiff plastic, and the card had a number and a date on it, and the date was in 2013, and it had a box on it with a tick in the box, and the box said which thing had been done.

"Which thing," said the woman of about forty-two.

"Nothing you want," said the man in his sixties. "That's for the gas at the meter, and it was done in 2013 by a man who is not coming back, and it's got his name on it, and he's dead, and the name's no use to anybody including him."

He said that, and he picked the card up, and he stood there with it in his hand, and then he went out of that hall through the door that shuts now, and across the porch, and stood the card in the frame against the outside wall of that porch beside the meter, with the face of it turned in towards the wall, and came back in and sat down and picked his tea up and said that the tea had gone cold and that it was not the tea's fault.

**AND THAT CARD LEANED AGAINST THE OUTSIDE WALL OF THAT PORCH ON THE THURSDAY AND THROUGH A FORTNIGHT OF RAIN AND NOBODY HAS PUT IT INSIDE AND NOBODY HAS STOOD IT UP AGAIN, AND ON THE FIRST OF APRIL SOMEBODY PUT A CHAIR IN FRONT OF IT TO KEEP THE RAIN OFF THE FRONT OF THAT SHOP.**
