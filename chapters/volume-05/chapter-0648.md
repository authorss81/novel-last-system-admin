# Chapter 0648 — Can I Take That Envelope

Saturday the nineteenth of June 2021, in this borough. She is in a kitchen off the parade from about ten in the morning until about half past twelve, and the whole of it is a table with one bad leg and a gas envelope that was face up on a kitchen table on the Thursday before and was in a drawer on the Saturday.

**THE WOMAN WHO CLEARS HOUSES COMES UP THAT PATH ON A SATURDAY MORNING TO PUT A TABLE LEG RIGHT AND DOES IT PROPERLY AND ASKS FOR A BIT OF PAPER TO MEASURE A SHIM WITH AND IS HANDED AN ENVELOPE WITH SIX THINGS WRITTEN ON THE BACK IN PENCIL, AND READS IT FOUR TIMES WITH A CLAMP ON, AND SAYS NOTHING, AND AT THE DOOR ASKS FOR THE ENVELOPE, AND IS GIVEN IT OUT OF THE DRAWER BY A WOMAN WHO PUT IT IN THE DRAWER FOUR MINUTES EARLIER, AND NEITHER OF THEM SAYS ONE WORD ABOUT WHY.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is a woman in her late seventies. There is no third person in that kitchen.

---

She comes at about ten on the Saturday with a canvas bag, a plane, a short length of ash off her own offcut pile and a clamp, and she has rung on the Friday and said she would come and look and she has come to look.

The table is under the window with the cloth on it. She takes the cloth off and folds it in three and puts it on the sill by the sink, which is the corner the stool used to be stood in, and neither of them says one word about that.

---

And here is what is wrong with the table, and it took her about four minutes, and it is not the leg.

She runs her hand down the leg and the leg moves at the foot and the whole table rocks. And she thinks that is a leg, the same as the chair, the same as the stool, a foot on a floor.

And then she gets down on one knee and puts her thumb and forefinger in the joint where the leg meets the rail, and the leg does not move at all in that joint, because it is out of its mortice, all the way out. The frame is not moving. The table goes over because one leg is being carried about by the other three like a shoe on a nail.

And she says, "There you are."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "What's there."

And the woman in her late forties says, "Your table's not got two bad legs. It's got one leg that's come out of the top of it and everything else about this table is sound. That's why it goes over. That's why it always felt like it was going over."

---

She does it, and it is worth setting down how she does it, because it is a woman who is very good at this and who has spent her working life being extremely good at this.

She gets the leg out of its mortice altogether, which takes her about twenty minutes and is the part everybody gets wrong, because you do not prise a tenon out of a rail, you drive it down from the top with a chisel and a mallet and it comes out of the bottom.

She cleans both surfaces with the back of a chisel blade, which is the whole of it, and does not use a plane because there is not enough to take off.

She puts the leg back in, dry, wiggled to seat, and she puts a thin card on the joint, no glue on the leg, because glue on a tenon in a house like this is a repair that lasts about a year.

She knocks the top rail down onto it with a mallet off a piece of pipe, four goes, and they are not very hard, and she counts them out loud which she has not done since she was an apprentice.

And then she puts the clamp on it and leaves it ten minutes.

---

And at about a quarter past ten she stops, because she has a mark to make.

She has the joint tight and the clamp on and she wants to know how proud that foot is going to sit before she shims it, because you get one go at a taper under a table leg and if you make it too big you start again, and there is no pencil on that table, and she says, "Have you got a bit of paper."

And the woman in her late seventies goes to the drawer under the telephone, past the string and the fuses and the shears that do not shear, and takes a gas envelope out of the back of it, which is flat and has nothing on the front of it at all.

And she hands it to her face up, which is the way it has been lying on that table since the Thursday.

---

And the woman in her late forties says, "Ta."

And it is face up in her hand, and there is writing on the face of it in pencil.

And there is nothing on the other side of it and she has no idea that there is.

---

And here is what happens, and it takes about a second and a half, and it is the whole of that morning.

She reads it.

Not all of it. The first two lines, in about a second and a half, because they are six words and it is a kitchen and somebody is standing in the doorway.

*the gate*

*the front room*

And then she stops with the envelope in her hand.

---

And her first thought is not a thought about the woman.

Her first thought is: *there's six of them.*

And her second thought is: *that's not an order.*

And her third thought, about a second later, and it is in about half the time the first one took, is that she has got a piece of paper in her hand with somebody's handwriting on it and she has no idea why she has been given it.

And she says, "Sorry. I didn't know there was anything on it."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "It's only a piece of paper."

And the woman in her late forties says, "I'll put it back."

And she puts it down on the table.

She puts it down face up, because it was face up when it was given to her, and she does not think about the face at all.

---

And then she goes to work on the table.

And the envelope is lying on that table for about half an hour, about eighteen inches from her left knee, with the clamp standing up out of the middle of it, and she looks at it about four times.

She does not pick it up. She reads it with her eyes from where she is kneeling on a kitchen floor, and she reads it four times, and on the second time she is looking for something and on the fourth time she has stopped looking for it and is just reading it again.

And here is what she is looking for, and she never finds it, and it is the thing that would let her put it down.

She is looking for who wrote it. Not the name. Something else. Whether it is a list a person made for themselves or a list a person made out of something somebody else said.

And she decides in about the fourth minute of the forty that it is the first one, and she has no evidence for that at all.

---

And while she is out at the van for the plane and the mallet, which is about four minutes, the woman in her late seventies takes the envelope off the table and puts it in the drawer under the telephone.

It takes her about four seconds.

And the reason she does it is not tidiness. It is that there is a line about a gate at the top of it and a young woman of about thirty came to that door about a week ago and was told no in three seconds, and a woman of seventy-eight has decided in the last quarter of an hour that nobody is to read the first line of that envelope in her kitchen.

---

And when the woman in her late forties comes back in, the table is bare.

And she notices. She notices straight away, the way you notice a thing that has been moved in a room you know. And she looks at the drawer under the telephone, which is shut, and she does not say anything about it, and she puts the clamp on.

---

And the whole of that exchange is about ten seconds long and neither of them knows the other one has just done exactly the same thing.

---

And she finishes it.

She takes the clamp off at about twelve, knocks the rail down onto the tenon again, puts the clamp back on, and then deals with the foot.

And what she does to the foot is the thing she came about and it takes about a quarter of an hour and she does it in about a minute.

She planes a taper off the end of the ash, wide at one end and nothing at the other, so that about three inches of it will go under that leg and be about the thickness of a coin where it goes in. She drives it in with the heel of a screwdriver. She saws it flush, because a shim you cannot get at again is worth nothing.

And she says why and she is talking to nobody, and the reason is that a card lasts a fortnight and a taper lasts twenty years, and she has been driving cards under table legs since she was nineteen.

And at about twenty past twelve she stands up and puts her bag on the table and looks at the four legs of it and puts her boot on it, hard, twice, and it does not move.

And she says, "That'll see you."

And she says, "That joint's glued. It's not, and that's on purpose. Glue on a tenon in a house like this is a repair that lasts a winter, and it'll want doing again, and when it does, ring me and I'll do it in twenty minutes."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "There's no number."

And the woman in her late forties says, "There's a card in the van."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Right."

And the woman in her late forties says, "I'll bring it round. Not today. I'm not going out to the van in this."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Whenever."

---

And the gate goes as she goes out, and it is not the wind.

It comes in about four inches on its own and bangs on the latch stop, and a woman of seventy-eight is standing in the doorway behind her and does not move.

And the woman in her late forties looks at it.

She looks at it for about two seconds and she knows that there is a line about a gate at the top of a piece of paper in a drawer in that kitchen, because she read it about half an hour ago on her knee, and she does not say one word about the gate.

And she gets in the van.

---

And here is where the whole of that Saturday actually happens, and it is in a driveway off the parade at about ten past one in the afternoon with the engine off.

She sits there with a gas envelope on the passenger seat.

And she has not stolen anything and she has not lied to anybody. She asked for a bit of paper, she was given a piece of paper, she apologised for the writing on it, she put it down, and then a woman of seventy-eight put it in a drawer while she was in the van, and neither of them mentioned that either.

And what she has now got is six lines of pencil in a hand that is not hers, and the fourth one is the hotplate on the left of a stove she has seen, and she has an element's worth of nothing and no way of getting at it, because the man who would do it is a man she rang once about a table and told him the truth about a chair on a bench on the parade and promised him she would not put their two heads together in that woman's front room.

And she cannot ring him.

That is the whole of it. She cannot ring him. She has made a promise about a chair and a stool on a bench on a Monday in May and the thing in her hand on the passenger seat is not a chair and it is not a stool and she has already decided twice that it is not the same.

---

And then she does the thing she is actually going to do about it, and she is aware that she is doing it while she is doing it.

She gets out of the van, goes back up the path, and knocks again.

And the woman in her late seventies opens the door and says, "You've forgotten something."

And the woman in her late forties says, "Can I take that envelope."

And there is about four seconds in that doorway.

And the woman in her late seventies does not say *why*. She does not say *what's on it*. She does not say *you've read it*.

She goes to the drawer under the telephone, opens it, takes it out, and puts it into the woman's hands.

---

And what she is doing by opening that drawer is not giving a piece of paper away.

It is making certain that she is not the person who put it in there.

**A WOMAN ASKED FOR A BIT OF PAPER AND WAS HANDED SIX THINGS, AND READ THEM ON HER KNEES FOUR TIMES WITHOUT PICKING THEM UP, AND SAID SHE WAS SORRY, AND PUT A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT'S OWN HIDING AWAY BY ASKING FOR THE ENVELOPE AT THE DOOR, AND TOOK IT OUT OF A DRAWER THAT A WOMAN HAD PUT IT IN FOUR MINUTES EARLIER SO THAT SHE WOULD NOT BE THE ONE WHO HAD PUT IT THERE, AND WENT DOWN THAT ROAD WITHOUT SAYING ONE WORD ABOUT A GATE SHE SAW MOVE.**

(End of chapter 0648)
