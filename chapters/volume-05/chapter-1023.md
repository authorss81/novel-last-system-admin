# Chapter 1023 — Whether You Would Be In The House

Undated in this draft.

**THE UPSTAIRS ROOM AT NUMBER TEN HAS ONE WINDOW WITH A CATCH ON IT.**

Up one flight at number ten there is a small room over the hall with a single window in the front and no fireplace at all, and a door that has been fitted too tight since before the summer, so that it has to be lifted at the handle before it will shut at all. The sill under that window is level inside and has a fall out on the outside, which is the way a window looks when it has been put in by a man who was careful about the room and in a hurry about the outside of it, and there is a catch on the frame that is stiff and that nobody in that house has ever got oil to.

The front room below has a new piece of hardwood in the bottom of its window and a board across the opening where the frame comes out of itself, and the whole of that room smells of new oak shavings, and there is more light in the passage underneath it than there was before the sash came out.

Her sister was up there with her coat off and her hands in her pockets when he came to the house, and she had gone up before he came to the door.

"You have not come up that stair for anything."

"I have not."

"You are standing in my room with your hands empty and nothing behind your face, which is a thing I have not seen you do since the spring." She stayed on the boards under the window. "So I am going to make it easy for you and put out what you have been made to come and put out, and then you can tell me whether I have got it right, and if I have not got it right you can go back down that stair and come up again."

"I do not know what I have come for."

"I know what you have come for, because you told me in a shed behind my mother's coal shed four weeks ago that you had something to ask me for and that you were not going to tell me what it was, and then you promised me it would come inside a fortnight. It has been four weeks and you have been up that frontage about forty times." She turned round and put her back to the window. "So here it is inside a fortnight, late, and you are going to ask me for a thing."

---

He had, and that was the thing he had got wrong in his own thinking about himself for a fortnight. She had put it to him in that shed that if he asked her for a thing to get a line in a book she would have to agree to it, and that then he would have it, and that she would be the woman who agreed, and he had taken it at the time that she was talking about the book.

She had not been talking about the book.

"Not that," she told him, when he got that far out. "I know what you are about to put into words and you have not put it out. You are going to tell me that a man came up that frontage for four months with something in his mouth and never got it out because I got there first every time. That is what you think this is."

"Then what is it."

"I have been on that book longer than you have been alive. Not this season. Every season. And I went down there in the spring and stood in that room while a woman read my six weeks out with my sister standing next to me, and the only thing anybody in that room put to me was that there was work against me that I did not do here." She did not raise her voice and there is nobody in that house to raise it at. "And a man stood at the end of that table with his fists in his pockets and nothing at all got put down about him, and every woman in that room let him stand there, and I have thought about which of us was the worst of it every day since."

"You asked me once for a person in a room."

"The woman at nine asked you for that. I asked you for a page." She waited for him to get at that and he did not get at it. "Both of them are the same thing, and I have not come up here to ask you for either of them. I have come because a woman in a pantry at my mother's asked you in her own mouth to do the wall behind that range, and you are going to take the range off that wall, and there is one thing in that fortnight that you cannot buy and cannot pay for and cannot put on a piece of paper, and it is me."
She took her hands out of her pockets.

"Be in the house when I start it," he told her. "Not because I want a man looked at. Because there is a sheet of paper in this borough that a man signed his name at the foot of, and I drew a pencil through what he had put there and set yours beneath it in your own hand, because you asked me to. And that means not one piece of paper anywhere can carry this job, and the only way anybody in this borough finds out that a man did a thing is because somebody was in a room while he did it."

He put the chisel down on the boards and pushed it away from himself across the floor so that it came to rest on her side of the room, and she looked at where it stopped and did not pick it up.

"So you want me to stand in a kitchen at my mother's on a working morning and see a man take a range off a wall, and there will be nobody in that room but me."

"There will be you."

"You have been on this frontage forty times in four months and you have never once asked anybody for a thing that was not work, and now you are asking me for something that is not work, and you are asking me for it because I am the only person in this house who can turn you down, which means you have worked out that you cannot do it without somebody who could turn you down being in the room." She stopped. "That is the truest thing anybody has put into words in this house since before the war, and I am not going to pretend I am not glad of it, and I am equally not going to pretend it makes me glad."

He came away from that room having asked a woman for a thing, and it is the second time in four days, and the first was at number nine on a stair with a cup on it, and the second one was the harder of the two because on the stair he was standing in a woman's way and in that room he was standing in a woman's house asking her to put herself in the place he is going to work.

Neither of the two asks was for himself in the ordinary sense of the word, and he worked that out on the stairs going down and had nothing to do with it, and there is a woman two houses along who has asked him nothing at all since a Thursday and is owed nothing and will not ask. There is a fourth woman on that frontage who has spent six weeks on a river and has come home to a house her daughter has been running, and she put her finger on the last line of a sheet of paper in her front room at the start of that week and did not go across it, and he has not been back into that house since.

Whether she will be standing in that kitchen on the day he starts is not settled and she is not going to settle it in a room over a hall, and her sister left the chisel where he had pushed it and put the catch down on the sill and did not turn it, and it is still lying on that sill with the window shut behind it and the room going dark.
