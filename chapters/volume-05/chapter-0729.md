# Chapter 0729 — The Three And A Half Inches

Tuesday the thirty-first of May 2022, in this borough. She is in her own kitchen and yard at number ten from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a roll with almost nothing left and a sheet of ply that will not do.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES MEASURES THE PLY AGAINST THE SHED FELT EDGE IN HER OWN YARD, FINDS IT SHORT, PUTS THE HAMMER AND SCREWDRIVER BACK ON THE TABLE, SPEAKS TO HER SISTER AT THE BACK DOOR ABOUT THE PAPER, AND PEGS THE WASHING LINE STRAIGHT BEFORE DARK.**

She is a woman in her early thirties at number ten on Tuesday. Her sister comes to the back door near midday and she speaks to her out there.

---

She starts at the table with the three-line paper lying where it has lain since April, out of the drawer under the worktop and under a stack of washing at dark and back on the table at first light.

A roll, a tin, a lip of timber. She reads the three lines and does not add to them. The drawer under the worktop is shut and empty, and she leaves it shut.

Beside the paper are the ply offcut, the hammer, the screwdriver, the tape and the pencil, all where she left them in May. She picks up the tape roll and feels its weight, three and a half inches left on it, and sets it down again without pulling any.

---

In the middle of the morning she carries the ply out to the shed and tries it against the front edge where the felt is gone.

The felt hangs loose and the timber under it is damp. She lays the ply along the edge and it falls short by the width of her hand, and she moves it left and right and it falls short both ways.

She stands with the ply in her hands and looks at the draught coming through, and then she carries the ply back in and leans it against the yard wall where it was.

It will not do. Ply will not do for a felt edge, and she knows that now from having carried it out twice.

She picks the hammer and the screwdriver off the table and puts them away in the drawer under the worktop, side by side, and shuts the drawer on them. The paper stays out on the table with the tape and the pencil beside it.

---

Her sister comes to the back door in the middle of the day with nothing in her hands.

"You have still got the paper out," her sister says, looking past her at the table.

"I have," she says. "It came out of the drawer on the twenty-sixth of April and it has been on the table since."

"What will you do with it?"

"Nothing yet," she says. "It wants a man who would come, and there is no man coming for a hall with nothing under the line."

Her sister looks at the hammer and the screwdriver and the pencil laid out like a row of teeth.

"You could put it back in the drawer," her sister says.

"I could," she says. "And then it would be where it was in December, shut and empty, and I would be where I was in December. I would rather have it on the table where I can see it."

Her sister nods and does not argue, and that is the most use anybody has been to her all spring.

---

She spends the afternoon with the washing, pegging it straight across the draught the way she has since the shed went.

The line is pegged straight and the shirts hang without turning. The tape stays on the table with its three and a half inches still on the roll. The outside light will burn on Saturday and she will switch it then, and not before.

At dark she takes the washing in, armful by armful, and folds it on the table beside the paper without covering the paper, so that the three lines show at the edge of the pile.

Then she goes out to the yard once more and looks at the shed with its felt gone and the ply leaning useless against the wall, and she pulls the line down from its peg and re-pegs it a finger tighter, so that it sings when she touches it, and she goes in with her hands damp and leaves the back mat empty behind her.
