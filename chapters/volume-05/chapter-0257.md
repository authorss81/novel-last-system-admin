# Chapter 0257 — Four, Checked

Monday the eighth of August 2016, from about half past eight in the morning, a parade of shops in Tideglass, Marsh Lane, a hall off the parade, and Carrow Lane.

**SHE WENT AND ASKED FOUR PEOPLE WHAT THEY SAW ON THE TWENTY-EIGHTH OF JUNE, AND THREE OF THEM SAID THE SAME AS THE ROOM HAD HEARD, AND THE FOURTH WAS A POUND IN A GAS METER AND IT WAS HERS, AND SHE PUT IT IN EVERY WEEK, AND THAT IS THE WHOLE OF WHAT A DATE CAME TO.**

She did them in the order they had happened in her head, and she wrote the four headings on the back of an envelope because she is a person who has got a bag in a corridor that is being emptied in a fortnight and who copies things out in her own hand, and the envelope went in the bag after the two pieces of A4.

She did not tell anybody she was doing it and she did not ask anybody to come.

---

The man from the boat crew was in the back of a van on the parade with the doors open at about half past eight, drinking tea out of a mug with the lid off, because the boat had gone out over the water at seven and there was a shift in it at ten.

She asked him about the pipe and the fitting on the kerb.

"Four foot and a bit and a elbow," he said, "and it went on the kerb outside the shop that shuts of a Sunday, and it was there Friday and Saturday and Monday, and it was gone by the Wednesday morning, and that's the last I knew about it."

"He didn't come back for it."

"No, and he didn't ask for it, and it wasn't his, and if he'd wanted it back he'd have rung the boat, and it's still in the yard of the house behind because a woman got fed up of it." He drank his tea. "She can have it any time she wants it. It's under a bit of roofing felt."

"I'll tell her it's there."

"Tell her it's there."

Then she went down Marsh Lane, which is off Peverell Street, and the woman in the second house was in her own yard with a line out.

She asked about the man off the river and the hour and the landing and the bath.

"That's right," said the woman in the second house, before she had been told what the question was. "He come about half three and he was under the stairs for about an hour because it goes under the stairs to get up, and I told him the whole time he didn't need to shift that. And he didn't charge me and he didn't want a tea and he was gone before four." She shook a line out. "I want to say one thing about him and then I'll not say it again. He did it because I said on the twenty-eighth of June that I could do it and couldn't. I had said it since March. So it was mine."

"That's fair."

"It wasn't his and it wasn't hers and it wasn't anybody's, and if anybody writes it down as a favour somebody did me I'll say again that I told him the date first."

At about eleven she was in the hall off the parade with the woman of about forty-two who has the key to it, and she asked about the pound that had gone into the meter in the last week of June that nobody had seen go in.

The woman put the booking book down on the table and left her finger in the page.

"I put it in," she said.

"What?"

"The pound. I put it in. I've put a pound in that meter every week since the February, and I have never once seen anybody else put one in, and if somebody had I'd have known, because I lock up and the gas's off and the money's in it." She took her finger out of the page. "That's why nobody saw who. There was nobody to see. There hasn't been anybody but me putting money in that meter since the February and there will not be anybody but me, and nobody has ever asked me that, and I have never said it out loud in this room."

"You told the room on the fourteenth of July there had been a pound in it in the last week of June and nobody had seen who."

"No, I didn't. I told the room what I was told, and the woman who told us that wasn't in the room either, and that is not me passing it on, because I did not have a thing to pass on, I had nothing at all about that meter, and I repeated what was in front of me in the way everybody in a room repeats a thing." She looked at the meter through the door. "Now, if you want the date, I have not got the date. I do it on a Monday or I do it on a Thursday and it depends what day I have remembered it, and I have never once written it and neither has anybody else, and that is not a secret, that is just a meter."

She left the hall at about a quarter to twelve with two of the four ticked on the back of an envelope and the other two exactly as they had been, and she walked up to Carrow Lane at about half past two, and the woman who has been in the Methodist hall about two years was in the hall doing nothing in particular with the chairs stacked at the side.

She put the envelope on the chair beside her, folded, and she did not open it.

"I'm not going to go through these with you," she said. "You weren't there for any of them and neither was I and I have not come to find you out. I want to ask you one thing I was told by you and did not check."

"Go on."

"You told that room on the fourteenth of July that somebody had put a pound in the meter of that hall in the last week of June and that nobody had seen who." She said it carefully, and she watched her while she said it. "That's the only one of the four I've gone and looked at, and it's the one that isn't true, and I want to know how it got out of you, because it came out of a room and not out of a mouth."

The woman thought about it for longer than the question deserved.

"It came out of the room before that," she said. "You said it. On the second of July, in this hall, in the gap, when we were talking about the boiler. You said your gas was on and that you'd not put a pound in and that somebody had. And I said it out loud on the fourteenth because you weren't in the room that night and somebody had to say it, and I have not once said to you that I saw it, and you have not once said to me that I did."

"I didn't tell you it went in that week."

"You told me somebody had put money in. That's what you told me, and you were not in the room, and neither was the woman who told you, and none of that is anybody being wrong. It's a room with about nine people in it going round a thing in a room, and by the time it gets to the fourteenth it's a week and a date and a stranger's back yard, and nobody in the chain ever held it." She looked at the envelope on the chair. "That's the one that didn't hold. The other three I can go and check on a person who was there, and I have, haven't you."

"I have."

"Then you've three."

---

The man of about eighteen was in the doorway of the shop next door to the one with the trade counter at about five, in the place he stands on a Monday, and the woman of about fifty-five came down the parade with the carrier bag on her arm and the envelope in it and stopped in front of him.

"That's three of them," she said, "and the fourth one I cannot go and ask about, because the fourth one is a man who bought a length of chain and said it was for a gate and would not say which gate, and he has not been asked anything since the Sunday and he is not going to be asked by me either."

"He'd say a gate."

"He'd say a gate, and he'd have said a gate in July and he'd say a gate in September, and I am not going to walk down there and be the one who goes, because the day somebody goes down there and asks is the day there are two of us going."

"And the pound."

"The pound was me." She said it as flatly as she had said it in the hall. "It was me, once a week, since February, and nobody saw me because there was nobody to see, and I have been carrying a week of it about this borough in my head for six weeks and it was in my own meter the whole time." She shifted the bag. "There's nowhere for that to go."

He looked down the parade at the shutter that goes up and down, and the two of them stood there for a moment with the rain starting.

"No," he said.

**AND SHE PUT THE ENVELOPE IN THE CARRIER BAG WITH THE TWO PIECES OF PAPER IN IT AND PUT THE BAG ON THE SHELF UNDER THE WINDOW IN THE ROOM OVER THE SHOP AT THE END OF THE PARADE, AND HE WENT DOWN TO THE KITCHEN AT THE BACK, AND IT WAS A MONDAY AND THERE WERE DISHES IN IT.**
