# Chapter 0954 — Under The Bowed Board

Saturday the sixteenth of November 2024, at the deck boards at the back of number ten with the morning gone grey. Under a bowed board.

**THE DECK AT THE BACK OF NUMBER TEN IS LAID ON FOUR TIMBER JOISTS WITH ITS BOARDS RUNNING ACROSS THEM AND AN OPEN JOINT AT THE HEAD OF THE RUN, AND ONE BOARD HAS CUPPED SO THAT IT STANDS A QUARTER OF AN INCH OFF ITS JOIST ALONG THE MIDDLE, AND THE HAND GOES FLAT AND IS DRAWN ALONG THAT HEAD JOINT WITH THE NAILS NOT TOUCHING THE TIMBER, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE BOARD STANDS OFF AND WHAT IS IN THE RUN BENEATH IT.**

The deck was laid last spring by a man with a nail gun and a circular saw and four joists cut on a bench that was not level. The boards are softwood, an inch thick, with the width of a finger between each pair of them and a channel left open at the head of the run about six inches deep.

It is that channel that is open. Every board in the run stops six inches short of the house wall, and the heads of all of them stand in a line where the saw left them, and that line is the only place along the whole deck where a hand can get under it.

The hand is laid flat on the boards and drawn along the head joint with the nails kept just clear of the timber. So that anything standing a hair proud of the surface is caught on the nails before the skin has any idea it is there.

For the first two feet the nails ride the joint without finding anything at all. Then the arris of one board comes past under them and they drop into it a quarter of an inch and stop, and the whole of that quarter of an inch is the thickness of the cup in a board that has dried once since it went down.

Nothing on the surface of those boards is proud anywhere else. The rest of the run is as flat as a table and the nails go over it the length of the head joint without dropping once, which is what makes the one place where they drop worth the whole of the drawing.

Where the nails go in, the hand can turn down after them. The gap between that board and its neighbour opens along its length and does not open evenly, and the widest place in it is the middle of the span, and in there the space under the board comes up to meet the hand off the joist.

---

A board dries and shrinks across its own width before it does anything along its length, and a board that shrinks across its width while it is nailed down at both ends cannot get the width back. So it goes into a curve, which is called a cup, and it takes the cup because the ends are nailed and the middle is free.

A cupped board sits on a joist on its two edges and stands its cup clear of the top of that joist along the middle of the span. It is the one way a deck board ever stands up off its own support, and it is why a board that was tight in June can be felt with the heel of a hand in November.

The quarter of an inch in this one is not a large cup. It is about as much as a softwood board will take without splitting, and a board that has gone further than this has usually started to check at its ends.

That board has not checked. Its ends are cut square and sound and the grain in them is still pale, and the arris at the head of it is blunt and slightly furry where the drying has raised the fibres.

The deck is laid on joists and not on a solid frame because a deck is meant to go off for the winter and come back, and a frame laid straight on the ground would lay the ground against it all winter and take the air out of the space underneath. There is a ventilation gap of about an inch under the lowest board on the side nearest the wall, and that gap is the reason the frame under a deck does not rot while the ground under it does, and it is the reason nobody has anything to say about that deck at all.

---

The four joists are the ordinary softwood sawn and set into the old footings at each end, and they are pitched a sixteenth of an inch out of level because the bench was not level, which is not enough to be seen and enough to be found under a board with a cup in it. Each board is fixed to each joist with two nails about a foot apart, and the nail heads are all of them below the surface of the boards except two at the near end of the run, where a nail head has stood proud and gone rusty and would catch a cloth on the way past.

The near end of that run is where the deck steps down into the yard. And the step there is a board on edge fixed to the joist ends, and it is the one piece of the whole thing that has been touched since it went down, because somebody caught a shoe on it last winter and put it back where it had been.

A nail gun sets its depth by the pressure in its own canister, and on softwood in a cold month it drives a nail about a sixteenth further than the same nail by hand in a warm one. Two of the heads on that run are proud for exactly that reason and the rest of them are not, because the man went along the whole run a second time with the canister turned down and drove every nail on it again from the same place each time.

The channel at the head of the run has been the same depth of dust for a fortnight and the dust has a line of grit in it where the rain comes off the house wall and falls. The wall behind that channel is the older brick of the back of the house, and the lime between those bricks is gone hard and brittle and has begun to fall out of three joints on the lowest course.

Where the fingers go in after the nails, the first thing they find is the arris of the joist, which is a softwood arris and is not brittle at all but blunt and furry from the saw. Above that arris there is the quarter of an inch of nothing that the cup in the board has made, and beyond the joist the run opens out again and the whole of it is as dark as the inside of a box.

Nothing at all lives in that run and not a grain of grit has got into it, because a deck with a ventilated gap under it does not collect anything. And the air in it has been going in and out under that board twice a day since the spring. Her sister is standing on the kerb of the lane opposite, in a coat with her hands away inside the pockets, looking at the house and not coming up the path.

"There is a cat on the wall at the end of the lane."

"There has been a cat on that wall all summer."

"Then somebody has been feeding it."

"Somebody has been feeding it since the spring at any rate."

They stay on the kerb a while. The talk goes on to whether the woman next door has had her windows washed, and whether a man came at all or whether it was a girl from the town, and neither of them mentions the deck or the boards behind the house, and the hand was off that deck before she came off the kerb of the lane.

The channel at the head of the run is six inches deep and the four joists keep their sixteenth of an inch of slope, and nothing has been done to either of them since the deck went down last spring.

A quarter of an inch of nothing under the middle of that board, a fortnight of dust in the channel behind it, and four joists still under it in the places they were put. That is what is left there, assuming that nobody comes out with a plane and a cold chisel and takes a shaving off that board to make it lie down again.
