# Chapter 0955 — Behind The Bowed Sheet

Tuesday the nineteenth of November 2024, at the corrugated sheet on the wall of the bay with the yard behind her out of the light. Behind a bowed sheet.

**THE SHEET HAS BOWED AWAY FROM THE BATTEN BEHIND IT AT ONE CROSSING AND STANDS AN INCH OFF IT ACROSS A FOOT OF ITS WIDTH, AND THE FOUR FINGERS OF ONE HAND GO UP BEHIND IT AND ARE LET GO ONE AT A TIME, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR BACK THE BATTEN IS AND WHAT IS BEHIND THE BATTEN.**

The bay is open the whole length of its front, and the sheet is on the wall at the back of it in two courses, the lower of them running from the top of an old concrete dado to about the height of a person's shoulder.

The battens behind it are sawn softwood three inches by one and a half, run across the bay at close intervals, and each is fixed with three bolts, one at each end and one in the middle, which is enough for a length of that size if the wall behind it is true. The wall behind the battens is the outside face of the next building along, and it is out of true by about two inches over the height of that bay. It was built on a plate that had been stood on end in the wet for a fortnight, and every batten fixed straight across it has to be bent very slightly to follow that lean, and a batten bent like that stands off the wall in the middle of its own length.

The sheet is fixed to each batten with a bolt in every trough it crosses, which comes to four bolts across a sheet two and a half feet wide. A wall of this kind goes on that way, by one man working alone off a tower with a drill. None of that has moved. At the third crossing along the lower course the bolt is not in the batten. The hole in the sheet is there, and the hole is an oval a quarter of an inch longer than it was cut, and the bolt is hanging by its head on the outside with its shank lying loose in the trough against the sheet.

That bolt is where it dropped out of, and that hole is left exactly as the weather widened it.

---

The four fingers go up behind the sheet together, palm to the timber, and they go up as far as they will and then they stop being asked to stay there.

They are released one after another, the index first and the little finger last, so that the sheet comes back onto each of them in its turn instead of being pushed away by all four at once. A sheet that is standing off its batten is a stiff thing, and a stiff thing wants to keep its own shape, and it comes back onto a hand the way a board comes back onto a bench that has been lifted off it.

With all four in, the space behind the sheet reads as a space with no depth to it at all, because the four fingers take the whole of the gap between the sheet and the batten and there is nothing left for them to measure. With them let go, one at a time, each finger takes the same gap and gives it a different name, and the difference between what the first finger has and what the fourth has is the bow.

What the first finger finds is the sheet itself, sitting on it with no weight in it at all, because the sheet has been standing clear of that batten since the bolt came out and it has had nothing to sit on. The second finger finds the same sheet and a little more of the same nothing. By the fourth, the finger has come off the trough and onto the flat of the sheet, and the flat is where the bow is greatest.

That space is an inch or so at the centre of the trough and shuts to nothing at the arris of the trough either side, which is the shape of anything that has bowed and not of anything that has been drawn away. Something pulled off a batten stands clear along the whole reach of its trough. Something bowed stands clear in the middle and comes back onto the timber at the edges.

The batten behind is where the batten has always been. It is an inch and a half deep and its own arris at the edge of it is blunt and furry from the saw, and the gap between the top of it and the back of the sheet is a full inch along a foot of the run and shut at both ends of the crossing.

Behind the batten is the wall, and the wall there is a solid old brick one with a lime render gone hard on it, and the gap between the back of the batten and the face of that wall is a quarter of an inch at the middle and nothing at either end, because the batten has been drawn to the wall at its two bolts and has come off it in between. So there are two spaces and not one, and they are on opposite sides of three inches of sawn softwood.

A sheet of corrugated iron is stiff for its weight in a way that a flat sheet of the same thickness is not, because every one of its troughs is a fold, and a fold is the cheapest way there is of making a thing stiff. It is also the reason a sheet keeps a bow. A flat sheet of iron wants to lie down and a corrugated sheet wants to keep whatever curve it was given, and the curve it has here is the curve of a batten that has gone out of true.

The bolt that ought to be drawing that trough onto the batten is not in the batten. A hole worked in a sheet of iron goes oval first and loses the head of the bolt next, and then the head comes off and the bolt comes out, and no amount of looking at that hole will put it right.

The bolt in the trough is a cup head with a square neck under it, because that is what was in the box that day, and the neck is rusted round where it lies in the trough and the head is still bright on the crown of it where nothing has touched it. The sheet above that crossing is still tight on the same battens, and its four bolts a foot higher are all in, so the two courses have not come down together and the course above is doing nothing at all about the one below.

What is behind the batten at that place is a wall, and what is on the wall is eleven courses of brick with a lime render on the face of it gone hard and gone pale, standing a hair off the brick at one place in every four feet along the run. The woman who books work is crossing the yard towards the mouth of the bay with the two doors standing open against the wall on either side of it. She stops a few feet out and looks at the strip light going along the roof of the bay, What passes between them is whether the landlord has put that yard up to be let, and whether it has gone, and whether the woman on the desk gave a date or did not.

The whole of it is about a letting, and not one word of it was put to anybody with that wall in front of them, and the hand was out of the gap and at her side before she was within ten feet of it. The bay is open and the yard is open and the strip light inside is burning away overhead, and the woman who books work goes on out through the far side of the yard and the bay goes on being a bay with a sheet on the back wall of it.

The four fingers that went up behind that sheet went up as four and came down as four, and the sheet between them took its own weight back onto the batten it had been standing clear of, and the crossing of the batten and the trough is the crossing of a batten and a trough.

Something that size makes no noise at all when a finger is let go against it, and the whole of that crossing was arrived at without one, on getting at the back of that batten by all four fingers at once and not by the first.
