# Chapter 0708 — The Bucket On The Yard Slab

Monday the seventh of March 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number nine from first light until dark and the whole of the day is a bucket carried and a stool looked at.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES SCRUBS A YARD SLAB WITH A BUCKET, LOOKS AT AN ASH STOOL IN HER FRONT ROOM WITH NOTHING UNDER IT, AND SPEAKS AT HER OWN DOOR TO HER SISTER ABOUT FELT AND ABOUT A DRIPPING TAP.**

She is a woman in her late fifties. She is at home all day. Her sister is at her own door at the middle of the day. She speaks to her at the door.

---

She starts with the yard before there is any light in it.

The bucket is under the sink with the scrubber in it, bristles worn, handle smooth. She fills it at the kitchen tap, watching the cold run clear, and carries it out in both hands, weight steady, and sets it down on the slab by the back door and scrubs the green off the stone in rounds. The back door shuts true behind her, with the card at the hinge she fixed herself in March. The water is cold and the slab comes clean in a half moon round where she kneels. She tips the dirty water to the drain and fills the bucket fresh and does the next slab.

While the water is out she thinks about other water. There is a bowl under a bath tap at number twelve, emptied at first light and at dark, and she has never seen it and knows it by heart, because her sister told her in December and in January and in February, and because a bowl that fills twice over is a thing anybody can picture.

---

She comes in at the middle of the morning and wipes the top of the fridge.

The six pads are on the fridge in a row, edges square, pencil alongside. She takes the first one and wipes the top in long passes, corner to corner, and crosses the first line twice with the pencil on the pad, blunt line over blunt line. The fire in the front room is unlit since Easter. The sixty-watt bulb is in the ceiling of that room since the seventh of December. She goes to the doorway of the front room with the cloth in her hand and looks in without going in, taking the shape of it: window, stool, chair, settee, bulb, cold fire. She has looked at it this way since Easter, and looking has become a habit with a shape of its own, doorway, cloth, look, away.

The ash stool is in the front room opposite the window, three oak and one ash, with the space under it empty since the fifth of November. Chair one side and settee other and the stool between them where a table would be if anybody sat there. Nobody sits there. She dusts the top of it with the cloth and does not move it.

---

She eats at the middle of the day with the bucket outside.

She puts bread and soup on the kitchen table and eats standing up with one hand round the bowl, steam on her face, spoon slow. The six pads sit on the fridge with the first crossed twice, pencil line gone over pencil line. She looks at them while she eats, at the blunt pencil beside the pad, at the clean fridge top showing where she wiped. Crossing the first twice was January, pencil blunt, line gone over, wrist cold. She has not crossed another since. The pad waits. The pencil waits. Both sit where she left them.

She thinks about the stool while she eats, ash opposite the window with nothing under it since the fifth of November. She dusted it before the soup and did not move it. Moving it would mean finding where to set it, and the room is set as it is, chair one side and settee other, and she does not move what is set.

---

Her sister comes at the middle of the day with a bit of shopping in a bag.

They stand at the door with the door wide and the bucket on the slab behind them, scrubber floating, water going cold. The sister looks at the clean half moons on the stone and at the dirty water in the drain, at the green still lying in the grooves where the brush will not reach.

"You have been at it," she says.

"I have been at the slabs," she says. "Green off the stone. It comes back if you leave it."

They go in to the kitchen and put the kettle on, flame blue under white enamel, and wait for the singing. The sister puts the shopping down on the worktop, bag rustling, and looks at the six pads on the fridge with the first line crossed twice, at the blunt pencil, at the clean top.

"You still cross them," she says.

"I cross them. Six pads and the first crossed twice. It is the only thing in this house that gets crossed."

---

They drink the tea standing up with their hands round the cups.

"How is the shed," she says.

"Felt gone at the front edge. Ply in the drawer. Tape with four inches left. Paper with three lines. Draught off the top of it. The coal man told me again."

"What did you say."

"What I always say."

The woman in her late fifties looks at her sister over the cup.

"You put no day on it."

"I put no day on it. Naming one would mean asking for hands, and asking would mean saying the job out loud in a warm kitchen, and I am not saying it, because saying it makes it mine to carry and I carry enough."

---

They go to the door after, to look at the sky.

The sister stands on the step and looks down the road where the gate lamp burns at number twelve, though it cannot be seen in daylight. She has never gone up the road, not once, and her sister walked it once on the seventeenth of December with a step ladder on her shoulder and knocked once and has never gone back.

"There is a bowl," the sister says. "Under a bath tap. White china. I empty it when I think about it and I think about it whenever I run a tap."

"I know the bowl," she says. "Emptied at first light and at dark. Full again between."

"Could nobody mend it."

"Somebody could. The cistern weeps at the far side of a joist and there is nobody to keep the frame while she goes up. The tap drips and there is nobody to sit with the bowl while it is off. So the water keeps coming."

---

The sister picks up the bag to go.

"Did you ask the man next door," she says. "That borrows."

"I did not ask him," she says. "I took a step ladder out of an open porch two doors down and was asked at my own door by a man from that house whose it was and said I did not know, and I did not ask the man next door that I was told borrows, and I told you I cannot put back what I cannot give, and you carried it away. Asking him now would mean saying the first half out loud, and I am not saying it."

The sister nods, because sisters know when a thing is finished.

---

She pegs a rug on the line after, though the air is cold.

The rug is small and square and she pegs it by two corners and knocks the dust out of it with her palm. The dust comes off in a cloud and settles on the clean slabs. She looks at the dust on the clean stone and fetches the bucket and rinses it off with a cup of water. The slab comes clean again in a half moon. She tips the cup back in the bucket.

The sister watches from the door with her arms wrapped round her.

"You cannot win," the sister says.

"I can win for an hour," she says. "Green off the stone. Dust off the rug. That is an hour."

---

They walk round the yard together with the bag in the sister's hand.

The sister looks at the drain where the water goes and at the wall where the damp shows. She does not go up the road and does not look up it. She has never gone up it. The lamp at number twelve burns where it burns and cannot be seen from here in daylight. Both know it is on.

"Sixty watt," the sister says, nodding at nothing. "Cold room."

"Cold room," she says. "Nothing under the stool. Fire unlit. It waits."

"And the bowl waits," the sister says. "Round and white."

"Aye. Emptied at first light and at dark. Full again between. Leaving water alone costs something seen."

---

She folds the washing at the end of the day on the kitchen table.

The basket is full from the line, cold and damp at the edges, smelling of outside air. She folds each thing in squares and sets the squares in a pile, sleeves flat, corners true, smoothing each square with her palm. The pile grows, white and grey and blue, neat as a shop stack. She sets her palm flat on the top of it when it is done and feels the cold come through to her skin. Clean and cold and hers. The bucket sits on the yard slab outside with clean water going cold. She leaves it while she folds, because folding wants two hands and the water waits.

The sister lifts the top square and looks at the fold.

"True," the sister says.

"True," she says. "Green off the stone. Dust off the rug. Washing folded. That is the day."

---

She spends the end of the day in the front room doorway with the cloth in her hand.

She looks at the ash stool opposite the window with nothing under it and at the sixty-watt bulb in the ceiling and at the cold fire with the ash grey in it. The room has been cold since Easter and will be cold in the spring, curtains thin, glass cold to the palm, because a cold room is a thing that waits without costing anybody but her. The bucket is on the yard slab with clean water in it going cold. The fridge has six pads with the first crossed twice. The back door shuts true.

She tips the bucket to the drain at dark, watching the grey water run, and sets it back under the sink with the scrubber in it, bristles down. She puts her hands round a cold cup at the kitchen table, palms to the china, and thinks about the bowl under the tap on the other side of the borough, filling round and white in an empty bathroom, waiting where it was not meant to stay, and about how leaving water alone costs something seen, a green slab, a cold room, a full bowl, and nobody is asked why, not once.
