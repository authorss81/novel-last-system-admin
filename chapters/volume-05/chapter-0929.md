# Chapter 0929 — Under The Lowest Washer

Tuesday the thirtieth of July 2024, at the bolted apron of the lock-up bay with the yard empty behind her. Beneath a stack of three.

**SHE HAS A BOLT STANDING OUT OF THE APRON AT THE LOCK-UP WITH THREE WASHERS STACKED ON IT AND SHE MEANS TO HANG THE FINGERS DOWN INTO THE GAP UNDER THE LOWEST WASHER WITH THE HAND KEPT LEVEL AND THE WRIST UNBENT, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THAT GAP RUNS AND WHAT THE LOWEST WASHER IS STANDING ON.**

Three washers on one bolt is a stack, and a stack goes on for a reason; this one has been on that apron since the shutter box was set up, has come off and been put back twice since, and has not shifted an eighth of an inch, and neither has the bolt it stands on. The whole of the assembly is set out of true and takes that angle as one piece.

It is a bolt about half an inch round standing an inch and a quarter out of the concrete, and the three washers on it are pressed steel, the largest at the foot and the smallest on top.

A washer is a ring, and a ring on a bolt takes only the weight of whatever is screwed to it, and what is standing under the lowest one is a gap she wants the depth of, so the fingers hang down beneath it with the wrist kept unbent. The steel is cool and slick across its face and gritty round the rim, and under it the apron has the warmth of the afternoon still in it.

The hand hangs and the fingers find a floor that is not concrete. The lowest washer stands on a pad of grit packed solid enough to take the print of a knuckle, and the pad is a quarter of an inch thick and spread wider than the washer.

The washer does not move when the hand presses it. It is packed, not resting, and the packing is between the washer and the apron over the whole width of its face.

---

The pad is the grit the broom cannot reach. The apron is swept every week and the washer stands in a ring of swept ground with a collar of dust round it, and what gets under the washer stays under it and is pressed down by every foot that crosses that corner.

A washer packed off its seat is a washer that is not doing its job. The washer exists to spread a load over a wide face, and standing on a third of an inch of grit it spreads that load over the grit and not over the concrete, which is the same amount of spreading as no washer at all. Give it three or four years and the grit under a washer goes to a hard cake, and the cake goes down into the surface of the concrete, and the washer then sits in a dip that will not close.

An ordinary washer of that size does not go anywhere much. It takes about a quarter inch out of level in a dozen years and then stops, because it has found the aggregate and is resting on it. This one has found its aggregate and has gone a sixteenth further into the apron than it should have, which is what four seasons of sweeping grit under a hot sun will do to a rim that is already turned. The apron outside that bay falls from the bay mouth towards the kerb, about one in forty, so that everything the bay throws out of the door runs down the apron and not under the door. Water off the roof above the shutter box goes down its own pipe and into a hopper, and the hopper drains through a grate set in the apron six feet from the bolt, which is the only reason that bolt is where it is.

Six feet from a drain is far enough for that bolt to stay dry all the life it has left. Three feet from it would have drowned the same bolt every winter for thirty years and would have it rusted through at the root now.

What is left of that bolt shows no rust at all. The thread under the washers is black with its own oil and bright on the crests, and the shank below the concrete has never seen the weather. The shank is set in the concrete at the time the slab was poured and not drilled in afterwards, which is why it leans a degree and a half out of true where a drilled bolt would be straight.

Three washers on a bolt is a stack, and a stack is put on for a reason and the reason is nine times in ten to take up the space something else needed. This bolt carries nothing now; it carried the shutter box's guide and it carries the bolt that keeps the guide to the wall. The guide itself is on the wall above at head height, and the bolt at the apron is the end of the strap that used to come down and go across the bottom of the guide so the shutter could not be lifted off its channel. A strap that goes across the bottom of a guide is a thing to be taken off eventually, and it was, and its bolt was left in the slab because a bolt in a slab is not a thing anybody takes out.

Washing steel rings on a bolt, two or three of them, are the usual answer to a slack strap. The slack comes back as soon as the ring is packed, and packing takes one winter with a broom and a grate and no attention whatever. Under the lowest ring sits a pad that could be lifted out with a chisel and would have to be put back with one, and no chisel goes near it this year. Grit worked into a joint under a washer packs in layers, coarse at the bottom and fine on top, and the layers can be read off the edge of it the way a section of ground can be read.

Coarse at the bottom means the coarse went there first, which means the pad began as a fall of coarse grit off the apron and not as something pushed under the ring. Fine on top means the sweeping went on after the coarse had stopped coming. The boundary between them is a soft line about a sixteenth from the top of the pad, and where the line is the pad is slackest, which is where a knife point would go in if anything went in.

Nothing goes in, and nothing has gone in there yet. What the line marks is the end of a summer three years back when the grate was lifted and the grit came out of it with the water, and the washer rode back down onto whatever was left of the pad underneath. The washer on top is the smallest and it has never been off in her time, and it has rust freckles round the rim where the damp stands in the spout of the bolt head. Where a damp stands, a steel ring goes orange along its outer third, and the ring below it stands at a quarter of an inch with grit under it, and the apron under the whole of it is sound to the depth of the slab and shows nothing on it at all.

The washing woman came to the office door from inside while her hand was still clear of the ring, and stood in the door with a docket folded in her fist and did not put it down. Docket in her fist, she stood in the door a moment and looked down at the apron, then up at the shutter box, then down at the apron again, and then she turned the docket over once in her fist and went back inside and left the door open behind her.

What went into words was a Friday, and whether Fridays were taken at the small window now, and that they were. Nothing of the three washers and the pad under them went into any of it.

The grit under the lowest ring has been packed there since the grate was lifted and it will go on standing on its own account, and the ring above it has never come off in her time.

The three washers are where they were stacked, the lowest one on its pad of packed grit a sixteenth into the apron, and the ground round it has been swept bare since the spring.
