# Chapter 0990 — She Put Her Own Name Down

Undated in this draft.

**THE PAPER HAS BEEN OFF THE WALLS OF THE BACK ROOM AT NUMBER NINE FOR SOME TIME.**

The back room of number nine is the room she was born in and it has been empty long enough to have lost the shape of a bedroom without losing the marks of one. The paper came off in strips and the strips came off with the size of the paper rather than with the edges of anything, so that the plaster under it is a patchwork of pale and a dozen different shades of glue stain, and one corner still has a length of paper on it about the width of a hand with the pattern of a rose left in the corners of it.

The picture hooks are still in. There are nine of them along the two long walls at about the height of a tall woman's eye, and three more along the end wall, and they stand out of the plaster on their bent wire because the skim that used to be round them has gone at the edges, so that each one has a bare ring of old lime round it about the width of a shilling and a shadow kept under the head of it. Somebody has gone round the room and knocked them back with the side of a hand, so that they all lean the same way and look like twelve bent fingers pointing at the door.

Underfoot the boards are bare and the old hearth rug has gone. They are polished pale in a strip from the doorway across to where the window light comes in and dark along the skirting, and they run out of true with the joists under them by the width of a shilling and a half from end to end, which in a floor this size means about two inches at the far corner, and you can see it from the doorway because the boards are bare and the light comes in flat and low across them.

---

She was in there before he came up the passage, which he saw at once, because the front room door was standing open and there was nobody in the front room and nobody in the passage either.

"You've come about the front room."

"I've come to tell you what the breast will cost to do properly, which is a fitter and about three times what you charged me in not-my-money, and I have got the number for you."

"Thank you." She was on her knees at the far end with a cloth and a tin. "Put it on the table in there. I'll read it after I've done this corner."

He put the sheet on the table in the front room and came back and stood in the doorway of the back room with the frame against his shoulder, because there is a limit to how long a man can stand in a room where somebody is down on their knees with a cloth, and the frame is where that limit is. He looked at the wall instead, at the patchwork of pale and glue stain, and at the one strip of paper with a rose still on it.

"I'm on it," she told him. "I've been on it since the day you asked me if I wanted to go." Then she sat back on her heels, put the cloth in the tin and looked at him, and she did it without hurrying, and she had been getting ready to do it since before he was at the door.

---

"You came to the house and asked me a question," the woman told him, "and when I wouldn't answer it you let me put my name on a piece of paper with a pencil off my own shelf. I have been thinking about that for two days."

"I remember."

"I did it on purpose. I want you to know I did it on purpose, because you came away thinking you'd done something for me and you hadn't." She put the tin on the floor beside her knee. "That sheet the woman brought to number twelve — that's the one that goes to people living on their own. I know that now and I didn't know it when you stood at my table. You asked me if I wanted to go, and there was no answer to that you were entitled to, and I gave you a pencil instead, and you stood there and let me."

"I did let you."

"And you've come back twice since, and both times you've begun by telling me what something costs. You know what I've been doing the whole time you've been learning how to ask? Learning how to be asked."

She stood up off her knees and put her hand on the wall for it, not because she needed to. "I have been on this earth sixty-one years and I have been asked for a thing about twice. Once for money, once for a form. And I've been asked nothing at all about whether I want anything, ever, by anybody, including you."

He stood in the doorway of that room and took it, because there was nothing else to do with it, and it went into him further than the front room breast and further than the flag step outside number two, because the flag step was a morning and this was a fact about himself that he had been carrying for ten years and had never once looked at.

"You're right," he told her. "I asked you a question I had no business asking and then I treated the answer as though you'd answered it. I did that because you didn't answer, and because you didn't answer I got to think I'd asked well. That's the whole of what I did."

"You did."

"I'll do it properly, then. Properly means I'll ask you a thing and then I'll leave it, and whatever you do afterwards is yours and I don't come back and ask you how it went."

"No."

"No?"

"Not yet."

"Ask me when you've something of your own at stake, because a man with nothing to lose will ask anybody anything at all, and he'll feel he's done you a favour into the bargain. You've had nothing at stake since the day I met you. You've turned up, you've done a bit of work, you've not charged me, and you've gone down the path. That's a good way for you. It's a poor way for me and I'd not have taken it from anybody else."

"That's fair."

"I know it's fair. It's just not enough." She went out past him into the passage with the tin under her arm and he heard it go on the shelf in the kitchen, and from the doorway she went on with it. "You've been asking me what I want for two days and I've been giving you a different answer each time to see whether you'd catch it. I don't want to be kept, if that's the size of it. I've been kept. Being kept is a room with a cold grate in it and a stopped clock and a clean carpet and nobody asking you anything. If I go up the river, I come back, and if I've been up the river for six weeks and I've come back, then I have been somewhere, and I am a woman who has been somewhere, and this time when somebody wants a pair of hands up the river they can ask me and I can go."

"Put your name down and be found without anybody asking you," he told her. "That is what you told me the other day and I thought I had understood it and I hadn't at all."

"That was true when I gave it to you. It isn't the whole of it either, and the whole of it is worse for me, because I would rather be asked and refused than entered and never asked, and I have been entered my whole life and never once asked, and I'd have gone up the river on Monday if somebody had stood in this kitchen and put it to me, and I would have gone, and I would have taken it from the wrong man in the wrong hour for no reason at all except that nobody had ever thought to ask me."

She came back into the doorway with her hands damp from the tin and stood on the boards with the light going across them low and flat.

"There were two places," she told him. "She read them out to me when she came the second time and one of them is me and one of them is the woman at number ten. Neither line is filled."

"You've told her you'll go?"

"I've told her nothing and I won't, and that's not a game, that's because there is a difference between a woman deciding to go and a woman being thanked for going, and I'm not spending the only thing I've got on being thanked." She went back into the room and knelt down again at the corner. "Go down to number ten. That's yours to do, and you've not done it, and she's told me the same thing about you that I told you about myself."

He got as far as the front door and then stood in the passage of a house where he had done two hours' work for nothing and had been told, by two people in two days, that not-nothing is not a currency anybody else can spend. The bare boards of that back room are two inches down at the far corner against the joists underneath them, which is a great deal of floor to be wrong about by, and there is nothing on that floor to catch the light except the shadows of twelve bent picture hooks all leaning the same way towards the door, and she is still in the corner with the cloth and the tin.
