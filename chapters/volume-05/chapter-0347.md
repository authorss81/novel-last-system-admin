# Chapter 0347 — Nobody's in That House

Wednesday the twenty-third of August 2017, from about ten in the morning until about twenty past seven in the evening, a hall on a meter, a shop off a parade, and the top of a lane, in Tideglass.

**A MAN SAID IN A HALL ON A SUNDAY THAT A HOUSE AT THE TOP OF A LANE IS EMPTY, THE WOMAN WHO HEARD IT SAID IT ON THE WEDNESDAY TO A MAN COMING OFF A SHIFT AND MEANT A DIFFERENT LANE, AND ON FRIDAY THAT MAN WALKED FOUR HUNDRED YARD FURTHER THAN HE DID THE WEEK BEFORE FOR NO REASON HE COULD PUT A WORD TO.**

The hall is on a meter and there are three mornings a week when it is open, and this was one of them. Somebody had been in early and taken the tables off their brackets and had them out by ten, because there was something on that afternoon. The man in his sixties had a cup and went and sat in the split seat at the end with the high back.

The woman who works in a shop was in at about half past ten. She goes to that hall on a Sunday about once in three weeks and has done since the winter, and she was in early that morning because the shop does not open on a Sunday and there is nothing else to be done with a Sunday.

The weather on that Sunday was the weather it had been all that month, which is too warm in the middle of the day and too cold by four o'clock, and there was a wind off the land and the door of that hall was propped with a wedge of wood about four inch high that has been there since February.

"They've emptied that house," said the man in his sixties.

"What house."

"The one at the top of the lane, going up off the side of the road past the place with the ironmonger's boards out front." He turned his cup round on his knee. "There's a woman there was in it on her own for years and she's gone, and there are boards on the front windows and a man in a high-visibility had a look at it with a tape in the middle of July, and there's a for sale board up and it came down again, and I would say she died or she went into something, and I would say whoever owns it has had it empty since about the middle of August."

"That's not a bad guess," said the woman who works in a shop.

"It's not a guess at all, it's what the windows say," said the man in his sixties. "I've been up that lane twice since July and I've not been past the top of it since the middle of August because there is nothing at the top of it."

He described the lane, because he describes things. It comes off a road about a quarter of a mile back from that parade, it is not a made-up lane but a road that a road used to run on, and there are no pavements on it and there is a kerb on one side only. The ironmonger's boards are the length of a delivery van and they lean against the front of that shop at about knee height with the prices in chalk on them. Past the boards the lane goes up for about a hundred and fifty yard and there are four houses on the left and two on the right and the last one on the right is set back off the road by about thirty foot with a front garden that has not been done since March.

"She was in that house about seven years," said the man in his sixties. "I knew she was in it because I saw her at that window most days in the winter with a cardigan on and a cup. And now there is nothing in it and there is a woman in that shop on the corner who tells me the owner is somebody in a city and won't answer the phone."

Nobody at that table said anything else about it. The man in his forties was not in that hall that morning and has not been since the middle of August, and there was no fourth person in that room that day who had ever been up that lane, and the man who does that hall was behind the urn and had the radio on low and had not heard any of it.

---

She was in the shop off that parade all day on the Wednesday, from about half past eight until about five to six, with the shutter going up at eight and coming down at about half past three the way it does, and there was a wind off the water in the afternoon that put the door open twice and made the bell on the back of it go.

The man who works nights came in at about a quarter to six, which he does about twice a week, and he had a carrier bag in his hand with his lunch things in it that he had carried home off the last shift, and she was on the counter with the till open in front of her.

"You've been up the lane past the ironmonger's boards," she said.

"I've been up it twice this month."

"It's empty."

"Which house," said the man who works nights.

"The one at the top."

He put the carrier bag down on the counter and looked at her for a second.

"Which house at the top," he said.

"The one at the very top," said the woman who works in a shop. "It was a woman on her own in it for years and she has gone, and there are boards on the front windows and a for sale board went up in July and came down again, and whoever owns it has had it empty since the middle of August. He was in that hall on Sunday telling me about it."

"Right," said the man who works nights.

He paid for a length of pipe and he did not say one word about which road that lane comes off, and she did not say one word about which road it comes off either, because she does not know and she has never been up it and she was not interested in it except that a man in his sixties had been talking about it for a quarter of an hour on Sunday morning and she had been the only person in that room listening.

She was wrong, and she was wrong by a few hundred yard and not by anything at all in the way she said it. The house the man in his sixties meant is at the top of a lane off a road three streets back from that parade, and the man who works nights has walked that lane twice this month. The lane she put it on is the one behind the parade that the three of them go down to the bus on, and it has seven houses in it, and the last of them has a family in it and a car on the drive and a light on over the door, and there has never been a board on a window in that lane in her life.

The man who works nights does not know that she is wrong. He has never once put a thing anybody has told him in a shop right and he is not going to start, and he is not going to ask her which road, because a man who asks which road a lane comes off has admitted that he does not know which road it comes off, and he has a lock-up on the Friday of that week and he was going to be at it either way.

---

He had used that lane for about three years and he had never told anybody he used it.

It is not much of a saving. It is about two hundred yards off the way at the end of a shift, and the footway is the whole way where the main road has standing water in the corner past the second bus stop that has been standing there since the summer, and the main road is lit and the lane is not lit past the ironmonger's boards, and he has never once met anybody in it, which he put down to the hour.

On the Friday after that he came off at about ten to six and stood in the dark at the mouth of it with his bag on his shoulder for about a minute and a half, and then he went up the main road instead, which is four hundred yards longer and takes about four minutes more, and he has gone the long way every night since, which is now about five weeks, and he could not tell anybody a single reason for it if he was asked.

There is a house at the top of that lane with a woman in it and a car on the drive and a light over the door, and she has been there the whole time, and there are no boards on those windows, and nobody in this borough is going to walk up that lane and put her right.

The man in his sixties is still in that hall twice a week with a cup, and he thinks of himself as a man who told somebody in this borough about a house that got emptied, and he did, and it was not an empty house, and he has no way of finding out that it is not and would not go and look if he had.

**A WOMAN REPEATED A SENTENCE ON A WEDNESDAY EVENING WITHOUT BEING ASKED FOR IT AND PUT THE PLACE A FEW HUNDRED YARD OUT, AND A MAN WHO WORKS NIGHTS HAS GONE A QUARTER OF A MILE FURTHER EVERY NIGHT FOR FIVE WEEKS BECAUSE OF IT, AND NOBODY HAS ASKED EITHER OF THEM ANYTHING.**
