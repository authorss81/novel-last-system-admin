# Chapter 0333 — Nobody Asked Him Anything

Monday the third of July 2017, from about half past seven in the morning until about seven in the evening, and in that order, a kitchen at the back of a parade, a greengrocer's off it, a house at the end of Marsh Lane, a first-floor corridor where the water comes in, and a hall off the parade, in Tideglass.

**FOUR OF THE FIVE ROOMS HE WENT INTO THAT DAY HAD SOMEBODY IN THEM, AND NOT ONE PERSON IN ANY OF THEM PUT A SINGLE TASK TO HIM, AND HE SAID SOMETHING OUT LOUD IN THE LAST ROOM, IN FRONT OF THREE PEOPLE, ABOUT A PERSON WHO WAS NOT THERE, AND NOBODY ANSWERED IT.**

He washes up in that kitchen four mornings a week and has done since about the middle of last year, and he is not on a list of anything and has never been asked to be on one, and there is no sheet of paper anywhere with his name at the top of it.

On the Monday morning of the last week in May he stood in that kitchen at half past seven with a cloth in his hand and nobody in it, and everything in it had been done and dried off and the man who cooks in there had walked out at about nine the previous night without leaving so much as a switch. That Monday there is a man sitting at the end of the bench with a cup and the radio turned down low, and there is a plate in the sink as well as the two things, and the man does not look up and does not speak and does not move his feet out of the way, and the lad washes the plate and the wooden spoon and the knife and puts them away and then stands at the sink with the cloth for about a quarter of an hour.

Nobody hands him anything. Nobody tells him where anything goes, and he knows where everything goes, and at about ten past eight the man at the end of the bench got up with his own cup and put it in that sink and went to work, and the cloth was still in the lad's hand.

The greengrocer's on that parade keeps its shutters up for the opening hour, and the woman behind that counter is on the telephone with her back to the whole shop for about twenty minutes, and a ladder is against the window and there is no lifting to be done anywhere in it.

At about twenty to twelve a man brought a sack of leeks in over his shoulder and set it down on the counter and said, to the middle of the room, that they were for the shop and were going on the account, and the woman said right and the man went out again.

The sack stood where he had put it for two hours and nobody in that shop asked anybody else to shift it. It is not heavy, the counter is about twelve foot, and there was clear space at the end of it, and it was not in anybody's way because everybody in that shop walks down the middle.

The woman came off the telephone at about ten past twelve, put a hand under the sack and carried it into the cold room herself, and then said, to a man who was not him, "Two and four," and that was the whole of the talking in that shop for the rest of the morning.

He was in the house at the end of Marsh Lane at about half past one and the man who is in that house four nights a week was in the front room with a newspaper.

"Morning."

"Morning."

The rest of that afternoon in that house consisted of a cup washed in that kitchen and put back on the hook it hangs on, and then at about four the same cup washed again and put back on the same hook, and about half an hour of a chair with a man in a newspaper in another room.

He was in the passage of that building by the water at about eight with a bucket and a mop, and the stop tap in that passage is shut from the last evening of each week until somebody turns it, and the board was back in its case and the case was shut, so somebody had gone down there before him and had gone back up again and had said nothing about it, which is the arrangement in that building and always has been.

The floor of that first-floor corridor has had nothing on it to mop since before Christmas. He went the length of the mop with the bucket anyway and turned round, and stood at the head of the outside stair for a while with his hands in his pockets.

At about a quarter to ten the woman of about fifty-five came out of the far room carrying two empty jugs, one in each hand, went past him and down that flight, and shut the street door at the bottom, and came back up at about ten past ten with both of them full and a kettle hanging off her other arm.

A bowl has been standing under the wet strip at the end of that corridor since the middle of June, and the person the water is coming in on empties it four or five times a day, and the woman in the room next door along goes past that bowl twice a day herself and has never once said a word about it.

He did not speak to her on that stair this time. He spoke to her once, in May, and she did not answer, and he carried it round with him for a fortnight, and he was not going to do that a second time.

He went along the corridor again at about half past ten and then left the bucket and the mop in the bin store at the head of that building and went out into the street.

The hall off that parade is a room that people put money in for the hour, and the woman of about forty-two holds the key to it, and at about half past six on that Monday the urn had been on for a while and there were two cups drying on a cloth beside it, and the table down the middle was laid for four, and there was no place at it for him and nobody had told him there was not.

The man in his forties was at the end nearest the door with a cup. The man who works nights was standing up in his coat by the door, as he is. The woman with the key was at the far end with her back to that window.

He put his bag down beside the counter and stood there, and nobody in that room said one word to him, which is the ordinary condition of that room.

At about ten to seven he said it, across the length of the table, in a voice that was not low, and the other two could both hear it.

"The woman next door along in that building by the water," he said. "I have been in that building every Monday since the middle of January and I have seen that woman in that corridor every Monday of that time, and I have not set eyes on her since a Wednesday nineteen days ago. She goes past that bowl twice a day. She is the only other person on that landing and she has been in and out of that door in front of me for seven months, and in seven months the two of them have not said one word to each other, and I have been on that landing the whole time and I have not worked out whether they are not speaking or whether they never were."

Nobody in that room answered him.

The man in his forties put his cup down on the cloth. The woman with the key had a hand flat on the table and neither of them moved. The man who works nights looked at the door for a second and then back at the table.

Then the woman with the key said that it was a great deal to have noticed about two people, and the man in his forties said, "It is," and that closed it, and she put the two cups away in the cupboard and the man who works nights said he had a shift at seven and went, and the meter went and the chairs went up on the wall.

He got the bag up off the boards at ten to seven and was through that door and out onto the pavement inside two minutes.

---

He walked it. A little over two miles of road between that parade and his own front door, and he has covered it upwards of seventy times since Christmas, and no one in this borough has ever offered him a lift or asked whether he has got money for it, and nobody has any idea what two miles takes him at the end of a day like that one, and nobody is going to be told.

The bucket and the mop were still in that bin store at the head of the parade when the lad came back out at half past four to fetch his own things, because nobody in that building goes into that bin store and nobody in that building would have said anything about them if they had.

What he said in that room is still in that room. The three people who heard it are not going into it, and the one who spoke said a thing that was a way of setting it down, and the one who said a word meant nothing by the word, and none of that is a finding and none of it is the answer to anything, and it is an ordinary Monday in the first week of July.

**HE WALKED THE TWO MILES HOME WITH NOTHING IN HIS HANDS, AND A MAN PUT A SACK OF LEEKS ON A COUNTER AT TWENTY TO TWELVE AND WENT OUT AGAIN, AND A BOWL HAS BEEN EMPTIED FOUR OR FIVE TIMES A DAY UNDER A STRIP OF WET PAPER AT THE END OF A FIRST-FLOOR CORRIDOR SINCE THE MIDDLE OF JUNE.**
