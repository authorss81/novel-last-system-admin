# Chapter 0948 — Down The Open Section

Monday the twenty-first of October 2024, at the gate stile of the lock-up yard with the flags dry since the morning. Down an open section.

**THE END STILE OF THE YARD GATE AT THE LOCK-UP IS A LENGTH OF HOLLOW STEEL SECTION STANDING WITH ITS TOP OPEN AND A BACKING PLATE WELDED ACROSS THE BOTTOM OF IT, AND THE SHUT HAND GOES DOWN INTO THAT TOP AND OPENS OUT INSIDE AGAINST THE FAR WALL, TO FIND OUT HOW BIG THE SECTION IS AND WHAT IS AT THE BOTTOM OF IT.**

The gate was made in this yard about nine years ago out of the sections that were on the rack then. And the two stiles are the strongest thing that was on the rack, which is four-inch square hollow section with a wall about a quarter of an inch thick and nothing whatever on the top of either of them.

The stile at the hinge end was capped with a piece of flat bar. The stile at the far end was not, and the reason is in the yard rather than in the gate: one cap was made, for a length of scaffold tube, and it went on the end that was done first, and there was never a second piece of flat bar cut for the other end, and nobody has come back to it since.

The gate leaf is six feet wide and four feet high and is hung on the hinge stile with a strap and a spindle. So that the whole weight of the leaf comes down the near face of that stile and nothing at all comes down the open one except the latch, and the latch is hooked.

A leaf like that is braced with a diagonal from the bottom of the hinge stile to the top of the open one, and the diagonal is why the square of that gate has stayed a square for nine years instead of going into a parallelogram on its own weight. A square gate with a brace across it is a triangle with three sides that have begun to sag, which is a shape that stays.

There are two gates in this yard and the other one is not like this. The other one is a timber gate hung on two oak stiles with a padlock through a hasp, and it was put up before the war and it has a different way of swinging, which is the way of a heavy leaf on a small spindle.

The hand goes down into that opening shut, palm in, and then opens out inside it against the far wall. So that the size of the section comes off where the hand stops opening rather than off the looking down at it.

A hand shut is about three inches across the knuckles and a hand open is nearer eight. And this section takes the open hand three-quarters of the way and stops it. With the thumb and the first finger a full inch short of meeting and the little finger bent back where it finds the corner of the section.

Inside, the four walls stand close enough together that most of the spread of the hand has to be given up to get at them, and what is under it is a scale of rust a sixteenth of an inch thick in the corners and thin on the flats.

The arris at that open top is brittle along the whole of it, because an arris with the weather on one side of it and nothing at all on the other goes that way and no other. That scale comes off in flakes the size of a five-pence piece and smells of wet iron, and the rust under it is black and smooth and does not flake at all.

Twelve inches down, the section is closed by a plate. The plate is flat bar of the same width as the section, welded across the inside at the bottom with a bead down each side and one along the near edge, and the bead stands about a sixteenth of an inch proud of the bar and is the only work on that gate that was done properly.

A hollow section is strong for its weight because the metal is all on the outside of it. A solid bar of the same size would be twice as heavy and no stronger in the direction a gate is pushed, and steel costs more by weight than it costs by length, so the section is what a man with a sheet and a press and no time reaches for.

A gate stile is the one place in a yard where a length of pipe is wanted and cannot easily be had, because a stile has to take a strap for a spindle and a bolt for a latch and neither of those wants a round bar. Round bar takes a flat bar bolted to it and a flat bar takes nothing, and the section takes both without a plate anywhere.

What is inside it is a length of four-inch pipe with no job at all. It is the ordinary state of a thing that was made to be strong and not to be looked into, and water gets in at the top the first wet day and stands in the bottom of it, and the scale on the inside is what forty winters of that does to the inside of a piece of steel.

The closing plate is not structural either. A flat bar across the bottom of a hollow section stops nothing and carries nothing, and it is there because the section was open at both ends when it came off the rack and the man who welded it in wanted it to be a box rather than a tube, and it has done that job for nine years without being asked to do another.

The paint on the outside is a different matter. It is hard and gloss and brittle over the whole of the stile where the sun has been on it, and it has crazed into a pattern of small cracks about the size of a fingernail, and those cracks do not go through to the metal under the first coat.

Where the gate swings most, on the arc nearest the alley, the paint has been worn back to the grey primer and then to the metal itself over about a foot of the face, because a gate that is opened every working day for nine years rubs its own face. That wear is on the hinge stile as well as on the latch one, and it is the reason both of them were capped in the first place by men who were thinking about water.

There is one nail-hole in the left-hand face of the stile about a foot above the flags. It is the second nail that hole has had, because the sheet was too thin there the first time and the first nail came out inside a year, and the second is sound and goes an inch into the flag.

The foot of the section stands on a pad of concrete about six inches square and an inch and a half thick, poured when the gate went up and gone hard and pale. And the stile is not loose on that pad by any amount that a hand could measure.

Of the four things that move at that gate, three of them do not move. The leaf does not move on its spindle except through a fraction of an inch, the diagonal does not move, and the section does not move, and the only part of the assembly with any play in it at all is the strap on the spindle where the paint has been rubbed off it by nine years of the leaf coming to.

---

From the alley behind, the rear gate of the yard is standing open, and the woman who books work comes through it with a coil of wire over her arm, and she puts the coil down on the flags as she comes in.

She stops a few feet inside the gate and stands there. The yard goes on round both of them with nobody else in it, and the hand was out of the section and down at her side before she was through the opening.

Four inches across, twelve inches deep, and a plate across the bottom of it with three welds round that plate, and the open hand inside stops where the corners are and brings back a palm of rust and a wrist that smells of wet iron, failing which the top of that section is a hole with a rust edge round it and nothing at all behind it.
