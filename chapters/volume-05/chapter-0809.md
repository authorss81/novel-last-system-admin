# Chapter 0809 — Both Ends Of One Step

Saturday the twenty-second of April 2023, in this borough. Out at the front of her own house at number twelve from the light coming up until she cannot stand in it, and a front step that has been settling for years is the whole of what she is out there for.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS A FRONT STEP THAT HAS BEEN SETTLING AT ONE END FOR YEARS AND MEANS TO PACK SAND UNDER IT AND SHIM IT UP; HER SISTER SHIMS THE OTHER END OF THE SAME STEP ON THE SAME DAY, AND WHERE THE TWO SHIMMED AREAS MEET THE STEP IS CARRIED THE WHOLE WAY INSTEAD OF AT TWO ENDS AND IT IS TRUE TO WITHIN THE THICKNESS OF A COIN FROM ONE CORNER TO THE OTHER, AND NEITHER OF THEM EVER FINDS OUT.**

Number twelve, on that Saturday. She is seventy-eight. The front step of that house is a single slab about five feet long and a bit over two feet wide, and it sits on the ground at the front door with the outer edge a shade lower than the inner edge, and it has sat like that for so long that nobody who comes to that door remembers it ever being otherwise.

The outer half of it has gone down into the ground and the inner half has not, because the inner half is on the house footing and the outer half is on the fill that came out of the front garden in the year the kitchen was done.

She has been meaning to do it since she came. She has a bar, a pack of hardwood wedges, a shovel, a spirit level and about half a barrow of sand she put through a sieve in the summer, and she is seventy-eight and it is the one thing on the list she has not asked anybody about.

The fault in the step is not that one end is lower than the other. She can level a step with two points under it and it will stay level for a while, and this one has sat at the same angle under the same door for as long as she has lived here without ever being any other shape.

The fault is the four feet of nothing between those two points.

A slab five feet long sitting on sand at the outer end and on the footing at the inner end has about four feet of span with air under it, and air under the middle of a slab is what makes it rock. She has felt it rock under her own foot at that house door for longer than she would say.

She has tried the other thing. She has taken a wedge off the top of the front edge and closed the gap with slate and it has gone the same way as the sand, and there is no wedge or pack of any kind that will carry the middle of a slab that has nothing under it, because the middle of the slab is not sinking. The middle of the slab is only being asked to be a beam.

The fill under the outer half came out of her own front garden. Whoever dug the kitchen out got a yard and a half of clay out of it and barrowed it round the side of that house and tipped it under the front step rather than pay for a skip, which in those days was the sensible thing to do and is the reason she has a step that rocks.

She gets the bar under it about two thirds of the way out and lifts.

The bar goes in on the second try and she has to get right down on one knee to get her weight on it, and the step comes up about two inches and she drives two of the hardwood wedges in under it and it stands on them.

Then she digs the sand out from under the outer half with a trowel, and it is sand and it is grey and it has been there since the kitchen was done, and it goes out into the barrow in lumps the size of a fist and the outer half of the slab comes down onto the hardcore at the bottom of the hole, which is hard and does not move.

She fills the hole back with the new sand, a shovelful at a time, and tamps each shovelful with the end of the bar, and the tamping is the whole of it. Sand packed with the end of a bar in thin layers will carry a slab for forty years. Sand tipped in and patted down will carry it for about two.

She knows what she is doing because her husband did it to a gate post in nineteen-something at a house two roads away, and he gave her the words while he did it, and the words were "Three inches underneath it, that is all it takes." He gave it to her with a bar in one hand and one knee down in the mud, and she has never once heard him be wrong about anything he said with a bar in his hands.

She goes at it for about two hours and she fills the hole no higher than the underside of the slab, and she leaves the ground beside the step alone, because the ground beside the step is what keeps the step from tipping out into the garden.

She takes about two hours over the outer half and then lets the slab down off the wedges.

It comes down onto the new pack and there is no movement in it, and she puts the spirit level on it along the length and along the width and takes about a third of the rise out at the wedges and lets it down again, and the level is better than it has ever been.

Then she stands on it, first at the outer end and then in the middle, and it does not rock at either end. It rocks in the middle, about a hand's width in from the house end, exactly as it has rocked for years, and she takes her foot off it and stands looking at the front of her own house.

---

Her sister comes round the side of the house at about the middle of the morning.

She has come at first thing, which is what she does, and she has a canvas bag and she has come for no reason anybody has written down, and she comes round the front of that house while the slab is up on two wedges and a bar with an old woman of seventy-eight lying on the ground behind it.

The sister sees the bar. She sees the wedges. She sees the hole in the ground under the outer half of that step and the grey sand going out of it in lumps, and she does not stop and she does not call out, and she stands on the path looking at it the way anybody looks at a job somebody else is in the middle of.

Then she goes round the corner of the porch.

---

The inner end of that slab is round the corner of the porch and in the shade, and from the front path you cannot see a hand's width past the front door frame.

That is where she gets herself. She goes down on her knees on the flags with her bag pushed under the porch out of the way and she works with a trowel and a short piece of timber she has carried in her hand since she came in.

There is about two feet of that slab's inner half with nothing at all under it. She can get the trowel in at the joint where the slab runs back to the door frame and she can feel the sand and she can dig it out and she can put new sand in behind the trowel in layers and tamp it with the end of the timber.

She does it the whole way along the inner half of that step, from the door frame out to a place about the middle of the slab where the old woman's hole is and the trowel would go through the wedge line, and she stops there because that is as far as it is safe to go with a trowel and a woman of seventy-eight working on the other side of it.

It takes her about three quarters of an hour.

Then she takes her bag from under the porch, stands up with a great deal of what seventy-eight does not get up from, and goes in at the front door of that house and out through the side door and away.

The woman of the house does not see her.

She is behind the slab for the whole of the middle of the morning with the bar, the wedges and the trowel, and when she comes out from under the outer half of it and stands up, there is nothing at the front of that house but her own bar and her own wedges and her own barrow.

She does not go round the corner of the porch. There is no reason on earth to go round the corner of the porch.

She spends the rest of that day on the outer half.

She takes it up again, and this time she packs it fuller than before, and she packs it right under the line where her half gives out and about a foot beyond it into the part she cannot see into, and she puts in two hardwood shims on the underside at the line where her packing stops, set in tight so that they do not move, and she lets it down and does not take them out.

That is a bad thing to have done and it is the right thing to have done. A shim is a piece of hard wood in a joint that cannot be packed tight enough. Put in tight, it does nothing but stand there and it will be there for the slab, and the slab does not know it is there.

It is not past six when the light goes off the front of that house and she cannot stand in it any longer.

She gets the bar out from under the step and puts the wedges in her pocket and leaves the sand sieved on the path in a heap with the shovel in it.

Then she lays the spirit level along it and across it and reads the bubble at each end twice before she shuts the lid, and takes a coin out of her apron and tries it at the four corners of that slab and along the middle of it, and it does not go under any of them. Four corners inside one coin's thickness of each other is as true as she is going to get out of a step that was never laid straight.

She has been putting a coin under that step at the front corner for the best part of a year, once a month or so, to see whether it had got any worse. It had got worse every single time. She does not do that any more and there is nothing in the apron pocket now but a folded tissue and a button.

It does not go under at the joint in the middle either, and that joint is a line across the whole width of the step with nothing in it and nothing loose in it, and it is there because the slab on that house was never laid to be carried along its whole length and now it is.

The sand under that step is one pack from the door frame to the front edge, packed in layers with the end of a bar and the end of a piece of timber, and two of the hardwood wedges are buried in the middle of it and will not be seen again, and the four feet of nothing that was there this morning under the middle of that step is not there now, and it has not been there since this morning and nobody saw it go.
