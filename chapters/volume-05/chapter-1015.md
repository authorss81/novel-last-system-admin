# Chapter 1015 — Four Foot Of Somebody Else's Hardwood

Undated in this draft.

**THE STORE AT THE UNIT HAS FOUR FOOT OF HARDWOOD STANDING ON A RACK AT THE DOOR END.**

The store at the unit is a long low room with its door on the yard side and one roof light along the middle of it, and the earth floor under a man's boots is as hard as a road in the middle and soft at the edges where forty years of men have stood about and turned. Both walls carry racks of Angle iron screwed to the brick and most of what is on them belongs to somebody else. At the door end there is a rack about eight foot long that is his own, and on it there is four foot of hardwood, and his own rule lies along the top of that rack where he left it when the summer went.

The four foot is stock. He had it in before the year turned with a load of other stuff off the same merchant, and it was bought against a job at Lowdale, which is a town twenty-two miles up the road and which is not this borough, and that job has still not started, and nobody has rung him about it, and the merchant has not either, and the four foot has been standing on his own rack in that store since before the year turned, on a rack that is level on two of its three feet and has been out of level on the third since before the war.

The rack is his own and he put it up himself on three uprights of four by three, and the third upright is set about half an inch further out than the other two, and there is a folded piece of paper under that foot of it that somebody put there before him and that nobody will ever take out. A rack does not care. But everything that has ever gone on that rack has gone down the slope towards the third foot and stopped there against the wall, and a man who builds knows that about his own work and does not enjoy the knowing of it.

He has no season to work. That shut at the end of eleven weeks ago with three names on the page and one name taken off it, and none of the three is him, and the yard has been running at a quarter of what it runs at since, and nothing is coming that anybody has told him about, and it is the first time since he was a lad of twenty-one that a man has had a fortnight in front of him and no reason to be anywhere.

There is one other thing in that yard that is his own unfinished business and that he has not told a soul at the unit about. Four feet of the brick kerb round the weigh pit came out of its own bed on an afternoon in the summer, and he put it out with a cold chisel and his thumb behind the blade. What was under it has washed out along the whole of that side, and there is a gap along the run you could get a finger into, and the plate over the corner of the pit is lipping on nothing. That unit has not a penny for a foot of dressed kerb and has not had one since before the year turned. He has told nobody. He has decided twice since that he will tell them before anybody finds it, and he has done it neither time.

He thought about going into the office about that kerb while he was standing at the rack, and he worked out what would happen if he went. Somebody would ask him what it had cost to put four feet of brick back, and he would have to put into words that he had taken it out himself on an afternoon when nobody asked him for it, and nothing in any office in this borough will answer that, and the second question would be worse than the first and the honest one would be about the money.

So the yard stays shut about the kerb, and he is a man who has told nobody anything, and it is now three weeks since the season shut and he has put the whole of it out loud to nobody but himself.

He got as far as the door and stopped, which is a thing he has done eleven times on that yard this winter.

He has been up that frontage four times since the coal shed with a thing to ask for and has been beaten to it four times. At the bottom of the yard in the autumn he knelt down in the cold with a chisel in his hand and decided there and then that he would go up and ask the mother at twelve to let him do that wall. In the scullery at twelve a woman put a sheet of paper on a scrubbed table with his coat still on and asked him for something else and he gave it her. In the front room at twelve on the morning she came back he came in with the wall behind the range ready in his mouth and a woman who had been down a river for six weeks turned her head and asked him whether he could tell her his name, and he stood at that window and did not answer her.

That is three. The fourth was the beginning of the summer and the sentence was about the bed frames at number ten, and that one he is not going to be asked about, and he gave it to a shed instead and a wall with four words on it that will be gone by the spring.

He put his hand down the length of that four foot of hardwood with his thumb against the end and walked the thumb along the top edge to see whether it had begun to move with the weather in the store, which it has, and which is a thing a man is told about and not a thing he can help. It had moved about a sixteenth along one edge over six months, which is what oak does in a dry room in a wet winter. The rule was still lying on the rack. He picked it up and laid his thumb along the end of the four foot and walked down the length again, and the answer was the same as it had been before the year turned, which is that none of it is his.

None of it is his and it is the best thing in the store. It came in on a load with a merchant's list and the merchant's list has a house on it in a town twenty-two miles off and the job was going to be a floor and a staircase and a lot of new joists, and the man who was having it has not started and has not sent for it and is not going to send for it before the spring, and every week that rack stands there with four foot of the best oak in it, in a yard where the only thing going is a quarter of what it went at.

---


He stood there in about the time it takes to turn round and decide a thing, and he decided it. He would go up the frontage that morning and put the wall behind that range into the mother's own mouth himself, before anybody can ask him for it. He would not take the page. He would not take a figure with him, because a figure in his hand is a thing a man does when he is sure of being asked, and he has not been sure of anything for ten years.

And if she said no he would come back down that frontage with his hands empty and having done nothing at all, which is a thing no man has ever wanted to do and which he was going to do.

Four foot of hardwood is standing on end against the wall of that store and it will be there on Monday when the door of the yard is open and the light comes in over the stacks.
