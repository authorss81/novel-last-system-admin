# Chapter 0701 — Posts Already In The Ground

Saturday the fifth of February 2022, in this borough. He is at a cottage with posts already in the ground from late morning until dark and the whole of the day is a pair of oak gates sighted against posts he cannot hang on his own.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES SIGHTS A PAIR OF OAK GATES AGAINST POSTS ALREADY IN THE GROUND AT A COTTAGE ON HIS OWN, FINDS THE LATCH STILE WANTS A PAIR OF HANDS ON A LADDER, SPEAKS TO THE MAN WHO HAS PAID ABOUT THE SPRING AND PUTS NO DATE ON IT, AND DOES NOT WRITE HIS NUMBER ON THE OTHER SIDE OF HIS CARD.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is on his own all day except for the man at the cottage in the afternoon. He speaks to him there.

---

He drives to the cottage late in the morning with the gates on the van, tied fore and aft with straps he checks twice at the unit and once at the end of the road.

The gates have been leaning against the back wall of his own unit since the summer, with a number chalked on the back of each one, squared and true. The posts are already in the ground at the cottage, set true and capped, waiting since before Christmas, the caps tarred against the wet. The man who wants them wanted them by the spring and paid up front and has the money down, and the money sits in an envelope in the drawer of the bench at the unit under a dust sheet, untouched.

He parks in the lane by the cottage garden and sits for a bit with the engine off, looking at the posts through the windscreen. The lane is narrow and the hedge is wet and drips steadily on the bonnet. A dog barks from the yard across the way and then stops, and a woman shuts a door somewhere and the sound carries flat in the cold.

He gets the gates off the van on his own into the lane by the cottage garden and stands them on their feet against the hedge, chocked with bricks from the garden wall. The hedge drips on his collar as he works. A tractor sounds from the field beyond and fades. He wipes his hands on his trousers twice and looks at the gates against the hedge, numbers out, true in the wet light.

The shoebox is on the top of his own bench back at the unit where he cannot come in without seeing it. The drawer under the bench is shut and has been shut since the sixteenth of November. The stranger's brush is down in the footwell in the side pocket of a canvas bag with a stone in the leather bottom of it so it does not go anywhere in a corner. The space behind the offcuts is a folded step ladder's wide and there is nothing in it.

He has not got a ladder. There is a packing bench, the little stool he made himself out of two offcuts in the summer, and a stub of a folding stool that came off a skip in the summer, and he has brought the stool.

---

He carries the gates one at a time to the posts.

The left gate goes to the left post and the right gate to the right one, and he stands them up and wedges them with offcuts at the foot so they stand on their own. The numbers chalked on the back of each one show. The card he put in the latch stile lifts out in a while, because on the latch stile it lifts out, and he said so to a man on a shop telephone in October.

He steps back into the lane and sights them, first with one eye shut and then with both open, the way he sights everything.

The posts are true. The gates are true. The gap at the latch stile is a finger's width at the top and a finger's width at the bottom, which is how it ought to be before hanging. Hanging a gate on a post that is already in the ground on your own wants a ladder, because you have to lift the gate to the hinges and sit it on and keep it there while you drive the pins, and you cannot do that from a stool and you cannot do it with one pair of hands, and the gate will twist on the straps and take the paint off the post if you try.

He sights them again from the other side, crouching to the height of the bottom hinge. The latch stile on the left gate wants a shave where it meets the post, no more than a curl. He could do that now with a plane and a shooting board on the tailgate. He does not do it now, because shaving it now without hanging it is work done blind, and a top run set a quarter of an inch out in a manky kitchen in January taught him what work done blind looks like, and he still sees that run when he shuts his eyes.

---

He has a piece of scribing card in the shirt pocket of the coat on the back of the cab door back at the unit, or rather he has it in his shirt pocket now, because he moved it this morning.

It is the corner of a card about the size of a playing card and there is TWELVE on it in his own writing in a scribing pencil, and he wrote it at a tip off the ring road on the sixteenth of November because there was a taped box on the top of that heap with a street on it that he has gone past and a number on it that is a house at the end of a road.

There is room on the other side of that card. He has got a pencil behind his ear. He could write his number on it here in this lane and give it to the man at the cottage and the man would put it on the mantel, and if the man ever met a woman in a white van with a ladder he would give it to her. That is not a plan. That is a wish with a pencil in it.

He does not write on it.

---

The man at the cottage comes out in the afternoon with two cups.

He is a man in his sixties with mud on his boots and he looks at the gates standing wedged against the posts and nods.

"They look right," he says.

"They are right," he says. "Posts are true."

"When will you hang them."

He looks at the gates and at the stool by the hedge and at the lane where a ladder ought to stand. On the twenty-ninth of November, in a street off the ring road, he took a folding aluminium step ladder out of his van and handed it over to a woman who clears houses, and neither of them said one word about when it would be back. It went into an open porch on the third of December with no note on it, and by the seventeenth it was folded against the wall under a window in a front room at the end of a road he has not walked, and it has not been opened out since and it has not been moved.

He cannot say any of that to a man with mud on his boots who has paid.

"In the spring," he says, and puts no date on it, because a date would mean saying he has no ladder and saying he lent it and saying he cannot ask for it back without saying that he lent it. "When the ground is firm enough to stand a ladder on without it sinking."

"Right," the man says. "You'll let me know."

"Aye."

---

They drink the tea in the lane with the gates between them, steam coming off the cups into the cold.

"That latch stile," the man says, nodding at the left gate where the card sits in the saw cut. "Will it want much."

"It wants a shave," he says. "No more than a curl. I put a card in it so it lifts out when the wet gets in. You'll get a good run out of it before it wants doing."

"That's a thing, is it," the man says, the way the man on the shop telephone said it in October, turning the cup in his hands.

"Aye. On the latch side it lifts out. On the hinge side it does not."

The man looks at the stool by the hedge, the little stool made of two offcuts, paint-spattered and true.

"Is that all you've got," he says, not unkind.

"I've got a stool and a bench," he says, the way he said it to a woman of about sixty-five in a manky kitchen in January. "I've lent my ladder out. Twenty-ninth of November. I did not say when it comes back."

"Right," the man says, and does not ask who and does not ask when, the way the man at the bench opposite did not ask, the way nobody in this borough asks. "You'll want it back by the spring."

"In the spring," he says again, looking at the posts. "It will stand like this until then. Posts true and gates true and nothing on them."

The man drinks the last of his tea and looks down the lane for a bit.

"I'll tell you what my wife said to me," he says. "She said, you paid a man in November and it is February and there is a gate leaning against a wall somewhere and no gate on my posts. Not angry. She just wanted to know what to tell her sister. So I have told her nothing, because there is nothing to tell her yet, and every week it is nothing is a week she keeps saying nothing to her sister."

"I understand."

"I know you do. I'm not having this out with you in a lane." He puts the cup on the wall. "But I'm going to keep looking at them out of that window, and if it turns into March I'm going to ring you and ask you a straight question, and you'll have to have something better than the spring."

"Then I'll have something better than the spring," he says, "or I'll have said so."

"Right." The man opens his own gate and goes back up the path, and it swings shut behind him out of its own weight and does not come in on the path either, which is the only thing that gate and the gates on the van have in common.

---

He loads the gates back on the van at the end of the afternoon, though the light is going and his back aches.

He does it on his own, gate by gate, lifting each end in turn and walking it up the tailgate with a plank, the way a man does who has done it often and does not like doing it and does it cleanly all the same. The numbers chalked on the back of each one streak where his hands have been. He wipes them true again with his thumb and chalks over the thin place. The straps go fore and aft. He checks them twice.

The posts stand empty in the cottage garden, capped and true, waiting against the dark. He looks at them for a bit before he shuts the tailgate, hands in his pockets. A pair of posts already in the ground with no gates on them is a small thing and a plain thing, and it will stand there through February with nothing on it because he cannot hang a gate on his own from a stool, and the man who paid will walk past them morning and dark and see nothing on them.

He drives back to the unit with the gates on the van and the straps singing a little in the wind, and puts the van in the bay with the lights on.

He puts them back against the back wall, squared, numbers out, chocked with offcuts at the foot so they cannot slip. The space behind the offcuts is still a folded step ladder's wide and there is still nothing in it. The shoebox is still on the bench with the lid on it. The drawer is still shut. The leads are still along the other side with the dust sheets folded on top of them. He stands in the doorway with the extractor going and looks at the shape of the bay, which has not altered since June except for the gates going out on the van and coming back on it.

The piece of scribing card is in his shirt pocket with TWELVE on one side of it and nothing at all on the other, soft at the corners now. He has a pencil behind his ear, sharpened that morning. He looks at the card for about as long as it takes to put the pencil behind the other ear, turning it over twice, and then he locks up tightly and goes home, and the card is in the pocket, and the gates are against the wall, and somewhere at the end of a road he has not walked there is an aluminium step ladder standing folded in a house that has it on a piece of paper, and the woman who has that paper does not know his name and he does not know hers.
