# Chapter 0262 — A Day With No Use For It

Wednesday the seventh of September 2016, from about half past eight in the morning, a parade of shops in Tideglass.

**HE ASKED FIVE PEOPLE, OUT LOUD, ONE AT A TIME, TO COME AND STAND ABOUT ON A DAY WHEN THERE WAS NOTHING WRONG ANYWHERE IN THIS BOROUGH, AND THREE OF THEM CAME, AND THE ONE PERSON WHO HAD OFFERED A DAY IN JUNE WAS NOT ONE OF THEM AND WAS NOT ASKED AGAIN, AND THE REASON HE WAS NOT ASKED AGAIN IS THAT HE IS IN LEEDS ON A WEDNESDAY AND HAS BEEN SINCE JUNE.**

It was raining when he came out of the kitchen at about half past eight and the parade was shiny and empty, and he went along it in the way he has gone along it since June, and he said it five times and he said it the same way each time and he did not write it down and he did not make a list, and the five were the man behind the counter, a woman going into the shop that shuts early, a man coming off a lorry, the man in his sixties, and the young woman of about twenty.

"Second pair of hands," he said. "Come to the parade at two. There's no floor, there's no water, there's nobody hurt, there's no landing and there's no hose. Just come and stand here for an hour if you've got nothing on. That's all it is. I'm not asking anybody for anything, it's the other way round."

The man behind the counter said, "What am I supposed to do with a second pair of hands."

"Stand there with them," he said.

That is the whole of what he said to any of them, and about half of them thought it was a wind-up and about half of them thought it was something else, and the man in his sixties looked at him for slightly too long and said nothing at all, which from him was an answer.

---

He had asked the fifth of them in August, on an evening, at about half past six, by knocking, which is the only time he has ever knocked on anybody's door in this borough.

The man from the works on the far side of the river had it open before the second knock.

"Two, on the parade," the boy said. "Any day you want. You pick it."

"Don't ring me about it."

"I won't."

"Go on, then."

"You'll come."

"I'll come if there's a job in it." The man put his hand on the back of his own door. "I said in June, I've got one day and it isn't a day of the week, it's a day, and you don't know the day until somebody needs something on it. If you want me for a Wednesday, I'm in Leeds till Wednesday night, and then I'm back on the plant till Saturday at two, and that's the whole of my autumn."

"You don't owe me a Wednesday."

"I don't owe you a Tuesday either," said the man, "and I said that in June, and I turned up, and I'd do it again if there was a floor in Marsh Lane. But you don't get a day off from your own life for standing about in the rain with a lad in a cardigan. If I came this afternoon it wouldn't be a favour, it would be the two of us pretending."

So the boy had not told anybody, and nobody rang him, and what the man had said about Wednesdays in August was still in the boy's pocket at two o'clock, and nobody else on that parade had heard a word of it.

---

At about two there were five people on that parade because of him.

Him. The young woman of about twenty, who had come in from the college and had not gone up to the college. The woman of about fifty-five, who was there anyway because there was nowhere else for her to be that week. The man from the parade, who had not been asked at all and turned up at ten past two and said, "Right," and stood about. And a man who works in a shop and had come out to see what was happening and had been offered nothing and stayed anyway.

And there was no floor, and there was no water, and there was no landing, and there was no hose, and there was nothing to carry, and they stood about in the rain for an hour and a half and talked about a bus timetable, and a dog, and a man on the weighbridge at Sarn whose tea had gone cold, and a roof.

At about twenty past three the woman of about fifty-five said the thing that was actually going on in her week, and she said it to nobody in particular, in the flat voice she uses.

"I have to be out of that corridor on Saturday morning at eight," she said. "The whole of it. And I have got a room and a half of furniture and a bag and nowhere to be, until the seventeenth, and I am not going to ask anybody in this borough for a floor because I know exactly what would happen if I asked for one, and I would rather be sitting in a corridor with a light on that I paid ten pound for than be the woman who asked a parade for a fortnight."

Nobody answered that, and after a bit the man in the shop said it was the first decent rain since about the middle of July, and the conversation went back to the bus timetable, and the man from the parade lit a cigarette and put it out again, and the three of them who had come for it had come because he had asked them standing in the rain to come and stand in the rain.

At about half past three the rain came on properly, the kind that gets into a front, and they went in separately — the man from the parade up the parade, the man who works in a shop out the other way with his coat over his head, the woman of about fifty-five up towards the shops with her bag on her arm, and the boy back down to the kitchen at the back, where there were plates.

**AND NOBODY WAS HURT, AND NOBODY RANG ANYBODY, AND AT ABOUT EIGHT THAT NIGHT A MAN CAME OVER THE BRIDGE FROM THE WORKS ON THE FAR SIDE OF THE RIVER WITH A BAG OF TOOLS IN HIS HAND AND WENT DOWN THE PARADE PAST THREE SHOPS THAT WERE OPEN, AND HE HAD SAID NO TO A WEDNESDAY IN AUGUST AT HALF PAST SIX IN THE EVENING, AND HE WAS NOT ASKED AGAIN, AND HE WALKED HOME.**
