# Chapter 0245 — Two Vans

Saturday the eleventh of June 2016, from about ten in the morning, a parade of shops in Tideglass, and the low end of Peverell Street.

**THE BOAT WENT OUT OF THIS BOROUGH ON A TUESDAY AND CAME BACK ON FRIDAY, AND THE ENGINE WENT OUT ON THE SAME MORNING AND CAME BACK ON WEDNESDAY, AND NEITHER OF THEM WAS ASKED TO GO BY ANYBODY, AND THE ONLY THING IN THIS BOROUGH THAT WAS ACTUALLY ARRANGED LAST WEEK WAS A VAN.**

Jonas Mercer had been in the doorway of the shop next door to the one with the trade counter since about half nine, and he had been in that doorway most mornings since the eleventh of May, and nobody had asked him anything in all that time, and he had stopped noticing that somewhere towards the end of May and had started noticing the parking instead.

The parade was the same parade. The shutter on the shop went up at the minute it always went up. The window card with the shop's own telephone number on it and nothing else on it was in the window where it has always been, and the woman of about sixty-five who stands behind that counter was standing behind it, and she has not been told anything, and nobody has ever gone in and told her anything.

Tomas Renn came out with a crate of rags on his shoulder, put it down on the kerb, and wiped his hands down the front of his apron.

"Both crews are out."

"Since when."

"Tuesday morning. Boat's over the water, engine's over the water, different jobs, they'll be back when they're back." He put his hands on his hips and looked up the parade. "I've been in this shop a long time and the thing you get used to isn't the work. It's knowing where two vans are. Right now I know where one is."

"The other one's on a forecourt at the end of that road."

"The other one's on a forecourt at the end of that road and it comes back when it comes back," said Tomas Renn. "That's the whole of it. That's what they've agreed. Our lot goes out first and we don't get told what we're getting back, and I want you to understand that I'm not having a go at anybody, because I said no to whatever it was and I've said it three times and I'd say it a fourth. This isn't me not liking it. This is me not knowing where a van is at eleven o'clock on a Wednesday for the first time since I was young enough to be in this shop before it was a shop."

"They used to say Sunday."

"They used to say Sunday. Everybody in this borough could have told you what day a van came back. My lot said Sunday. The lad at Lowdale said Sunday. The man at the bottom house said Sunday, and he said it because he'd heard the Lowdale lad say it, and none of those three had ever been asked by anybody, and that's the mechanism, that's the whole mechanism, and you can put it in whatever you like." He picked the crate up again. "Nine years it was Sunday. It isn't Sunday now and there isn't a new word for it, and that's the whole of what's happened down here since the twentieth of May."

---

A woman came up the parade with a child on each hip and stopped at the kerb to shift one of them, and the shop she was going into was shut, and she stood there for a second doing arithmetic about the shutter the way everybody in a parade does.

Then a van came off the forecourt at the end of the road.

It is white, it has a scabbed corner on the offside wing where somebody has already put a colour that does not match, and it is off Lowdale, and it came round the corner at about ten past ten on a Saturday morning, and it slowed, and it came in on the forecourt, and the driver put the handbrake on and left the engine running and got out to look at the nearside wheel as though something had told him to.

Nobody on the parade looked up.

Tomas Renn looked up, and then looked back down at the crate, and went inside, and shut the door, and did not say anything about it, and there was nothing to say and he had not said it.

Jonas Mercer asked one question.

"Who is it for."

"Nobody can tell you that either." Tomas Renn had the door half shut and his eye on the gap. "That's the answer. Six weeks and the answer's *nobody can tell you that either*, and that's not a complaint, that's a state of things, and it is exactly the same as it was before the twentieth of May except that before the twentieth of May it was *nobody can tell you which day*."

---

The bottom of Peverell Street is a ten-minute walk from the parade and it is where the road gives up and becomes three houses and a wall and then a bank.

He went down it at about half ten because he had nothing else to do with the morning, which was the position he had been in since the eleventh of May, and he was not going to pretend to anybody, including himself, that it was research.

There is a pump in the doorway of number forty-one. It is not plumbed to anything. It runs off a standpipe in the yard at the back that a man put in himself, and there is a length of hose coiled on the wall beside it that has not been touched since the water went down in the November before last. The door is open a hand's width, because it always is.

The man was inside doing something with a radio. He has a name in a drawer in his kitchen, four doors up the street, and he is not on anything, and he took it off a board in a lock-up about three years ago because a name on a board is a thing that gets asked about and he had been on enough lists.

Jonas Mercer stood at the top of the street and did not go down it. That was not modesty and it was not a decision. There was a woman at number thirty-eight taking a bucket out of a back yard because the water was off, and she stopped at the pump, and the door was open a hand's width, and she said, "Is he in?"

"He's in."

"I only want a bucket. The water's been off since the middle of the week."

"He's in."

"Right." She waited a bit and then said, "Have the men been?"

"Who?"

"Any of them." She tipped her head up the street. "I heard the boat go out. You don't hear it, you don't know, and I'd not be asking if the water hadn't gone off. Have they been?"

"They've been and gone," said the man in the doorway.

"That's all right," she said, and took the bucket, and went back up her yard, and that was the whole of the exchange and neither side of it raised its voice.

Jonas Mercer walked back up Peverell Street at about five to eleven and did not do anything the rest of that morning and there was nothing waiting for him and that had been the position for seven weeks.

**AND A VAN THAT USED TO COME BACK ON A SUNDAY CAME BACK AT TEN PAST TEN ON A SATURDAY AND NOBODY ON THAT PARADE LOOKED UP, AND A MAN AT THE BOTTOM OF PEVERELL STREET ANSWERED ONE QUESTION ABOUT A BUCKET AND WAS NOT ASKED A SECOND ONE, AND A SHOP STAYED SHUT, AND THAT WAS THE ORDINARY WEEK.**
