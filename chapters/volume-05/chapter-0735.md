# Chapter 0735 — The Fifth Thing

Monday the twentieth of June 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number twelve from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a tap under the landing and a bowl off the floor of a bathroom.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT GETS THE HANDLE OFF HER OWN BATH TAP AND PUTS A SCRATCH IN THE CHROME WITH A PAIR OF PLIERS SHE SHOULD NOT HAVE USED, LETS HER SISTER TAKE THE CLOTH AND NOT THE FIXTURE, PUTS A WASHER IN WITH AN ADJUSTABLE AND HEARS THE DRIP STOP, SAYS FIVE OUT LOUD TO SOMEBODY FOR THE FIRST TIME EVER, AND PUTS THE BOWL ON THE SHELF ABOVE THE SINK.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight at number twelve on Monday. Her sister comes to her back door in the morning, stands in the passage while she speaks to her through the door of the bathroom, and then sits with her in the kitchen.

---

She is in the kitchen before the light is properly on, with a folded cloth under her arm. The adjustable came out of the drawer in the front room where the fuses are, and it is lying on the table with the pliers beside it, and the ballpoint pen that went dry in May is in the kitchen drawer where it has been since May and she does not open that drawer for anything.

The table is dead flat where the window-side foot was put back in dry card joint with no glue. On the right of it the gas envelope lies face up, square with the edge, pencil side up, six things on the back of it in pencil and nothing crossed off. On the left, under its cloth, is the box the size of two loaves with tape round every edge. Between them the table is bare, which it has not been for some months.

She looks at the envelope long enough for the window to come up grey behind her, and then she goes out of the kitchen and down the passage, stepping over the brush with her hand on the wall the way she does.

The bathroom is under the landing and it is colder than the kitchen by a long way. The bowl is white china on the floor and there is a folded cloth beside it, and the tap above the bowl drips into it the way it has dripped since before Christmas.

She kneels on the folded cloth with the adjustable in one hand and puts the cloth round the handle with the other.

---

The handle comes off easier than she expected, which is the first thing that goes wrong with the morning.

Underneath it there is a gland, and the gland is round. She wants a hexagon to put the adjustable to and there is not one, and she has a pair of pliers in the front room drawer with the fuses she has never had a fuse out of.

She tries the pliers on it. They go round the round and find nothing, and then they find the scratch, and the scratch is bright and new and goes across the chrome in a line she can see from the doorway without the light on.

She looks at the line for a while.

That is the handle off and back on once. She will take it off again before the middle of the day and this time it stays off, and there is nothing to be done about knowing that in advance.

Then she puts the handle back on, which is the part she does not enjoy, and turns it till it feels right, and goes back along the passage to the kitchen to wash her hands, and the drip comes into the bowl behind her the whole way.

---

Her sister knocks at the back door at about the middle of the morning and she lets her in.

"What have you been doing," her sister says. "You have got a cloth in your pocket and you have been on the floor."

"I have had a go at the tap," she says.

"You have had a go at it," her sister says. "With what."

"Pliers, first."

"Were they no good."

"They were no good," she says. "It is round. It is not one of the square ones. I have put a scratch across it and I am not sorry about the scratch and I am sorry about the scratch."

She gets the adjustable out of the drawer in the front room and shows her the jaws of it, and her sister looks at them and does not touch them.

"I want you to take this cloth," she says, and carries it out to the door of the bathroom and puts it on the sill in the doorway where her sister can reach it without coming into the room.

Her sister picks it up.

"You can take that," her sister says.

"You can take that," she says. "You will not come past that door." And she taps the frame with two fingers, and her sister stops there with the cloth against her chest, and the work goes on with the two of them in two rooms and a doorframe in between.

---

It takes longer than the morning had room for and neither of them says anything for most of it.

She backs the handle off, and the drip starts off the washer while the washer is still on, and she keeps a thumb under it so that it runs onto her hand and not into the bowl.

This is the handle off the second time and this is the time it stays off.

The round part comes up far enough to show a washer sitting flat and gone hard, the way a washer goes when it has been crushed rather than worn. She takes it off with the point of a knife she warmed on the hotplate, which is the fourth thing on the envelope and has been done since March.

She puts the new one in. The new one is off a roll of sheet in the kitchen drawer, cut with scissors, and it is not the right size and it is the right kind.

She puts the adjustable on the flats under the gland and brings it up a quarter at a time and stops each time to look at it, and her sister has the cloth against her chest in the doorway and does not move her feet.

And then the drip stops.

Not slow. Not once. It stops, the way a sound stops when you have been hearing it so long that you only find out it was there by its going.

Neither of them says anything for a moment. The bowl is on the floor with nothing in it.

"It's gone," her sister says.

"It has stopped," she says.

"You could have had that with the pliers."

"No," she says. "The pliers were the scratch. This was the round."

---

Her sister wipes the bowl out from the doorway with the end of the cloth, because the bowl is just inside it and she can get an arm that far, and puts the bowl on the sill, and then folds the cloth in three and lays it square on the sill too.

"That is a job off your kitchen wall," her sister says.

She looks at the envelope on the table at the far side of the kitchen, at the right-hand side of it where it has been for months, with six things on the back in pencil and nothing crossed off.

"That is the fifth one," she says.

Her sister comes and stands at her shoulder and reads the six out loud in pencil that is nearly gone now, slowly, the way you read a thing that has been written a long time ago.

"Gate," her sister says. "Front room. Window. Hotplate. Bath tap. Passage."

"That is the fifth one," she says.

"That is five," her sister says.

"That is five," she says. "And I cannot put a line through it, and I am not going to try, so you had better get used to me saying it like that."

Her sister looks at her.

"You have never once said a number out loud about that paper," she says.

"No," she says. "There has been nobody here to say it to."

---

She empties the bowl into the sink in the kitchen and washes it under the cold tap, which runs when the stopcock is open and gives her a thin cold thread of water that is enough.

She dries it with the cloth and puts it on the shelf above the sink, on its side, where a bowl lives when it is not catching anything.

Then she goes into the bathroom under the landing and stands looking at the place on the floor where the bowl was, and the cloth is not on the floor any more, and there is nothing under the tap at all now but the pipe and the new washer with its edge showing, which is the first thing she has put right in this house in a year.

She puts the adjustable and the pliers back in the drawer in the front room and shuts it on the fuses she has never had a fuse out of, and she does not open the kitchen drawer and does not go into the front room.

She stands in the passage with the passage light burning on above her, as it has since June, and listens to the bathroom, and there is nothing in it.

Then she goes back into the bathroom and looks at the tiles for a while. There is a ring on them where the bowl stood, a clean ring about as big as a saucer and a shade paler than the rest of the floor, and it is the only thing in this house that has got better this month and she did it out of a bag of sheet in a drawer with her own hands.
