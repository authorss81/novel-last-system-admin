# Chapter 0259 — Three Gates

Friday the nineteenth of August 2016, from about ten past four in the afternoon, the lane above the low end of Peverell Street in Tideglass.

**THE MAN AT THE BOTTOM OF THAT STREET SAID IT WAS FOR A GATE AND WOULD NOT SAY WHICH GATE, AND THERE ARE THREE, AND ONE OF THEM HAS A PADLOCK ON IT THAT HAS BEEN THERE SINCE ANYBODY AROUND HERE COULD REMEMBER.**

The bus from the college comes in on the lane, goes down Peverell Street, and stops at the bottom of it, which is past the field gate and short of the bank. The rest of the walk from there is up the top of the parade, and doing it that way is quicker than going round by the marshes, and the young woman of about twenty has been going down there every Friday since the Easter holidays.

She has never once had to open anything, because there has never been anything on the gate.

There was a length of chain on it.

Bright galvanised, new, about the length of a forearm, hooked over the latch and dropped into the crook, and not fastened, and the padlock on the gate itself was hanging open on its hasp with nothing in it, and there was a scuff on the gravel on the bank side where a shoe had turned round.

She had to unhook it to get through to the stop, and she did, and she stood in the drizzle at the bottom of the street, and then she hooked it back exactly the way she had found it, which took longer than unhooking it.

Then she walked back up the whole of that street, because she wanted to have a look at the other two gates and because she was annoyed with it.

Number thirty-eight has a side gate, and it is a wooden gate and it has hung crooked since before she was born, and there was nothing on it.

Number forty-one has a back gate, and it has a hasp on it and a padlock, and the padlock is a five-lever and it is as thick as a wrist, and there has been a padlock on that gate the whole time she has been coming down here.

So.

She stood on the top step outside number forty-one for about a minute, because she was working something out, and then she went up the street without knocking, and the door was open a hand's width the way it always is, and there was a radio on inside it very faintly, and nobody said anything to her, because nobody says anything to anybody on that street.

---

The man of about eighteen is in the doorway of the shop next door to the one with the trade counter from about half past four on a Friday because he does the dishes in there and comes out at the wrong time, and she came up the parade straight at him and said:

"I know which gate."

"You've been all of ten minutes."

"I've been all of ten minutes and I know which gate," she said. "And I'm not going to tell you who told me, because it was me and I found it out, and there's nobody in this town who told me anything. It's the field gate at the bottom. At the bottom of the street, at the bottom end, the one that's on the field side. It's new. It's about eight feet and it's hooked over the latch and it isn't fastened, and the padlock's hanging open with nothing in it."

He came out of the doorway about a foot.

"The field gate."

"His own back gate has a padlock on it the size of my head on it and it has had since I was about seven. I look at it twice a week. So it isn't his gate and it isn't number thirty-eight's, because number thirty-eight's gate has hung crooked since the war and there is nothing on it. So it's the field one. That's all I have got and it's took me ten minutes and a walk."

"Right," he said.

"There's a reason for it," she said. "He didn't buy it to look at a gate."

"He said it was for a gate," said the man of about eighteen. "And he's not wrong."

"He said it was for *a* gate. That's not the same as it being the one you want it to be. He could have said the one at the top there and he wouldn't have been lying."

"So why didn't he."

"Because you don't, that's why. If you tell somebody it's for a gate, then when it turns out on a gate that isn't yours, they've still got a gate, and they haven't had to say it was yours, and you haven't had to say anything at all." He was looking down the parade and not at her. "I've been thinking about that for seven weeks and that's the only bit of it I've got, and it's not even good."

"Are you going to go and look at it."

"No."

"You've just told me it isn't a gate you can walk down to in about nine minutes."

"I've just told you it's a chain and a gate, and I'm not going down there to stand and look at it, because the second I stand and look at a thing in this borough it becomes a thing somebody's been stood and looked at, and then it isn't a chain any more, it's a question, and he'd know in a week." He put his hands in his pockets. "And I'm not asking him either. Not about the chain, not about the gate, and not whether he'd like somebody to stop, because I've got about a half an hour of my own thinking to do before I could open my mouth on any of that."

She said, "What's the half an hour for."

"It's for working out whether I'd want somebody turning up on my doorstep, if it was me," he said, "and I haven't worked it out, and it is not his business and it is not a thing anybody has ever asked him about."

"That's the worst thing you've ever said to me."

"It's the first thing I've said to you in a fortnight," he said, "and I don't want to talk about it any more, and if we go on I'm going to turn it into something I can carry about, and I've not got anywhere to put it."

They stood there. The rain came on properly then, the kind that gets into the front of a coat, and the woman of about fifty-five went past with a bag of nothing in particular and did not stop.

**AND THE GATE AT THE BOTTOM OF THAT STREET WAS HOOKED BACK THE WAY A GIRL GOING TO A BUS STOP HAD FOUND IT, AND SHE TOLD ONE PERSON AND HE TOLD HER HE WAS NOT GOING, AND THE TWO OF THEM WENT IN AND THE RAIN CAME ON HARDER.**
