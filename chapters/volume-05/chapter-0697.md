# Chapter 0697 — What Goes On The Van

Thursday the twentieth of January 2022, in this borough. She is in a house she is clearing from first light until dark and the whole of the day is a front room with a blanket on a chair arm and a landing with a ladder on it that is not hers to move.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES SORTS A FRONT ROOM IN A HOUSE SHE IS CLEARING WITHOUT TAKING A BLANKET OFF A CHAIR ARM, SPEAKS TO A DAUGHTER WITH KEYS ABOUT WHAT GOES ON A VAN AND WHAT DOES NOT, LEAVES A FOLDED STEP LADDER SHE SEES ON A LANDING WHERE IT IS BECAUSE IT IS NOT HERS TO MOVE, AND WRITES THE FIGURE FOR THE JOB ON HER PAD AND PUTS NO LINE FOR ANYTHING ELSE ON IT.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is on her own all day except for a woman in her thirties with keys and a man with a van at the end of the day. She speaks to both of them.

---

The house is a terrace and it is a woman's and she has gone to live with her daughter, and the daughter has the keys and has the say, and today is the day for the front room and the landing and nothing else.

She gets there before the daughter with her own white van and parks it tight to the kerb and opens the back. The drawer under the passenger bench is shut and has been shut since the middle of November and her own bolster is on the leads and the tin of screws is under the bolster, and she has not opened it in this borough since the summer and there is no reason to open it now.

The parcel is behind the passenger bench on the floor against the bulkhead with a bag of rags on top of it. The label under the rags has gone soft along the bottom edge where the damp got into it, and the number on it is under that, and she has not moved the rags since the twenty-second of October and she could put a hand under them and read the number any morning she likes and has not done it on any morning since.

It is taped. It is a box the size of two loaves. It is a box of something in tins, and she worked that out on the twenty-second of October by putting her hand flat on the top of it and pushing, and she has not found out what is in it at any point since.

And she has read a number off it twice and written it nowhere, and there is a bag of her own rags in the back of that van with somebody else's blanket in it since the twelfth of November.

---

The daughter comes with the keys on a ring and a list on a phone.

"Front room and landing," the daughter says. "That's the lot for today. The rest can wait."

"Right."

"She wants the chair kept. The one with the blanket on it. The blanket goes with it."

She looks through the doorway at the front room. There is a chair with a blanket over the arm of it. The blanket is somebody's and the chair is somebody's and the two go together, and her rule is that she does not take other people's things.

"That stays," she says.

"That stays," the daughter says. "Horse van won't take a chair with a blanket on it. They said."

They go through the hall. The landing is up a short flight with a rail on one side and a window at the turn, and there is a folded step ladder on that landing, standing against the rail under the window with its feet on the boards.

She sees it at once.

---

It is not the one she put in a porch on the third of December.

It has no strip of dark tape round its second rail. It has no chalk mark on its third tread. The maker's name on the rail is whole. It is a small ladder, shorter than hers by a tread, with paint on one foot, and it lives in this house, on this landing, under this window.

She stands on the stairs and looks at it for a moment longer than she needs to, because since the seventh of January she has looked at every folded ladder in this borough twice, once for what it is and once for what it might be.

On the seventh she saw a step ladder with the same two marks in a back room she was clearing and said out loud that she could not tell whether it was the same one. That one had the tape and the chalk and the maker's name gone down to about half of itself, leaning two inches off the wall under a window, and a man on two steps said that is my dad's, that, and she put no line for it on her pad.

This one is not that one. This one is a house's own ladder, kept on a landing for a window and a loft hatch at the turn.

"That stays as well," the daughter says, following her look. "That's ours. That lives there."

"Right," she says. "I wasn't going to move it."

---

She does the front room first, because she always does the front room first when there is a chair in it that is staying.

She takes the loose paper off the mantel and stacks it for the daughter. She takes the ornaments off the shelves and wraps them in newspaper and puts them in a crate. She takes the cushions off the settee and shakes the dust out of them in the yard, slapping them against the wall by the back door until the dust comes off in clouds. She does not take the blanket off the arm of the chair. She folds the edge of it back where it has slipped and leaves the nap smooth, because a blanket that stays wants leaving straight.

The daughter watches her for a bit.

"You don't want it," the daughter says. "For the rags."

"No."

"It's only a blanket."

"It's on a chair," she says. "I'm not taking a blanket off a chair that's staying. That's the rule. If I take it, it's in my van with my rags, and then it's in a bag with somebody else's blanket that's been there since November, and then I've got two I can't give back instead of one."

The daughter looks at her.

"Fair enough," the daughter says.

---

At the middle of the day they eat sandwiches on the front step.

The white van is at the kerb with the tailgate down. The horse van will come at the end and go away with the lot. The street is quiet.

"How long have you been doing this," the daughter says.

"Long enough to know what not to take," she says.

"My mum said you were good."

"She's kind."

They sit for a bit. A man goes past with a dog. A bus goes past at the end of the road.

"You turned work down before Christmas," the daughter says. "Mum said."

"I turned half a job down for the space," she says. "I had a ladder across the load I couldn't get back to the man I borrowed it from. Twenty-ninth of November. A street off the ring road. A man in a fleece with a tape round his neck. I left it in an open porch on the third of December with no note on it because there was no third place for it, and I have not thought about it since, except I have."

The daughter nods. She does not ask whose it was. Nobody asks whose it was. That is the whole of the borough.

---

In the afternoon she does the landing.

She takes the books off the shelf at the turn and boxes them. She takes the linen off the airing shelf and bags it. She wipes the sill under the window at the turn. She does not touch the folded ladder against the rail.

It would be easy to move it to wipe behind it. It would be easy to take it down to the van to get it out of the way. It would be easy to ask the daughter whether she wants it out. She does none of those, because the daughter has said it lives there, and because a ladder that lives somewhere is not a thing she shifts without being asked, and because she has had a ladder across her load once and turned work down for it and put one in a porch and seen one in a back room she could not place, and she is not starting again on a landing in a terrace in January.

She works round it. The paint on its foot shows where it has stood for years. The boards under it are paler. She wipes up to the feet and no further.

---

The horse van man comes at the end.

He is a man in his fifties with a strap over his shoulder and he goes through the front room and nods at the chair.

"That stays," she says.

"That stays," he says.

"Front room and landing boxes go," she says. "Nothing else."

He takes the crates and the bags down to the van. He does not look at the ladder on the landing. He does not ask about it. She does not name it.

The daughter signs for the load on the step.

"What do I owe you," the daughter says.

She goes to the passenger seat and gets her pad off it. The band is round it and the band is tight and the pad is shut and on the page inside there is the house from the Tuesday and the terrace from today and a figure against each, and there is a line for the front room that is blank.

She writes the figure.

She does not write a line for the blanket. The pad is for work that is paid for. A blanket is not work that is paid for. A box behind a bench is not work that is paid for. A ladder across a load is not work that is paid for.

Three things, and not one of them has been on a page, and the band round that pad is the tidiest object she owns, and it is the tidiest object she owns because of a rule that has nothing to do with tidiness.

---

She shuts the house and the daughter locks it and the horse van goes.

She stands at the back of her own van with the tailgate down and looks at the load. The drawer under the bench is shut. The bolster is on the leads. The parcel is behind the bench under the rags. The bag of rags has somebody else's blanket in it.

And the third thing she cannot place is a piece of aluminium. It is not in her van. It has not been in her van since the third of December. She put it in a porch and left it there. A woman came out of a house and took it in out of the rain and it went into her shed and stayed a fortnight, and then that same woman carried it away from her own house in the dark on a Wednesday and carried it up a road on the Monday after, on her shoulder, and then it stopped being a thing anybody was doing anything with. It stands now folded in a front room at the end of that road, against the wall under the window, with tape round its second rail, and the woman who put it there is not the woman who carried it, and neither of them is the woman writing this down.

She has not found out where it went and she is not going to, and that is the only one of the three where the not-finding-out is her own decision.

"You'll be all right in the spring," the daughter says, locking the door. "With work."

"In the spring," she says, and does not give him a day, because there is no day on it to give and because the van goes where the van goes until it does not. "There is always houses in the spring."

"Right," the daughter says.

She puts her gloves in the door pocket next to the tape and the pen. She gets in and starts it and goes out to the ring road. The heater blows cold and then warm. The wipers clear the glass once and leave it.

There is one thing in this borough with two addresses on it and it is a box under a cloth on the left of a kitchen table at the end of a road, and it has not moved since the twenty-second of November and she has no idea what is in it, and it is the only object in this borough that anybody could put in an envelope and post.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES HAS SORTED A FRONT ROOM WITHOUT TAKING A BLANKET OFF A CHAIR ARM, HAS SPOKEN TO A DAUGHTER WITH KEYS ABOUT WHAT GOES ON A VAN AND WHAT DOES NOT, HAS LEFT A FOLDED STEP LADDER ON A LANDING WHERE IT LIVES BECAUSE IT IS NOT HERS TO MOVE, HAS WRITTEN THE FIGURE FOR THE JOB ON HER PAD AND PUT NO LINE FOR ANYTHING ELSE ON IT, AND HAS SAID IN THE SPRING AND PUT NO DATE ON IT.**
