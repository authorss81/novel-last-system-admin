# Chapter 0528 — Twenty Minutes at the Front

The Friday of the eighth of November 2019, from about half past two in the afternoon until about ten past three, in a row of houses in this borough.

**THE HOUSE IS NUMBER TWENTY-SIX AND IT HAS BEEN EMPTY SINCE 2011, AND BOTH SISTERS HAVE HALF OF IT AND NEITHER HAS LET IT AND NEITHER HAS SOLD IT, AND NEITHER HAS BEEN INSIDE IT SINCE THE FUNERAL, AND THEY HAVE NOT AGREED ABOUT ANYTHING SINCE 1999.**

The sisters are a woman of about seventy and a woman of about sixty-five, and the house was their mother's, and their mother was in it for fifty years, and she died in it in 2011 on a Sunday afternoon with neither of them in the borough.

What this is about is the October of 1999.

Their mother was eighty-one and had come through a bad run of months. A woman from the surgery had said in the September, in the room at the front of number sixteen, in front of both of them, that there was a thing about going up and down in the dark that she would want somebody to look at. She had used the word twice and had not said what the word was.

The older sister said that night that their mother was to be moved to be nearer her. There was a place she had in mind. She had not rung it.

The younger sister said that she was not going anywhere.

That is the whole of the disagreement, and it has never once been about what anything costs. Neither of them has brought the price of a thing into it in twenty years, and there is a great deal of price in the arrangement of two sisters and one empty house, and neither of them has ever mentioned it.

What the older sister held was that a woman of eighty-one who has slept in the same four rooms for fifty years is finished, and that the finishing would happen in a strange room inside a fortnight, and that this was the only argument that mattered.

What the younger sister held was that their mother had said, in the August of 1998, in the room at the front, that she was not going to be put anywhere, and that a woman of eighty-one is allowed to say that and be right, and that the whole of the trouble would be that the saying of it would be overridden by two people who loved her.

Both of those are good. That has never been the difficulty. The difficulty is that they are about different things, and each of them has known that since about 1999, and neither has ever said it to the other.

They have not quarrelled about it. They have spoken on a Wednesday and a Sunday for twenty years, and there has not been a raised voice between them since about 1993, and different people who have met them at different times have both described them as close.

What there is instead is a shape. Once a year there is a Sunday when the subject comes up between them without either of them arranging it, and it comes up in the same words, and it lasts about four minutes, and then one of them mentions the weather. It happened on the Sunday before last. It will happen again.

There is a woman at number fourteen who lived there from 1988 until 2013 and who was in that house on a great many evenings in 1999 because their mother liked her and did not want the afternoons to themselves. She is a woman of about eighty-one. She told the older sister something outside a shop on the Tuesday of the fifth of November and has not mentioned it since and is not going to.

**WHAT SHE TOLD HER WAS A SENTENCE THAT HER SISTER SAID IN THAT HOUSE IN 1999 THAT NOBODY ELSE IN THIS BOROUGH HAS HEARD, AND THE OLDER SISTER HAS SAID NOTHING TO ANYBODY ABOUT IT, INCLUDING THE SISTER IT IS ABOUT.**

It was said in the room at the front of number sixteen in the middle of October 1999, about three weeks after the argument, and the woman at number fourteen heard it because she was in that room and their mother was in the other one and the two sisters did not know the woman was there. The younger sister had a cloth in her hand at the time and the sentence has stayed in the woman's head in the way that a sentence stays in a head when the people who said it did not know it was being heard.

What the sentence was is this: if it comes to it, I will do it myself, and I will not be the one who says so out loud.

The woman at number fourteen said it to the older sister in front of the shop, and then said that she had sat on it since and had never let it out to anybody, and that she was saying it now because she was eighty-one and there would not be many more of those Tuesdays.

The older sister has not let it out at all, least of all to the sister it is about. She has not rung. She has not written. She has not been in a room with her since the Sunday before last, when the subject came up in the same words it always comes up in, and the younger sister said, "I am not going anywhere," and the older sister said, "Right," and the rain came on and they went in.

What the older sister has done with the sentence is put it in a different place than the one it was in. For twenty years she has held the shape in this: that she was the one who wanted their mother moved and that she was therefore the one who was wrong, because her sister wanted her to stay and their mother stayed, and everybody in that family including her mother was quite certain that staying was the right thing, and the person who said the other thing was her.

She has had that shape for twenty years and it is a thing that can be carried.

She has had the sentence for three days and it says that her sister had already decided in November 1999 to move their mother, and had decided to be the one who did it, and had then gone on for twelve years in that house saying in front of everybody that their mother was not going anywhere. Which means the two of them did not disagree, or did not disagree in the way either of them has always said, and it means the sister who argued for the staying was the sister who had already made the arrangements, and it means that the one who has been wrong for twenty years is the one who said it out loud.

She has not worked out what to do with that, and there is no way of holding it that brings the twenty years back.

---

She went up that row on the Friday of the eighth of November at about half past two in the afternoon and she stood at the front of number sixteen.

There was no reason for it. She has been asked by nobody, she wanted nothing from the house, the grass had been cut about a week before and there is a man who comes, and she had told her sister on the Sunday before last that she was going to a shop in the town, and she went up the row and stopped at number sixteen because it is there, the way you stop at a thing on the way to somewhere.

She stood at the bottom of the front path with nothing at her feet and she looked at the front of the house.

The brick is a dirty yellow. There is a porch that has been painted about four times and the last lot was a cream that nobody chose. At the top of the gable there is the mark of a guttering that was replaced in about 2004, and the old fixings are still in it. There is a patch over the pipe at the left-hand side in a different colour of brick from the rest. None of it is wrong. All of it is exactly as it was, and the older sister looked at all of it and thought about none of it.

The trees at the end of the row have gone over.

At about five past three a boy of about ten came up the row on a bicycle, too fast, the way they do, and he slowed and stopped about thirty feet short of number sixteen and sat there with one foot on the ground and looked at her.

She looked back at him.

Nobody said anything. The boy stayed where he was for perhaps a minute and a half and then he went on up the row and she heard him go and then nothing.

He was still in her head when the woman at number eighteen came out, which is the house on the other side. That woman is in her forties and she has been there since 2016 and she does not know the older sister, and she came down her own path because she was going out, and she saw somebody standing in front of number sixteen in the middle of a Friday afternoon.

"You waiting on somebody?" she said.

"No," the older sister said.

The woman at eighteen said the house had been empty a long time and that she had wondered about it, and the older sister said it was her mother's and had been empty since 2011, and the woman said, "Oh," and then said the guttering on it was in a bad way and that there was a man in the town who did that, and the older sister said her sister would see to it, and the woman said, "Right," and stood there a moment, and then said the boy on the bicycle belonged to the people at number twelve and that he was fast and that you got used to it.

The older sister said, "Yes."

The woman at twenty-eight looked at her front path, and at the house, and said, "It's a nice house, that," and the older sister said, "It is," and the woman said, "You going to do something with it?" and the older sister said, "No," and the woman said, "Fair enough," and went off up the row.

That was four minutes and it was the whole conversation and the woman at twenty-eight was quite pleasant about it and asked nothing that was not asked.

After the woman had gone the older sister stood where she had been standing and did not go up the front path, and she has not gone up a front path anywhere for eight years and there was no particular reason on that Friday why she should have.

She had thought about it on the way up the row. She had thought about it at the end of the row and at the corner and at the mouth of it, and what she had thought about was that if she went up the path and put her hand on the thing that is the front of a house, then this Friday afternoon would turn into something, and she would not be able to be the person who walked away afterwards without it being the thing everybody knew about, and her sister is sixty-five and gets up at six and there is no way of putting a thing like that back into a person.

And under that, which she did not say to herself in those words, was that she does not know what she would say if she got in.

So she stood at the bottom of the front path for about the best part of twenty minutes with nothing at her feet, and at some point in the middle of it she understood that the twenty years are not going to be settled by anything she does today, and at some point after that she understood that the sentence the woman at number fourteen gave her on Wednesday is not going to be settled by anything she does today either, and she stood there anyway, and at about ten past three she turned round, walked to the end of the row, turned again, and went home by the other way.

**THE BOY WENT UP THE ROW AND DID NOT COME BACK DOWN IT. SHE WENT HOME BY THE OTHER WAY, AND IT HAD BEEN TWENTY MINUTES.**
