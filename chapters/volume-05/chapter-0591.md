# Chapter 0591 — The Bit in the Middle

The Thursday of the third of September 2020, from about half past one in the afternoon until about half past eight in the evening, in a hall off a road in this borough.

**HE IS BEING PAID FOUR HUNDRED POUNDS TO BE IN THAT ROOM FOR TWO HOURS AND HE SAYS ONE SENTENCE IN THE MIDDLE OF IT THAT IS NOT ON ANYTHING, AND NINE PEOPLE HEAR IT, AND IT IS ABOUT SOMEBODY SITTING IN THE THIRD ROW, AND NOBODY IN THAT ROOM KNEW IT WAS COMING INCLUDING HIM.**

The hall is a municipal one on a road off a bigger road. There are about sixty folding chairs in it and the ones in front are full and the ones at the back are not, and on that Thursday afternoon at about half past one there were a good few dozen people in it and a man on a low stage at the front with a clip-on microphone and a card in his other hand.

He is a man in his early fifties and he does this four or five days a week across the borough and the county and he is good at it and he is not lying in anything he says up there, and the council pays him four hundred pounds for the afternoon and the tea, which is more than the hall gets for the hall.

The subject is how to fill your days when you have retired. It is a talk that has been given in this borough before and the hall was not full last time.

---

The first hour and a half is the material. It is very good material. He has a story about a man he met in a supermarket queue about a set of keys, and a thing about the difference between being busy and having something to do, and a slide he does not need because the room is too bright for it.

The woman in her early forties is in the third row, on the aisle, with a daughter of nineteen beside her who has come along because it is the school holidays and there is nowhere else for her to be. The woman came because her mother died in the February and she has been at home since and the letter said it was about something for people who had recently stopped working.

The daughter came to be polite. She has her phone in her hand under the seat of the chair in front of her and she is about a third of the way through a text to somebody.

Nobody in that hall knows either of them. Nobody in that hall has seen either of them before. That is a thing about that hall and about every hall like it.

---

The sentence is said at about four o'clock.

He is halfway through the bit about the supermarket queue, or rather he has just finished it, and the room has done what a room does, which is a short laugh and then settling, and he is standing with the card down at his side and he is not looking at the card.

He says:

"And I'll say this to the one person in this room who'll know it. You will be all right. Not this year. But you will."

And then he goes straight on, without a break, into the next thing on the card, which is about the service that goes into town at a quarter to ten.

That is the sentence. It is nine words and it is not on the card and it is not in the notes and he has not said it in this hall before and he will not say it in this hall again.

Nine people heard it and one of them was the person it was about.

Here is how the count of nine happens, and it is not a count anybody took. The room was doing what a room that size does when a man who is being paid says something with his own voice in it, which is that there are about nine seconds of total silence and then everybody carries on.

The woman in her early forties heard it. The daughter beside her heard it. A man in his late sixties three rows back heard it and turned round afterwards to see who had been looking at, and there was nobody looking at anybody, which is what happens when a sentence like that goes into a room, because it is not aimed at anybody in any way a room can read.

Two women in their sixties on the other side of the aisle heard it. A man in his early forties in the second row heard it and thought it was a bit much and went back to his programme. A woman of seventy-eight in the front row heard it and did not hear it, because she had brought the hearing aid she uses in church and had not put it in.

A man in his late thirties at the back heard it and made a note on the back of his programme, because he is the sort of person who does that, and he has never in his life written down anything anybody has said to him in that hall.

The woman who runs the refreshment table at the back heard it over the urn going.

And that is nine, and none of the nine of them knew it was about the woman in the third row, and two of the nine have never given it another thought, and one of the nine is going to think about it in a yard later in the week.

The woman in her early forties did not move.

She sat with her hands in her lap with her card folded in two and she did not look round and she did not say anything to the nineteen-year-old beside her, and the nineteen-year-old did not look up from the phone, because a girl of nineteen hears a great deal of nothing at half past three on a Thursday afternoon.

She sat there for another hour and ten minutes while a man who is being paid four hundred pounds talked about the service at a quarter to ten, and then she stood up at about twenty to six while everybody was putting programmes under their chairs, and she came down the aisle and out of the door at the side, and she did not go past the refreshment table and she did not speak to anybody.

She was not upset. That is worth saying because it is the part that would be written down by somebody. She was not upset and she has not told anybody and there is nobody in that hall she would tell.

What she did, and this is the whole of the afternoon as far as she is concerned, was sit in her car in the yard behind that hall for about ten minutes and then drive home, and the sentence was still true when she got there.

**A PAID MAN SAID NINE WORDS HE WAS NOT ASKED FOR IN THE MIDDLE OF SOMETHING ELSE AND NINE PEOPLE HEARD THEM AND THE ONE PERSON THEY WERE ABOUT WENT OUT OF THE SIDE DOOR AT TWENTY TO SIX AND WENT HOME, AND NOBODY IN THAT HALL KNEW IT WAS GOING TO BE SAID, AND HE HAS NOT THOUGHT ABOUT IT SINCE.**
