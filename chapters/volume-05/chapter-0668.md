# Chapter 0668 — The Stool Against The Wall

Friday the seventeenth of September 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house at number nine from dark until late with her sister at the kitchen table, and the whole of the night is a stool she does not move into use.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES DUSTS ROUND AN ASH STOOL AGAINST THE WALL OF HER OWN FRONT ROOM WITHOUT LIFTING IT OFF THE FLOOR, ANSWERS A QUESTION ABOUT THAT ROOM WITH SOMETHING TRUE AND SMALL, LEAVES THE STOOL WHERE IT IS LOOKED AT, AND DOES NOT GO UP THAT ROAD.**

She is a woman in her late fifties. She is at home all night. Her sister is at the kitchen table for most of it.

---

She comes in from lates with her coat damp and her shoes loud on the tiles, and the house smells of soap and toast, and her sister looks up from a form from the council spread over the kitchen table with a pen in her hand and a mug beside it.

"How bad was it?"

"Bad enough. Two of them in."

"Two?"

"And a man who wasn't booked, which was the best bit, because he was the only one who said he was sorry about the parking."

"Was it the school run?"

"It's always the school run." She hangs her coat on the back of the door. The back door is the one she fixed herself with folded card packed at the hinge, and it shuts true now without lifting, and she shuts it behind her out of habit even though there is nothing outside. "You all right?"

"I'm all right. Sit down, I've been on this since six."

She sits, and her sister pushes the mug across, and the tea in it has gone cold and she drinks it anyway, and they do not talk about the form for a while. They talk about the bus being late and the rain on the windows and the price of the bin, and her sister says the bin is going up in the spring and there is nothing anybody can do, and she says that is the first sensible thing anybody has said to her all week.

---

Her sister keeps the form in front of her like a tablecloth, and she works down a column of it, and then she turns the pen over twice and looks up.

"They've put a light in at number twelve."

"Number twelve."

"Twelve. Up the end."

"Right."

"They've had one up. Proper one, on a bracket, by the gate." Her sister taps the pen on the form. "Not the sort we had at number four when the lamplighter came round in the afternoon."

"So somebody's asked them for it."

"Somebody's asked them for it. Mains light. There's a woman there, seventy-something, hasn't asked anybody for anything in her life, I'd bet on it."

"She'd have asked somebody. You can't get a mains light without asking."

"You can if you don't, and then somebody else does and mentions your name, which is what the woman next door to me did in June and got a letter about it. 'Thank you for your interest in the following property.'"

"Then the somebody else has your name on paper and you've not asked for a thing, which is the whole trouble with it."

"Yeah." Her sister goes back to the form. "It'd be the girl at ten, wouldn't it. She's round there all the time."

"Round there is different from up there."

"Round there is three doors, not thirty."

She stands at her own sink with a cloth in her hand and listens without asking, because asking would make it a thing she was pursuing. Then she asks one question, and it is only a place.

"Which twelve?"

Her sister looks up. "On the water side. Past the shop with the ironmonger's on it, then the last row."

"Right."

"You don't know it?"

"Know it," she says. "Never been."

Her sister puts the pen down and looks at her properly, and there is a space of a second or two in which she could have asked the next thing, and she does not, and instead she says, "You could go and see the light."

"I could."

"You could just walk up."

"I'm not going to walk up."

"You might as well. It's a light."

"Forty years on that street and I've not walked up it once, and it's not a light that's going to change my mind on a Friday." She turns round and puts the cloth in the bucket. "Is that your line or theirs?"

Her sister looks at the form. "Mine. Why?"

"Does it say what's wrong with it?"

"It says what's wanted. That's a lamp on a bracket outside the property." Her sister reads a bit further down. "It says any resident may request. It doesn't say what happens if you request one and there's nothing wrong with yours."

"That's what I thought."

"There's a box. That's all that is, there's a box for it."

"Then that's the answer to the question," she says, and her sister looks at her, and she picks the cloth up out of the bucket and wrings it, and the subject is finished, and neither of them mentions the light again that night.

---

On the fridge is a pad with six things on it in the order she would do them, with the first one crossed off, twice, in her own hand. She wipes the worktop and the sill over the sink, and she sweeps the floor into a pile by the back door and leaves the pile there for the morning, and she does not go into the front room.

She works out, standing there with the cloth, what she worked out on the twenty-third of August, that the man who has been in that passage is the man who made her stool, because the woman at number twelve told her on a step in May that he made it himself on a bench in a unit off the industrial estate.

Three rails oak and one ash. She knows the pale one by sight now without going to look.

She has never gone up that road. She is still the woman who has never gone up that road.

---

She goes through to the front room with the dusting cloth, because the front room is the one room that has not been done since the spring, and it is the room the stool is in.

The stool is against the wall opposite the window, with a chair on one side of it and a settee on the other, and there is nowhere in that room to sit that is not the front of somebody's house. It is not a place to sit. It is a place to look at a thing.

Her sister stays at the kitchen table with the form and does not follow, and does not give the stool a name or a story.

She dusts the rails and the seat and the feet without lifting it right off the floor, rocking it a little to get under each foot, and she puts it back square to the wall and checks it with her eye the way you check a picture.

Against that is the thank you she cannot get at, for a stool given for nothing by a stranger she has never asked a question of, and carried out of a kitchen into a front room, because the kitchen is where you use a thing and the front room is where you look at one.

She stands back with the cloth in her hand and looks at it between the chair and the settee, and it looks right there and it looks wrong to sit on. It is the only thing in that room that anybody made.

She does not sit on it. Nobody sits on it. That is the whole of its use, and she has known that since August, and she is not going to be the person who finds out otherwise on a Friday night.

---

Her sister calls through about a line on the form and she goes back to the kitchen and reads it over her sister's shoulder and tells her what she thinks it means, which is that they want somebody to have been in the house, and her sister writes that in.

Then her sister says, "You've not sat in there since the summer, have you."

"No."

"Since the stool came."

"Since the stool came."

Her sister waits for the other half of it, and the other half of it is that a man came and made her a stool and did not say why, and she cannot say that, because she does not know why, and she cannot say that she has not asked why, because that is the whole of what has happened and it is not a thing she wants laid out on a table with a form on it.

"It's a cold room," she says. "That's all it is."

"It is a cold room. You've not had the fire lit in there since Easter."

"It's not worth lighting for a room you walk through."

"You walk through it to get to the front door."

"Then it's not worth lighting on that basis either."

Her sister puts the pen in the clip and closes the form, and that is the end of it, and neither of them ever refers to it again, and the woman at number nine is the one who asked a question in the end, and it was about the fire, and she is the one who has never sat in there.

---

They drink tea at the table with the form between them, and the talk goes to ordinary things, to rotas and to a back door that shuts true now, and to a curtain rail at number ten that wants mending and a drill that is loud in a thin wall, and her sister says she will do it on Sunday and she says there is no rush on it, and her sister says there is no rush on anything.

She does not mention the stool. Her sister does not mention the stool. It is in the next room against a wall and they both know it is there.

At late night her sister goes back to number ten with the form in a bag, and she bolts the back door and checks the front, and she goes through to the front room once more before bed.

The stool is still against the wall opposite the window. The chair is on one side of it and the settee is on the other. She turns the lamp off in there and leaves the door open a little, the way she always does.

She gets ready for bed with the radio on low, and she thinks about the woman at number twelve eating at a kitchen table under a window on a green chair with a cushion on it, and about a gate standing open on a road with nobody on it, and about a form with a box on it for a request anybody can make.

She cannot get at the thank you for the stool and cannot ask anybody anything, and asking her sister one question about a place in August was the nearest she has got, and tonight she got as far as asking about a fire.

She is still the woman who has never gone up that road, and she will still be that woman in the morning.

---

She writes nothing down. The pad on the fridge stays as it was, six things with the first one crossed off, twice.

She is aware, standing at the fridge with the door open, that the list has been on that door since the spring, and that the first thing on it is the back door, and that the back door was done in about an hour on a wet Tuesday in March with a folded card and a hammer and no help at all, and that she has never once made herself cross it off properly, and that crossing it off twice was the whole of the ceremony.

The second thing on that pad is the front room, and it has been second since March, and it is a room with a stool in it and a chair on one side of the stool and a settee on the other, and she has never once put a date on it, and she knows exactly why she has never once put a date on it, and she shuts the fridge.

The woman at number ten mends what is hers without knocking up the road. The woman who clears houses keeps a drawer shut in a van. The man who built the stool leaves a drawer open in another van.

None of them is in this house tonight, and none of them has been told any of this.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES HAS GOT THROUGH A NIGHT WITHOUT MOVING A STOOL FROM WHERE IT IS LOOKED AT INTO WHERE IT WOULD BE USED, AND HAS ANSWERED A QUESTION ABOUT HER OWN FRONT ROOM WITH SOMETHING TRUE AND SMALL, AND WITHOUT GOING UP A ROAD SHE HAS NEVER GONE UP, AND THE STOOL IS STILL THREE RAILS OAK AND ONE ASH AGAINST THE WALL OF HER OWN FRONT ROOM.**
