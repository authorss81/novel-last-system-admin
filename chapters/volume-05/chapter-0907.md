# Chapter 0907 — Down The Rail Bore

Sunday the fifth of May 2024, at the fence rail at the side of number nine from mid-morning until the sun leaves the timber. The bore in the end of a rail.

**SHE HAS A FENCE RAIL OF TIMBER AT THE SIDE OF NUMBER NINE WITH A BORED HOLE IN ITS END AND SHE MEANS TO PUSH THE THUMB ALONE INTO THAT BORE WITH FINGERS FOLDED TO THE PALM AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING IS AT THE SIDE PASSAGE FROM THE STREET WITH A BASKET ON HER ARM.**

The rail came to that side when the fence went up, and the posts each side of it have been tarred once while the rail has kept its place.

It is a rail of pine about five feet long, with the top edge rounded off to throw rain and the end cut square where it meets the post.

She wants the bore in that square end, where the auger went in for a dowel that was cut short and put aside.

A bore in an end reads best by thumb, because the eye sees the mouth and the thumb learns the bottom out of sight.

So the thumb alone enters the bore in the rail end with all fingers folded to the palm.

The timber round the mouth feels dry and warm, the way pine dries by mid-morning, and the inside drags on the skin where dust lines it.

The thumb pushes in about a joint and stops on something soft. Not timber. Dust, packed loose at first and then firm further in, grey and dry, with a flake of bark in it and a nail point rusted at one side.

She turns the thumb a little, and the dust turns with it for half a turn and then locks where it was, because dust in a blind bore packs down the more it is turned.

What stops it further in is soil, washed in off the top of the rail, firm and dark, with sand in it that grits under the nail.

So the bore runs deeper than the thumb by a good inch, with dust at the mouth and soil at the bottom, and the bottom is what keeps the bore from taking a dowel now.

The woman who does her shopping comes to the side passage from the street with a basket on her arm and does not set it down.

She stood in the passage with the basket hooked over her elbow and looked at the rail and then at the house and back again, and she shifted the basket to the other arm and stood there a while longer, and then she went on up the passage with both hands on the basket and left the rail where it was.

---

A rail bored for a dowel that was cut short is a common thing on a side, because a fence is framed and then reframed, and each framing leaves its own marks.

The bore went in on the same day the rail was cut, with the same auger that bored the posts, and the auger ran true for the posts and wandered for the rail, which is why the mouth sits a fraction high of the middle.

Pine takes an auger clean when the timber sits flat to the bench, and tears it when the timber lifts at one end, and the rail lifted that day on a chip under it.

That day sits years back, when the side was to have a second rail and the second rail was to be doweled through the post with pegs.

The second rail was cut short in the yard and used for a shelf by the back door, and the bore was left open to the weather.

An open bore in pine does not rot the rail. Rain runs in and runs out again, and the sides go dark and then go flat with age, and the bore becomes part of the rail the way a knot becomes part of it.

What fills it fills from the top down. Dust off the path at the mouth, soil off the rail in the middle, and sand blown in by wind at the bottom to sit where water leaves it.

The sand at the bottom lies loose on top and firm underneath, with the firm part as hard as the timber round it.

It has settled a hair away from the sides all round, because sand shrinks as it dries and pine moves with the seasons, and the hair of air between is what lets the bore breathe in wet weather.

She puts the thumb in again and feels how the dust sits. Loose on top and settled underneath, with the settled part dark where rain sat in it.

A blind bore with dust loose in it will take a screw for a month. The screw cuts into the dust and the dust locks round it, and the fixing lasts till the first long rain and then works loose when the timber swells.

A blind bore with soil firm in it will not take iron at all. Soil swells with rain and shrinks with sun, and iron in soil moves every week until it falls out.

A blind bore with sand firm at the bottom will take a dowel if the sand is picked out first. The dowel goes through to sound timber and the timber spreads the load, and the rail carries it the way it would carry a knot.

That is why the bore was left open and the dowel put aside. The man who bored it saw the auger wander, and kept the dowel for the shelf, and left the bore to the rain.

The timber of the rail stays flat where the top edge meets the sky, with no soft place in it and no shake along its face.

Pine goes dark where water sits in it because the sap in the rain meets the sour stuff in the pine and stains it, and the stain goes in a little way and then stops where the timber turns flat and hard. Sour pine smells faintly of turpentine after rain, and the scent leaves by noon as the face dries flat again. The smell stays longest inside the bore, where sun does not reach to bake it out.

That is why the bore mouth shows dark all round and the rail top only at the drip line. The bore takes the drip off the post and keeps it, while the top throws it off down the face.

An auger wanders in pine faster than in oak because pine grows soft in spring and hard in autumn, and each ring turns the iron a little toward the soft.

On this bore the iron turned up toward the spring growth, so the bottom tilts up a hair and the dust sits deeper on the low side than on the high.

---

The iron of the post nails stays bright where the heads meet the air, and the stone of the footings stays flat where the posts meet the soil.

The screws that face the rail sit in slots choked with paint, with the slots running across the pull so the driver bites. Slotted screws bed flat where the head meets the timber, and each head throws a half-moon of rust where rain sits under it. The rust runs down the flat rail face in thin streaks and stops where the timber turns hard, and each streak marks one winter of shrink and swell.

She draws the thumb out of the bore and rubs the dust off on the side of the rail, and studies the end from the passage for what the square gives away.

At eye level the bore mouth looks like a dark spot in flat pine, round and true from the front, with the flat end grain tight all round it.

Seen slantwise the mouth shows oval, where the auger broke the edge on its way in, while the rail end stays flat round it.

Round from the front and oval from the side, and the thumb knows which part would take wood, namely the bottom that was cut on the day the bench stood flat, unless the sand is left where the wind put it.
