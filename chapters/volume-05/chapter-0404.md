# Chapter 0404 — The Awning

Wednesday the eighteenth of April 2018, from about half past seven in the morning until about six in the evening, a parade, a shop off it, and a hall on a meter at the bottom of it, in Tideglass.

**THE WIND GOT UNDER AN AWNING ON THAT PARADE AT TWENTY PAST ONE ON THE AFTERNOON OF THE EIGHTEENTH OF APRIL AND THE MAN WHO PUTS SHUTTERS UP WENT UP A PAIR OF STEPS AND PUT IT BACK BY TWO, AND NOBODY HAS SAID ANYTHING ABOUT IT, AND THE SAME TWO WRONG THINGS ON THAT PARADE ARE STILL WRONG, AND A LINE UNDER THE LAST ENTRY IN A BOOK IN THIS BOROUGH IS BLANK AND WAS NOT TOUCHED.**

That awning is on a steel frame about nine foot out from the wall and it has been on that shop for about thirty years, and it goes up and down on a windlass with a crank and a chain. It has a rail across the front of it and a bar across the front of that, and it is a green one that has gone grey, and it is the only one on that parade.

A wind came up the river out of the estuary at about one o'clock and held for about half an hour. It did nothing to a tree on that parade and nothing to a bin or a sign or a line of washing, and then the front rail of that awning came up about a foot, and the bar across the front of it came down onto the shutter with a noise two doors away.

---

It was heard first by the woman who works in a shop.

She is on that counter from ten until about five, and she was at the near end of it at about ten past one with a roll of paper and a packet of nuts, and she went to the door of that shop and put her head out.

The awning was hanging about a foot and a half down and the front bar was against the shutter and it was moving with the wind, about three inches each way, and the chain on the left-hand side had come out of its cleat and was swinging loose about nine inch below it.

She stood in that doorway for about half a minute and she did not go out into it.

At that moment the man who puts shutters up was four doors away at the top of it, on the other side, working the last four of them, and she could see the top of his van from where she was standing.

He came down that parade at about ten past one with his steps over his shoulder and he did not walk fast.

He put the steps up outside that shop at about two foot from the kerb and went up them and got the top of his steps level with the front bar, and he got both hands on the windlass.

He put his foot on the crank and wound it up, and the awning came up about a foot, and the front rail came off the shutter.

The chain had come out of its cleat on the left and had dropped. He put it back over the cleat and got the pin through it and wound it up about a foot and a half more, which is where that awning sits when it is down.

Then he came down those steps and got a piece of cord out of his pocket, which is a thing he has done about four times a year since about 1991, and he tied the front bar of that awning to the ring of the frame on the left-hand side with two turns and a half hitch, about two foot above where the bar sits, so that the front cannot lift and the bar cannot come down.

He tested it with the heel of his hand. He wound it down and up twice to see whether that windlass would hold it, and it did, and he let it down and left it.

He was up and down those steps and on that awning for about nine minutes and he was gone along that parade at about two o'clock, and the whole of the rest of that day was already finished.

Then she came out and stood below his steps and looked up at the awning for about ten seconds.

He was already back on the other side of that van getting his things.

Neither of them said one word. He did not say it was his fault and she did not say what that awning had been doing an hour before, and she did not go back in and stand in that doorway again, and she has not mentioned it to anybody, and there is nobody else in this borough she would mention it to.

He has not mentioned it to anybody either. He does the whole length of that parade every weekday morning, and he has been asked about nothing on it in the last nine years, and he is not going to start.

---

Two things on that parade are wrong and were still wrong at six o'clock that evening.

The upper one is the shutter at the top, four doors above the awning he tied. It has been standing open a foot above that pavement since the twelfth of February. He has to put his hand under the bottom rail and lift it and let it go over the catch, twice every morning of his working week, and it goes over that catch and hangs there.

The other is a slab about ninety foot below the awning, in the middle of the footway, eighteen inch by twelve, and the whole of it is standing an inch and a half proud of the bed it sits in, with a step of its own along the front edge of it. It was laid in 2011.

Going out at about half past three the woman who works in a shop went over that lip and put her hand on the frame of the door going in, which she did not need to do and which she has not done on any other day of her life at that door.

Neither of those has been reported to anybody. There is nothing in this borough to report either of them on.

Four answers to one question went round this borough nine months ago and are still going round, and there is still no way of setting one of them beside another, because the four of them are not talking about the same sort of object and never were. Nobody at that counter on that Wednesday said which of them they hold. Nobody has taken one of them back. Nothing new has come out of anybody in a fortnight, and what the four of them are answers to has still not been said by anybody and has not been written down anywhere.

The hall at the bottom of that parade is on a meter and it was shut on that Wednesday, and the man who does that hall went past it at about half past four on his way home, unlocked the door, put his head in, shut it again and went on. The light in there is on a clock and the clock switched it off at four by itself.

There are about nine people on that parade most days and there were nine of them out on the eighteenth of April, and every one of them was where they had business being, and not one of the nine was holding anything at all for any of the other eight, least of all for the woman who works in a shop, who spent about nine minutes of that afternoon looking at the bar of a canvas awning from ten foot below it and said nothing to anybody about it.

Behind a door in a block by the water that has never caught on its latch there is a shelf, and on the shelf there is a book with nineteen entries written in it, and under the entry at the bottom there is a line that has been open for one thousand two hundred and thirty-nine days.

Nobody has been in that room on that day or on the day before it or on the day after it. Nobody counted anything in it, in that room or in that building, in the fortnight before or the fortnight after, and the number is written down nowhere in this borough.

One woman in this borough has ever held that book, and she has not walked that corridor since before Christmas, and she is not going, and nobody has asked her about the bottom of that page, because nobody in this borough knows there is a bottom of that page.

**HE CAME DOWN OFF THAT STEPS, PUT THE THING BACK WHERE HE FOUND IT AND WENT ON UP THE PARADE WITH IT OVER HIS SHOULDER, AND SHE STOOD IN HER DOORWAY AND WATCHED HIM GO.**
