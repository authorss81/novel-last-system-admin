# Chapter 1000 — One Board The Wrong Way Up

Undated in this draft.

**THE BACK ROOM AT NUMBER NINE HAS ITS FLOOR TWO INCHES DOWN AT THE FAR CORNER.** He goes up the frontage at eight in the morning with the bag over his shoulder and the yard door of number nine standing open off the yard the way it stands open in all weathers, and he goes through it into the scullery and across the scullery and into the kitchen and along the passage to the back room, and the house lets him do all of that, because a house with nobody in it does not ask a man anything.

He stood in this room in the autumn and looked at the floor and gave her the number, which was nine boards, two joists, and somewhere between a day and a half and two days, and he has been in three rooms of this house since and has not gone past this door. The room is the one she was born in and it has been empty long enough to have lost the shape of a bedroom without losing the marks of one: strips of paper came off it in the size of the paper and not the size of anything else, and the plaster underneath is a patchwork of a dozen shades of pale and old glue, and one corner still has a strip about the width of a hand with a rose in it.

The boards go down all round the room, and from the doorway you can see that the far corner is not where it should be. One joist has come off its seat at the wall end and has dropped, and the boards over it have dropped with it and stayed down, and the gap between the boards and the skirting at that corner is about two inches, which is a great deal of floor to be wrong by, and you can see it from the doorway because the boards are bare and the light comes in low and flat across them from the front room. The small mirror is still standing against the chimney breast where he put it back in the autumn, propped at the corner above the firebrick that came away, and it has been standing there for four months with the corner of the room reflected in it and nobody to look. He goes down on his knees on the boards, which is not a thing he does easily, and he gets the end of his bar under the fourth board along the wall at the dropped corner and lifts it, and then he lifts the one next to it, and the gap underneath is black and about two inches deep at the wall and comes up to nothing at the joist line, and he can feel along it with two fingers of his left hand as far as the hand will go and no further. The floorboards under the rest of it are the original boards and they are about an inch thick and the nails in them are driven in a line as neat as any nails he has ever seen, which is the one thing about this floor that gives away that somebody was proud of it, and he has spent ten years walking over it and being pleased with his own line and there was a woman in this room for a good many years before he came and she never once looked down. The corner of the skirting has come away from the board behind it as well and the plaster above has opened along a crack that is not a lath crack, because it is out of the corner, and it follows the line of the joist exactly, and anybody who had been in this room in the winter would have seen it, and nobody was, because she does not sleep in this room and has not for some years.

"Ask me when you have got something at stake."

He puts it out loud in an empty house, and it goes about eight feet and comes back off the paper, and nobody answers it, and it is the answer to the condition she set him in the front room at nine a fortnight ago, and the answer is that he has got something at stake and it is the two inches and not the man. It took him a fortnight and three houses and a bed frame and a wall at a weigh end of a yard to arrive at that, and every step of the way somebody else was in the room, and the only person who could have told him it was the wrong thing to arrive at is four days gone up the water with a case and a basket. He puts the boards down over the joist and then lifts the second one up again, because he has put it down the wrong way up, with the sap face up and the heart face down, which makes the whole corner of the room a different colour from the rest of the floor, and a woman would see that before she had looked twice, and it is the one thing about this job and so he takes it up and turns it and puts it back the right way and stands over the corner and looks at it and it is the same colour as the boards round it.

That is all he does about the joist. He does not take it up, and he does not put a piece under it, and he does not shim anything, because he decided one morning last week in a cold scullery with a bag at his feet that he would not begin anything nobody had asked him for, and he has now broken that rule once, and he has broken it for a corner of floor in a house where nobody lives, and he has not broken it for the wall at the weigh end of a yard that a man asked him for and wants priced by Friday.

He stands up and his knees tell him about it and he stands with his back against the door frame for a minute, and while he is standing there he works out, without meaning to, what the job will cost a man who wanted to do it properly, which is the joist and two days and about the price of the number he gave her in the autumn, and he has not asked her for it and she has not asked him for it and she will be back down that path in five weeks and find a corner of floor that has been lifted and put back and a man at the frontage who did it for nothing. The joist along that corner is an old sawn pine joist about nine inches deep and the top of it is even along the whole of it except at that one place, with nothing in it anywhere that rises, and the hollow in the top of it is about the thickness of a coin from the wall to the middle and then stops, and the floor gave there and the two inches are there.

The work he has just done will take a man about half an hour and it is the only work on this frontage that matters this week, and he has done it well, and not one page in this street will carry it. The six jobs of his season would go on a page at the unit in six lines and there is not one page in this borough that will have his name on it anywhere at all, and he has known that since the middle of the afternoon on Thursday, and he has got here anyway, because the floor was down two inches and she is not in it. The corner of the chimney breast stands in the far corner of the room with the small mirror propped against it in the place it has been in since the autumn, and the corner behind the mirror is the same two inches and a hand's width it was when he came to this room in the autumn and read it with a lath and a bit of looking glass, and it will be that in six weeks unless somebody gets his shoulder under that floor.
