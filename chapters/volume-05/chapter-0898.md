# Chapter 0898 — Between Two Path Boards

Monday the first of April 2024, along the path boards at the side of the bed at number ten after the rain has gone off the timber. Between two boards and what is under the slot.

**SHE HAS TWO PATH BOARDS LAID ALONG THE SIDE OF THE BED AT NUMBER TEN AND SHE MEANS TO LAY THE SIDE OF THE FIRST FINGER ALONG THE SLOT BETWEEN THEM AND DRAW IT THE LENGTH WITH THE NAIL BRUSHING THE TIMBER AND HER SISTER IS AT THE YARD GATE FROM OUTSIDE STANDING ON THE PATH WITH A PARCEL UNDER HER ARM.**

Those boards have lain along that side for two summers, and the bed beside them has been dug twice while they have sat where they were put.

They are boards of pine about six inches wide, with the grain running along them and the ends cut square, and they lie end to end with a slot between them about a finger wide.

She wants what is under that slot, where the foot never lands and the rain runs through.

A slot between two boards is the one place on a path where a finger can go and learn something, because the tops of the boards show the wear and the slot shows what the wear stands on.

So the side of the first finger goes down into the slot and draws along it, with the nail brushing one side all the way.

The boards are damp on top where the rain sat this morning, and dry underneath where the air moves, and the side of the slot is dark for the top half inch and pale below it.

The finger goes down about an inch and stops on something firm. Not timber. Soil, risen up between the boards in a ridge, firm and damp, with grit in it and a root cut clean across where the spade went through in spring.

She draws the finger the length of the slot, and the ridge runs the whole length without a break, high at the south end and low at the north.

What makes it high at one end is the bed. Soil from the bed washes out under the boards in rain and settles in the slot, and each rain adds a little more at the end where the water comes in.

Her sister stands at the yard gate from outside on the path with a parcel under her arm and does not come in.

She waited there while the parcel softened in mist and gazed toward the boards, and she learned the slot fed from the bed rather than the walk.

She was told the parcel would want to be put under cover before the next shower, and she clutched it closer under her coat and took the path back with it, gate resting as found.

---

A path of boards along a bed fills from the side and not from above, because feet keep the tops clean while rain throws soil in from the dug ground beside.

Pine goes dark where soil sits on it and stays pale where air moves over it, and the sides of these two boards show the line where the soil has risen to.

That line is half an inch higher than last autumn, when she swept the slot out with a stick and left it clear to the soil.

Soil rises in a slot because worms throw it up from below and rain washes it in from the side, and the two together fill faster than either would alone.

Worm casts stand in little towers under boards where the birds cannot get at them, and each tower spreads in the next rain and adds to the ridge.

That is why the ridge is firm on top where feet press it and loose underneath where worms work it.

Worms work under boards all winter because the timber keeps the cold off the soil and the damp in it, and the soil under a path never freezes the way open soil does.

That is why the ridge rises fastest in March, when the worms are at their busiest and the bed beside is still too wet to dig.

A spade in March throws soil further than in May, because wet soil sticks to the iron and flies off in lumps, and each lump that lands by the boards breaks in the next rain and runs into the slot.

She swept the slot last autumn with a stick of hazel cut to the width of it, and the stick came out black at the end where it had scraped the sides.

Hazel makes a good slot stick because it is springy enough to follow the boards and hard enough to scrape the sides clean without tearing the grain.

Pine tears along the grain if the stick is driven hard, because pine splits between the autumn growth and the spring growth, and the soft spring wood comes away first.

That is why she draws the finger light along the slot and lets the nail do the scraping, and why the sides show pale streaks where the nail has been.

She draws the finger along again slower, to feel how much the ridge gives under the nail.

It gives about a quarter of an inch under a firm push and then stops on something harder.

What stops it is a stone, flat and wide, lying under the slot about two feet from the south end.

It is a stone out of the bed, thrown aside in digging and trodden down under the boards, and the soil has closed round it until it sits firm in the ridge the way a plum sits in a pudding.

A stone under a path does not move the boards. Water runs round it and soil packs over it, and the boards ride over the top of it without ever touching it.

That is why the south ends of the boards stand a little higher than the north. The south rides on soil packed over stone, while the north rides on soil alone.

Pine boards cup when one side stays damp and the other dries, because the damp side swells and the dry side shrinks, and the board curls up at the edges.

These two have cupped a little toward the slot, which throws more rain into it and fills it faster each year.

Cupped boards throw water to the middle in rain and the middle throws it down the slot, so a cupped path waters its own footing every time it rains.

Flat boards throw it off to the bed, where the plants take it, and the slot stays clear twice as long.

That is why the south board, which cups more, feeds the ridge while the north one, which lies flatter, throws its share to the bed.

---

A board path that fills like this lasts about five years before the boards sit in soil instead of on it, and then the ends go soft where they meet the ground.

The ends here are still firm, with the grain tight and the cuts square, and a tap with the knuckle rings true along both.

The timber is firm on top where the foot wears it smooth, and firm underneath where the air keeps it dry, and the soil between is firm where worms have worked it and feet have pressed it.

Pine wears smooth underfoot in a year because the soft spring growth wears first and the hard autumn lines stand proud, and then the proud lines wear too until the whole top shines. The shine turns slick in drizzle and grips again by evening as the grain drinks the surface damp.

She takes the finger out of the slot and wipes the soil off on the side of the board, and looks along the two from the path to see what they show from above.

From above the slot looks like a dark line between two pale boards, straight and true from end to end.

From the side the south end stands proud by half an inch, where the stone lifts the soil under it.

Both views are true, and the finger knows which end will want lifting first, which is the south one, where the ridge runs highest and the boards ride over stone packed firm against the damp.
