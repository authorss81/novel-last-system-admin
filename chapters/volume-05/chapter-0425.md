# Chapter 0425 — Two Things

Saturday the seventh of July 2018, and again on five other days between that one and the eighteenth of August, a shop off that parade, and a blind end off a side street, in Tideglass.

**ON THE MORNING OF THAT FIRST SATURDAY A WOMAN BEHIND A COUNTER TOLD A MAN IN HIS THIRTIES SOMETHING TRUE ABOUT A MAN IN HIS FORTIES, AND SIX WEEKS LATER A MAN IN HIS SIXTIES TOLD THAT FIRST MAN SOMETHING TRUE ABOUT THE OTHER, AND NEITHER MAN HAS PUT EITHER THING TO ANYBODY AND NEITHER OF THEM KNOWS THAT THE OTHER HAS ANYTHING AT ALL.**

She opened at half past eight and he came in at about ten past nine with a box of sandpaper under his arm.

He put it on the counter and she put it on the scales and wrote the figure on a pad and turned the pad round so he could see it, and he put the money on the counter, and she took it, and her hand went into the till and came out with the difference, and there was nothing else on that counter for him that day.

Then she said, "You've not had a hot dinner in this town since Christmas, have you."

He was in the middle of getting the box down off the counter and he stopped for about a second and then finished getting it down.

"I haven't," he said.

"You used to come in at one o'clock," she said. "Every day of the week, near enough, for as long as she could remember."

"Aye."

She looked at him for a moment and then said, "Well," and turned to the till and put the money in it, and that was the end of it.

Nothing else was said. He carried the box out to the pavement, shoved it up against the side of his own van, and got the doors shut. The woman behind that counter has not mentioned it since to anybody, including him, and she did not know that she had said anything at all. If you asked her what she had said to that man that morning she would tell you she had asked him whether he was eating, which is a thing she might have said whether it were true or not, and nobody who is told something true about somebody else is asked to do a single thing about it afterwards, least of all by the person who said it.

---

Between that day and the seventeenth of August there were thirty-nine other days and he did not go up that street once, and he did not go into that shop again, and he did not ask anybody anything.

He had reasons for not going back and they were the ordinary ones. A man can be told a true thing about another man he has spoken to four times in six years and there is nothing in it that can be acted on. It is not an arrangement. It is not an opening. It does not mean that the other man is unwell, and it does not mean that he is any worse than the last time, and there is no way of finding out from that counter whether it held on the Tuesday or whether it holds now, and a man who goes back in and asks a second question about a first one becomes a man who asks second questions, and this borough has very few of those and he is not going to be the first.

He went on eating. There is a ring in that van with a primus on it and a billy that only works if you hold the edge of it down with your boot, and on the days when he was in this borough he used them at about half past one, sitting in the driver's seat with the sliding door open and the box of sandpaper on the passenger seat beside him. If anybody had come down that parade at half past one on any day between the seventh of July and the seventeenth of August they would have seen him doing it, and nobody did, because nobody on that parade has ever once looked down it for that.

The thing came back on him about nine or ten times in those six weeks. It came back in a car park four miles off at about half past four in the afternoon, when a man he was working beside said he was going to fetch something to eat and offered, and he said no. It came back on a Monday outside a house on a side street when a woman came out with a plate covered with a cloth and looked at him and then went back in, and he stood there for a second and a half and then went on up the path. It came back on the second of August, which was a hot day, and he was sorry for having been told and would not have wanted to have been told and was not going to do anything about it, and those three things were all true at once and none of them cost him a minute's work.

What he did with what she said was nothing at all, and he did it well. He did not mention it to a second person. He did not find an occasion to check it. He did not drive eight miles into the town and ask a man in a shop what another man has for his dinner, which is the only thing anybody could have done with it, and which nobody in this borough did.

On the Friday the seventeenth of August he was at the top of that street with his own van standing at the kerb with the back open and about nine foot of a hardwood worktop out of it on the pavement, which is not the same nine foot he carries about and does not go with the rest of this.

The man in his sixties came up that street at about half past three on his own account. He comes up it most Fridays and comes back down again and the errand has never existed, and the two of them have been doing that for about six years without either of them once arranging it.

They stopped and stood about a yard apart on the pavement on either side of the open back of that van, the way two men do, and they had about four minutes.

They talked about the weather, which was not interesting, and about the worktop, which was a job and did not need discussing, and about a man who has been parking across the mouth of that street since the June, and how the postman had started going round the wrong side of his van. Then the man in his sixties said, in the middle of something else and without any change in his voice at all, "That one's got no telephone on him."

The man in his forties said, "Hasn't he."

"He hasn't had one since the November before last," said the man in his sixties. "I've seen him in that box at the end of this street four or five times since the beginning of the year. Four or five, and always after six, and always with something written on the side of his face because he can't get the light right in there and never has."

"That box has never worked in my day."

"It works," said the man in his sixties. "You want to ring a number, you go and stand in it and you get the light from the road."

And then he said something else about the parking, and the man in his forties said he had to get on with it, and they parted there at the kerb, and the sentence stayed exactly where it had fallen between them, and neither of them turned round at the corner to see the other one go.

That is the whole of what he was told. It is true. It is the only true thing anybody in this borough has said to that man about the other one, and it was not said to him about the other one, it was said beside him while the two of them were standing on either side of a worktop.

He has had the other one's number in his own telephone for about six years. It came off a bit of paper at a kitchen table in 2012 and it has been in there ever since with a dozen other numbers on the same page and he has never rung it, not once, not in a hurry and not otherwise. He looked at it on the Friday night at about ten o'clock with the van parked on that man's own drive, and it was still there, and he put the telephone back in his coat pocket and made a cup of tea and did not think about it again for a week.

---

They went past each other on the Saturday morning after, at about ten past eight, at the blind end of that street where the footway narrows past the end wall of a yard to about two foot wide and a man has to turn sideways to get by a man coming the other way.

The man in his thirties was going up with a bag over his shoulder. The man in his forties was coming down with a toolbox in one hand and his telephone in the other, which he has not rung anybody on and has not looked at since about ten to eight.

The one going up said, "Morning."

The one coming down lifted his free hand about four inches off his side and said nothing at all, and went past, and neither of them stopped and neither of them turned round afterwards.

A man who had spent six weeks knowing that there was no way whatever of telephoning that man, and who had wanted to telephone him about nothing for about four of those weeks without once putting it into a form that could be acted on, walked to his van and put the toolbox in the back of it and shut both doors and stood in the street for a moment with his hand on the near side of it.

**AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS ASKED THE OTHER ONE A SINGLE QUESTION ABOUT ANYTHING, AND EACH OF THEM KNOWS ONE THING THAT THE OTHER DOES NOT, AND BOTH OF THEM WENT HOME.**
