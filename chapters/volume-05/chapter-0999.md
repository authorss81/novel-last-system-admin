# Chapter 0999 — The Line Taken Back

Undated in this draft.

**THE LANDING AT NUMBER TWELVE HAS THE STAIR GOING UP INTO THE DARK AND EVERY DOOR OFF IT SHUT.**

The landing at number twelve is at the half of the house and it is the width of the stair with the newel against the wall and a small window at the turn, and the boards of it run even all the way from the foot of the flight to the window with nothing in them to make a dish except the two women who have gone up the river, and the flight from the front door comes up past the front room door and goes on to the top, and the top two steps are in the dark at this time of day because the bulb over the landing is not in and has not been in since before the summer. The sister is standing at the foot of it with her coat on and her hand on the newel, and she has been standing there, he can tell, for longer than it takes him to come up the frontage from the gate.

Her mother went up the river on Friday morning with a case and a basket and the woman from the front of the row who does her shopping, and she is four days gone. The fire in the front room went out on Thursday night because nobody lit it, and there is an inch of ash on the hearth that has gone back to being powder, and the range in the back kitchen has been out since the spring and the bottom of the wall behind it stands half an inch out of true with the wall behind it, and he has not looked at that wall since Friday because there was nothing in the chapter about Friday that he wanted to be in. The sheet of stiff paper is in his bag where it has been since Wednesday morning and he has carried it about four days without opening it. It is a sheet of stiff paper about nine inches by thirteen with a fold across it and it is the only piece of paper in this street with his hand on it, and he has been carrying it the way a man carries a thing he is going to have to produce.

"We need to go up and look at the second-floor boards while the weather is like this," she tells him without moving off the newel, which is her way of putting a piece of work in front of him so that there is something ordinary between them before either of them gets to the other thing.

"Then I will want to be in that room with the door shut and no one in it for an hour, so go down to your own front room and stay there."

"That is not what I have got you here for and we both know it." She came down one step towards him. "It is the note to my sister. It has to go on the same paper, because a separate paper does not arrive and I am not having two pieces of paper in this house that a postman can lose."

---

He takes the bag off his shoulder in the hall and the sheet out of it in the hall and gives it to her to read, because she read it last and she is quicker at it than he is, and he does not want to be standing over the top of it while she finds the word. It is folded on its own crease and it goes into her hands and comes open along the crease the way it was folded, which is the only thing about that sheet that is still straight.

There are four lines on it now, in three hands. His own line at the top, which is the heading and which gives out that the resident at number twelve has no objection to the arrangements, and which she struck a word out of on Wednesday and set down underneath, in her own hand, that she has not been asked. Her sentence, which is that she has been in that room since the range went out and she would like to go and look at a river. His own second line, which is the one he set down at her own instruction and which tells her daughter not to come, and which is under her sentence and over his initials and the day. She has got as far as the second line and has stopped with her thumb on it.

"This is what she asked you for."

"It is. On Wednesday, at that table, with her hand on it while I did it."

"And the line above it is yours, and the line under that one is hers." She reads them both again. "You have put a woman down twice in one piece of paper, once giving out that she does not object and once telling her daughter to keep away from her, and now I am asking you to add a fifth line telling the daughter to come and live here for six weeks, because I am on the middle shift at the works until five and this house is shut at the front with nobody in it and she is the only family my mother has left on this side of the water." [break]

---

He takes the paper back off her and keeps it in the two hands and does not put it down.

"You are asking me to take the second line out."

"I am asking you to put a third one under it. Take it out if you like. I have not the least care which of the two she gets, because both of them are mine to read and neither of them is hers, because she is four days gone up a river and will not see this paper until the end of the season." She had her hand flat on the newel and her other hand on her coat pocket. "You are the man who put down the second one. I am not asking a stranger. I am asking the man who put a hand to it, and I want a line in his hand, because my sister will believe my mother put that down and will not believe my mother asked for it."

[break] He does it in the front room at the table she offered him the second time he came into it, standing, with the back edge of the deal against his hip and the pencil off behind his ear, and he sets it down in ink under the second line and puts his initials and the day under that, and the ink on the second line from the bottom has been dry for four days and the ink on the new one has to be put straight beside it or it looks like a copy.

Then he stands at that table and looks at the whole of the sheet for the first time since Wednesday and finds that he cannot answer one question about it, which is what it is. Five lines and three hands and every one of them is about whether somebody else is spoken for.

The cost of it arrives while he is doing the initials and it arrives sideways, the way costs have arrived all summer. Nobody at number twelve was ever asked what she would like; he asked her, and it cost her the only comfortable thing she had left, which was being managed. He has come back up this frontage twice since and been handed a piece of paper that belongs to an old woman, and the first time he took it away in his bag and the second time he has added three lines to it in his own hand for a woman who is not his business, and if that old woman comes back up that path at the end of the season and reads what her daughter has had set down at her own table about her house, the line she will be reading at the bottom of the second of his sentences is the one he promised to put there on Wednesday and the one he has now promised somebody else he would undo. The tread of the step at the turn is worn into a hollow at the front edge about the width of a thumb, and that hollow is fifty years of women coming up this stair carrying nothing in particular, and the nosing in front of the second tread from the bottom has the same hollow in it, and nowhere on that flight is there a hollow anywhere else in it at all.
