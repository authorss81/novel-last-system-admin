# Chapter 0937 — Inside The Slat Lap

Friday the sixth of September 2024, at the lap of the bay's shutter at the lock-up with the yard quiet after nine. Inside a lapped slat.

**SHE HAS A ROLLER SHUTTER IN THE BAY OF THE LOCK-UP WITH ONE SLAT LYING OVER THE SLAT BELOW IT, AND SHE MEANS TO PUT TWO FINGERS OF ONE HAND FLAT INTO THAT LAP WITH THE OTHER TWO BENT BACK OUT OF IT, TO FIND OUT HOW DEEP THE LAP RUNS AND WHAT IS LYING IN THE STEP OF IT.**

The shutter was put up in a fortnight in the spring and it has worked every working day since, and the slats have lain over one another with the shutter up and come apart with the shutter down about ten thousand times over. The lap at the bottom of the bay is the one nobody has ever looked at, because the shutter is up in the daytime and the bottom of it is eight feet off the flags.

It is a shutter of pressed steel slats about four inches deep, each one curved from edge to edge and each one lying over the slat below it by about an inch, all of them running in a pair of channels at each side of the opening. She wants the lap, and the lap is the step where the bottom edge of one slat crosses the top edge of the one under it.

---

Nobody made that lap. It was pressed, in a mill, out of two pieces of steel rolled together in one go. So the two fingers go in flat and together with the pads of them on the steel, and the other two bend back out of the way. The steel along the step is slick from the rubbing of slats over one another, and where the bottom edge of the upper slat is turned it is blunt from having been rolled in a machine and not from having been worn down by anything.

The lap is the depth of a new penny at the far end of the bay. Two thirds of the way along it it closes to a paper's thickness, and at the middle of the opening the step is gone altogether and left a crease, and the crease is where the man who rolled the slats set the rolls closest together to get that bay to shut.

In the step of the lap there is a ledge, and the ledge carries the grey dust that comes up off the floor of the bay. There is a great deal of it and it lies along the whole length in a line, and at the two ends of the bay it has built up into a small ridge, because the lap has closed on them and there is nothing left there for it to run along.

The two fingers go along the whole of that step and come off at the other end of the bay with the pads of them grey to the first joint. Nothing in the lap moves and nothing in it catches.

The shutter box at that bay was set a half inch proud of the wall when it was fixed, and every slat in the bay has run against it ever since. The roller is about four inches through and it turns in a bearing at each end, and the slats are hooked over it by their own curl and nothing else.
They have no rail to run in and nothing to wear against except the box itself and the two channels, which are galvanised and were the shiniest thing on the front of that building when it was new.

A shutter proud of its own wall has to be lifted as it goes down, because the slats have to clear the lip of the box to get past it, and the man who runs it does that with one hand and gets the curtain down about half an inch further every time than the last. The slats come off the roller about four inches above the bottom of the box in the closed position and they come off it crooked, and the crookedness is the box.

The steel in the lap is thin. It is about the thickness of a coin at the edge and rather more in the middle of the roll, and a lap pressed tight against the slat under it is a joint with no play in it anywhere. It works at all on that, and the whole of the reason is this: a slat that can move a fraction at its edge scrapes, and a slat that cannot move slides.

The dust in that step is the floor of the bay coming up through the gap between the slats and being trapped where the lap closes, and it is trapped because a closed lap has no way out of it and the shutter is up most of the day.
A bay like that one is swept out in the morning and the dust is back on the flags by the afternoon, and the part of the dust that never comes back is the part that went up into the shutter in the first hour it was raised.

Under the ledge of the lap the steel is bright. That is the only bright part of that shutter and it is a line about a foot and a half long at the bottom of the bay, and it is bright because it is the one surface in the whole of the curtain that two pieces of steel never touch.

The crease at the middle of the bay came out of the mill. Creases in pressed slats are made there, when the rolls are set to make a slat that will go round a roller of a given size, and a slat with a crease in it has a smaller effective radius than one without and hangs a shade closer to the shutter box than the rest of them go.
That is the reason the middle of that bay shuts tighter than either end, and it is a difference of a hair and not enough to be called anything.

A lap like that one is pressed in the making rather than in the using, and it stays pressed for as long as the steel lasts. The bottom edge of a slat is rolled to a radius of about the sixth of an inch and that roll is what the slat under it lies against, so the two of them make a shallow dovetail with the wide side out.
It is a poor dovetail and it does not tighten, but it stops the slat lifting and lifting is the only thing that has to be stopped.

What wears in a lap is the lower edge of the upper slat, and what it wears is the turned corner where the roll was finished off. On a new shutter that corner is blunt and full; on this one it is blunt and thin, and the slick line along the step is the record of ten thousand closures passing over the same width of steel.

She takes the two fingers out and stands back from the opening and looks at the bottom of the curtain for a while without touching anything else on it. The lap is where the curtain's own weight does the work of keeping the weather out, and it does that by two pieces of steel pressing on one another, and nothing in that arrangement has come loose.

The woman who books work comes out of the gate of that yard on foot with nothing in her hands and goes on along the frontage until the wall turns and she is out of sight. She keeps to the frontage and her eyes to the gate, and her head never goes back at anything; the gate is against the wall behind her by the time the shutter comes down on its own weight.

What is left in the step of that lap is a line of grey dust the length of the bay and a bright line under it, and both of those were put there by the curtain working and neither of them is going anywhere while the slats go on lying over one another exactly as they are, notwithstanding the box itself, which was fixed half an inch proud of the wall by a man who wanted the guide to clear the render and got exactly that.
