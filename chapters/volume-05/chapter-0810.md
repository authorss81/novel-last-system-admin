# Chapter 0810 — Four Feet Of Privet

Friday the twenty-eighth of April 2023, in this borough. It is along the side wall of her own house at number nine for most of that Friday, and a privet hedge that has gone over in the middle is the whole of what is in front of her.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES HAS FOUR FEET OF PRIVET ALONG THE SIDE WALL OF THAT HOUSE THAT HAS GONE OVER IN THE MIDDLE AND MEANS TO CUT IT BACK AND PUT TWO POSTS IN; THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING PUTS THE OTHER TWO POSTS IN ON THE SAME AFTERNOON, AND WHERE THE TWO PAIRS MEET IN THE MIDDLE THE HEDGE IS ON FOUR POSTS INSTEAD OF TWO AND STANDS UP STRAIGHT, AND NEITHER OF THEM EVER FINDS OUT.**

Number nine, on that Friday. She is in her late fifties. Four feet of privet stands along the side wall of that house between the front step and the side gate, planted in a strip about two feet wide that was made when the path was made, and it has been leaning out over the path for about a year and this winter it went over properly in the middle.

The middle of a privet goes over for one reason, which is that privet puts its weight on the outside of a hedge and none on the inside, and four feet of it on a strip two feet wide has nothing under the middle of it worth the name.

It needs two posts and a wire between them and a tie every year.

She has been meaning to do it since the summer. She has four posts in the grass under the front window, three-by-three softwood about five feet long and sawn off square at the bottom, and a roll of galvanised wire, and she only ever intended to put two of them in.

She gets at it from the middle of the morning with a spade and a pruning saw.

The first thing is to get it out of the ground, and you get a privet out by cutting the roots rather than pulling at the top, because pulling at the top tears the root plate and a privet that has been torn comes back as two.

She cuts in on three sides of the root plate with the spade and prises it up and rolls it onto the grass on a sheet of carpet so that the soil comes up with it and not across the path.

It comes out whole. The root ball is about a foot across and there are five main stems in it and two of them have gone into each other and grown together where they crossed about a foot above the ground, which is the middle that has gone over.

Then she cuts it back.

That is the part that decides whether the job works or not. Privet is cut back to a bare stub and it does not mind, and a privet cut back to a bare stub makes twice the growth in a year, and a privet left where it is gets taller and thinner every year until there is nothing at the bottom of it and then the whole thing lies on the path.

She cuts the five stems back to about a foot above the root plate with the saw, one at a time, and takes the two grown-together stems apart with a chisel and a hammer and separates them where they have been married for two years.

The two separated stems are the worst of the five. They are the two that had all the weight on them, because they were on the outside of the hedge and the other three were inside it. Everything about that plant says it was relying on the other three and now it has to carry itself.

The posts go in next.

She has a bar, a lump hammer, a spirit level, and about a bucket of sharp sand and stone out of the bag the posts came in. She marks the line with two lumps of mortar on the path first — that is the whole of what a mortar is for in this yard — a foot and a bit in from each end of the four feet, so that the wire runs past the ends of the hedge by a foot at either side.

A hole in a made-up side passage beside a house is not a hole you can ram the bottom of. She goes down about two feet with the bar and barrow out the loose stuff and puts six inches of the sharp stuff in the bottom and rams it with the end of the bar until the bar will not go in further, and then she stands the post on it and packs stone round it in layers and tamps each layer.

That is how a post is put in beside a house and it takes as long as it takes.

She gets the first post in about an hour and the second an hour after that, and they are four feet apart and about a foot each side of the middle of the hedge, and they are true to the line of the path and about eighteen inches deep with four inches of stone under them.

Then she goes into the house, because her hands are not going to get any better, and she washes them at the sink in the kitchen and stands with a cup of tea and looks out of the window at the front step and the coal hole and the gate lying across the path on its two wedges with the spanner on the flags where it was dropped, and none of that is the middle of her morning.

---

The woman who does her shopping comes round the side of that house at about the middle of the afternoon.

She has come round to the side, not to the front, and she has come because the privet is lying on the grass with its roots out and two posts standing in the path with nothing at all tied to them, and any woman who has ever watched a privet go over knows exactly what that looks like.

She looks at the two posts and she looks at the four feet of privet and she looks at the other two posts lying in the grass under the window that came in the bag with the first two.

She has watched privet go over in the middle for thirty years and she knows that two posts and a wire across four feet is two posts and a wire and the middle of the privet leaning out over the path again by the second winter, because that is what happens to four feet of privet on two posts.

So she puts in two more.

She puts them in the middle, one either side of the centre, about eighteen inches apart, in the strip of privet roots between the first two posts, and she does it the same way she has done it a hundred times, which is to dig down to the bottom, put stone in the bottom, stand the post, pack and tamp, and check the two faces against the spirit level and then against the line of the path with an eye.

She does them one after the other and she checks the second one against the first and against the two she has not touched at the ends, and the four posts stand in a line about a foot and a half apart in the middle and four feet apart at the ends.

A four-foot length of hedge on two posts is a length of hedge with its middle in the air. That is all that has gone wrong with this one and that is what will go wrong with the next one anybody plants on two posts, and it is not a fault in the plants or in the posts. It is a fault in the arithmetic of two posts and four feet, and a person who has been doing it for thirty years knows it the way they know which way a door swings.

She puts the wire on.

One strand through all four posts and stapled to each, and not tight, because wire put on tight in the spring pulls the posts in a curve by the summer.

Then the privet goes back in. She puts it in the strip with the root ball where it was and backfills with the soil that came out of it and tamps it down around the ball with the heel of her hand and packs the rest in loose, because a privet root ball wants loose earth against it and not a fist round it.

Then she ties it in. Soft tie, in a figure of eight round the stem and twice round the wire, three ties on each of the five stems, and the figure of eight is not decoration — it is what stops a stem rubbing on a wire in the wind and cutting itself off.

Then she puts an old piece of matting over the root ball and a brick on it so that the earth round it does not wash off the path in the first rain.

Then she picks up her spade and her fork and goes back round the front of that house and out of the gate.

The woman of the house comes out of the front door about twenty minutes after that and stands at the front step and looks down the side passage.

The privet is back in the ground, cut back to a stub a foot high, on four posts instead of two, tied in, and there is a brick on a piece of matting over the middle of it.

She does not go down the side passage after her. She stands where she is for a while and then she goes back inside and puts the kettle on again.

---

Then she goes out and does the one thing left to do, which is look at the cut.

Five sawn faces, all of them about the same height and all of them pale and all of them on stems that are cut square and not broken off. Two of them are the married pair and the wood on those two has gone round each other where they grew together, and there is a black line round both of them about where the join is, and it will be inside the hedge for ever and it will not be a bad thing.

On the side of the front stub, low down, right where the saw went in, there is a bud.

It is green and it is no bigger than a grain of rice and it is at the side of the stem rather than the end of it, which means it is a side shoot and not the leader, and that is the good kind to have on a privet cut back this hard, because a privet cut back to a stub puts three or four of them along every stem and they come away in the summer like a hedge that has been cut back to ground and let go.

That bud was there the whole time the plant was growing over the path and had gone over and had been leaned on by a wet winter, and it is on the front stub, which is the stub nearest the house, and it is the front stub because that is the stem that was on the inside of the hedge all along and had the least of the weight on it and is the one that will throw the most back.
