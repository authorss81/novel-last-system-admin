# Chapter 0965 — Along The Paving Joint

Undated in this draft.

**THE PAVING ALONG THE FRONT AT NUMBER NINE HAS SETTLED BY A FINGER WIDTH AND THE JOINT SAND HAS GONE FROM BETWEEN TWO SLABS.**

The front path at number nine runs from the pavement to the door in slabs of cast stone, each a two-foot square and two inches deep, sand-laid twenty years past by a man who worked alone with a bar and a rubber mallet.

The slabs have settled by different amounts, because the sand beneath them was never of one depth and the rain off the roof has run across the same corner for years. At the second joint from the pavement the near slab has gone down by about the width of a finger, and the far slab has stayed where it was put, so the joint between them stands open along its whole length.

The joint sand has washed out of that opening grain by grain, and the run of the water can be seen in the way the remaining sand falls away toward the grass. Where the sand has gone the joint is dark even at midday, and a dry leaf has folded itself into one end of it with the stalk still on.

That joint is about the width of a little finger at the top and narrower below, and it goes down between the two slabs for about the depth of a finger before it meets what is left of the bedding sand. The sand that is left is pale and loose at the top and darker and firmer further down, and it falls away under the arris of the lower slab for a short distance.

A paving slab feels cold down one side of a finger, for cast mix pulls heat out of skin quickly. Neighbouring sand lies arid, tender, gifting warmth to none. The arris of the slab is square and sharp from the mould, and it has lost none of its edge along the length of the joint except where a corner has been rubbed by feet.

The bedding sand under the path was put down on soil, and the soil beneath it is dark and close and full of small stones. At the open joint the sand has gone down into the soil in a narrow cone, and the sides of that cone are lined with grains that have stuck together with damp. Nothing has been dug there and nothing has been swept out of it.


The hinge of the iron gate runs on a pin driven into the pier, and the pin there stands bright where the gate moves. The gate itself hangs true despite its years, swinging without scraping the flags. Its lower rail carries rust in flakes that fall onto the pavement in dry weather, thin and orange, with bright metal showing where each flake parted. A child once chalked a mark on the pier beside the hinge, and the chalk survives in the pores of the brick though rain has paled it to a ghost.

The pavement before the path runs in bays divided by joints, each bay crowned to throw water sideways. One bay has sunk at its centre and water stands there after rain in a mirror that breaks underfoot. The mirror reforms as the foot lifts, rippled first and then still. Grit collects along the joints between bays, pale and sharp, washed from the crowns by feet and rain.
---

Two fingers go down into that joint together, with the backs of them against the higher slab and the tips toward the lower one, and they ease along it from the pavement end toward the door.

The point of sending two is that one alone turns sideways in a joint that narrow and gives a false depth, while two together keep each other upright and read the bottom truly. They go down until the tips meet the remaining sand, and then they move forward a little at a time, feeling the bottom rise and fall.

First to return is depth. At the pavement end the joint takes the two fingers to the second joint of each, and in the middle it takes them a little further, and toward the door end it takes them less far again, because the lower slab has tipped toward the grass and the joint is deeper where the tip has gone.

Next to return is what lies at the bottom. Across the greater part sand beds the bottom, loose above and packed beneath, with here and there a small stone that has worked up out of the soil. Toward the centre squats a thin cast-stone spall, shed from the upper slab arris and jammed athwart the cleft.

That flake draws heat fast, thin and hard against the skin. The stone around it draws heat in the slow way of a mass. The sand under the fingers gives a little under pressure and stays where it is pushed, and the flake sits dead to a downward prod yet tips to a sideways flick.

The fingers ease along the whole length of the joint and come out at the door end with grains of sand under the nails and nothing else on them. Joint width stands unaltered afterwards, the floor sand scored only by twin hairlines tracking the fingers.

Behind the joint the slabs go on being slabs, each with its own cast face and its own small pits from the mould. The higher slab rings a little when tapped with a knuckle, because it spans a hollow where the sand has gone, while the lower one sounds dull, because it sits full on its bed. Neither flagstone stirs to a boot, nor has either split through.

A cast slab spans a hollow differently from a stone slab, because the cast mix is the same through its thickness and has no bed of its own to split along. A slab two inches thick will carry a person across a hollow of two fingers wide without bending, and will only crack when the hollow runs under half its length. Here the hollow runs under one edge for about a foot, which is well within what the slab can carry, and the ring when tapped is the sound of that carrying.

The sawn edge of a cast slab is rougher than the moulded face, and it takes damp differently. Water that gets into the joint sits against those sawn sides and darkens them for a day after rain, while the moulded tops dry within an hour. The dark line along the higher slab marks how far the water stood in winter, and the pale band overhead records the noon sun limit.

Sward beside the walk has crept an inch across both flags, rootlets trussing loam against further sluicing. A worm cast stands at the pavement end of the joint, fresh from the night, and a second one further along has been trodden flat.

Rain water runs off these slabs toward the grass and not toward the house, which is why the joint opened where it did and not elsewhere. The fall of the path is slight but true, and a cup of water tipped at the door would reach the pavement without stopping. Dew sits on the slabs until mid-morning, and roof water from the bay has cut a shallow channel in the grass beyond.

The iron gate at the pavement stands open against the wall on its hook, and the brick pier beside it shows where old lime has gone hard in the joints and where it has fallen away to leave a shallow groove. That pier furrow lies thirsty, dusty; a fingertip traversal returns ashen. Fired clay in that pier bites cold and deep against a fingertip. It is the same kind of opening as the joint in the path, only upright and in old lime instead of sand, and it goes back only to the first knuckle.

---

The woman who does her shopping is at the corner of the front path where it meets the pavement at number nine from the pavement, with both feet on the pavement and not stepping onto the path and nothing in her hands.

Both hands hang loose at the sides with grit under the nails while the shopper measures the door from the pavement. Talk runs on whether the corner shop has moved its opening hours, and whether the morning bus still waits at the stop, with replies traded easily across the path.

The shopper bides on public paving, never mounting the path, surveying slabs, then door, then roadway in turn. The path stays where it is with its joint open and its sand fallen away, and the door stays shut with the paint on it unmarked.

The drift of grit at the bottom of that joint lies where the water left it and spreads a little further with every rain, as though the path were learning to sort what it sheds by weight.
