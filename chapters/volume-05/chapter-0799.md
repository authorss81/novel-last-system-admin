# Chapter 0799 — The Door That Crosses The Stone

Friday the tenth of March 2023, in this borough. She is out at the back of number nine for the whole of that day with the back door off its hinges, and the hand's width that door is out of line with itself is the whole of the job.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES HAS A BACK DOOR OUT OF LINE WITH ITSELF BY A HAND'S WIDTH WHERE IT CROSSES A STONE BECAUSE IT WAS HUNG TOO FAR CLOSED IN THE AUTUMN AND MEANS TO PLANE THE BOTTOM OF IT, THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING TAKES THE DOOR OFF ITS HINGES AND RE-HANGS IT A HAND'S WIDTH HIGHER ON NEW LONGER KEEPS SO THAT IT SHUTS ON ITS OWN FRAME AND NOT ON THE STONE, AND IT SHUTS.**

She is in her late fifties and has been a good while. Her visitor walks the length of the side of that house to the back of it in the middle of the morning with her hands empty, because that door has to come off its hinges before anybody can find out what is wrong with it. It is shut at the front of that house and she does not go through the house.

---

That back door has been out of line with itself since October.

It is a solid door and it was hung properly once. It is hung now on three butt hinges, and it was rehung in the autumn by a man who has not been in that yard since, and the fault is in the keeps and not in the door.

Where it closes, the leaf comes to a hand's width further back into the doorway than the frame it belongs to. It stands out of line with itself by the width of a hand, and the reason you can see it from the yard is her own old step stone: the stone comes about a hand's width into the opening at the bottom, and where the door comes across it, the door crosses it.

So it does not shut. It comes across and has to be lifted over the edge of that stone and pushed in at the same time, and it has to be done with two hands, and it has been done that way every day since October by a woman of her age with a bad hip who has not said anything about it.

The offcut of her old front step is lying across the hollow on the stone. She put it there in the spring to keep the level of that step while it settled, and she has never taken it off, and she does not take it off now.

The step itself is two mouldings and a stone. A galvanised pail hangs on a nail by the door. The fire is out inside that house and has been since before she came. The coal hole on the side wall is shut, and the load standing in it stays exactly where it has been since November.

Her plan is to plane the bottom of the door.

---

"You are planing the bottom of that door, then."

"That is what I am doing to it."

"What off it."

"As much as it wants." She has got a block plane out and it is on the flags with the iron out of it. "It is sitting on that stone. Every time it shuts it lands on the top of that stone, and every time it lands on the top of that stone it knocks the bottom of the frame in, and that is why the frame is loose where it meets the step. Take the wood off the bottom of the leaf and it stops landing on the stone."

"Where are you taking it off."

"Off the bottom."

"Off the bottom all round, or off the bottom where the door closes."

"Where it lands."

---

The woman who does her shopping does not touch the plane. She goes and looks at the door standing open on its hinges with the wind coming through it, and she lays the back of that hand against the face of the leaf, and then she goes down to where it stands and lays her hand against that same face lower down, at the height where the leaf crosses that stone, and then she stands with her back to the wind for a moment and does not say anything.

"You have taken your hand off it now. Go on."

"It is a hand's width out of line with itself and you can see where it crosses the stone."

"You can see where it crosses the stone." She comes back. "That is not the wood in the door. That is the door being in the wrong place. You are not planing a door. You are planing the bottom of a door that is hung in a hole about six inches deeper than it wants to be, and every shaving you take off that leaf is a shaving off the only thing that is not broken. Take an inch off the bottom of it and it stops landing on the stone for ever and it also lets the wind and the rain under that door for ever and it does not shut any better where it shuts now."

"Then I shall have to move it."

"Then you shall have to move it."

---

Then it comes off its hinges, and that wants two of them and the best of the morning.

A solid door of that size comes off three hinges by being lifted rather than levered. She takes the weight of it at the latch stile and tells her to take it at the hinge stile, and then she works the pins out of the top hinge and knocks the knuckles up off the door with a block of wood, and they walk it clear of the opening and lay it down on the flags on its face, with the hinges hanging out to one side.

It goes down on the flags and it goes down hard, and the knock of it goes across that yard and comes back off the boundary wall. They had to put a third block under the head of it because the top corner of a door that size does not stay where you put it down.

The woman of the house stands over it and looks at the bottom edge, which is sound and square and about as good as a bottom of a door gets.

"That is the wood you were going to take four or five shavings off. Now tell me what the door is doing."

"It is in too deep."

"It is in too deep. It was hung on keeps that were set at the back of that jamb, and somebody wanted that door to shut and it would not, so they set it further in." She crouches down at the hinge stile. "And the fix is not on the bottom of the leaf. The fix is that it comes up and out."

---

They take the old keeps off the jamb and put new ones on.

Three hinges, so three keeps. The old ones are an ordinary screwed plate about four inches long, screwed into the back of a jamb that is not four inches deep, which is how a door gets shut by somebody who does not know what he is doing.

The new keeps are longer — about nine inches, where the old ones were four — and they are let into the jamb so that the head of each one sits in the outer half of it and the barrel of the hinge comes out into the opening rather than back against the stone. Five inches more keep on the back of a hinge moves the axis five inches further into the opening, and everything hanging off that axis moves out with it, and the door is no longer on the wrong side of that stone.

A keep that long has to be let in rather than screwed on to the face, and letting one in takes a chisel and a bar and about twenty minutes a hinge, and there are three of them.

They go in a hand's width higher than the old three did, which means every old hole is plugged and every new hole is somewhere else in the jamb, and the woman who does her shopping finds them with a bradawl and opens them with a cold chisel because a jamb of that thickness does not take a screw into new oak very well and it takes it into old oak very badly.

And the head has to be made good for it, because a leaf hung four inches higher than it was has to have four inches of head over it or it will not shut at all. The head of that frame is a timber lintel with four inches of old frame under it that nobody has looked at since it went in, and she takes those four inches off the underside of it with a saw and a chisel, because the top of that door is four inches of solid oak and it has just gone up four inches.

Then the door goes back on, one hinge at a time, and the leaf comes into the opening five inches further out and four inches higher than it hung before, and the bottom of it comes clear of the top of that stone by the better part of an inch.

---

And it shuts.

She swings it once and it comes across on its own and goes into the keep and the latch drops and it is shut.

The woman who does her shopping stands and looks at the gap under the bottom of it and puts the edge of her hand against the stone and against the bottom of the door and there is nothing like a draught coming through them, and the door is not landing on that stone any more and it does not knock the frame in.

The leaf is in the plane of its own frame. It was a hand's width out of line and it is a hair out of line now, at the hinge stile, which is where a door is out of line whatever a person does to it.

"That is better than it was."

"That is on its own frame," her visitor says, "and it was not on its own frame and the frame was never the problem. The stone is the same stone it was in the spring. The jamb is the same jamb. There was a door hung in the wrong place and there is not one."

---

Then they carry it and stand it against the wall, and it stays where it is put.

She goes round the yard and comes back to it and closes it, and then opens it and comes in through it, and then shuts it on herself from the inside, and it shuts on its own frame from inside that house as well as from outside it.

She stands in her own yard and runs her thumb along the head of that door where it now sits four inches higher than it did, and along the jamb where the new keeps are let into the front half of it, and along the bottom edge that she was going to take four shavings off and has not touched.

Then she goes and puts the edge of her hand on the stone. The stone is where it was and it is the same stone it was in the spring and it will be there when the next thing on that house comes off its hinges. That stone is not going anywhere and it never has, and whatever gets hung in that opening after this will have to shut over it as well.
