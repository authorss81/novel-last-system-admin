# Chapter 0886 — Down Inside The Pipe

Tuesday the nineteenth of February 2024, in the corner at the gate end of the yard at the unit, for as long as the light lasts on the store roof. The inside of a length of pipe.

**HE HAS A LENGTH OF FOUR-INCH CAST IRON PIPE STOOD ON END IN THE CORNER AT THE GATE END OF THAT YARD WITH ITS SOCKET UPPERMOST, AND HE MEANS TO PUT ONE HAND DOWN FLAT INTO THAT SOCKET PALM TO THE GROUND AND THEN TURN THE FOREARM ON ITS EDGE AFTER THE HAND AND DRIVE THE ARM DOWN AS FAR AS THE SHOULDER WILL LIFT IT, ONE INCH PAST THE PLACE WHERE THE ARM WANTS TO STOP, AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN IS AT THE NEAR GATE WITH HER HAND ON THE BAR ACROSS IT.**

The pipe has been in that corner since the summer, and nothing has ever been said out loud about what it is there for.

It is a length of four-inch pipe about three feet long, cut off square at both ends, and it is stood on end with the socket at the top, which is the way a length of pipe gets stood up when whoever stood it there was thinking of it as a thing you put things into.

A length of four-inch pipe carries things. It will take a stake, or a length of hazel, or a row of bottles, and a yard is a place where those things get wanted.

He put his hand in the top of it on the Saturday to see whether there was anything in it, because there was nothing in it, and that was that.

Then he left it and thought about it in the way a person thinks about a thing that is going to bother them.

The mouth of that pipe is four inches across and two inches down to the shoulder of the socket, and the shoulder is the thing that makes it useful as a holder, because a bottle pushed into a socket cannot fall out of it.

What a socket also does is stop a hand, and when the second hand comes in after the first, nothing at the end of it will take a grip.

So the hand goes in palm downwards, flat, over the lip of the socket and down inside it, and the fingers touch the shoulder and stop, and then the arm comes down after the hand, turned on its edge so that the forearm goes into the bore at the narrowest it will take it.

It goes a good deal further than the hand.

That is what a bore does. Once the forearm is past the socket the arm is inside a tube, and the tube is only as wide as the arm, and the arm can go down it as far as the arm is long.

He reaches the place where the arm will not go any further, which is the place where the elbow wants to turn.

Then he lifts the shoulder, which brings the whole arm up an inch, and gets that inch as well, and the fingers arrive at the bottom of the pipe and stop there, because the bottom of that pipe is standing on the flags and a hand has nowhere past it to go.

The bore is clear. From the socket down to the grit at the bottom, nothing in it at all.

There is a leaf stuck flat in the grout on the shoulder where the rainwater got in and dried, and at the bottom of the pipe there is about an inch of grit, and the grit has gone grey and into the pores of the iron.

The grit has gathered over the joint between two flags that runs across the yard underneath the pipe, four inches in from its near edge, because grit gathers on a joint and on nothing else. The fingers find the ridge of that joint through the grit if they are pushed, and they can, which is how the pipe came to be stood over that line.

Then he puts the arm back down, slower, because now he is looking for a thing rather than putting a thing in.

Coming up the bore with the arm is what finds it.

The woman from two doors down turns up at the near gate from the pavement side about eleven with her hand on the bar that is across it, and she does not lift it.

"Is the water still off at yours?"

"It came on at ten and went off again at eleven."

"Ours has been on since Tuesday."

"Then do not trust it until the Friday."

She shifted her weight off the bar and left it where it was, and told him the fence on her side had come off its bottom rail again in the wind and would go again, and that she was not doing it in February. He told her a fence with a sound bottom rail comes off at the joint above the rail and not at the rail, so the rail was sound and something above it was loose. She told him that was the same as saying it wanted doing. He told her it was not. She took that, and went up the path.

---

What the arm finds on the way up, about two feet above the flags, is a step.

Not a soft thing and not something wedged. An even step all the way round the bore, with the bore above it four inches across and the bore below it about three and a quarter.

He gets the hand down onto the step and turns the hand over and feels the whole of it with the flat of the fingers.

It is a ring of iron. It is as even as a ring can be, and the top edge of it is free of rust.

A ring that is even and clean on its top edge has not come out of the casting. Rust eating into a bore leaves a ring that varies; a burr off a mould leaves one edge lifted. Neither of those is what this is.

It is the end of a pipe, and there is a second length inside the first one.

The step runs round without a break and it runs level, which says the inner length is standing square in the outer one and went down the whole way until it could not go any further. A pipe pushed into a socket does not stand square unless the two were made to fit.

The outer length is a four-inch one with its socket at this end. The inner one is a length of three-inch pipe, and what the hand has been feeling is the spigot end of it, three and a quarter inches across, standing up in a four-inch bore and stopped by the shoulder of the socket.

So somebody had a length of four-inch pipe and a length of three-inch pipe, and put the second one inside the first, and pushed it down until the spigot came hard against the shoulder.

Nothing wedges them and nothing ties them. They stand on one another in metal to metal contact all the way round the step, and that contact is the whole of what keeps them where they are.

And a hand cannot get past it.

The fingers get down onto the ring of iron, and above the step there is three-quarters of an inch of gap between the ring and the bore, and a hand reaches nothing beyond that.

The bottom of that pipe is the one part of it that has not been looked at, and the only way to look at it is to turn the whole thing over, and turning a three-foot length of cast iron over on hard ground is a job for two people and February is not the month for it.

None of that is a fault. A length of pipe stood on end in a corner with a length of pipe inside it will take a stake, because a stake is a straight thing that stands on the bottom and does not care what is under the bottom.

The stake will fail at the bottom. A stake driven to the bottom of that pipe stands in the grit on the flags, and grit on flags keeps water through a winter, and the bottom of a hazel stake in wet grit goes soft in two seasons and then snaps at the line where it comes out of the grit.

So the pipe will take a stake, and the stake will last a summer, and whoever set it will find the break at the bottom of the pipe rather than at the top of it, and will not find out why until the bottom of the stake is in his hand.

The corner will do what the corner was left to do, and the two lengths are inside each other, and they will be inside each other next year as well, if either of them is wanted for something else this year.
