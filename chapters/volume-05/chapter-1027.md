# Chapter 1027 — A Plate Off The Corner Of A Pit

Undated in this draft.

**THE SOUTH SIDE OF THAT KERB HAS A PLATE LIPPING AT ONE CORNER AND A BED GONE TO THE BOTTOM.**

The weighbridge at that yard sits down in a pit with brick kerb laid round three sides of it, and the south side is a length of its own about twelve foot long, and it is the only part of that kerb a man can get a cold chisel into at all. It came out before the summer turned. He took it out himself, on an afternoon when that yard was shut and nobody asked him for anything, with a bar and his thumb behind the blade, and what had been underneath had washed away along the whole run and left a gap you could put a finger into and turn it over in.

A fortnight has gone by since and what was a gap has become a trough about a foot deep at the deepest part of it, which is deeper than the kerb was and is the part he has not been able to put out of his own head for two days. He has been down into it with a lump bar and worked the last of the old bed out to the bottom along the whole length of it, and what came out has gone from that yard a barrow-load at a time to the place where that kind of thing goes, and what is left down there is subsoil and a pan of old lime gone hard as a stone, and the four foot of kerb that used to be sitting on the top of it is now standing up against the wall of that office with the mud still on the face of it.

Nobody in that building has the faintest idea. Twice he has decided to walk in there and put the whole of it in front of that desk, and twice he has stopped in the yard instead, and the reason is the same both times, which is that nobody in an office can do one single thing with a fault a man put in his own ground on his own afternoon.

The first job was right and he did it right. Open the bed out to the bottom so that it can be filled and left to go off for a month before anybody goes near it with a stone at all, which is how a man does a bed that has washed out and wants to stay down. What he has not got is dressed kerb, and he has not got lime, and the yard has had neither since before the year turned, and the fault has gone from being a thing four feet long to being a thing four feet long and a foot deep.

On a Thursday that yard does not run at all. On a Friday there is a shutter down over the office window and one man at a desk inside it, and the rest of the week there is a car standing on the weighbridge and a man in it who has come for a load and is waiting for it to be ready. He has stood in that yard on a wet Tuesday with the gate open behind him and nothing in it to do and nowhere on this earth he wanted to be, and he has got through a good many of them exactly that way, and nowhere else in this borough can a man be so certain of his own ground and so short of anything to put on it.

He got his hand into his coat and brought the coins out onto his open palm there in the open, where anybody coming in off the frontage could have seen them, and he could not put a name to one single thing he would buy with them, and the reason is not that he is careful with it.

A man's own money goes into his own yard. The whole of the arrangement he has lived in for ten years runs that way, and it is the reason nobody has ever had to ask him a question about his own ground. The day he puts money out of a woman's apron into the kerb round his own weigh pit, that kerb stops being his fault and becomes a thing somebody has paid for, and the woman who paid for it has a right to know whether it was done, and there is nowhere in this borough that she can put that kind of question.

So he closed his hand on the coins and put them back where they were, and the trough along the south side of that pit is still open, and it is going to be open until somebody puts dressed kerb into it that this borough cannot afford.

---

Then he went and looked at the plate.

It is a sheet of chequer plate about two foot by three that has been over the corner of that pit for longer than anybody in that yard has been on that book, and it is lipping on nothing whatever, because the kerb under its short side has been out since the summer and the plate has been bridging a hole along one edge and hanging off the other ever since. He has known that for a fortnight and told himself it would do, and what it is going to be by Friday night is a man coming in the dark with his weight on one corner of it going through into four foot of lime with a barrow wheel standing on his foot.

He got his fingers under the near edge and lifted, and it came off the angle at once, because nothing at all had been underneath any part of it for a long time, and it is heavier than it looks, and it is a good plate.

That told him more about his own yard in one second than the four feet of brick had done in a fortnight, because a man who leaves a cover over a hole he has made himself is a man who thinks the hole belongs to somebody else, and he had been thinking that, and he had been doing it since before the summer turned.

He stood there and went over the arithmetic of it, and it comes out the same every time he does it, and he has done it now with four women and once on his own. There are two men in that yard on a Tuesday and on a Thursday and they are the only two men in this borough he knows who will turn up for money, and that yard is shut and there is nothing on the page and no money in it to pay anybody, and there is a fortnight of work at number twelve that wants two men under a range and one man cannot get under it. He has got a sum in his pocket now and it is enough to pay a man for a day or two and it is not enough to pay two men for a fortnight, and there is nobody else in this borough he can go to, and what he will not do is go to a woman and ask her to find him a second man, because a woman finding a second man is a thing that ends with two people talking about him.

"Right," he put out loud into an empty yard with a plate in his two hands.

Then he carried it in and stood it on end against the inside of the office wall with the chequer face out where it would not fall, and came back out through the gate and put the gate back off its latch with his hip.

Leaving that plate standing against the inside of the wall with its chequer side to the room, he came out through the gate with nothing in his hands, because the corner of that kerb is open from this side to the middle with nothing over it at all, and the only person alive who could take it as far as it will go is a woman in an office four streets away who has never taken anything off him, so he is going to carry it up there and put it in front of her in words.
