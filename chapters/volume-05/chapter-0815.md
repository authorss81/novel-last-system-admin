# Chapter 0815 — The Fourth Flag Goes Back Down

Friday the twenty-sixth of May 2023, in this borough. She is out in her own side passage at number twelve from the middle of the morning until the sun has come off the top of the boundary wall, and about nine feet of paving that has heaved at one end is the whole of that Friday.

**SHE IS SEVENTY-EIGHT AND THERE ARE NINE FEET OF PAVING IN HER OWN SIDE PASSAGE AT NUMBER TWELVE THAT HAS RISEN AN INCH AND A HALF AT ONE END WHERE THE ROOT OF A LILAC GOES UNDER IT, AND SHE MEANS TO TAKE THE THREE FLAGS UP, PUT THE GROUND BACK AND LAY THEM AGAIN, AND HER SISTER IS IN THE KITCHEN WITH THE DOOR OPEN THE WHOLE OF THAT DAY AND DOES NOT COME OUT.**

Number twelve, in that side passage. It is about four feet wide between the wall of the house and a boundary wall of old brick, and it runs from the back door of the house to the gate, and it is paved the whole length.

She laid it herself, most of it, in one of those years when there was nothing else going on.

The lilac is against the house wall about a third of the way along, in a strip of ground about two feet wide between the flags and the wall, and it has been in that strip since the house was built, and it is taller than the kitchen window and it flowers at the end of May in a way that the whole passage can smell from the front door.

It has also put one root straight down under the flags and about a foot and a half along under them, and in the winter that root took up water faster than the ground around it could give it back, and the ground under the second flag went soft, and the second flag has come up over the winter and sat there, proud of the two either side of it, about an inch and a half proud at the wall end.

That is the whole of what is wrong with nine feet of paving.

She has a bar, a bolster, a short pick, a spade with the blade ground down on one edge, a piece of board about three feet long and a trowel. She has a spirit level she trusts and a shorter one she does not, and she has a bucket and about half a bag of sharp sand left over from the winter.

She starts at the near end.

The first flag comes up easier than anything she has lifted in a long time. That is not good news and she knows it before she has it clear of the ground, because a flag that comes up on a bar is a flag with nothing under it, and the whole point of a flag is what is under it.

There is nothing under it.

She gets the bar in under the edge and lifts, and the slab comes up flat on the blade with about four inches of sand on top of it and nothing underneath it, and she puts it down against the wall and looks at the hole.

What is in the hole is rubble. Not hardcore laid on purpose; the small stuff that comes out of a wall when a wall has been leaning on the ground beside it for fifty years, brick broken small, and the odd lump of the old foundation of the house itself, which is a different colour and a lot older than anything else in that passage.

She takes it all out in a bucket and not a barrow, because a bucket is easier to lift at seventy-eight and a wheelbarrow will not go round the corner of that passage.

Under the rubble there is ground. That ground is dark and it is not soft, and she puts the bar down on it and leans on it and the bar goes in a couple of inches and stops.

That is the difference and it is the whole of the job.

A bar going in and stopping is ground. A bar going in the whole length of its shaft is loose, and loose ground under a flag means the flag is standing on nothing and the water goes down through it and out somewhere under the house, and it comes up again as frost. Take the loose stuff out and put something back that you can build a base on, and the flag sits on that and does not go anywhere.

She puts four inches of the rubble back in, broken down so that nothing in it is bigger than a fist, and packs it with the back of the spade until the bar will not go into it at all, then screeds the top, working the high places down with the bar and building the low ones up with more rubble.

Sand goes on top. Sharp sand, not building sand, and she has been told so by everybody who has ever laid a flag and she has never had reason to find out otherwise. Sand goes on a hardcore base about half an inch thick and it is bedded down into it and not laid on it, and a flag on sand wants to be worked down with the heel until the sand comes up round its edges and will not let it up again.

---

The second flag is the one that has come up and she knows it before she touches it.

It stands up at the wall end of that run and is level with the third at the other, and it rocks if you put a heel on it, and she has been putting a heel on it since February and has been meaning to do something about it since February.

She takes it up at the wall end with a bolster and a lever because there is soil under there now and a small root about the thickness of a pencil lying across the bed of it, and she cuts through it with the pick, and it is the tail end of the thing that has lifted the flag and it goes on into the ground on the other side of the hollow without a fuss.

Under that flag the ground is loose all the way across, and about a foot and a half along, and she takes it out to the bottom of the loose, which is where the bar stops, and she builds it back in the same way.

And then there is the other half of that flag, the part along the house wall, which is not loose at all.

She can tell from the way the bar goes in before she has got the flag off it.

So the fault is not the whole flag. The fault is nine inches of it at the wall end and a foot and a half of it in the middle, and the rest of that flag is sitting on ground that has never moved and never will.

She lays that flag back on the same bed it came off.

---

The third flag is up, and the fourth is up, and that is where the Friday stops being about the lilac.

There was a root under the fourth one.

She has the bar in under the edge of it and she can see where it has been: a hollow in the sand about four inches deep and eighteen inches long, the shape of a finger, running away under the third flag towards the house. The root is the thickness of a wrist at that point and it has gone along there since the flag was laid, and it has taken water into itself every winter and given a little of it back and taken some more, and the sand has washed out of the ground under it and gone somewhere under the house.

She gets the pick and goes at it, and it is root and not stone and it does not want to be cut. She gets the pick behind it and puts a lump of it in the bucket and cuts another lump off and puts that in the bucket too, and it goes on for the better part of a quarter of an hour.

She stops cutting it because she can see where it goes.

It goes down about a foot and a half and turns and goes along under the next two flags, back towards the house wall, and there is no point in a woman of seventy-eight taking a lilac out of a passage on her own. She knows exactly what a lilac does about a root and it is not that the root goes away. A lilac is a bush that makes root on purpose. Cut the top off a lilac and you have three lilacs. Cut a root off a lilac and you have a lilac that spends the next two years putting out four where there was one, and it does that at the wall end of a passage four feet wide, and it will lift that flag again in about four winters and probably sooner.

So the root stays and the top of it is cut off and it is cut square with a spade, not with a saw, because a root cut with a spade goes back to soil and a root cut with a saw goes back to a face that will not.

The hole where the root was comes up to the level of the ground either side of it, and that is not easy to do with a bar because the ground either side of it is firm and the ground over it has been gone for years, and she builds it up with the rubble and packs it and builds it up again until the bar will not go in.

Before she puts anything back she goes along the whole run with the bar and puts it down at each end of every flag, because a fault like that has a length and the only way to find where one ends is to put a bar down all along it and feel for the place where it stops being soft.

It is soft under four of them and it is not soft under the fifth. She puts the bar down under the far edge of that fifth flag, at the wall side, and leans on it, and it goes in about a couple of inches and stops, the way the first one did in the morning. She puts it down again at the gate end of the same flag and it stops there too.

So the fault is four flags long and not five.

She stands in that passage with the bar in her hand for a while. The foot of paving in front of the gate is as sound as the day it was laid and there is nothing whatever under it to be sorry for, and a woman of seventy-eight does not take up a foot that has not moved because the four feet beside it have.

The flags go back in the order the gaps are, which is the near one first and then the two over the hollow and then the fourth and the fifth, and the whole of that Friday is the nine feet.

She beds each one down and works it with the heel of her boot until the sand comes up around the edge of it, and she stands on each one and rocks it, and when it goes down solid she leaves it and does not touch it again, because a flag that is walked on is a flag that beds itself and a flag that is hammered is a flag with a cracked corner.

The joints she does with the edge of the board, which is about the width of a joint, and running it along the line between two flags puts the sand in and takes the high arris off the higher one, and the joint is what a path is judged on and not the flags.

The fall goes the right way. She set it that way when she laid it. It falls about a quarter of an inch to the foot away from the house, so that if it rains hard the water goes down the passage and out through the gate and not under the kitchen floor.

Her sister calls out from the kitchen about the front step, which has been leaning since the winter and is not the side passage, and about the flue, which nobody has been up.

"There was a man on the front step on Tuesday," her sister calls.

"He was on the front step on Tuesday," she tells her.

"Then he can come back to it, and he can look at the flue while he is stood there."

"He can look at the flue," she tells her.

That is the hour of it, and there is nothing in it about this passage. Neither of them mentions the flags or the lilac or the root, and the door stands open behind her sister the whole time, and her sister does not come out into that passage at all.

From where she is kneeling the woman can hear the chair move twice and a plate go down on a table, and none of it is about what she is doing, and she gets on with it.

By the time the light has come off the top of the boundary wall she is sitting on the step with her tea gone cold on the flags beside her, and the whole nine feet is down, the joints are tight, the fall is the fall it was, and there is no flag on that path now she would put a coin under.

The lilac is cut back off the passage by about two feet and its cut face is pale and wet where the spade went through it.

She gets up and looks at it and then she leaves it, because that face is wet and will go grey in about a fortnight on its own, and by then it will be a foot above the path and out of the way of anybody's head.

It is not going to be this summer. The flags are down and the sand is under them and the joints are tight and she has got her boot on the last one and it does not move at all.

And under the middle of that path, where the passage has been laid back down over the top of it, the cut end of that root is in the ground with the hole filled and made good and the ground round it firm to a bar. It will push a flag up again some day. She knows the year it will do it and not which one.
