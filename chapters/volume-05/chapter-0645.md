# Chapter 0645 — Six Things On An Envelope

Thursday the third of June 2021, in this borough. She is at her own kitchen table from about ten past ten in the morning until about a quarter past twelve, and she does not get up from it, and the whole of it is a pencil and an envelope and six lines written on the wrong side of it.

**SHE WAS ASKED IN HER OWN PASSAGE ON THE MONDAY WHICH OF THE TWO PEOPLE COMING UP THAT PATH SHE WAS GOING TO TELL, AND THE OTHER ONE SAID SHE DID NOT KNOW, AND ON THE THURSDAY SHE STOPS TRYING TO ANSWER THAT AND STARTS INSTEAD ON WHAT SHE ACTUALLY WANTS, AND SHE WRITES SIX THINGS ON THE BACK OF AN ENVELOPE AND LEAVES IT FACE UP ON A KITCHEN TABLE, AND SHE PUTS THE GATE AT THE TOP AND THE STOVE FOURTH AND A LIGHT IN THE PASSAGE LAST, AND SHE CANNOT WORK OUT WHY SHE PUT THEM IN THAT ORDER, AND THE ONLY PERSON WHO WOULD BE ABLE TO WORK IT OUT IS A MAN SHE HAS ASKED NOTHING AND WHO IS NOT COMING UP THAT PATH UNTIL WEDNESDAY.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. There are six lines on it and five of the six are about that house and one of the six is not.

---

Here is the thing that is sitting in her passage, and it has been sitting there since Monday morning.

*Which of you do I tell.*

And she said she did not know and she was not lying, and then the other one said *well, neither do I*, and then the gate went, and that was the end of the best hour the two of them have ever had.

And what happens next is not that she decides.

What happens next is that she waits, on the Tuesday and on the Wednesday, for either of them to knock, and neither of them does, and the reason neither of them does is that the last thing that happened in that kitchen was the two of them agreeing out loud, in the plainest words, to something that was not true, and there is not one of the three of them who wants to be the first one back in the room.

---

So on the Thursday she goes to the drawer.

The drawer under the telephone is the drawer in this house where everything goes that has a use and no place. There is string in it in three thicknesses. There are fuses. There are two screws and a wall plug and a battery that is not the right battery. There is a pair of shears that do not shear. There is a folded instruction leaflet for an appliance that was taken away in the winter before last.

And there is a gas envelope. It came on the second and she has opened it and put the bill in the cupboard drawer over the sink and left the envelope in this drawer, because the back of a gas envelope is a good place to write on and she has known that for about fifty years.

She takes it out. And she has not got a pen.

She goes and gets a pencil out of the tin on the shelf over the oven, and the tin is on the shelf because a woman of seventy-eight cannot reach the top of a cupboard, and the pencil is the same pencil she has had since before Christmas and it is about four inches long.

She sits down at the table.

---

Here is what she writes, and it takes her about ten minutes, and the ten minutes are not ten minutes of writing.

She writes:

the gate

and then she stops.

And here is the first thing about the order of this list, and it is the thing the whole of this is about, and she has not worked it out.

The gate is at the top.

The gate swings. It has swung since it was put on and it comes in on the path about four inches and it bangs at about four in the morning when there is a wind from the west, and she has not slept properly through a night of wind since before Christmas.

And she has been asked by nobody to fix it. She has never even mentioned it to a soul. There is no person in this borough who knows the gate bangs.

---

She puts the pencil down and looks at the first line.

And she writes, underneath it:

the front room

and she does not know why that is second either, and then she does know why that is second, and the knowing is not a comfort.

The front room has not been painted since before Christmas and she has not mentioned it to anybody, and it is the room where the two of them sat on a Wednesday in May with a bag of frozen vegetables on a table beside her, and it is the best room in the house.

She writes it down because it is on her mind.

And then she sits looking at it for about a minute, because that is the one of the six she cannot put any reason to at all, and the other five all have a reason, and the second thing on the page is a room with nothing wrong with it.

And then she writes nothing under it, and moves on.

---

She writes, third:

the window in the back room

and this one she writes and does not stop on, because this one is easy and true and cold.

It is a small window in a room she does not use, except to go through it to the outside, and the catch on it has been broken since the winter before last, and it does not shut, and in a wind the room the window is in is four degrees colder than the rest of the house, and she has had a folded blanket over it since before Christmas because a blanket is cheaper than a window.

---

And then she writes, fourth:

the hotplate on the left

and her hand goes slower on this one.

Here is what she has done with the stove.

It has been going since before Christmas, because the man at the shop told her it would go at the end of the summer, and she said so out loud to a woman in her late forties in her own kitchen on the Monday, and the woman said it had already gone.

And so there are two of them now who have said that sentence in that room and it belongs to neither of them.

And now she is writing it down, and writing it down is a different act, and she cannot say why one was easy and one is not.

What she has worked out, sitting at this table with the pencil in her hand, is that saying a thing out loud to a person in a kitchen is a thing that can be denied later. Saying it to a person in a kitchen leaves it in the air. It can be taken back. You can say *I only said it to be talking*.

And writing it down on an envelope is not that. Writing it down makes it a thing that exists in the world. And once it exists it is only a matter of somebody reading it and being in the room when it happens.

So she writes it fourth, which is in the middle, which is not where a person puts the thing that is actually going to hurt her.

---

She writes, fifth:

the bath tap

and this one drips, and has dripped since before Christmas, and she has put a folded cloth in the bowl under it, which is a thing a person does in a house where nobody is going to come.

---

And she writes, sixth:

the passage light

and then she stops, because that is the sixth one, and she looks at the whole of it.

---

Here is what is on the envelope, and she reads it back to herself in the kitchen, and she does not say it out loud:

the gate

the front room

the window in the back room

the hotplate on the left

the bath tap

the passage light

---

And she reads it four times, and it is in no order she can defend, and she has the pencil in her hand and she puts the point on the envelope about two inches under the first line, and she rubs the gate out.

That is the last thing that happens to it.

She does not rub it out because it is wrong. Every one of the six is a real thing in that house and she can point at all of them.

She rubs the gate out because it is the only one of the six that is about somebody else, and she has been in this borough since before Christmas and she has worked out in about a week what everybody in it has not: that a woman of seventy-eight who writes down the gate is a woman who has written down that she is expecting somebody, and there is no way on earth she is going to hand that to a person in a van.

She rubs it out with the side of her thumb and the pad of her finger, because there is no rubber in this house and there has not been a rubber in this house since before Christmas, and pencil does not come off paper under a thumb, it only goes soft and grey.

And then she writes it again, higher up, above the front room, and it goes back to being first.

---

And then she thinks about tearing it up.

That is the honest part and she sits with it for a while and she does not do it and the reason she does not do it is worth putting down, because it is the whole of what she has worked out about herself in ten days.

If she tears it up then there is no list. And if there is no list then on Wednesday, when the van comes up the path, she is a woman of seventy-eight with a hotplate that is going, which is exactly what she was on the Wednesday before last, and it will go on going, and nobody will be told anything by anybody, and in a month she will be the woman at the end of the road with the gate.

And if there is a list then there is a piece of paper in this kitchen with six things on it, and one of the six is a gate, and a person standing in this kitchen can read it.

She does not know which of those two she is doing it for. That is the honest position at a table in a kitchen at a quarter past twelve on the third of June 2021.

And then she picks the pencil up and puts the point on the envelope and writes the gate a second time higher up, and it goes back to being first, and it is a cleaner line than the first one.

---

She puts the pencil down and the envelope is on the table in front of her and the table is under the window where she put it on the Thursday, and there is a ring on the wood where a cup stood every day until the Thursday.

She does not move the table.

And she leaves the envelope face up.

That is not a decision and she would say so if anybody asked her. She does not put paper face down. Her mother did not put paper face down. There is a bin in the passage and the bin is the right place for paper that is finished with, and she has not finished with this, and it is not in the bin.

And there is one more thing about the face, and she is aware of it and does not look at it.

She has put it face up because she has not thought about it.

---

And then she gets up at about a quarter past twelve and goes and stands at the sink, and the corner by the sink where a stool used to stand on its side against the wall on top of nothing is empty, and has been empty since the Thursday of the twenty-seventh of May.

There is a woman three doors down who has a stool by her table now and who works lates four nights out of seven and who stood in a doorway on the Thursday of the twenty-seventh of May and said *that's not mine* to a stranger.

And there is a man who comes on Wednesdays in a white van and who built something on a bench in a unit off an industrial estate and put it down in the middle of the floor of a woman standing at a sill, and who was in her front room on the Wednesday of the nineteenth of May and was told that a council came about the damp, and who has not been back since.

And there is a woman in her late forties who mended a leg on her own back step with a bracket off a shelf, and who was in her kitchen on the Monday and said the stove was not sound, and who went out into the light at half past ten and let the gate go behind her.

Three people. Three doors or a road between. And a woman of seventy-eight has now written down six things and shown it to none of them.

---

And the gate bangs at about four in the morning when there is a wind from the west, and it has been banging since before Christmas, and on the third of June 2021 it is the first thing on a piece of paper in a kitchen in this borough, and it was rubbed out and written again, and it is still the first thing.

**SHE WROTE SIX REAL THINGS ON THE BACK OF A GAS ENVELOPE AND RUBBED THE ONLY ONE OF THE SIX THAT WAS ABOUT SOMEBODY ELSE OUT AND WROTE IT AGAIN, AND LEFT IT FACE UP ON A KITCHEN TABLE, AND SHE CANNOT DEFEND THE ORDER, AND THE ONE THING ON IT THAT IS ACTUALLY GOING TO HURT HER IS FOURTH AND THE ONE SHE PUTS AT THE TOP IS THE ONE SHE ACTUALLY WANTS.**

(End of chapter 0645)
