# Chapter 1021 — Down To The Bottom Of The Bed

Undated in this draft.

**THE DRYING FLOOR OVER THE STORE AT THE UNIT IS COLD AT THE TIMBER.**

The drying floor over that store at the unit is up a ladder from the middle of the store and it runs the whole length of the building with a slope off it that way, which is the only slope in that yard anybody has ever built on purpose. It is a low room under a roof that has been felted over so many times that the felt is the roof, and there is one roof light in the middle of it and the light in there is the colour of weak tea whatever the weather is doing. A brick chimney breast comes up through the middle of the floor with a stove flue in it, and the flue belongs to the stove in the room below, and the brick round it is the warmest thing in that building and the boards at the far end from it are as cold as the yard is.

The whole floor used to be stacked with the store's own timber. There are joists at eighteen inches and they are old pine and they are level across two of them and have dipped about a quarter of an inch at the third where something heavy stood on it for a long time in a war, and the boards laid across them are the floor and they are the boards that were once the bottom of a stack. The stack that is up there now is the top of that pile: about nine foot of ordinary deal in lengths of ten and twelve and sixteen, stood on edge against the brick chimney breast and against the far wall, and it has been there since before the war and has been dry since before the war, and it has never once been covered up because nobody in that yard has ever believed a roof.

He went up the ladder at the middle of the day with a bar in his hand and stood in the weak tea light with it and did not put it down. He had come up there because it is the one room in that yard where nobody comes, and because a man who has decided to ask a woman what money is needs an hour on his own before he does anything else with the week.

There is a tray wanted under a range at number twelve when that range comes off that wall. Not a fancy one and not anything with a marble top on it. A galvanised tray about two feet by three with a lip an inch and a half turned up all round, so that what comes off a floor behind a fire has something to run along and away instead of standing in a puddle going back into brick. It is the least difficult thing in the whole of that fortnight of work and it is the first thing in it, and there is not a pound of it in that store and not a penny of it on him.

There is, however, four foot of deal on that floor.

He put his hand flat on the face of the bottom board in the stack and turned his head and looked along the bottom of it, which is what a man does when he has been avoiding looking at something for two days. It is four inch deal, the width of a shelf board, and it has been in a dry loft since before the war and it is the driest and the best thing he owns. You take a tray off the bottom of a stack like that and you take the best timber in the building, because you cannot take it off the top without cutting the stack up about the place you want it and because the bottom of a dry stack is the piece that has never moved.

That is the decision and it took him the whole of the middle of the day and about four feet of a board.

A tray wants to be out of a board that has been dry for forty years, because a tray is a thing that has to sit under a fire with water coming at it and a board that has never dried properly will go soft in a fortnight, and there will not be a second time coming on that. The bottom two lengths of that stack are the answer. He measured them against the wall with his own two hands because the rule lies on a rack two floors down and he was not coming down for it, and both are over the length he wants and both are wide enough to come to two feet by three with the lip turned up off the edge of the board and nothing bought in.

And here is what it costs and he worked it out standing on the joists with the bar in his hand.

Cut four feet off the bottom of that stack and the stack is not a stack. It is nine foot of deal with a hole in the foot of it, and the boards above the cut will want to move across and down into the gap over the first wet winter, and everything in the building above that is out of level with everything else by the time anybody looks at it again. He cannot put those four feet back. Deal that has been drying since before the war does not go back on a stack and stand up square for another forty, and there is not one person alive who could put it back the way it is.

And the oak on that rack two floors down is not the answer and never was. That is four foot of stock bought in the spring against a set of new joists and a staircase in a town a long way off up the road, and it belongs to a job that has not started, and it will be wanted back, and he marked on the rack at the end of the summer that he would not touch it, and he is not going to touch it now that he is short and has been asked for a wall.

One thing in that yard got worse while he was up a ladder with four feet of deal in his head, and it is four feet of brick out of the kerb round the weigh pit on the south side with the bed of it washed out along the whole run and a gap in it you could put a finger into.

He has not told anybody at that unit and he is not going to. What it wants first is the bed opened out to the bottom so that it can be filled and left to go off for a month before anybody goes near it with a stone at all, which is a week's work for one man with a bar, and he has not got the week, and the week he has got is the week he has promised a woman at number twelve.

So the bed will stay open at the top and the sheet of chequer plate over the near corner of that pit will go on lipping on nothing until somebody puts a boot through it in the dark, which is further off now than it was three weeks ago, and nobody in that building has the faintest idea there is a fault at all.

He came down the ladder at the end of it with the bar over his shoulder and the stack lying open above him in the dark of that loft, and the boards under his boots on the way down were colder than they had been when he went up them, which is the whole of what a drying floor is for and the whole of what he got out of going up there, and he got the money for the tin out of the lining of his coat where it had lain for the best part of two days, and put it in the pocket with the other money from number ten, and neither lot of it is enough and both lots of it are the first money he has taken in ten years.

That stack is lying open along its whole length in a loft over his own store, four feet gone out of the bottom of it, with the cold coming up through the boards.
