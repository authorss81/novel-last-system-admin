# Chapter 0800 — The Cowl And The Plate

Thursday the sixteenth of March 2023, in this borough. All of that Thursday goes on the air brick at the back of that unit and on the wall behind it, from first light to the last, and the damp coming up that wall is what it is about.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES HAS A DAMP PATCH COMING UP THE WALL BEHIND A COWL ON AN AIR BRICK AND MEANS TO PATCH THE WALL, THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN WILL NOT, AND TAKES THE COWL OFF AND OPENS A WAY AT THE RUBBLE PACKED BEHIND THE AIR BRICK AND LAYS A NEW SLATE PLATE OVER IT AND BRACES THE COWL TO THE BRICK BESIDE IT AND MAKES GOOD THE FACE BELOW, AND THE WALL IS DRY TO THE SHOULDER BY THE SUMMER.**

He has run that unit for years and nobody at that address thinks about how old he is any more. The woman from two doors down comes in through the roller door in the morning and the two of them go out at the back of the building and spend the day on that wall.

---

That wall has been dark from the floor up since before the weather turned.

It is the inside face of the back wall of that unit and it is nine feet long and about six of it is dark from the skirting upward, and the dark goes up in a soft edge the way damp goes up, and at the top of it the paint has blown off in blisters the size of a thumbnail.

The air brick is on the outside of that same wall, about two feet up, with a cowl on it. The cowl has been on it since the unit was somebody else's.

The man means to patch the wall.

He has the patching filler and a filling knife and a bag of powdered plaster, and he means to take the blisters off, fill them, and put two coats over the whole six feet of it, and that is what he told her he was going to do, and she told him it would not last a winter.

---

"What is that brick for."

"It is an air brick."

"That is what it is called." She is standing in front of it with her back to the unit and her face to the wall. "What is it for."

"It lets air out."

"It lets air out of something." She goes and gets the crowbar off the wall and puts it down on the flags. "You have got a floor in that unit. There was a void under it. That brick has been letting air out of that void for forty years, and the cowl on it is what stops the rain going in at it, and while there was air coming out of it that brick never once had anything behind it but air."

"There is nothing behind it now."

"There is rubble." She puts the point of the bar into the head of that brick and leans on it. "When they laid that floor they filled that void up, and they filled it hard to the top of that brick and a good foot past it, and then they laid the boards over it. And there has been no air path out of that brick since the year it was laid, and a brick with nothing behind it does not do nothing. It has a hole in it and something on the other side of the hole that is wet ground, and it draws."

"Draws what."

"Draws the damp. It has been drawing it through that brick and up the inside of that wall all winter, and it will go on drawing until there is somewhere for the air to go again."

---

Then she takes the cowl off, and that is where the whole of it is.

The cowl is a hood of sheet iron on two brackets. The brackets are screwed into the brick joints on either side of the air brick and they have both rusted where they go in and the right-hand one has pulled about a quarter of an inch out of the joint.

Behind the cowl there is a plate. The plate is a piece of slate about a foot across, bedded on the wall in the usual way, and it has been bedded there so long that the whole of it has gone down the face of the wall about the thickness of a coin at the bottom edge, and it has cracked through the middle from the screw that went through it, and the gap behind the bottom edge of it is the width of a knife.

She puts her fingers into that gap and brings about a half inch of wet grit out of it, and it is grey and it is cold and it has been behind there for two winters.

---

Then she opens the air path, and it is the whole of the job.

The air brick is four bricks set into the face of the wall with the courses above and below going across it, and the throat of it is open, because it has been open since the year it was built and that is what it is for. What is behind the throat is what is in the way: a pocket of rubble packed hard against the back of it with no way at it from anywhere. She knocks the perpends out of the course directly under that brick with a cold chisel and takes that course out entire, so that the opening goes down a course and there is flat ground at the bottom of it to work a bar and a hook into, and then she goes in with the bar and a length of scaffold tube she has bent into a hook at the end, and she works the rubble out of the void behind.

It comes out in lumps. The packing behind that brick is a pocket of rubble about the depth of a bucket, going back and up into a pocket under the floor that was filled in the year it was laid, and she gets at it from the outside on the bar and then she gets at it from the inside on the hook through the gap where the boards have lifted at the skirting. It takes most of the morning and both of them and it makes about nine or ten bucketfuls, and most of it went out through the gap at the skirting and was shovelled out of the way of that door.

There is nothing wet about any part of that. Nothing is mixed and nothing is poured and the boards on that floor come up on the dry side and go down on the dry side.

By the middle of the day there is air behind that brick for the first time since the floor was laid.

---

Then the plate.

She does not point the hole and she does not put a fill over it and she does not put anything wet on that wall anywhere at any point of that day.

She takes the old slate off and breaks it up, and then she dresses a new one on the back of a bucket with a bolster, an inch over on every side than what came off, and she lays it on a bed of the broken-up pieces of the old one. A slate goes on a bed of slate and not on anything else, which is why the head of the old one has never come out of the groove that was cut for it and is not going to in another forty years, and which is the whole of what he was trying to do with a bag of powdered plaster on the other side of the wall. The bottom edge is a different matter, and the bottom edge of the old one has dropped the thickness of a coin down the face of that wall in about ten years, and it will go on dropping the same.

The top edge of the new plate goes into a groove cut in the joint above the air brick with the bolster and it is packed at the back of it with small offcuts of slate so that it cannot come out, and it is fixed with four copper nails into the joints either side of it.

---

And then the face of the brick below it.

Under the course she had taken out to open the path there are two more courses of facing brick, and those are the blown ones: four of those eight bricks have come out of the face altogether and the other four are spalled to a chisel, and the joints under them are open the width of a coin and you can see daylight through two of them from inside.

She takes the spalled faces off those four with a bolster, which is what makes good a brick you cannot replace, and then she puts four bricks back into the opening from the stack by the gate, taken from the back of that stack and not off the face of any wall, and she beds them on the chippings that came out of the joints and packs the perpends with the same, and she does not put a trowel to any of it and she does not mix anything, and the face of that brick is not as good as it was when it was laid and it is a great deal better than four holes and two days of rain.

---

Then the cowl goes back on, and it goes back on differently.

She puts the brackets in new, into fresh joints, and she does not hang the cowl on the two brackets it came off. She puts a flat bar of iron under the front of that hood, from the lip of it back and down into the joint below the air brick — one bolt through the front plate of the cowl and one fixing into the brick, on the diagonal, so that the wind cannot get under the front of that hood and lift it and work the screws back out of the wall the way it worked the old ones.

"That is the brace."

"That is a brace and it is there for one reason and that is that a cowl on a wall is fixed at the top and gets pushed up at the bottom, and a plate with nothing keeping it down comes off that wall in the first gale after it is on." She puts her hand flat on the slate and pushes it, and it does not move. "And you are not putting anything on the inside of that wall."

"I was not going to put anything on the inside of that wall in the end."

"You were going to fill six feet of it and put two coats over the top of it." She turns round to the wall inside and puts her hand flat on it. "There is a man in this row who did that in the spring of the year before last and it is all down again by November and his is worse than yours. You do not stop water coming through a wall by sealing the face of the wall on the dry side. You stop it by stopping it coming."

---

They go out at the front and he shuts the roller door behind them, and that door goes down on its rail without a sound and always has.

The near gate comes home with the palm of a hand and has done every day since the end of February. There is a length of softwood with two housings in it lying on the bench behind him and he has not cut one of them.

And the post that gate hangs on has gone over at the height of his hip. He found it in the middle of March with his own hands, with his weight on it, a thumbnail the first time and twice that the second, and it has not come back since, and the gate comes home regardless, because the brace in that gate does not care what the post is doing and a gate is only as good as the post it hangs on.

And by the summer that wall will be dry to the shoulder, and there will not be a single thing put on the inside of it.

There is a thing on the end of that pipe now that was not on the end of it a week ago — a cowl, braced, on a slate, on slate, over air that has got somewhere to go — and he cannot get a bar in behind the back of that plate to draw the old one out, because the rubble that was packed behind it has to come out through a hole in a floor he cannot lift.

He has the end of a bar and the rest of the morning.
