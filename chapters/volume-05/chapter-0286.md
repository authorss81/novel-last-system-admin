# Chapter 0286 — One Bag

Tuesday the third of January 2017, from about half past seven in the morning, the top of Marsh Lane in Tideglass, and a parade of shops.

**A LINE FORMED AT HALF PAST EIGHT IN THE COLD FOR SOMETHING A DOZEN PEOPLE HAD BEEN TOLD THEY MIGHT GET, AND IT MOVED ONCE, AND A MAN CAME OUT HOLDING A BAG IN EACH HAND, AND THE WOMAN AT THE FRONT OF IT ASKED HIM A QUESTION HE COULD NOT ANSWER, AND THE LINE WENT.**

There was ice on the top of Marsh Lane again at about ten past eight and the gritting lorry had been down and had gone up the Cleeve road instead, which everybody in that street has a theory about and none of them has ever asked anybody.

It was four degrees and it felt like about one, and the sun was coming up flat and yellow over the low end of the road and doing nothing with it, and about a dozen people were standing in a line along the front of the parade outside the shop with the green sign, from the shelter end back towards the bank.

They had been there since about twenty past eight and the line had not moved once.

There was nothing written on anything. There was a man in a hi-vis in the doorway of that shop with his hands in his pockets, and behind him in the back a lorry backed up to the kerb with its tailgate open, and on the tailgate was a pallet with the shrink still on the bottom row of it.

"You waiting for the coal?" said a woman to a man in a fleece.

"Apparently," said the man in the fleece.

The woman who works in a shop had been at that counter for two years and had not been outside it in a hat before ten in her life, and what she had been told on the Friday came from the woman who has been in that hall about two years, and what the woman who has been in that hall about two years had been told came off a man at the yard at Sarn on the Thursday, and what the man at the yard at Sarn had been told he could not say where from, and he had not said it, and the woman who works in a shop had put a word in on it herself on the way in, which is how it got to the top of Marsh Lane by eight o'clock in the morning.

She had told two people. One of them had told the woman at the front of the line with the two children.

---

The woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall had been in that hall at half past seven with her coat on and had had her hand in her pocket for the meter twice since she got in, and she came out at a quarter to eight and locked up, and she was in that line at about twenty to nine with her bag on her shoulder, third in from the front, and she had not told anybody she was going and had told nobody she was there.

The man who works nights came off at half past six and had a cup of tea in him and stood at the back of it, and he had been at that hall on the sixth of December with his coat still on and had not slept since about ten that morning.

There was a woman at the front of it with two children and a folded pushchair and a child in a hat she had put on the wrong way round, and there was a man with a dog on a length of blue string, and there was a man who was not from the street at all and had a van with no sign on it parked at the end of Marsh Lane where you are not supposed to park.

At about ten to nine the man in the hi-vis came out with the door shut behind him and stood on the pavement in front of the line, and the whole line looked at him, and nobody said anything.

"Right," he said. "One each. One bag. That's it, and then that's it."

The woman at the front of it said, "Right."

But she did not go up, and neither did the next four, and the man in the hi-vis came down the line a step holding a bag out in each hand like a man holding two coats, and he got to the woman with the two children and put one down at his own feet on the pavement and said, "That's yours, love."

"I didn't come for it," said the woman with the two children.

"Everybody in this line came for it."

"Who's it from?"

"He's over there," said the man in the hi-vis, and put his thumb over his shoulder at the back of the shop.

"Who's over there."

"That," said the man in the hi-vis, "is the exact thing I can't tell you. I've been told to bring it off and leave it with the shop and not to give it out till about nine and not to say whose it is, and I've been doing this for twenty years and I still don't know whose anything is, and I can tell you the pallet's got a town on it about sixty miles off and the town is not this one."

The woman with the two children looked at the bag on the pavement at his feet for a bit, and then she said, "Then I'll not take it off you," and she turned round and said down the line, loud enough for the middle of it, "It's off a lorry. He can't say whose it's off. I'm not standing here two hours for that."

And that was the whole of what happened on the top of Marsh Lane, and the line went.

It went the way a thing goes when everybody in it has said the same sentence at the same moment. The man with the dog went first, back down towards the bus shelter. The woman with the two children folded the pushchair and went up the parade. The man who works nights got his tea and went. The man in the hi-vis stood on the pavement holding two bags, then put them both back on the pallet and shut the tailgate on it.

The woman of about forty-two was still standing where she was when it went.

"You not going either?" said the man in the hi-vis.

"I've been in about eight of these lines in this town in my life," said the woman of about forty-two, "and I have never once been in one that came to nothing. This one's come to nothing at about ten to nine, and I have got a hall to open at half past six tonight and I would rather be cold than spend another morning on a pavement with a load of other people's nothing."

And she went down to the parade and got her tea from the machine in the one that opens early and did not look back up the lane. The man in the hi-vis went inside, and the man at the end of Marsh Lane with the van that has no sign on it did not move his van, and the shop opened at half past nine and sold the same thing it has sold since about 1970.

**AND THE PALLET WENT BACK ON THAT LORRY AT HALF PAST FOUR AND WENT OFF UP THE CLEEVE ROAD, AND IT WAS NOT OFF AGAIN UNTIL THE SEVENTH OF MARCH, AND IN THAT PARADE NINE PEOPLE SAID ON THE THURSDAY THEY HAD BEEN TOLD IT WAS ON AND NOT ONE OF THEM SAID ON THE THURSDAY WHO THEY HAD BEEN TOLD IT BY.**
