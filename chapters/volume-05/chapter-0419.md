# Chapter 0419 — The End of the Lane

Tuesday the twelfth of June 2018, beginning at about a quarter to nine in the morning and finishing at about four in the afternoon, a shop off that parade, and a lane off a side street, in Tideglass.

**HE CARRIED A SASH WINDOW ABOUT A HUNDRED AND TWENTY YARD DOWN A LANE THAT HE HAD BEEN AT THE TOP OF FOR SIX YEARS, AND HE PUT IT DOWN ON THE GRASS AT THE BOTTOM AGAINST A WALL, AND HE WAS GOING TO THE BOTTOM OF THAT LANE ON HIS OWN ACCOUNT, AND HE TOLD NOBODY.**

There is a merchant's about sixty yard back from that parade where the man behind the counter sells pipe and fixings and cuts keys and has been in that building since 1988. Nothing in this chapter happens in there for longer than about nine minutes.

The lane is off a side street two hundred yard from that parade. It is a private access track with a gate at the top and about a hundred and twenty yard of it, and there are five houses on the west side of it and nothing on the east, and the east side is a brick wall about six foot high that belongs to a yard nobody uses, and there is a grass verge about three foot wide at the foot of that wall for most of the length of it.

At the bottom of that lane it opens onto a field head with a five-bar field gate in the hedge, and the ground there is a made head about thirty foot across where a farm track used to turn, and it has been made up over about twenty years, a bit at a time, by everybody who has ever turned round there.

The man in his forties has a job in this borough on the west side of that lane and has been at the top of it about four times a week since the February. He has not been to the bottom of it once in that time.

---

At about a quarter to nine on the Tuesday he was at a counter off that parade for a sheet of something, and the man behind it cut the sheet in two while he waited and said nothing about anything, and he paid and carried it out and put it in his own van, and that van is on a hardstanding eight miles off until about two in the afternoon because the merchant he is waiting on has a delivery in.

The house is the second on the west side of that lane and it has been empty since about October and he has been in it a dozen times.

He has gutted the two rooms at the back. He has taken out about four sheets of plasterboard and lifted a floor and pulled a chimney breast out to the steel in the room that was the kitchen, and a man who owns that house has said he will do what he likes with the back of it and leave the front alone.

On the Monday he took a top sash out of the window in the room that was the kitchen.

About three foot six by two foot two, in its original frame, glazed in one old heavy pane, the putty original, both horns broken off. It weighs about twenty-five pound. He has it standing on edge against the wall of that room with the face of it in, and the room is bare boards and a stripped chimney and there is nowhere in that house to stand it that is not in the way of the work.

He could have left it in the house. He could have gone back for his own van at half past one and taken it to a merchant's yard and left it there, and he has done that with other people's windows and other people's doors about four times in six years, and that is the ordinary way and it costs nothing.

What he did with it instead took about four minutes and the rest of the morning.

He came out of the front of that house at about a quarter past ten with it on his shoulder, the bottom member on his hip and his hand under the top corner, which is how a man carries a three foot six window the length of a lane, and he shut that front door behind him with his heel, which does not lock, and went down the west side of that lane.

And he went all the way to the bottom. Which he had not done in six years.

The man in his thirties was in the second gateway of that lane at about half past ten.

He is in that house on the same job, in the front rooms, for a different man who has nothing to do with the man in his forties' owner and does not know there is a man at the back of the house. He had a skip outside that front door and he had been clearing the front rooms since eight and he came out into the gateway for a cigarette at about half past ten.

The man in his forties came down that lane past him with a window on his shoulder, at about a foot off the wall, in a place where there is a foot of grass and then a wall, so a man in a doorway has to stand back or get hit.

The man in his thirties said, "I could take the bottom of that if you're stuck."

"I'm all right," the man in his forties said, and went on down.

That was all that either of them said that day. The man in his thirties finished his cigarette and went back into those front rooms and has not thought about that window since, and does not know what it came out of or where it went or that it is a window at all, because he had a skip outside a front door at eight in the morning and was carrying a door frame out of it in his head.

The man in his forties went to the bottom of that lane and put the window down on the grass.

---

Here is why he went to the bottom, and it is not about the window.

A block delivery of about three thousand bricks and a pallet of sand is coming to that lane on the Thursday from a merchant in another town, and it is going to be tipped on that grass head at the bottom because there is nowhere else, and it is going to be tipped there by a lorry the size of a bus, and a lorry of that size goes on the head at the bottom of a lane or it does not go at all, and nobody else knows whether it goes.

He has been at the top of that lane four times a week since the February and has never once been to the bottom of it. Every time he has gone he has had something to do at the top, and in this borough a man who stops at the top of a lane and has a job to do at the top of a lane stops at the top.

He wanted to know about the ground.

So he went down with a window on his shoulder because that is what he had in his hands when he came out of that house, and he got to the head at about twenty to twelve and put the window down on the grass against the brick wall, on its edge, face in, with a lump of the broken putty side.

Then he walked out onto that head and looked at it.

Made ground. It is obvious it is made ground, because you can see where one load ends and the next one starts, and there is about four inch of hardcore coming through in two places and a strip of old concrete about nine foot long in the middle of it that somebody put in there about ten years ago with the best of intentions.

It has got grass on it about four inch long, which is a year's growth and is enough to be worth something.

He put his boot in it in three places and it went in about three inch each time and it did not move sideways at all, and it rang under the sole. He walked out to about the middle of it and did it again where it is thin over a lump of hard stuff and it was better there.

Then he took a piece of chalk out of his pocket — he is a man who has a piece of chalk — and put a mark about a foot long on the brick wall of that field gate and the gate post beside it, and put the chalk back, and photographed nothing because he has not got a camera and would not think of it.

He stood at the bottom of that lane for about ten minutes, on his own, on that Tuesday morning, with a window lying on the grass behind him, and then he walked back up the lane, about a hundred and twenty yard, in about four minutes, and went into the back of that house and did what he had come for, and finished at about half past two and got his own van at about a quarter to three and went home.

The lorry went on that head on the Thursday at about half past seven in the morning.

The man in his forties was in that house at eight and came down to the bottom of that lane at about ten past ten to look at it, and it took the load, and the load is about four foot deep now on that head and it has been built up twice since and there is no sign of any of it.

And the window was still lying against that wall, on its edge, face in, with a lump of broken putty on the bottom member, where he had put it at about twenty to twelve on that Tuesday.

He came down the lane and saw it, and stopped short of it, and stood there a moment or two, and did not put it in his van, and he did not go back up the lane and get his own ladder and put it back in the house either, and he did not think of it as a thing that was going anywhere.

The reason is that he is not going to put it back, and he is not going to use it, and it is not his, and it came out of a house he is gutting for a man who has a skip outside it.

A window in a skip, in about October, or a window on a grass head at the bottom of a lane for as long as the grass takes to grow over it. He has decided which and he did not decide it on the Tuesday and he does not intend to decide it again.

The man in his thirties was in that house again on the Wednesday of that week for half a day and was not in that lane at the bottom and has not been in that lane since the June and does not know there is anything on that grass head at all.

The man behind that counter in that shop has not said a word to anybody about any of it and was not there for any of it.

Nobody has been up that lane to look at that window. It is on its edge against a brick wall in a place where the grass is about four inch long, and the field gate beside it is tied back and always has been, and the wall is six foot high and a man standing in the road at the top of that lane cannot see anything at all.

**HE PUT A PIECE OF CHALK ON THE GATE POST AND WALKED BACK UP THE LANE AND LEFT A WINDOW ON THE GRASS AT THE BOTTOM OF IT, AND NOBODY HAS BEEN TO THE BOTTOM OF THAT LANE AND A WINDOW IS STILL THERE.**
