# Chapter 0687 — Two Doors Down, In An Open Porch

Tuesday the seventh of December 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house at number nine in the morning and out at lates, and the whole of the day is a step ladder in a porch she does not own that she has taken in because it is going to be rained on.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES TAKES A STEP LADDER OUT OF THE OPEN PORCH OF A HOUSE TWO DOORS FROM HER OWN AND IS ASKED BY A MAN FROM THAT HOUSE WHO IT BELONGS TO AND SAYS SHE DOES NOT KNOW, USES IT ON A BULB IN A FRONT ROOM SHE HAS NOT HEATED SINCE EASTER, PUTS IT BACK IN A PORCH WITH NO LADDER IN IT AND DOES NOT, WATCHES HER SISTER CARRY IT ROUND TO NUMBER TEN, AND THE SPACE UNDER THE STOOL IS STILL EMPTY.**

She is a woman in her late fifties. She is in the house in the morning and out at lates. A woman in a dressing gown answers the door once for her. A man from the emptied house asks her at the door about the ladder. Her sister is in the kitchen for most of the evening.

---

There is a house being emptied two doors down and it has been emptied in bits since August and the door is off its hinges and standing against the inside wall and there is a man with a horse van coming on Friday who is not going to wait, and from the lane on the Tuesday morning she can see a step ladder standing against the porch wall of that house with the top of it about a foot above the porch roof line.

It has been there since Friday.

That is the whole of her knowledge of it and it is better knowledge than most. She has seen the horse van come and go. She has seen the man of the house in his sixties and a woman with papers who does not stand in the porches. She has not seen either of them put a ladder in that porch, and she has been past that porch at both ends of every day since the summer, because she works lates and she is in and out at both ends of it.

The rain is coming in from the west and the porch of that house faces west and there is nothing over that ladder but a bit of lead somebody has bent over the edge of the roof.

---

She is not in her own house when the man comes, because she is out, and the man knocks at number nine and the woman who answers the door is a woman in a dressing gown with a cup in her hand and it is the wrong woman and she says so.

"He'll not want me."

"He said number nine."

"Number nine's got a step ladder in it," the woman in the dressing gown says. "Not in the garden. In the front room. He's welcome to come and see it."

That is a thing she has said out loud to a stranger before she has decided to say it and she stands in her own passage afterwards for a bit with her hand on the door.

---

He comes at about the middle of the morning and he is not in a hurry and he is the man of that house and he is not in a hurry and he has a set of keys and he is turning them over the way a man turns keys he has not decided are his.

"Morning. Was it you took the ladder out of our porch?"

"It came in."

"It's a good one, that. Better than ours." He looks at the front of number nine, at the low wall, at the bins. "It's just we're doing Friday and I don't want to be standing in that porch with a horse van man asking me where a ladder's come from."

"Right."

"You know whose it is?"

"I don't," she says.

And that is true, and it is the first true sentence she has said to anybody about it, and it is also the end of it, and he waits a moment longer than a man waits who has been told the truth and knows it.

"You could ask round. Number eleven's got a man in at the weekend, he's the sort that borrows."

"Aye."

"You could ask him."

"I could," she says, and does not, and he goes, and she shuts the door and it shuts true because she fixed it herself in March with folded card packed at the hinge and a hammer and no help at all, and she did not shut it out of habit because there was nothing outside. There was a man outside.

---

Here is what she has done, and she can put it in a sentence, and the sentence is this: she has taken a thing out of a porch that is not hers, and the man has just told her that the man next door at number eleven is the sort that borrows, and she has not gone and asked him.

She could go and ask him. He is the sort that borrows. It is fifty yards of pavement and a door and a sentence she could say standing on a step.

She does not do it, and the reason she does not do it is not that she is shy, and it is not that she is afraid of a man, and it is that a woman who borrows a thing and gives it back is a woman who has borrowed a thing, and a woman who borrows a thing has a person in her life who had one to borrow, and that is a fact about somebody else in her own street that she would be bringing into her own kitchen for no reason at all.

So the ladder is in her front room and there is no man at number eleven who knows it and no man in a van who has a number for anybody.

---

The bulb has been out in that room since Easter.

That is the whole of the job. There is a ceiling rose in the front room and one bulb in it and it went in the spring and she has had the room unheated since Easter, so a bulb in a cold room is a bulb in a room that is only ever looked at from the doorway, and there has never been a reason to have it on, and there is one now because she cannot put a ladder back in an empty porch and she needs somewhere to stand to look at the tape round the second rail on the second tread.

She goes and gets a bulb out of the drawer in the kitchen with the fuses in it, and she gets the step ladder out of the passage, and she opens it out for the first time since it came into her house.

That is a thing she had not thought about and it turns out to be the whole difficulty. It is a folding one and it has been folded since the summer and it is stiff at the middle hinge, and she has to get her fingers in the fold to work it, and it opens about a third of the way and stops, and she has to put it down on its side and get at the hinge with the tip of a knife she does not have, so she puts it down and opens it with her thumbs on the floor with her foot against the bottom tread and it comes open all at once and she nearly has it over.

---

The room is the cold way an unheated room is cold, which is a different cold from the kitchen and is the kind of cold you can stand in.

The stool is against the wall opposite the window, three rails oak and one ash, and there is a chair on one side of it and a settee on the other, and the space under the stool is empty and has been since the fifth of November, and there is nothing under it at all now, not even a mark on the boards, because a thing that sat on a floor and then was not there does not leave a mark on a varnished floor and the only thing left of it is a woman who remembers.

She stands the ladder under the rose and gets up on the second tread with the strip of dark tape on the rail above her shoulder, and puts the new bulb in and it comes up.

It is a sixty-watt and it is the wrong one and it makes the room about as bright as a biscuit tin and it puts a circle of light on the boards under it that the stool stands in.

---

And there it is, and it is the only thing that has gone in her favour, and it goes in her favour the wrong way round.

The room is lit.

Not warm. Lit. There is a bulb in the ceiling of a room she has not sat in since Easter and there is a circle of it on the floorboards and a stool in the middle of the circle and a chair on one side of the stool and a settee on the other, and there is a woman in her work clothes standing in her own front room on a step ladder looking at all of it.

She stands there long enough for the bulb to settle.

Then she comes down and puts the ladder against the wall opposite the window, on the other side of the stool from the settee, and the tape round the second rail is against the plaster at about the height of her hip, and she looks at it.

It is a strip of dark tape about two inches long, gone round the rail, gone hard at the edges, and it has been cut and stuck over something, and there is a chalk mark on the third tread that is not hers, and the maker's name on the rail has gone down to about half of itself.

That is the entire of it. There is no number on it. There is no address on it. There is no name on it and no paper stuck to it and no strip of gummed anything with a street on it and a number at the end of it that is not the right one.

There is a piece of aluminium with a bit of tape on it and a ladder can go up the whole road past number eleven and there is not one person in this borough who could tell her who owns it.

She looks at it for a while and then she goes and does the washing up.

---

Her sister comes at about the middle of the evening with a form in a bag and does not knock, because she never knocks, and comes round to the back and lets herself in off the yard through a door that shuts true and makes a sound that goes all the way down the passage at number nine, which is the sound the same door makes three doors down and which neither of them has ever once mentioned and which neither of them is going to.

"That shed of yours."

"I know."

"It's doing your roof in."

"I know it's doing my roof in."

"There's a man at number eleven with a workshop. He had a bit of felt on his bench all summer, I saw it through the window." Her sister sits down and takes her boots off and puts her feet under her. "You could get a bit of felt off him."

"He'd want paying."

"He'd want paying. I didn't say he wouldn't."

She does not say anything to that.

"There's a man made that stool."

"I know there's a man made that stool."

"He's got a workshop. He's in this borough somewhere with a unit and a bench and a van."

"I've never been in it and I've never spoken to him," she says. "I couldn't tell you his trade or what his van looks like and if I went up a shop and asked for him they'd think I was a madwoman, because the only thing I've got to say is a woman gave me a stool in the summer and I never asked her where it came from. That's not a thing you turn up at a shop with."

"You'd turn up. You'd say that to the man in the trade and he'd know."

"That man would not know. Nobody in this borough knows that stool came out of that man's hands except the woman at the end of the road and me, and the woman at the end of the road doesn't answer."

---

"Put that ladder back," her sister says, and nods at the front room.

"Put it back where?"

"Where you got it."

"It's not got a where. There's no house it's from. The man at number eleven said it's the sort that borrows and he might have lent it and I haven't asked him and I'm not going to now." She stands up and takes the two cups to the sink. "I can't put back what I can't give. That's the whole of it. If I put it in that porch it'll be gone by Friday and if it isn't gone it'll be in a skip and either way it's the same as being on a heap."

Her sister looks at her for a bit.

"You've thought about that a lot for a ladder."

"I haven't thought about it at all. That's the trouble. I took it because it was raining."

"You didn't take it because it was raining. You took it because you're you."

---

They drink tea standing up in the kitchen because the kitchen is the warm room and the front room has a bulb in it tonight and neither of them has any business in there.

The pad on the fridge has six things on it in the order she would do them, with the first one crossed off twice, in her own hand, and she has never once made herself cross it off properly and the crossing off twice was the whole of the ceremony, and the second thing on that pad is the front room and it has been second since March.

She writes nothing down tonight.

---

Her sister takes it when she goes.

She stands in the doorway of number nine with it folded under her arm and does not say anything about taking it, and the woman at number nine watches her go down the path and does not say put it back, because putting it back was the whole of what she could not do and saying it a second time would be asking for the thing she has just said she cannot ask for.

Her sister says it in the lane without turning round. "I'll have it round to mine tomorrow."

And that is a date, and it is the only one anybody in this borough has had about a step ladder, and it is a date about taking a thing away and not a date about giving one back.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES HAS ANSWERED A MAN WHO ASKED HER DIRECTLY THAT SHE DOES NOT KNOW WHOSE A STEP LADDER IS, HAS NOT ASKED THE MAN AT NUMBER ELEVEN WHO THE MAN NEXT DOOR SAID BORROWS, HAS PUT A BULB IN A FRONT ROOM SHE HAS NOT HEATED SINCE EASTER AND STOOD IN IT AND NOT SAT DOWN IN IT, HAS LOOKED AT A STRIP OF TAPE ON THE SECOND RAIL AND FOUND NO NAME AND NO NUMBER AND NO ADDRESS ON IT AT ALL, AND HAS TOLD HER SISTER SHE CANNOT PUT BACK WHAT SHE CANNOT GIVE, AND THE ONLY DATE ANYBODY HAS HAD ABOUT A STEP LADDER IN THIS BOROUGH IS A DATE FOR TAKING ONE AWAY.**
