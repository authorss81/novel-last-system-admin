# Chapter 0994 — One Word Struck Out

Undated in this draft.

**THE FRONT ROOM AT TWELVE HAS THE FIRE OUT AND A SHEET OF PAPER ON THE TABLE.**

There is a deal table in front of the window with the top scrubbed pale and one corner of it standing half an inch proud of its own legs. On the table there is the printed form that came to this house a week ago, with her name at the bottom of it, and beside that there is a half sheet of stiff paper with a fold across it and his hand on the fold. The front room at number twelve has a fireplace in it with a fire that has not been lit since the spring, and the grate in it is a cast one with a hinged front that has been left open since before the range went cold, and the room is full to the depth of an inch with ash that has come out of the grate and gone to powder and gone back to being ash and is now the colour and weight of flour.

The sister is in the room with a case open on the floor by the door and things half into it and half out of it, and the lid has come out of true with the bottom at the back hinge by most of a quarter of an inch, which is what a case does when a young man has sat on the edge of it. She has been at that case since before he came and she will be at it after he goes, and it has not moved an inch while he was in the kitchen.

"I found this in your bag at the store." She does not get out of that chair for him. "Your sister brought it down when she came for me on Sunday and I put it to her whose hand it was and she looked at it and told me it belonged to the man who did the range wall."

"Your sister came for you?"

"To tell me about the river. And she put her case down in the hall, and I have not let her go at it." She set her palm flat on the scrubbed deal. "Sit down. You have been standing about in my front room for two days since you were last in it."

---

"I'd rather you had the table," he told her, "because it's a table and not a chair, and I've not come to stay." He did not sit down.

"You have come to stay, because there is a chair and you have not taken it and you have taken the table instead, and you have done it twice that way." She turned the sheet round. "Those are my words, given to you on Thursday and set down in your hand. I have had that sheet in my apron since Sunday reading it, and I know every word of it. There is one word in it that is not mine."

"It is. It's not the one I expected."

"No." She turned the half sheet back the other way and put her finger on the top line, which is in his hand and not hers. "That line at the top is what you set down over my sentence, and it gives out that the resident at number twelve has no objection to the arrangements. Then there is my sentence underneath. Then your hand again at the bottom with the date beside it."

"I set that heading down so that whoever came for the sheet would see it had been answered properly."

"I know why you set it down. I have had that paper in my apron since Sunday." Her finger came off the paper altogether. "Nobody has put a question to me for fifty years and I learned very early how to let people have things about me that are easier than asking anybody. And you set a line over the top of my one sentence giving out that I have no objection, and it is a kind thing, and I have not got one."

The sister came in from the hall with her hands full and put a folded coat down on the end of the deal table and stood there, going on folding things into that case while she talked.

"She is not going."

"You have put it to her."

"I put it to her Wednesday and she has not given me an answer, and not giving me an answer is the answer, and I have been going at that case since Wednesday for a woman who is not going." She looked at the old woman. "You can tell him."

"I am not going to be left." She puts it to the room rather than to him, and her hands stay in her lap throughout. "That is what I would like on the sheet, and I have not told my daughter, because she will take it as me making a fuss. I am going because there has been nobody on that path but me since the summer before last and I am seventy-eight and I would like to see some water that is not in a tin. I have been ashamed for about a fortnight of how small that is, compared with what everybody is talking about."

"Now put the second line on," she told him. "Tell her not to come. Not because I am ill and not because I have fallen out with her. Because I am going up the river, and she would come, and she would be here the whole six weeks and I would spend the whole six weeks being looked at." The old woman turned the half sheet round to the bottom of the deal table so that it was the right way up for her and pushed it towards him across the scrubbed deal.

"Mrs—"

"You will not argue with me about the second line." Her palm came down flat on the top of the table. "You have told me three times since Monday that you are not the man who decides things for other people. Well, I am the woman being decided, and I am telling you what I would like, and you will set it down in ink and put your mark to it, and then it will be a thing I chose and not a thing that happened."

He got the pencil out of the bag, or off behind his ear, whichever it turned out to be, and set the second line down in ink on the half sheet under her own sentence, and put his initials and the day under that, and then squared the pencil up against the edge of the deal table because it had been lying at a slight angle to it and he could not leave it at an angle. He did not argue with her about the second line.

That was the cost of it, and he knew it while his hand was doing it: nobody at number twelve had ever been asked what she would like. He had asked, and it had cost her the only comfortable thing left, which was being managed.

"I want a minute," she told him, and the sister went out into the hall and shut the door, and he stood at the table with his hands behind him and did not look at what she was doing to it, which in a room the size of that one is the only way there is to let a person do it. She read the second line twice, then read her own sentence above it, then read the top line again, and then she asked him for the pencil back.

When she gave the pencil back the sheet had a line through one word near the top of it in a stroke that went well below the line and did not stop at it.

"Not *objection*," the old woman told him. "I do have an objection to the arrangements, because they are arrangements and not questions put to anybody. I would like it to read that I have not been asked."

She had put that underneath the top line in her own hand, which shook a little on the downstrokes and is the same hand as the one on the rent book. "And then my sentence, and then your second line. And nobody is to take that paper to anybody's office. It is mine."

"It is yours."

"It was never anything else." She folded it on its own crease and pushed it back across the table to him, and it went into his bag against the folding rule, and he did not fold it over again or lay anything on top of it, and the case in the hall was still half full and the ash on the hearth of the front room at number twelve is about an inch deep and quite undisturbed.
