# Chapter 0643 — The Step At Number Nine

Thursday the twenty-seventh of May 2021, in this borough. She is at her own window from about ten past six until about half past seven and she is at number nine at about nine o'clock and she is back in her own kitchen by a quarter past nine, and the whole of it is one stool and one woman she has never spoken to.

**SHE DECIDES ON THE SUNDAY TO STOP SWAPPING A CHAIR AND A STOOL TWICE A DAY, AND THE WAY SHE STOPS IS TO PUT THE STOOL ON THE STEP AT NUMBER NINE, WHICH IS THREE DOORS DOWN, WHERE A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES ALSO EATS STANDING AND HAS NEVER BEEN SPOKEN TO BY EITHER OF THEM, AND SHE TELLS THAT WOMAN SOMETHING TRUE, WHICH IS THE FIRST THING SHE HAS SAID IN A LONG TIME THAT IS SIMPLY AND ONLY TRUE, AND THE TWO CHAIRS IN HER KITCHEN GO DOWN TO ONE, AND THE MAN WHO CAME FOR THE FRIDGE IS NOW A STORY ABOUT A THING THAT IS NOT THERE.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. There is a woman at number nine in her late fifties who has been at a kitchen window at about half past nine at night since before Christmas and has not worked out why.

---

Here is what she decided on the Sunday, and she did it at the window, and it came to her in about four seconds and it was not complicated.

She has been swapping them since the fifth of May. Morning and evening. For nobody, and then for one of them, and then for both of them on the same Wednesday, and on the Wednesday she told two lies in one afternoon to keep them apart, and since the Wednesday she has worked out, standing at that window, in about a second, that they have not met and are not going to, and she was not far off.

And then on the Tuesday the woman in her late forties came, or she did not, and the table is still against the wall, and the marks on the floorboards are still there, and the tin is still by the oven where it went across the room.

And what she worked out on the Sunday, at about ten past six in the morning, is that the swapping has stopped being a way of keeping two people apart and has become the only part of her day that is about the two of them. That is all it is now. She gets up at seven and moves a chair. She gets in at seven and moves a stool. It is the only appointment she has and it is with a piece of furniture, and she is seventy-eight.

And she is not going to do that any more.

And then the hard part, which took about ten minutes at that window on the Sunday, and it is this.

She could choose. That is available to her and she has had it since the Wednesday and she has not done it, and she is not going to do it, because choosing means saying *I know about the other one* to one of them, and there is no version of that in which the other one does not find out before the summer is out.

And the only way to take a thing out of a story is to give it to somebody else.

---

She has been at that window at about half past six every morning since before Christmas and at about seven every evening, and that is not a habit anybody put there. That is a woman of seventy-eight in a house with nobody in it, standing where the light is.

And on the Monday she looked down the road at number nine and the kitchen light was on, at about half past seven in the evening, and there was a shape at the window.

Not a shape of somebody sitting. A shape of somebody standing, with a plate.

And she stood at her own window and watched it for about ten minutes without moving, and it did not go in and out once, and she could see an arm go up and down.

And on the Tuesday it was the same. And on the Wednesday.

And she has not mentioned it to anybody and she is not going to, and the reason she is not going to is that she is seventy-eight and it is not a thing she can say to a stranger without being a woman of seventy-eight telling that stranger what she has been doing at her own window since the house came, and there is no version of that either.

She takes the stool down at about nine on the Thursday morning.

She goes up the path at number nine and she does not knock and she puts the stool down on the top step, on its feet, facing the road, the way you put a chair out.

And she stands there for a second looking at it, because it is a beautiful thing and it is not hers and she has watched a man put it together at a bench in a unit off an industrial estate, and she has sat on it, and it is going to be in somebody else's kitchen by lunchtime.

The light upstairs goes off at about ten past nine and the woman at number nine comes down.

She is a woman in her late fifties. She has a kitchen with a table in it and two chairs at that table, one of which has a leg that moves if you look at it and one of which has a back that has been out at the same joint since she inherited the house. She eats at the table with the cloth on it and she eats standing when she is on a late, and she is on a late four nights out of seven, which is most nights, which is the whole reason the shape at that window was a standing shape.

She opens her door and there is a stool on her step.

And she says, "That's not mine."

And the woman at number twelve says, "No."

And the woman at number nine says, "Is it for me."

And the woman at number twelve says, "It's for you if you want it. That's the whole of it. There's nobody else after it."

---

And here is the moment, and the woman in her late fifties is not going to be able to explain it to anybody either, and it is a moment in a front doorway on a Thursday morning on a road off the parade.

And the woman at number nine says, "Where did you get it."

There is the man who came for the fridge, and the man came for the fridge in March and could be asked to have left a stool as well as a chair, and that is the answer that keeps everything else exactly where it is.

There is the council, and the council is spent, and she spent it on Wednesday in her own front room and a man has not mentioned it since and a man not mentioning it is a man who has put it away for a reason.

There is the garden man, and the garden man was used on Friday to a woman with a spanner and it is a real-enough story but it is now a story that has been told, and a story that has been told cannot be told again without a second thing attached to it, and she worked that out on the Sunday.

And there is the fourth one, and she has never used it, and it is nine words long and it is the first thing she has said in this borough since before Christmas that has no story in it at all.

And she says, "I had two and I couldn't stand the room with them in it."

The woman at number nine looks at her.

And she does not say anything for about four seconds, which is a long time in a doorway, and what she is doing in the four seconds is what the woman at number seventy-eight has been doing at her own window since the start of May, which is putting a thing where it goes.

And then the woman at number nine says, "You've had two."

And the woman at number seventy-eight says, "I've had two since April."

And the woman at number nine says, "Right."

And the woman at number seventy-eight says, "And I've been putting one away and bringing the other out twice a day and there's nobody coming, and I have got a green chair in my kitchen that a woman in her forties gave me and a stool a man made me, and they have never been in this kitchen at the same time, and I am not going to be the one that puts them in the same kitchen."

And the woman at number nine says, "Why not."

And the woman at number seventy-eight says, "Because then one of them has to be told about the other and neither of them has asked me a single question and I am not going to make them ask."

And the woman at number seventy-eight says, "One of them will."

And the woman at number nine says, "What are you going to tell her."

And the woman at number nine says, "Ah."

And the woman at number seventy-eight says, "And if I tell the truth then the man that made it finds out that I have been telling a woman in his van's worth of lies about where a green chair came from, and he has not asked me for one word of it since the start of May and I am not going to make him."

---

And the woman at number nine bends down and puts a hand on the seat of the stool, the way you do, and does not lift it.

And she says, "It's a good bit of kit."

And the woman at number seventy-eight says, "It's ash."

And the woman at number nine says, "Is it."

And the woman at number seventy-eight says, "He used oak for the rails. Three out of four are oak and one is ash, and it's the pale one, and you can see it from where you're standing."

And the woman at number nine says, "Right."

And the woman at number seventy-eight says, "He made it himself. On a bench in a unit off the industrial estate, on a Monday and a Tuesday, and he told me he made it, and that was the truth, and it was the wrong thing to say to me, and I have thought about that at about four in the morning more than once."

And the woman at number nine says, "Well."

And she picks the stool up off the step, with one hand, easily, and carries it inside, and puts it down by the table, and does not say anything else, and closes the door.

And the woman at number seventy-eight stands on number nine's path for about a minute.

Here is what has happened, and she works it out going back up her own road with nothing in her hands.

There is one chair in her kitchen.

There is one stool in a kitchen three doors down, and a woman in her late fifties has put it by a table with two bad chairs in it, and that woman has eaten standing at a window on four nights out of seven and has not worked out why, and has now got somewhere to sit.

And the man who came for the fridge is a story about a thing that is not in her house any more.

That is the last of it and it is the good part. She has been making a story to put in front of a chair since April, and the chair is the only thing the story was ever about, and the chair is a chair, and the story now belongs to nothing. If the woman in her late forties asks her on Friday where the stool came from she can say *it went down the road, I have given it away*, and that is not a lie and it does not require a man in a van or a fridge or a garden round the corner.

And she has got a sentence ready for the first time in a long time and it took a stool to get it.

And she can go and put the green chair by the table and leave it there.

And here is the other thing, and she has it before she gets to her own gate, and it is the thing that makes her stand on her own step for a minute before she goes in.

She has just told a stranger the truth.

Not the whole of it. But she has told a woman she has never spoken to before that she had two, and that she was putting one away twice a day for nobody, and that a woman in her forties gave her a chair and a man made her a stool, and that neither of them has asked her a question.

And that is in a kitchen three doors down now. In a kitchen in her late fifties, on a table with a cloth on it.

And if the woman at number nine ever talks to the woman at number ten — and the woman at number nine is a woman in her late fifties who has a bin and a hedge in common with the woman at number ten — then the two stories touch, and the man who came for the fridge is in the same road as a stool that came from a woman who has told the truth about two chairs.

She has made a join that will not close. She has taken a thing that was only inside her own four walls and put it three doors down, and there is no way to take it back, and she knows exactly what she has done, and she is aware, standing on her own step, that this is the first decision she has made in this business that she could not have made at half past six at her own window with a cloth in her hand, and that it is the first one that was made for somebody else.

And she goes in.

---

She puts the green chair by the table at about a quarter past nine and she does not move it again.

That is the whole of the rest of it and it is worth setting down, because the rest of it is four hours of an ordinary Thursday in a house where nothing is happening.

She washes the cup. She puts the table back under the window, which she has not done since before Christmas, and she wipes the marks off the floorboards with a cloth, and she puts the tin back on the top shelf with a chair from the passage, and she is never going to do that again and she knows that while she is still standing on the table with the tin in her hand.

She has a piece of toast at about half past twelve at the table, sitting down.

**SHE PUT A STOOL THAT A MAN BUILT AT A BENCH ON THE STEP AT NUMBER NINE BECAUSE SHE HAD BEEN WATCHING A SHAPE AT THAT KITCHEN WINDOW FROM HER OWN WINDOW SINCE BEFORE CHRISTMAS, AND SHE TOLD A WOMAN SHE HAD NEVER SPOKEN TO THAT SHE HAD HAD TWO CHAIRS AND COULD NOT STAND THE ROOM WITH THEM IN IT, WHICH IS THE FIRST THING SHE HAS SAID IN THIS BOROUGH IN A LONG TIME THAT IS SIMPLY AND ONLY TRUE, AND THE MAN WHO CAME FOR THE FRIDGE IS NOW A STORY ABOUT A THING THAT IS NOT IN HER HOUSE, AND THAT JOIN IS THREE DOORS DOWN AND SHE CANNOT CLOSE IT AGAIN.**

(End of chapter 0643)
