# Chapter 0296 — The Closer

Friday the third of February 2017, from about half past six in the morning, a ground-floor passage in a building by the water in Tideglass.

**THE DOOR AT THE BOTTOM OF THAT STAIR CAME BACK ON ITS OWN FRAME AT TWENTY PAST TWO IN THE MORNING AND CAUGHT THE WOMAN WHO LIVES AT THE END OF THAT CORRIDOR ACROSS THE FOREARM, AND BY ABOUT TEN TO SEVEN A MAN HAD THE ARM OFF THAT DOOR AND A PIECE OF TIMBER WEDGED ACROSS THE CORNER UNDER IT, AND NOBODY HAS ASKED HIM WHY HE DID IT.**

The passage runs from the street door at one end to the foot of the outside stair at the other, and it is about four feet wide and about twenty long, and the gas meter is on the left-hand wall about halfway along it, in a wooden case with a hinged flap, and the light in it is a strip fitting in the ceiling that came on in the summer and is on a switch by the meter.

The street door at that end is a hardwood door with a fanlight over it and a closer on the top of it, and the closer has been off its bracket since before anybody in that building could remember and the arm hangs down against the face of the door doing nothing, and nobody has had it looked at.

When the wind comes off the estuary and gets under that door it comes in about a foot, and it swings, and it comes back, and the corner of the leaf goes into the plaster of the opposite wall at about the height of a man's shoulder.

There are three places on that plaster where it has been, and about the biggest of them the paint has come off in flakes and the flakes have been painted over at some point, because the flaking is under a coat that is a different colour from the paint round it.

---

The woman of about fifty-five had been awake since about one and had gone down that passage at about twenty past two for a glass of water, in a dressing gown and slippers, with the light off the switch by the meter because the strip fitting over her head makes the whole passage look like a corridor in a place she does not work.

She had the glass in her hand and she was turning round to go back up the stair when the door came in off the wind and went back.

It caught her across the forearm above the wrist on the side she turns, and the pain of it took her breath for about a second, and she said something out loud into an empty passage that the man who works a night shift four streets away did not hear.

She stood and held her arm, and then she opened the tap over the meter and put her forearm under the cold water of it for a minute, and then she took a tea towel off the rail in that passage and put it round the place and went back up.

The skin across the forearm was through in two places by about ten past three and the tea towel had gone brown in the middle of it. She got a plaster out of a box on the shelf above the sink in the kitchen at the back of that room and put it on and went back to bed.

She was up at about half past six and the plaster had gone yellow and she left it on, and she lay in that room with the light off for another half hour, and she came down that passage at about ten past seven to get the water on for a cup of tea.

---

The man in his sixties had come in off the lane that goes along the bottom of that building at about twenty-five to six in the morning, and he stood in that passage for a while in the dark with the street light coming in under a door that was not doing anything.

He could see the marks on the wall without switching the light on. He put his hand flat on the biggest one and moved it.

He went out to his car and got a screwdriver and a length of four by two about two foot long and two offcuts of the same in a bit off the back of a shelf, and he came back in and did three things in about twenty minutes.

The first was the arm off the door. The screws that hold the arm to the leaf had been painted over at some point and the first one he put the blade into slipped and went across the ball of his thumb, and he stood there for a second and then went at it with the end of the screwdriver and got it, and the other two came out, and the arm came off the door and went on the shelf over the meter with the rest of the metal on that shelf that came out of that building over the years and went nowhere.

The second was a plate on the jamb. He found a length of flat bar in the passage cupboard with the fire blanket in it, which is a thing nobody in that building has ever wanted a fire blanket, and he drilled two small holes in the jamb at the height of the arm, one of them an old hole, and fixed the flat bar on with two plugs, so that the arm can be pushed back on there and made to work by anybody who ever comes to look at it.

The third was the timber. He put the length of four by two on edge on the floor of that corner and wedged it under the head of the door with the two offcuts, and then shut the door on it as hard as he could with his foot and it stopped about a hand's width short of the wall and stayed there, and he put his hand on it and pushed and it did not go past.

The strip fitting over his head came on when he put his hand on the switch by the meter.

He looked at the chair against the fire door on the first floor as he went up, and the open three inches of it, and the bulb at the head of that stair, which has flickered since the summer, and he came back down and shut the street door behind him and the door came back onto the timber and stopped.

The woman of about fifty-five came down that passage at about ten past seven and found him on the step outside with his bag at his feet.

"You've had the door off it," she said.

"I've had the arm off it," said the man in his sixties. "You can push it back on if you ever want it. It'll work. I fixed a plate on the jamb for it so there's somebody to know what to do. And I've put a piece of timber in the corner so it can't get back to the wall whatever the wind does."

She stood in the passage and looked at the timber and then at the marks on the wall opposite, and she put her good hand out and touched the plaster where the paint was flaking.

"That's been hitting that for years," she said.

"It has," said the man in his sixties. "You could put your hand there any night this month."

"I could," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"You'd not have got to it, though, once it's swinging. It comes back faster than a person can put a hand out. It's the arm off it that's done it. The timber's the belt to the braces."

He said that and picked his bag up off the step and did not stay on the step, and he did not say anything else about any of it, and he went out to his car.

She said, "Well," and went in and put the tap on.

The light on the first floor had gone off again by the time she got up there. The door at the end of that corridor, on the inside of it, had a hasp on it, put there at the end of October, and she put her shoulder to it and lifted the handle and got her elbow past the frame the way she has got past that frame about a hundred times since, and that fitting has never once come up between her and anybody in that building and she is not the one who would raise it.

**THE TIMBER IS STILL ON EDGE IN THAT CORNER UNDER THE HEAD OF THAT DOOR AND THE FLAT BAR IS ON THE JAMB WITH TWO PLUGS IN IT, AND THE THREE MARKS ARE STILL ON THE PLASTER OPPOSITE WITH A FOURTH BESIDE THEM THAT HAS A BRANCH IN IT, AND A GLASS OF WATER ON A PASSAGE FLOOR AT TWENTY PAST TWO IN THE MORNING IS A THING THAT HAPPENS IN A BUILDING THAT IS A HUNDRED AND SIXTY YEARS OLD AND HAS NO ONE LOOKING AFTER ANY PART OF IT, AND SHE IS STILL IN THAT ROOM ON A MONTH AND THE FIRST OF NEXT MONTH IS THE SAME AS THE FIRST OF THIS ONE.**
