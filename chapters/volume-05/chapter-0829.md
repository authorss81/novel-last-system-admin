# Chapter 0829 — Grass Side Down

Tuesday the eighteenth of July 2023, along the front of that house at number nine from the time the sun comes off the kerb until it has gone off the flags, and a strip of turf that is going to be dug up is the whole of that Tuesday.

**THERE IS TWELVE FEET OF TURF ALONG THE FRONT OF THE HOUSE AT NUMBER NINE AND SHE MEANS TO LIFT IT HERSELF AND PUT IT IN A STACK ALONG THE FRONT WALL, AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING IS LET IN AT THE SIDE GATE AND STANDS AT THE FRONT STEP AND GOES OUT THE SAME WAY.**

That turf is a strip about twelve feet long and two feet wide along the front wall, grass over soil, with daisies in it and moss at the shady end, laid years ago and never fed.

Twelve feet by two is twenty-four squares of about a foot across, and there is a corner of the bed by the step that goes to nothing and will come up as half a square and a bit, so the whole of it is twenty-five pieces and the last one is the awkward one. She knows that because she did the count in the spring when she cut the edging out of that same front, and she knows it now without doing it again.

She starts at the kerb end with a spade with a clean edge and a fork, cutting down through the roots in squares about a foot across, then sliding the spade under each square about two inches down and lifting it whole.

Two inches is the whole of what a sod is. The grass and its mat of root is about a quarter of an inch and the soil under it is what makes it lift as one piece instead of as a mat on a heap of loose earth. Cut at two inches and there is soil enough to carry the piece in your hands without it breaking up. Cut at one inch and you get the grass mat and half the roots and it comes up in your hand like a wet tissue. Cut at three and you are lifting a brick of the garden and the roots are in the bottom of it and it will not go back anywhere it came from.

The edge wants to be cut down, not in, or the spade goes under the next square as well and lifts two at a time along the old cut line where the roots have not been cut through.

Turf lifts best in the cool of the morning before the sun is on it. The roots are tight then and the soil sticks to them, and a square comes up like a tile with the grass on top and the dark underneath showing where the worms have been.

The worms are all in the top inch at the front of the bed and not at the back, where the moss has the ground to itself and the ground under the moss is sour. She lifts the front eleven squares and they each come up with three or four of them in the underside and she does not take them off, because a worm in a sod is a worm that goes back into the next bed.

She lays each square grass up on the path at first, in a row, while she works along.

The daisies are in the front six feet and not at the back. The roots of a daisy go down about a hand and they are in every square of that six feet, and a square with daisies in it cannot be cut to a foot across and be lifted whole, because the crowns come away and go back into the bed as little green buttons and there is no way of putting them back where they were. She cuts those six feet in half-squares and lays the halves together as they come off, which is twice the work and twice the pieces, and it means the whole of the daisy bed comes up and not a third of it.

At the fourth square she has to look at what she is doing, because the stack along the wall is the question and not the lifting.

Everybody she ever knew stacked turf grass up. Grass up to the air, roots down to the ground, the way it grew. That is how you keep it if you want to lay it again, and how you pile it if you want it to go on growing.

She read somewhere, and cannot remember where, that grass down keeps the root side off the air and grass up puts it in it. That is right, and it is the reason nobody in her life has ever stacked a sod any other way, and it is the reason the daisy beds at number eight and along this street are bare by the second week of July every year and the turf from them is in a heap behind somebody's garage drying out.

The other half of it is the half nobody writes down. Grass to grass is grass up, because a sod laid grass up and the next laid grass up puts the two green faces together. Those two faces are shut, and shut green faces go yellow and go soft and go down, and that is what everybody means when they say a stack of turf will keep. It keeps long enough.

The roots are the other face of it. Grass to grass leaves every root face in the stack open on its own side to whatever is above and below it, and a root face is fine soil with the fine roots in the top of it, and fine soil in the air in July is powder by the evening.

Root against root is a different thing altogether. Root face to root face, the two dark sides shut together with no light and no air at all between them, and everything in that joint stays what it was when it was in the ground. Grass against soil on the outside of the stack and soil against grass inside it, and the whole thing goes on being turf for as long as it is shaded and damp, which is longer than anybody has ever had a stack of turf for.

So there it is. Grass to grass for a fortnight, and grass to soil for everything after that, and what she has got against the front wall is not going back down before spring.

Here is the middle of the day with her hands on the fourth square and the first three in a row on the flags.

She turns the first square over.

Grass down on the soil at the foot of the front wall, soil up to the sky. The second goes on top of it the same way, roots to the roots of the one below, soil face to soil face. The third the same. A stack of turf grass side down, sod on sod, against everything she was ever told, with the white roots pressed to each other in the dark between the squares.

She does not push them true and she does not ram them down, because a stack of turf that is packed is a stack with the air driven out of the joints between the roots, and the joints are the only thing in it that matters. She lays each one where the one under it is not directly over a soft place, and she lays them at a lean back against the wall about the thickness of a hand, and she leaves the joints where the joint wants to be.

The woman who does her shopping is let in at the side gate of number nine in the middle of it, stands at the front step a minute, looks at the turf stacked along the front wall, and goes out by the same gate. She does not come down the path and she does not touch a square and nothing is asked about it, and the gate stays open across the path on its two wedges the whole time and the spanner on the flags by the step is not picked up.

“Your milk is on the step,” she tells her.

“Leave it under the shade and I will take it in,” she tells her.

There is a woman at the step looking at a row of turf lying the wrong way up against a wall, and there is nothing she can usefully say to the woman at the step about it, and the woman at the step does not say anything.

---

The rest of the afternoon is lifting and turning.

Twelve feet of turf comes up square by square and goes on the stack grass down, with the soil brushed off the path as she goes and the edge of the bed swept clean where the turf was.

The bed under the turf is better than it has any business being. Four inches of dark soil with worms in it and grass roots going down through it in a mat, and after twenty years the bottom of that mat is a hard boundary between grass soil and whatever the garden had before it, and the spade goes into it and stops at that boundary every time, because a spade goes through a boundary that has not been dug and comes up on it again next year if it is not dug.

She is not digging. She is lifting turf off the top of the bed and stopping, and the soil she is brushing off the path goes back into the bucket and into the bottom of the bed, and the bed is left four inches low all along twelve feet with a hard bottom to it, which is not a thing she is going to be asked about and is not a thing she is going to be able to forget either.

The last piece is the corner by the step that goes to nothing, and it comes up as a half square and a bit with the edge of the step line cut into it, and it goes on the stack where it fits and not where it would go if it were square.

The paving at number nine is not relaid in any of it, and the slab at the gate end stays tilting to the rail, and the coal hole stays shut, and the stone across the hollow is not lifted.

When the sun has gone off the flags she stands at the step and looks along the front wall to the stack. The turf is stacked grass side down along the wall, sod on sod, against advice, and the top square with its soil to the sky is already flat and dark at the edges and doing what she expects it to do.
