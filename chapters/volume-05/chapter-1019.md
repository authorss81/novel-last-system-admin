# Chapter 1019 — The Tin On The Kitchen Table

Undated in this draft.

**THE KITCHEN AT NUMBER TEN HAS A SASH STOOD ON TWO TRESTLES IN IT.**

The kitchen at number ten is a room about nine feet by eight with a stone sink under the window and a dresser against one wall with the plates on it and a deal table in the middle with the edge worn through to the wood in two places. The floor is stone flags and they are not level from the sink to the far corner, and everything that has ever been washed down that floor in that house has ended up in the low place by the door. There is a door to the yard at the bottom of the left-hand wall and no fire in it since before the summer. Two trestles stand about four feet apart in the middle of the floor with the sash lying across the top of them on its side, out of its own frame since the start of the week, and the long pale scratch in the architrave of the front room is on the floor by the dresser where it came off when they took the sash out.

That is the day she named herself at the start of the summer. She named it in her own hall, before he had looked at anything, and she got to the figure by herself across those two days, and she had told him she would have her sister in when he did it and that she could not see one reason on this earth why he should do it in a house with nobody in it.

Her sister came down the stair behind him at the middle of the morning and did not go back up. She stood in that kitchen with her sleeves turned back for three hours while a man worked, and put four questions to him, three of them about money.

The job was what he had told her sister it would be in the hall. The soft corner comes out of the sill and a new piece goes in, and the reveal comes back to a true line and is cut to it and not to the ragged edge of what is left, and a board goes across the opening so the window can stand shut until somebody can look at it properly. The new piece is four foot of his own hardwood, and he had bought it out of his own money at the end of the summer when the woman of the house made him promise in a hall that he would not touch four foot of stock on a rack in his own store that was not his.

He worked at it with a block and a chisel and a hand saw and he got the soft corner out in four pieces, and he could not get the new piece level along its whole length. The old sill has gone soft and uneven and the front of it has dropped about half an inch over the last forty feet, and the new piece is planed to the line of the back of it and the front of it follows what the front of the old one now does, which is a curve, and a curved sill with a straight window in it lets water into the wall of that room every winter it has been doing it.

---

"Is that your own wood or the yard's."

"That is my own and I bought it and paid for it out of my own money and it is not coming off a rack in my store that belongs to a job in another town."

"How much was it."

"Thirty-eight pounds and it was four foot of oak at a merchant's and the merchant's price went up in the autumn and I have been glad of that ever since."

"And you have not charged my sister for the wood."

"I have not charged your sister for the wood and I am not going to and you already know I have not charged her for the rest of it." He did not look up. "It is not the same thing as a figure and it is not going to look like one."

"Then what is it."

"It is a man putting his own money into a window in a house that is not his because the figure your sister offered me did not buy a piece of oak and somebody had to put one in." He turned the piece round and looked at the front edge of it again. "And I am not handing that to your sister as a bill. I am handing it to you because you asked me for it and because you would have it out of the wood inside a month and I would rather you had it from me than worked it out."

The sister at ten stood at the end of the deal table with her arms folded and looked at the four foot of oak lying across two trestles in that kitchen and at her sister, and neither of them said anything for a bit.

"Then there is not one figure in this at all," the sister said at last. "Which is the thing you have been doing to us for six months and neither of us has been able to name."

"There is going to be a figure in it. I am not going to do this one for nothing and neither of you is having me back on that basis."

---

The woman of the house went to the dresser and took down a tin and put it in the middle of the deal table and took the lid off it, and there was money in it, and she put none of it out and offered him none of it.

"You take it here, in front of us both, and you put it out on this table so that I can see every piece of it go down, and then you put it in your pocket while my sister and I are both looking at you do it." She stood across that table with her hands flat on the deal. "I told you at the start of the summer that I had that offer off two men in this street in six years and I was not having it off a third, and you stood in my hall and agreed with my figure, and now I am going to stand here and not blink while you take money off me, and neither of those two things is going to feel the same to either of us when you have done it."

"You worked it out yourself."

"I worked it out over two days at a table with a cloth and a chair and nobody helping me, and it took me the whole of that, which is the reason I am making you do it in front of me and not the reason I think you are any good." She did not move her hands. "Take it."

He put it out on the deal table where she could see every piece go down, and then he put it into his own pocket with her looking at his hands the whole way, and she put the lid back on that tin and set it on the dresser where it had come from.

He had not taken money off that frontage in ten years. He had been entered against work five times in that time and one of the five was his own fault, and the whole of those ten years came down this morning to a man putting coins onto a deal table while a woman who is on a book at that yard had her eyes on his hands.

He understood what the woman of the house had done and it took him until he was out in the yard. She had made him take it because taking it in front of two women, one of whom can be asked about him at a yard two streets away, is the hardest thing on offer, and she had put a price on the difficulty and had not shown it to him.

He will not forget that morning for the sound of coins going onto a deal table in that kitchen, and the tin is on the dresser above it with the lid off and nothing inside.
