# Chapter 0764 — The Floor Of The Shed

Thursday the thirteenth of October 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house and shed at number ten from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a shed floor swept out with a bench left where it stands.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES SWEEPS THE FLOOR OUT OF HER OWN SHED WITH HER SISTER AND NEITHER OF THEM SHIFTS THE BENCH OFF THE WALL, AND THE GRIT IS LEFT IN A CRESCENT UNDER IT.**

She is a woman in her early thirties at number ten on Thursday. Her sister comes to the back door in the morning, takes a broom, and works the floor of the shed with her.

---

The back door shuts true and the washing is in and folded on the table. The drawer under the worktop is shut with the hammer and the screwdriver and the folded paper in it and none of them comes out. The line stands bare across the yard. The strip of ply stands against the front edge of the shed where the felt has gone, not nailed, and the felt's own corner is still lifting at the top of it and has been lifting since the summer.

The shed floor has not been swept out since the spring. It is a hard floor with a bench along the back wall that came with the shed and has not been moved in the years she has had it, and in the four inches between the front of that bench and the wall there is the accumulation of a season — grit off the yard in through the gap under the door, sawdust and bark from the two apple trees, the husks off the sweetcorn, and a season of leaves that has come in under the door and gone to a black felt along the skirting.

She has a broom and a shovel and a barrow, and she means to move the bench, sweep the whole floor, and put the bench back where it was against the wall.

Her sister comes to the back door at the middle of the morning with her coat folded over her arm.

---

"You have not moved it."

"I have not moved it yet."

"That bench has not been off that wall since the man brought it." Her sister looks past her at the shed. "It will not come out of that door on its own and it will not go back two inches square once it has been out. You will take it off the wall and put it back wrong and be looking at that gap every day until you die."

"Then I will sweep to it."

"Then you will sweep to it and leave a gutter behind it." Her sister comes in past her and puts her hand on the back of the bench and does not shift it. "And you will not even know what is in the gutter until you move it in the spring to get at something behind it, and by then it will be a mat."

"I can move it again in the spring."

"You can move it again in the spring." Her sister takes the broom off the wall. "Give me the front and I will do the sides and we will not touch that bench at all."

---

They do the whole floor that way. She starts at the door with the shovel and skims the grit into a heap and her sister works behind her with the broom, and where the heap gets to the wall her sister puts her hand flat on the boards and shoves it along with the side of the broom so that it goes under nothing and around nothing, and where there is a corner she turns the broom on its side and gets the bristles into it.

The leaves come up in slabs. They have been pressed into the floor by three months of doors and they come away in pieces about the size of a hand, black underneath and grey on top, and there is more of them than either of them expected.

"That is only the door end."

"That is only the door end." Her sister stands on the swept floor and looks back along it. "That is the leaves. There is the grit as well. And I can see the far end of the bench from here and there is a crescent of it along the front of that bench and a black line up the skirting behind it and neither of us is going to get at either."

"Then we get at it."

"Get at it how." Her sister puts the broom down. "You are not lifting that bench and I am not lifting that bench, and we have had that out twice now."

"I was going to try the broom on its side."

"Try the broom on its side." Her sister gets down on her knees on the swept floor. "You can get three inches behind that bench with a broom on its side. There are four inches behind that bench. Go on."

She gets down on the floor beside her sister and puts the broom on its side and pushes it in along the boards under the edge of the bench.

It works further than either of them thought. It goes back a good way with the grit coming out in a wave ahead of the bristles and the dark line of the skirting coming into the light, and it comes back out with its head full and the grit dropping off it onto the clean part of the floor, and she does it most of the length of the bench before the trestle leg.

Then it stops.

"Where does it stop."

"It stops where it has stopped for twenty years." Her sister has her cheek nearly on the floor looking in. "There is a trestle leg on that bench and it stands at the corner, and the floor under the leg is where nothing has been since the shed was built, and it is not going to be got with a broom."

"Then I will get it with my hands."

"You will not get it with your hands, because your hands will not go round a leg and under a bench and back out again with anything in them." Her sister sits back on her heels. "It will be there in the spring. And it will be there next summer. And there is a broom in this shed and there is a shovel and there is a bar, and not one of the three of them will do that corner, and that is the corner."

She sits on the swept floor and looks at the corner under the bench leg, where the floor is a clean pale grey out to about the width of her hand and then goes straight to black.

---

They get the heap out through the door and up the yard to the lane end in two goes with the barrow, and they tip it in the corner by the fence where it is not on the path, and the second go is mostly leaves and it comes down light and blows about a bit on the ground before it settles. Her sister takes the barrow back down the yard with the shovel across the tray so that it does not bang, and she shuts the gate behind them both with her foot so that it could not be left standing open.

Then they stand at the shed door with the door open on a swept floor.

It is the best the floor has been in a year. You can see the boards. The gap under the door where the grit came in all summer is swept back to the boards, and the bark off the apple trees is in the heap at the lane end and not on the floor of her own shed, and the sweetcorn husks are gone, and the black line along the skirting has come out from about half its length.

In the middle of it, along the front of the bench, there is a crescent about a hand wide and the length of the bench, and at the near end of that crescent there is a corner where the floor is still black where it has been black since the shed was new.

The sister stands beside her in the doorway and looks at the same corner and says nothing about it, and then goes to the gate with her coat and out of it and up the lane, and the woman at number ten stands in the yard and listens to the steps go from hearing.

Then she goes back into the shed and gets down on the floor at the near end of the bench and puts the bar under the front rail of the bench and lifts it, so that she can see the whole of the floor under the bench from one end to the other without moving the bench off that wall or shifting it a single inch.

The corner is exactly as black as it was.
