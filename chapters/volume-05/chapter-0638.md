# Chapter 0638 — Two Things In One Kitchen

Wednesday the fifth of May 2021, in this borough. He is on her step at about ten past eight in the evening with a stool under his arm and he is gone inside a quarter of an hour, and she is in that kitchen until the light goes and she is at that window at about half past six the next morning, and the whole of it is one house at number twelve.

**A STOOL HE HAS BUILT AT HIS OWN BENCH IS PUT DOWN IN A KITCHEN THAT ALREADY HAS A MENDED FOLDING CHAIR IN IT, AND WHEN SHE ASKS HIM WHERE HE GOT IT HE SAYS HE MADE IT, WHICH IS TRUE, AND IT IS THE ANSWER THAT STARTS THE PROBLEM, BECAUSE A THING SOMEBODY MADE HAS NO STORY AND A THING IN THIS KITCHEN NOW HAS TO HAVE ONE.**

He is a man in his early forties. She is a woman in her late seventies. They have spoken twice and neither of them has ever asked the other a question, and one of the two things now in that kitchen is a lie of hers and the other one is the truth of his.

---

The knock is at about ten past eight and it is two knocks and a pause and one more, and it is her knock, and he has copied it.

She knows that in about a second and she does not know what to do with it for about two minutes.

Somebody has come to that door in this borough on a Wednesday evening and copied her knock.

She opens it and he is on the step with something under his arm wrapped in a blanket off a bed, and there is a smell of oil and sawdust coming off it that is stronger than the smell of the man.

And she says, "You'll be the one off the road."

And he says, "That's right."

And she says, "I know the van. I've heard it go past at seven in the morning."

And he says, "Not me. I'm in a white one."

And she says, "Well."

And he says, "I brought you something."

And she says, "You brought me a chair on Saturday and nobody has told me a thing about it and I have not asked, and I would have said that was the end of it."

That is the most she says all night and it is the truest thing she says all night and she has been waiting on the step for two minutes for it.

And he says, "Right."

And she says, "Come in and put it down and go home, because it is cold."

---

It is the coldest night in this borough since before the Easter. That is not a matter of opinion, that is a fact on the radio, and she has had the radio on all afternoon and off since about six, and the gas fire is lit and there is a blanket on the chair she has not looked at.

The kitchen is four steps from the front door and the whole of it is the four steps and the doorway.

He comes in with the stool and he has to turn sideways to get it through, and he puts it down on the tiles in the middle of the floor, and unwraps the blanket off it and folds the blanket over his arm because he has brought it in a van and it is his.

And the stool goes on the tiles and the kitchen is very small and the stool is in the middle of it where she would walk.

She looks at it for a while.

It is ash. The seat is one solid piece about as big as a folded newspaper and it is darker in the middle where the grain is and paler at the two edges. The legs are square-cut and splayed out a little at the bottom. There are four rails between the legs and three of them are darker than the legs and one of them is pale, and she can see the pale one from where she is standing, and she thinks about that for longer than she should.

And it is the right height.

That is the thing. She has been sitting at a table since she could reach one and she knows what a thing at the right height is like without touching it, and this is not a table height, and it is not the height of the wooden stool that is in the passage. It is somewhere between the two, and it is the height of a person who is on their feet.

And the surface of it has a sheen on it that is not varnish, because there are four chairs round that table and she knows what varnish looks like, and this has been oiled, and somebody has rubbed it with something with a cloth, and it has gone the colour of a hazelnut.

And she puts her hand on it, once, flat, and the oil is dry enough that her palm does not come away shining.

And he says, "I can put it by the window."

And she says, "Leave it."

And he says, "Right."

And she says, "Where did you get it."

Here it is. She has been in a kitchen with a woman and a man in it for about four minutes and this is the question and she has got it ready because she has had it ready since Saturday, and she has had it ready since Saturday for a chair and not for a stool.

And he says, "I made it."

She does not say anything.

And he says, "I'm on the road most days. I do kitchens and I do other bits and I made that on my own bench on Monday and Tuesday."

And she says, "You made that."

And he says, "Wednesday morning."

And she says, "For me."

And he says, "For you."

And she says, "Right."

And he says, "Sorry."

And she says, "What for."

And he says, "Nothing. It's not a — " and he does not finish it, and she can see him decide not to finish it, and she lets him.

And he says, "The seat's a bit proud of the frame. It'll bed in."

And she says, "Right."

And she says, "It's nice, that."
---

He is at the step at about ten past eight with the blanket over his arm and the cold coming off him and he says, "If it's in the way—"

And she says, "It won't be in the way."

And he says, "Right."

And she says, "It'll do for the parcel."

And he says, "Right."

And the gate goes.

Here is what he does on the pavement, and it is the whole of the evening as far as he is concerned, and he does not know it.

He said he made it.

He goes up the road about thirty yards and then he stops in the middle of the pavement because he can feel it going round, and it is a bad feeling, and it is the feeling of a man who has taken a thing to a house and been asked where it came from and given the correct answer and knows, on a pavement, that the correct answer was the wrong one.

Because the woman on Saturday came with a chair and said nothing, and the woman on Saturday did not have to say anything, because nothing had been said, and a thing that nobody has said anything about is a thing that can be anything.

And he has told her the truth about a stool.

And a woman in her late seventies in this borough is now in possession of a thing with a man attached to it. Not a story. A man. A man who was in her kitchen, who made it, who was here on a Wednesday evening, and who can be here again, and who is at this moment walking up a road thirty yards away in a white van.

And he cannot think of one single thing he could have said that would have been better, and he goes over the whole of it twice before he gets to the end of the road.

He could have said where it came from. That would have been a lie and it would have been the same lie twice, and she would have had to carry two identical stories about two different things, and a person can do that, and he does not know that she can.

He could have said it was for a mate down the road. That is what he told the man at the bench, and it is a lie, and if she ever meets the mate there is no mate.

He could have said nothing. But he could not have said nothing, because she asked, and a man who is asked where a thing came from and says nothing has told the woman he made it by refusing to deny it.

He gets in the van. He sits in it. He does not start it for about two minutes.

Here is what she does, and she does not go to bed until about half past twelve, and none of it is in the room with him in it because he has gone.

The first thing is that she moves the stool.

Not because it is in the way. It is in the way. It is in the middle of four steps of floor and there is a chair against the dresser and there is a table with a cloth on it, and if she leaves the stool in the middle she will be going round it for the rest of her life, and if she moves it to the window it will be against the damp, and if she moves it under the table it will be a thing in a room she does not use.

She puts it against the wall by the sink, on its side, on top of nothing, and it looks wrong, and she leaves it there.

Then she sits down in the chair with the cushion and tries the stool, and the stool is the right height, and the stool has no story.

That is the whole of what is wrong with the stool and it takes her about twenty minutes to get to it and it is not the fault of the stool.

The green chair has a man who came for a fridge. The man who came for a fridge is in a van and is not going to come back and has never been in this kitchen and could not be asked about anything by anybody, and he is the most useful thing in this house.

The stool has a man who made it, who was in this kitchen, who was at this door, and who has a van she can hear from the kitchen window.

And she cannot put the man who came for the fridge on a stool. Not without it being a second thing, and a second thing about a man who came for a fridge, and a woman at number ten who has already got the first one and will be told before the summer is out that the same man also left a stool, and the man did not, and there was no man, and the first one was already a lie, and the second one would be the same lie twice.

So the stool is a thing in her kitchen that has to have a man, and the only man it has is a real one who is alive and in this borough.

And she sits on the stool at about ten o'clock at night with the cold still on the tiles and works out that she has got two things in this kitchen and one story, and the story is about a man who is not real, and it is now about two things.

At about half past six the next morning she is at the window over the sink, because she is at the window every morning and has been since before Christmas, and the road is doing what a road off the parade does at half past six.

And she looks down it and thinks about the two things.

And the thing she arrives at, standing at the window with the cloth in her hand, is not a good one and she has had it by about ten past six and it is going to be there all day.

Here is what it is.

If the two people who have brought her things do not come at the same time, she can get by. She can have the chair out and the stool on its side, or the stool out and the chair against the dresser, and she can change them over in about a minute, and nobody will ever know, and a minute is not a thing anybody has to feel bad about.

What she cannot do is be in the kitchen with both of them at once.

And she is going to have to be in the kitchen with both of them at once one morning, because there is going to be a wet Tuesday or a bank holiday or a bad run when one of them is short, and on that day two people who have each decided, separately, and without telling each other, that she is a woman in a kitchen who needs sitting down, will be in the same four steps of floor with a stool between them.

And what will happen then is that they will find out about each other.

And then the man who made the stool will find out that there is a green chair in this kitchen, and the woman who brought the chair will find out that there is a stool in this kitchen, and both of them will have to decide what to do about it in about four seconds, standing in a doorway, with her looking at them.

And she has worked out that she will have about four seconds too, and that in four seconds she will have to look at one of them and not at the other.

And she does not know which one. That is the whole of it. She has been at that window since half past six and she does not know which one she will look at, and she has not looked at the green chair once since it came in and she has not looked at the stool once since about ten o'clock last night.

And then she rinses the cloth out and puts it on the rail and the road goes on.

---

**HE PUT A STOOL HE HAD BUILT AT HIS OWN BENCH DOWN IN THE MIDDLE OF FOUR STEPS OF TILE AND SAID HE HAD MADE IT, AND ON THE PAVEMENT THIRTY YARDS AWAY HE WORKED OUT THAT THE CORRECT ANSWER WAS THE ONE THAT PUT A LIVE MAN IN HER KITCHEN, AND SHE STOOD AT HER WINDOW AT HALF PAST SIX THE NEXT MORNING AND WORKED OUT THAT SHE COULD SWAP ONE THING FOR THE OTHER IN A MINUTE BUT COULD NOT SURVIVE THEM BOTH BEING IN THE ROOM, AND THAT SHE DID NOT KNOW WHICH ONE SHE WOULD LOOK AT.**

(End of chapter 0638)
