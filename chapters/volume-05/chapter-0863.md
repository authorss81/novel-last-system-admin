# Chapter 0863 — The Thread Off The Sack Mouth

Friday the twenty-fourth of November 2023, in that shed at number ten from the time the door has gone light until the middle of the afternoon. A thread, and the length of it.

**THERE IS A LOOSE THREAD OFF THE MOUTH OF THE HESSIAN SACK IN THE SHED AT NUMBER TEN AND SHE MEANS TO DRAW IT OUT BETWEEN TWO FINGERS AND PUT IT DOWN AGAIN, AND HER SISTER COMES OUT OF THE FRONT DOOR AND GOES IN AT THE BACK ONCE WITHOUT STOPPING.**

The sack has been standing in the corner of that shed since the day it was filled, and it was filled off the front bed in May, and the soil in it was turned over inside it in the October and has been going exactly nowhere since.

The mouth of it is folded over.

When a sack is filled the neck is turned down over the body of it, twice, so that the soil cannot get at the fold, and the fold of a hessian sack is the strongest part of the sack and the ugliest. It is a doubled strip of cloth about four inches wide lying along the top, and it is the part that is handled, and it is the part that rubs against itself every time the sack is moved.

The mouth has not been moved since May. It has been stood against a wall of the shed with the fold along the top and one corner of it pushed down behind the sack by the door shutting in the wind.

There is a thread coming out of that corner.

---

She sees it against the light from the open door, because that is the only way to see a loose thread on hessian. It is about three inches long and it stands away from the cloth about as far as a hair does off a head, and it is the same colour as the cloth and about four times the thickness.

She pinches it close to the cloth, between two fingers, and draws it out.

Not hard. The whole question is how much comes away, and the only way to find out is to draw it and stop the moment it stops.

It comes away about three inches, easily, in one long piece. Then it stops.

The reason a loose thread has to be drawn out and not merely looked at is that the grain of jute runs in a ribbon and a ribbon unwinds along its length. Anything loose in a hessian weave is loose along the grain of a thread and never across it, so a thread that will come away will come away in one direction as a whole length, and a thread that will not will stop where the next thread crosses it and there is no half of that.

Three inches of jute out of the mouth of a sack is nothing.

That is the first thing and it is worth saying because a loose thread on cloth looks like a fault and mostly it is not. The thread at a fold is a fibre that has been rubbed off the outside of the cloth by cloth. Every fold of a sack for six months has a few. They are not going anywhere and they are not going to spread, and a sack with three of them on it is a sack that is in use.

What tells you whether it is a fibre that has come loose or the beginning of a hole is what is at the end of it.

If the cloth has gone at that spot, the thread comes away in a length and the end of it is a mess of fine fibres, frayed, the width of a hair and about two inches of them, and that is a hole starting. You would then cut the loose bit off and tape the fold, and even that is only worth doing on a day when there is nothing else.

If the cloth is sound, the thread runs into the weave, under the fold, and stops at a knot.

And it stopped.

So she goes along the fold with her hands behind her and looks at the rest of it.

There are four other loose threads on that fold in about three feet of it, and they are all on the outside of the fold and all on the crease, where the two halves of the cloth have been lying across one another since May. She does not draw them out. She looks at the ends of them against the light, and four of them is a fibre and the fifth has a tail on it where she knotted the neck of this sack herself when it was filled, in the May, with the end of the jute and about half a turn to save it going.

That one is hers, it is fast, and she leaves it.

What she is looking for on the whole fold is not threads. It is the cloth, and cloth that is going goes thinner before it goes loose, and the sign of it is the light coming through. She gets the fold up against the door with the sun behind it and looks through about a foot of it at a time. It is the same all the way along and it is not going thin anywhere except at the very corner the door pushes, and at that corner the threads are rubbed down and one or two of them are about to go and the cloth behind them has not begun to.

A corner that has lost a few fibres is a corner that has been doing its job for six months. The fold is the part of that sack designed to be rubbed and it has been rubbed and it is still cloth.

She puts her thumbnail on the spot where it stopped and works it gently and the end of the thread is there under her nail, and it is not frayed and it is not a mess of fibres. It is a knot. Small, about the size of a pin head, hard, and the thread goes into it and stops dead and the other side of it does not come out.

That is a knot a loom tied. Nobody in this yard has ever tied a knot in that sack and she knows it, because a knot a hand ties has a tail, and this has none. A knot in a piece of hessian sacking is in the cloth itself, put in by the weaver as the pattern went along, and it is there because that is what the cloth is made of.

Which means the whole of that mouth is one piece of cloth and not two, and the fold has not worn through at that corner, and it is not going to open and spill about a barrow-load of the best soil off that bed onto a shed floor in the middle of the night.

Her sister comes out of the front door in the middle of the day and crosses the yard towards the back of the house.

"Is the back door on the latch?"

"It's on the latch."

"Then I'll go through the back."

"Go on."

She gets past the shed door with a basket on her arm and does not slow down.

"My hands have gone."

"Mine went in the summer."

"You'll want to get somebody to look at them."

"There's nobody to look at them," and she goes in at the back and the door shuts, and that is the whole of the crossing.

She puts the thread down on the flag where it came off, in the same place, because a thread pulled out of a fold and left in a heap on the floor is a thread somebody will stand on.

She does not cut it. She does not tie it back into the cloth and she does not put tape over it. There is nothing to do about a thread that has come loose out of a fold and stopped at a knot three inches in, and there is nothing in that shed that would go near it.

The sack stands in the corner where it has stood since May, with the fold along the top of it and the soil turned over inside it and nothing in that corner moving.

Three inches in, the thread stops, and what stops it is a knot about the size of a pin head with no tail on it, and a knot that size in a piece of hessian is a knot the loom tied, and it is the reason the mouth of that sack has not opened in six months of being folded and rubbed and is not going to open in the six months after that.
