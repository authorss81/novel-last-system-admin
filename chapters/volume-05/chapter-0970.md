# Chapter 0970 — Down The Stone Crack

Undated in this draft.

**TWO STONES AT THE FOOT OF THE BACK WALL HAVE PARTED BY A CRACK FULL OF OLD DUST.**

The back wall at number twelve runs along the end of the yard in stone rubble with old lime pointing, built generations back and repointed in part some thirty years since.

Two stones at the foot of that wall, each about the size of a loaf, have parted by a crack that runs upright between them for about six inches. The crack is narrow at the top where the stones still touch and wider below where they have settled apart, and it runs full of old dust from top to bottom.

The dust in the crack is soft and grey, drifted in from the yard through years and packed by rain that ran down the wall. At the top it lies loose and falls away at a touch, while further down it has set into a soft cake that keeps the shape of the crack when picked out.

The stones themselves are of sandstone, rough from the quarry and rounded by weather along their exposed faces. Each carries flecks of mica that glitter when the light strikes, and each shows tool marks along one edge where it was dressed to fit. The faces inside the crack are paler than the outer faces, because rain has never reached them directly.

Quarried sandstone strikes cold through the fingertip, deep and unhurried. Neighbouring veteran lime sips scant warmth, floury to touch. Crack powder offers the skin no warmth whatever.

The pointing round the two stones is old lime gone hard and pale, cracked along two joints and fallen away in part. Old lime goes hard by losing water to the air through years, and once hard it cracks rather than gives. The cracks here run along the bond between lime and stone, because the stone moves a hair with heat and rain while the lime does not. The bond has failed cleanly, leaving the stone face bare and the lime edge sharp. Survivors jut a whisker beyond ashlar, casting hairline umbrae. Where it has fallen the joint lies open to the width of a fingernail, and dust has gathered in the opening in a soft line.

The foot of the wall stands on soil with no plinth, and the soil there has risen against the stones through years of leaf mould and sweepings. Grass grows along the foot in tufts, each tuft of cocksfoot and fescue mixed, and one tuft has rooted into the crack itself, sending fine roots down among the dust. The roots bind the dust where they pass and keep it from washing out entire, while the blades above throw rain outward onto the stones. A snail shell lies empty beside that tuft, pale and spiralled, with a chip at its mouth where a bird tried it. The roots are pale and threadlike and break at a touch.

The yard before the wall is of beaten earth with flags laid as stepping stones from the door to the gate. The earth between them is beaten hard by feet and pale with dust in dry weather, darkening to brown after rain. Each flag was laid on sand that has long since washed into the earth, so the flags sit now on soil directly and rock a little in wet weather. Wobble stays whisper-faint, a whisker under sole, each stone re-seating as damp lifts. The flags are worn smooth in the middle of each and rough at their edges, and each spans its bed without rocking. Grit lies scattered between them, sharp underfoot, washed from the joints by rain.

Roof water falls from the eaves above the wall in a sheet in heavy rain and drips from the slates in light rain, inking the sandstone in stripes that pale crown-first. Spring night dew pearls the sandstone and vanishes toward noon, the cleft powder abiding parched.

A slate from the roof above lies broken at the foot of the wall, split along its grain into two pieces that fit together along the break. Slate splits along its grain when a heavy fall finds a weakness, and the split runs true for the length of the stone. The two pieces here fit without gap along three inches and part by a hair beyond, where grit has lodged in the break. Each piece is about a foot long and eight inches wide, with a nail hole near one end still round and unbroken. The break is fresh on one edge and weathered on the other, showing where it lay after it fell. The slate draws little heat and rings faintly when tapped.

The wall above the two stones rises in courses that step back by a hair at every third course, throwing the rain outward. Only dust and grass mark the foot of the wall. A spider has drawn a web from the wall to the grass, low and grey, and dew hangs on it till morning. The web spans from a stone arris to a grass stem and sags in the middle under the weight of the dew. By midday it dries and tightens again, and the spider sits at its edge through the heat with legs folded.
The tip of one finger works down into the crack between the two stones and along it from the top to the soil.

It goes in upright at first and then turns to follow the widening below, reading the sides by touch. It moves a little at a time, pressing the dust aside and feeling the stone beneath.

Depth answers first. At the top the finger reaches the back of the crack after about an inch, where the stones meet behind. Below it goes deeper, to the second joint, where the crack opens through to the soil and the tip finds earth soft and damp.

Contents answer after. Down the greater cleft dust alone packs, fluffy at the lip and caked beneath. Hard by the soil perches a lime splinter sloughed from overhead bond, wedged athwart, wafer-thin, pallid. It plays dead to downward touch, slewing sideways under lateral push till the root tuft stops it.

Splinter sips faint warmth, disintegrating to nail pressure. Aged lime flanking the cleft nips cold either side of the finger jointly. Subsoil lends nil warmth, exhaling rotted foliage.

The fingertip abandons the cleft tip-powdered, nail lined grey, otherwise immaculate. The cleft remains as met, the lime flake lodged where touch wedged it, one knuckle lower than found.

Both boulders bide untouched, coursed tonnage overhead and earth gripping their soles. Thumb shove wobbles neither a whisker; each re-seats into its socket.

Eaves drip lands shy of the footing in drizzle, spattering it in downpours; hence loose crest powder over set depths. The roots in the crack take the splash and keep the dust from washing out entire.


The shell by the grass tuft spirals sunwise to a sharp apex, banded faintly brown on cream. Its mouth lips outward where growth paused for drought, then resumed with rain. A bird tested that lip and failed, chipping it in one place yet leaving the whorl intact. The shell interior gleams faintly in sun, pearly despite its size. Ants patrol its coils at noon, finding salt where rain evaporated.

Cocksfoot grass at the wall foot grows in tussocks that bleach at the tips through summer. Each blade folds lengthwise against drought, unfolding silently post-rain. The roots mat densely to the depth of two inches, then dive singly seeking damp. That mat lifts like felt when tugged, revealing beetles beneath. Felted roots sip first of any rainfall, sparing little for masonry below.
---

Her sister waits under the washing line at number twelve outward of the back door, line overhead and prop aside, hands empty at her sides.

The fingertip leaves the stone cleft and brushes dust from the thumb while the sister reaches for the line overhead. Stones and foothold weeds earn not a syllable. She stands a long moment eyeing the pale sky before going back toward the house door.

The wooden prop beside the line leans just off upright and hums faintly to a fingertip.

Twin weathered stones guard their narrow cleft and its grey powder unfailingly, when the line overhead dries fully and the whole yard empties for the day at dusk.
