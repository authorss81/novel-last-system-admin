# Chapter 0385 — What Was Not There

Sunday the twenty-first of January 2018, from about half past seven in the morning until about half past nine at night, a yard off a side turning behind a row, in Tideglass.

**A MAN WENT BACK TO THAT YARD ON THE MORNING OF THE TWENTY-FIRST OF JANUARY FOR A TIN OF GREASE HE HAD LEFT ON A BENCH AGAINST A BRICK WALL, AND IT WAS NOT ON THE BENCH, AND HE STOOD THERE AND LOOKED AT THE BENCH TWICE, AND HE SAID NOTHING TO THE MAN WHO KEEPS THAT PLACE ABOUT IT, AND THERE WAS A CRATE OF APPLES ON THAT BENCH INSTEAD, AND HE LIFTED IT AND CARRIED IT OUT TO THE GATE AND PUT IT IN THE BACK OF HIS VAN AND DROVE OFF, AND HE HAS NOT SAID A WORD TO ANYBODY ABOUT ANY PART OF THAT MORNING SINCE.**

The yard is behind the last six of a row of shops and you go into it through a gate about four foot wide that stands open from about eight until about half past four on weekdays. It is about thirty foot across and thirty-five the other way and it is concrete, and it is not a yard anybody hires, and there are two lorries that come and go in it most days and a man who keeps it who does not own any of it.

The man who keeps that place came in at about a quarter to eight on the Sunday, which is later than he comes on a weekday, and let the gate open, and started on the far end with a yard broom.

The bench is against the brick of the building at the back, on the side the morning does not get to until about half past nine. It is a slab of concrete about six foot long and about sixteen inch wide and about eighteen inch off the ground, and it is part of the building, and the brick behind it is nine courses up and stops there.

---

He had put the tin on that bench on the Thursday before, at about half past three.

He remembers putting it down because he had both hands full. He had come in to look at a length of pipework for a man who wanted it doing by the middle of February, and he had a bag over his shoulder and a length of conduit under his arm, and the man who keeps that place had come out of the small building and said the water was off at the far end, and he had set the tin down on the bench to shift the conduit off his shoulder onto the other one, and he had not picked it up again because the water had come back on inside two minutes and he went straight on to the other end of the yard with the conduit.

That is the whole of what he knows about it. He does not know that he decided to leave it. He put it down and he left it there and he has not known until this morning that he had.

He came in through that gate at about ten past ten and the man who keeps that place was at the far end with the broom, working along a line about nine foot in from the wall, and the two of them said good morning and nothing else.

The man in his forties went straight down the side the bench is on. He looked at the bench.

There was nothing on it. There was a leaf or two and a cigarette end and a ring of grey where something round had been standing, and that ring had been there in December and was not new.

He stood and looked at it. Then he went along the length of it, about six foot, from one end to the other, and looked at it again, and there was no tin of grease at any point on it.

He put his hand flat on the concrete where the tin had been and it was cold and wet at the edges and dry in the middle, which is a bench that has had nothing heavy on it since the rain stopped.

Then he looked at the wall behind it, in case it had gone behind, and at the ground in front of it, and under it, and there was nothing anywhere.

The man who keeps that place carried on with the broom at the far end and did not come along, and did not call across, and has not thought about that morning since.

Nobody has taken it. That is not what happened. The man who keeps that place did not take it and there is nobody else in that yard. It is on the ground somewhere in that yard, or it went out of that gate in a lorry, or it is under the bench in about two inches of dirt and grit that comes off the concrete and gets into everything, and there is a spade in the small building and he could turn that bench over in four minutes if anybody asked him to.

Nobody has asked him to, and he does not know it is missing, and he will not be told, and if it were said to him in a fortnight he would look at the bench and say there is nothing on it and that would be the whole of his part in it.

And the man in his forties did not ask.

There is a question available to him in that yard on the morning of the twenty-first of January and it is not a hard one. The man who keeps that place was thirty foot away with a broom. The question would have taken about four seconds and it would have been: did you move my tin.

He did not ask it, and it is not that he decided against asking. A tin of grease is about two pounds ten and it is not his to a degree anybody could check, and a man who asks after a tin of grease in a yard he has been let into is a man who has told the other one that he left something, and there is nothing at the end of that for him. The other one would have said he had not seen it and that would have been the end of it and the man in his forties would have walked out with nothing said and been exactly where he was.

---

What was on that bench instead was a crate of apples.

A wooden slatted crate, the kind that comes in with a load and does not come back, about eighteen inch by twelve and ten, with a bit of newspaper laid over the bottom of it and a wooden lid standing on its edge against the brick behind, propped where it had been put.

It had about nine pounds of apples in it. They were Cox's, and about a third of them had gone soft on the side facing out, and the rest were sound, and somebody had put a layer of newspaper over them and taken it off again and put it back crooked.

They had not been in that yard more than a day. The newspaper was dry in the middle and the outside of it was not.

Nobody in that yard had said where the crate came from. The man who keeps that place had not brought it in — he comes in a car and his car is outside on the street, not in that yard, and he had been at that far end with a broom since a quarter to eight and had not gone near the bench.

The man in his forties stood at that bench and looked at the crate for about half a minute.

Then he lifted the lid off it and put it back against the brick the way it had been, and he got his hands under the crate and it was heavier than a crate of apples that size ought to be, and he shifted it to the edge of the bench, and got it down onto the concrete, and it went about an inch into the grit.

He picked it up again properly and stood holding it against his chest.

He could have asked. That is the thing about it. He was thirty foot from a man who keeps that yard and could have walked over with the crate in his arms and said, is this one yours, and that man could have said yes or no, and either answer would have cost about six seconds and neither of them would have thought about it again.

He did not do that either, and he would not have been able to say why afterwards, and it is not that he wanted it. If he had asked and been told yes, he would have put it down and it would have been a thing he had done. If he had asked and been told no, he would have been a man who had picked up somebody's crate and asked about it in a yard, and there is a version of that conversation where a man hears what a stranger is doing on a Sunday morning behind a row of shops.

So he carried it.

He carried it about thirty foot across that yard to the gate and out onto the turning and round to the front of his van, and the man who keeps that place came off the broom at the far end and stood and watched him come all the way to the gate with it, and neither of them said one word, and the man who keeps that place did not ask and has not asked and will not.

He got it into the back of the van on the sack and put the sack round it and put the tailgate up and drove off at about twenty past ten.

The apples went in that van for a fortnight.

He ate four of them that night, standing up, and the rest stayed in the crate in the back of the van with the sides of a paper bag folded over the top, and he took about four of them a day for a week and then there were six left and the two at the bottom had gone, and on the Monday morning of the fifth of February he put the crate out on the kerb at the top of his own street with nothing written on it, and it was gone by two o'clock, and he does not know who has it and did not look.

He has not been back to that yard. He has been to that turning maybe four times in two years and there was no reason to go a fifth, and he is not going to make one, and the tin of grease is in that yard or is not and it is about two pounds ten.

What is left of the whole morning is a man who keeps a yard and a bench on it. He was asked nothing and he asked nothing and he has not told his employer and his employer does not know that there is a bench.

**HE WALKED THAT CRATE ACROSS THIRTY FOOT OF THAT CONCRETE PAST A MAN WITH A BROOM WHO LOOKED UP AND WATCHED HIM GO ALL THE WAY TO THE GATE, AND NEITHER OF THEM SAID A WORD, AND THE MAN WHO KEEPS THAT PLACE WENT BACK TO THE FAR END AFTERWARDS AND DID NOT THINK ABOUT IT AGAIN THAT DAY.**
