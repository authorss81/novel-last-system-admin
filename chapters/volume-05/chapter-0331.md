# Chapter 0331 — Three Lengths and a Gap

Sunday the twenty-fifth of June 2017, from about half past nine in the morning until about half past twelve, the low end of Cleeve Street in Tideglass.

**A MAN SAID OUT LOUD AT A SHOP DOOR THAT HE WOULD HAVE THREE LENGTHS OF CLOSE-BOARD INTO A GAP IN A BOUNDARY BEFORE THE END OF THE MONTH, A MAN WITH A FLEA SAID SO TO A THIRD PERSON, AND WHEN THE FIRST MAN WAS ASKED ABOUT IT HE DID NOT DENY SAYING IT, AND THE THREE LENGTHS ARE STILL AGAINST A BRICK WALL.**

The low end of Cleeve Street is where it comes out at a T, and the two yards at the end of it are the backs of two houses that face a road somewhere else, and between them there was a close-board fence about five foot high on a frame, and the two posts at the end of it have been in since the middle of the sixties and the bottom of the fence has rotted through in a line about a foot above the ground for the last three or four of those years.

Three lengths of close-board fencing are lying against the brick end wall of the yard on the left. They are three foot high, and they have the nails still in them at one end, and they came off a job and were put against that wall and not put up.

The gap they came off is about four foot wide and goes from the ground to the height of a man's shoulder, and nobody has boarded it, and anybody coming up the low end of that street knows it is there, and on that Sunday morning between about ten and about half past twelve about nine people went through it.

---

The man in his thirties said it at a door at about a quarter to ten on the Friday before, and the man he said it to was standing on the step of that door with his coat over his arm, and he said it to him and not to anybody else on that street.

He had been in that street at the half-hour look at the gap on the Thursday, at the end of a round, in about four minutes, and he had set out what the job wanted to the man in the fleece, and got back the answer a man in a fleece always gives, which is that anybody can say that.

"That's a day off, that," said the man in his thirties. "And a wet one. You want a post out of the ground at that end and a hole in it about two foot square, and you want somebody to hold the post while the other packs it, and I'll not do that on my own and I'd not do it in the middle of a week with a cold bit over a path."

"When would you do it."

"Before the end of the month," said the man in his thirties. "I'll have those three lengths into that gap before the end of the month."

The man in a fleece said that was fine and went on down the street, and the man in his thirties shut his van and went off down it about ten foot and then stood still in the road, because he had heard the sentence he had just said going away from him down the pavement and he knew the shape of it.

He had not said he would look at it. That is the whole of what was wrong with it, and it took him about four seconds on that Sunday morning nine days later to work out what it was, and by then it had been said at a door to a man in a fleece and there was nobody to unsay it to.

---

On the Sunday morning he went down there with his bag and looked at it properly and it was exactly as it had been on the Thursday.

He got down and pulled the bottom rail on each side of the gap to see how much of the post was still in the ground, and the left one has about four inches of solid post under it and the right one has a soft foot, and the concrete on that side is cracked through about two inches from the edge. The three lengths on the ground are sound and the nails in them are not, and he would want fresh nails and a length of treated post and about a bag of post fixings for the right-hand foot, and he would want to get the end of that fence into a yard that is not his, through a gate on a latch, on a day when there is somebody in that house or not.

He stood in the road at the end of that street for about ten minutes and then he went and got his cup from the machine he had parked at the top of it and came back and had it.

The woman with a clipboard was on that beat at about a quarter past ten and she has one, and a pencil on a string, and a laminated sheet with the boxes ticked on it, and she counts the things a street puts in the way of a person, which is posts, bollards, gratings, stands, trees, gaps and steps, and she is not on anything.

She was about thirty houses down that one side when she got to the low end and there were three lengths of close-board against a wall inside a gate and a man standing in the road drinking something out of a cup with a bag at his feet.

She wrote a figure against that gate on her sheet and then she did not write the second thing, and the man in his thirties has never found out that there was a space on it, and neither has anybody else.

"You're on that gate twice," she said, to him rather than to the yard. "I've had a fella tell me one number and a woman tell me another and I know it's one gate."

"It's one gate," said the man in his thirties.

"A man in a fleece stopped me at the shelter at the top end at about ten and said a man had told him he'd have that closed before the end of the month." She said it the way she says everything, which is to say it once, cleanly, and then shut the lid on it. "I'm not saying you did. I'm saying a man said you did."

"I said it," said the man in his thirties.

She waited, and he was not going to be let off, and he had not expected to be.

"I said it at that door last week," said the man in his thirties, "to a man in a fleece, standing on the step, at about a quarter to ten. I said I'd have those three lengths into that gap before the end of the month. I did say it, and I am not going to come at you now and say I said something else, because I didn't, and because a man in a fleece has already said to you what I said and there is no version of this where I get to be the one that was misheard."

"Well," said the woman with a clipboard.

"It's the wrong sentence to have said and I know it's the wrong one now, and I'm not going to tell you I said it by accident, because I didn't say it by accident either."

"Will you do it."

He drank his coffee and did not answer that for about ten seconds.

"No," he said.

The woman with a clipboard wrote something on the edge of her sheet, about a quarter of an inch of it, and looked at it, and rubbed it out with a thumb, and did not say that out loud and did not say anything about having done it.

"Right," she said.

"The reason is not the rain and it's not that I can't be bothered," said the man in his thirties. "It's that to start it I have to walk into that yard through a gate on a latch and unbolt nothing and shut nothing behind me, and there is nobody in that house I know and nobody has asked me for anything, and a man in a fleece was standing on a doorstep and not in that yard, and a man who promises a thing in a yard from a doorstep has promised a thing about somebody else's house. I'm not going to go in there on a wet morning in July and be a man in a yard that a person comes home to. And I said it anyway, out loud, on a doorstep in a road, to a man who was not going to have to live next to it."

"You could say that to him now."

"I could say that to a man in a fleece in a road any time I want and it would not be a thing that happened," said the man in his thirties. "It has to be done in a yard, on a wet morning, and the only two people who will ever hear about it are me and him, and he's not here."

The woman with a clipboard put her pencil behind her ear.

"I've got thirty houses on this sheet and there's room on it for about two more," she said. "And I'm not going to write a man's name on this sheet because a man said a thing in a road and did not do it, and I'm not going to put a tick or a number against it either, because the second one of those is a thing about that yard and the first one is a thing about you, and neither of them is a thing that street is."

"Then what is it."

"It's a thing that happened to me on a Sunday morning," said the woman with a clipboard, "and I work a street, and I have never in my life written that column, and I am not starting it at about a quarter past ten on a Sunday in June for a man who told me straight."

He said that was fair, and she said it was, and she went on up the street and did the rest of that side.

He got the pad out of the box of the van in the road at about a quarter to twelve, because it is a habit and not a decision, and it is the last page he has written on, and the last page of a pad is the one a man is least likely to lose, because it is on top.

There was a line on it for a man in a fleece's own round, which is not a street and not a name, and then a gap of about two inches.

He put the pencil on the roof of that van next to the pad and looked at that gap.

If he wrote the low end of Cleeve Street in it, then in about a month there would be one piece of paper in this borough with his handwriting on it and a line of that street in it, and it would be in a van, and it would be about a fence.

And if that were the only line he ever wrote anywhere, then it would also be the only thing in this borough anybody could put next to a boundary that had not been closed.

He shut the pad and put it in the box and shut the lid on the box and put the pencil in his pocket.

**A MAN'S NAME IS NOT ON ANYTHING, A GATE IN A ROAD IS WRITTEN ON A SHEET TWICE WITH A DIFFERENT NUMBER AGAINST IT, AND THREE LENGTHS OF CLOSE-BOARD ARE LYING AGAINST A BRICK WALL AT THE LOW END OF CLEEVE STREET WITH THE NAILS STILL IN THEM AT ONE END, AND A MAN WITH A FLEA WENT DOWN THAT STREET AFTERWARDS WITH HIS COAT OVER HIS ARM.**
