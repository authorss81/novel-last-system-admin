# Chapter 1018 — A Figure Wants A Woman

Undated in this draft.

**THE BAY AT THE LOCK-UP HAS ITS SHUTTER DOWN AND BOARDS IN A RACK.**

Three bays come off that yard. The first, at the office end, is the one a man walks past eight times a day without looking in, and its shutter goes up on an iron runner laid along the head of the opening about four feet off the ground, and the shutter stands down over about half of the opening and has been since before the summer. Along the wall at the back of it stands a rack made of angle iron, eight feet long and about five high, with about sixty boards stood on edge in it the way boards are stood so that the air can get at both faces. Most of them are shuttering boards and have been in that rack for eleven years and are grey and have gone slightly bow on the flat. A good half of them have a figure in chalk on the face near the top end, where the man who cut them to length put down what they were for, and nobody has taken the chalk off any of them in a decade, and a piece of chalk costs nothing.

The sill along the front of that bay is a rolled steel angle bedded into the concrete of the sill line, and it is level for about nine feet out of the twelve and then comes up about a quarter of an inch where the drain goes under it, and everything that has ever been wheeled in over that sill has gone in level for nine feet and then has had to be lifted an inch, and a man who has wheeled anything into that bay has felt it under his hands and has not thought about it once.

He came up that yard in the middle of the day with what he had been handed the day before still in him and with his hands empty, and the yard was shut. The season went out of that yard eleven weeks ago and what is left of it is two men in the far bay on a Tuesday and a Thursday and a shutter down over the office window on a Friday, and on a wet Tuesday in between there is nobody in there at all and the boards in the rack are all that bay is for and nothing in there has been shifted by a soul since the spring.

He stood in front of the rack for a while, because it is the sort of thing a man looks at when he has been handed a job and has not worked out what the job is yet. The answer came to him in that bay the way it always does, which is as a list of what a thing wants and not as a figure.

The wall behind a range at number twelve is wet the whole way down and has been wet since before the summer and there is a damp line on it a foot high with a soft curved edge to it where the limewash has gone. The reason is not the rain and it is not the wall being bad. The reason is that the range stands against that wall on four legs with its back six inches off the brick and nothing at all behind it, so that no air has been able to move there since the range went in, and the slab beside it has an edge that does not run square to the wall it stands against, so the wash comes off that wall and has nowhere to go and sits in the corner and goes back up into the brick.

That wall cannot be mended. He has known that for a year and has never told anybody. A wall that has stood wet for forty years behind a fire is no better because a man cuts into it and lets in something new, and it is no better because he repoints it, and the only thing that has ever dried a wall of that kind is to move the fire off it and leave a space behind and let two summers of air at it. So the job is not a wall at all. The job is a range coming off a wall.

To take a range off a wall you draw the legs, and you get two men under it, and you take the flue off the chimney breast and re-hang it, and you set the range down clear of the brick with its own legs on a tray so that what comes off the floor can get away, and you cut the edge of that slab back to a true line so the wash runs off instead of sitting in the corner. It is about a fortnight of work and it wants a tray and a length of flue and two men who are not him and a great deal of patience, and the woman who owns that kitchen wants to cook in it every day of her life.

He put one hand at the end of a board in the rack and the other at the far end of it and read the distance between them by moving one along while the other stayed, which is a thing he does with everything, and the answer came back that a man would want a rule for that and the rule is lying along the top of a rack in his own store four streets away on a rack that stands true on two of its three legs.

He stood in that bay and worked out the whole of it, and then he stood in it a while longer because the whole of it needed one more thing and he wanted to be sure.

A man can take a range off a wall in this borough for nothing, and he has, and there is nobody in this borough who will pay him for it, and the reason is not the work. The reason is that a payment goes onto a piece of paper, and the piece of paper has a name at one end of it, and he gave the only piece of paper that ever carried his name to a woman in a coal shed a month ago at her own request and let her put her own name in the same place in her own hand, and she did it with her back turned and he stood at the table and let her.


He could have taken the whole of it to the woman who keeps that book. He went into that office four times after the summer and got turned away four times and has never once been angry with her, and there is a page in that office with one ruled column on it and a season shut and a new one ruled underneath it waiting for the spring.

That is where the answer is not, and he worked that out in about four seconds standing in front of a rack of boards, and what he worked out instead was this.

In her own hall, and before he had laid eyes on a thing, the woman of the house at number ten put a figure into words at the start of the summer, and she got there before he could put one of his own, and the figure was a round one and it belonged to her and she had arrived at it by herself over two days, and she told him that she had had that same offer off two men in that street in six years and was not going to have it off a third. He had not repeated any of that to anybody since. He had not stopped hearing it either.

He stood in that bay and got to the bottom of it, which is that he had never in his life worked a figure out in his own head. That a man who has never asked a woman for money cannot put a number on a fortnight of his own work, and that the woman at number ten had arrived at hers by herself over two days at a table at the back of that house, on her own with the cloth and the chair, and that the reason he is the man she told that to is not that she likes him.

"Right," he put out loud into an empty bay with a shutter down over half of it. "Then go and ask her how she did it."

The boards stood on edge in that rack in the cold with the chalk on their faces, and the sill came up where the drain goes underneath it.

He cannot put a figure on that fortnight until somebody who is not him puts one there, and the board with the biggest figure chalked on the face of it is stood back on edge among the others and stays there until the spring.
