# Chapter 0301 — The List

Wednesday the twenty-second of February 2017, from about half past ten in the morning, the low end of Peverell Street in Tideglass.

**HE SAID A LIST OUT LOUD ON A PAVEMENT AT THE LOW END OF THAT STREET AND HE DID NOT WRITE ANY OF IT DOWN, AND A WOMAN OFFERED HIM SOMETHING SHE HAD AND HE DID NOT TAKE IT, AND BY THE MIDDLE OF THE AFTERNOON THERE WAS NO LIST ON ANYTHING ANYWHERE.**

In his left hand he had a pad of work notes with the elastic round it and he did not open it, and it stayed shut in his hand the whole time he was on that pavement, which was about half an hour.

He was there because a white van had come up that street at about half past ten with a fridge in the back of it and had stopped level with the second house on the left and had to reverse back down about thirty foot to turn out again, and had done that in front of him, and the driver had put his hand up in apology, and gone.

The man in his thirties watched it go and then stood on the pavement outside the low end of that street and said, out loud, to the street, "Right."

---

The woman with a shopping bag came down that street at about ten to ten on her way back from the shops and stopped.

"That's the third one this month," she said.

"So it is," said the man in his thirties.

"You could put a sign on that corner telling them not to come up here."

"You could," said the man in his thirties. "That's one. That's the first one."

---

He got about nine things out, and he said them out loud, and he said them the way a man reads a list he has not written down, which is to say that he did not hesitate over any of them and he did not explain any of them either.

He held the pad against his leg the whole time and it did not open.

The one about the turning, which was a sign on the corner of the wall at the bottom of that street, because three vans had come up it in a fortnight and none of them could get back out again without reversing the length of the front gardens.

The one about the grit, which was that the top of that street is not on the gritting round and never has been, and that the grit bin at the top of it has been standing there empty in the bottom since before the summer, and that nobody has ever been told that a street can be left off a round, and that the man who comes down with the shovel puts what he puts down at the corner by the bank and carries on up the parade.

The one about the pump in the doorway at the bottom of that street, which has had its stopcock off since before Christmas, and which is a thing you cannot ring anybody about because there is nobody to ring, and which is not a fault in anybody's pipes because the stopcock is off on purpose, and which stands there in a doorway that a person walks past four times a day.

The one about the step outside the shop that has been closed since the summer, which goes down about two inches at the outside corner and has a piece of blue lino over it that has come away at one end and is a trip, and which nobody has looked at, and which is nobody's because the shop is shut and the landlord is in a town sixty miles off.

The one about the gulls on the roof of the block at the top of that street, which are up there all day every day from about February, and which get into the loft of the top flat through a ventilation tile that has a piece of polystyrene against it, and which is not a thing anybody can be told off for.

The one about the buses, which is that the last one down that street is at about ten past four and that if a person works in this town and finishes at five they are walking, and that this is not a complaint, it is a fact about the last bus, and that he has not been able to find out what the first one is without asking somebody.

The one about the fact that a man can fall down here and there is nobody to ring, and that he has said that one out loud twice before, in December and at Christmas, to two different people on that pavement, and that both of them said aye, and that he has stopped saying it.

The man in a fleece going to the works came down that street at about ten past ten on his way to the bus stop and stopped about ten foot off and listened to about a minute and a half of it.

"That's a list," said the man in a fleece.

"That's a list," said the man in his thirties. "It's not a piece of paper. I want to say that before anybody else does. There's no paper on it. It's a Wednesday morning and I'm saying it out loud and it's going nowhere and I'd rather it went nowhere out loud than nowhere on paper in a van."

"What's the good of that to anybody."

"Nothing," said the man in his thirties. "It's the good to me. I have been thinking about that step outside the shut shop for about four months and I have never said it out loud to anybody, and now I have said it, and I am not any better off, and I know exactly what the list is, because I can read it."

The woman with a shopping bag had gone up the street about twenty foot and then come back.

"I've got about half a bag of salt," she said. "Rock salt. It came in December because I gritted my own steps and I put a jug of it out and the woman from the top of the street came and took some out of it and I never went to fetch the jug back and it's been in my porch ever since."

"Right," said the man in his thirties.

"You could have that for the step outside the shut shop," said the woman with a shopping bag. "It's in a paper sack. I'd not want anything for it and I'd not want it in writing."

"No," said the man in his thirties. "No, leave it. If I take that off you I have taken a thing off a woman for a step in a shut shop, and then I've got her in it, and she'll tell somebody she gave a bag of salt to a man about a step, and then in a fortnight she'll have to say to me what she wanted for it, and there isn't anything she can want for it because I can't put it in anywhere."

"You could put it in the step," said the woman with a shopping bag.

"I can't put a sack of salt in a step," said the man in his thirties. "Salt goes to ice and comes up the stone and puts a white bloom on it and in a month that step's a different colour than the one next to it and whoever's responsible for that step is not going to be me."

The woman with a shopping bag stood on that pavement with the bag in her hand for a second and then said, "Right," and went on up the street and did not come back with it and did not say anything else about the salt, and the jug is still in her porch.

The man in a fleece said, "That's a good no," and went to the bus stop.

The man in his thirties put the pad in the front of the van with the elastic still round it and shut the door, and the pad had not been opened once between half past ten and twenty to twelve, and the white van did not come up that street again that day, and the man who lives at number forty-one had a light on in his front room in the afternoon as he has in the afternoon, and the stopcock in the pump in the doorway at the bottom of that street was still off, and the step outside the shut shop still had its blue lino coming away at one end.

**AND ON THE THURSDAY HE SAID ONE OF THE NINE OUT LOUD AGAIN TO A MAN HE WAS WORKING BESIDE IN A KITCHEN IN CORRANCE LANE, AND THE MAN ASKED HIM WHICH NINE, AND HE COULD NOT REMEMBER THE SEQUENCE OF THEM, AND HE WROTE NONE OF THEM DOWN THEN EITHER.**
