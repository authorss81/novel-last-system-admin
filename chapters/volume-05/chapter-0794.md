# Chapter 0794 — The Sack Of Gravel

Monday the twentieth of February 2023, in this borough. From the middle of the morning until it is going dark she is in her own yard at number ten with the door of her own shed open behind her, and a sack of gravel standing there since May is the whole of the day.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES HAS A SACK OF GRAVEL STANDING IN HER SHED SINCE MAY WITH A SHOVELFUL GONE OUT OF IT AND MEANS TO PUT IT DOWN ON THE PATH, HER SISTER OPENS IT AND PUTS IT DOWN AND RAKES IT LEVEL, AND THE PATH IS THE BEST IT HAS BEEN SINCE SHE CAME, AND SHE FILLS THE SACK WITH THE TOPSOIL THAT CAME OFF THE FRONT OF THAT PATH IN A BUCKET LAST SPRING AND LEAVES A HOLE IN IT.**

She has been in her early thirties since before she came to number ten, and her sister knocks at the front door in the middle of the morning and the two of them go out through the house and into the shed.

---

Inside, the bulb in the hall has been in since she came and the one over the sink was put in by somebody in the autumn. There is a socket in the kitchen passage of a different make to the two in the front room and always has been. The meter cupboard is on its latch. The back passage takes a barrow sideways and has done since she came, and there is a smear of old mortar on the skirting at the end of it about the height of a hand, where the sink used to come out of the wall and was never made good.

The shed roof has lost three slates off the far side and they are in the grass where they fell, and the guttering on the front of it is down on its brackets in two places. Behind the shed the lawn is about the length of a carpet and has not been cut since the summer, and the length of close boarding behind that has come off its two screws and stands against the neighbour's fence at an angle. A sheet of ply is stood against the front of the shed with nothing fastening it to anything. There is a sheet of felt lying over the shed roof with the top corner of it lifted off the boarding where it has been since the summer, and nothing fixing it anywhere. In the drawer under the worktop there is a hammer and a screwdriver and a piece of paper folded with three lines on it, and that drawer does not get opened. Along the bottom of that yard the close boarding leans in under a raking strut, and where the strut crosses the far post there are four turns of wire round the two of them, and there is no way into the run at all without a wire cutter. And at the corner where the hedge turns, the gap is closed with three lengths pulled up out of the dead wood and four stakes, and nothing goes through there either. The shed door shuts on a new stile and a new ledge and does not have to be lifted.

The path is two yards long and it is the worst thing in that yard.

The three boards that went down it in October are down it still, at the near end, put there to get across the mud from the back door as far as the middle of the yard. At the far end the water comes off the low end of the shed and has made a hole in the corner about the size of a dinner plate that is digging itself deeper every winter. Between the last board and the beginning of that hole there is about a yard of bare ground and it has been bare since she came, and everything that has ever gone between that back door and that shed has gone across it.

It is mud for most of the year and it is hard as a floor for the rest of it and there is nothing on it.

Her plan is the sack.

The sack is standing in the corner of the shed on its end with its mouth folded over, and it has been standing there since May. It is a builder's sack about three feet high and it is full of a grey washed gravel about the size of a marble, and every time anything in that yard wanted laying she dug into it with a shovel from the top and never went anywhere near the bottom, so that the gravel in it has a crater in the middle of it about the depth of a bucket, and there is a spoil heap of what came out of it beside the sack on the shed floor.

She means to open it, put the whole of it down on that yard of path, and spread it and level it with the back of a piece of batten.

---

Her sister knocks at the front door and they go through the house and out into the yard and she goes straight to the sack and puts her hand in it.

"It is not full."

"It is a sack of gravel. It is three quarters full."

"That is not the way to empty a sack." Her sister has a hand in after hers. "And that is not going to come down in one go. That is going to come down in seven or eight goes, and every one of them is you dragging a heavy sack out of a shed door and tipping what is left of it on a path and sweeping it up again."

"Then it is a job."

"Then it is a job done the wrong number of times." She stands up and looks at the sack. "Bring it out. We will tip it and rake it and be finished."

---

So they do it properly, and it takes an hour.

They get the sack out of the corner and on its side in the doorway of the shed, which takes both of them, and they fold the mouth of it back and she cuts the side of the sack open along the stitching with a knife, because a gravel sack sewed shut at the top is not a thing you can get gravel out of without wrecking the sack.

Then they tip it.

It goes down on the bare yard of path in one go and it makes a heap about as high as her knee in the middle of it, and it runs up to the last board at the near end and stops there, and it comes up against the plinth of the shed at the far end, and half of it stands about a foot deep on top of the rest.

They spread it with the back of the shovel while the other shovels it forward, from the middle out to the two sides, and the boards stay where they were laid and the tops of them stay out of it where they were, and they leave the hole at the shed corner where it is, and then the rake comes out.

The rake is an old garden rake with a broken tooth that she has been meaning to do something about since the summer.

They rake it level from one side of the yard of path to the other, and they bring the top of it up to the height of the tops of the boards at the near end and to the height of the sound ground at the edge of the hole at the far end, so that the gravel runs into the boards and stops and the boards still stand a finger proud of it. She puts a straightedge of batten down on it and works the top of it to that, and they rake the last of it with the rake and with the back of the shovel handle, and the marks of the teeth go right across the middle in one direction and then the other where she turned it.

---

It is the best that path has been since she came.

It is gravel the whole of that yard and it is level with the sound ground at the corner and level with the boards at the top end, and it does not turn to mud in the wet and it does not dig itself out at the low end, and she goes out of that back door with a full barrow and it goes across that yard on a wheel and does not sink an inch anywhere.

Her sister stands in the middle of it with her boots in the gravel.

"That is better than the boards."

"And in ten years it will be better than the boards still. You can put a barrow across that and you can put the wheelbarrow and the sack barrow and a bicycle across it, and you will not have to think about where the mud is." She puts her heel down and turns it over. "And you have done it in an hour instead of eleven weeks."

---

Then the sack goes back in the shed and the topsoil goes into it.

That is the end of the day and it is not part of the job.

The sack goes back into the corner of the shed, and it will not stand up on its own. It has been on its end on the floor of that shed since May with about six inches of gravel in the bottom of it and the gravel has been wet and dry on that concrete all summer and the bottom of that bag has gone through in two places, about the size of a coin each, where it has lain. She turns it round and stands it up on its bottom and pushes it and the bottom of it gives under her hand.

So she lays it on its side along the wall of the shed, which is the only way it will sit.

And she fills it.

The turf and the topsoil came off the front of that path in a bucket last spring. She carried it round to the back of the shed and put about half of it out on the beds and stood the rest back under the bench, and it has been there ever since. There is a half bucket of it left and it is dark and fine and it is the best thing in that shed for putting round bulbs in.

She carries it out of the shed in the bucket and tips it into the sack on its side, and it stands up in the sack as a bank against the wall, and she pats the top of it flat with the back of her hand.

Then she goes out into the yard to look at that path one more time in what is left of the light, because she wants to.

She stands in the doorway of the shed and the gravel is raked level across the whole of that yard with the marks of the teeth in it, and her sister is at the gate with her coat on and the gate is open behind her, and neither of them goes out of the yard and nothing is said about the time.

The sack is on its side along the wall of that shed with a hole worn through the bottom of it in two places and half a bucket of topsoil banked up inside it, and it will run out of the bottom of that bag before it is finished, and it can never stand on its own again.
