# Chapter 0916 — Under The Slab Corner

Monday the tenth of June 2024, at the lifted path slab at number ten when the sun stands clear of the fence. The hollow under a slab corner.

**SHE HAS A LIFTED PATH SLAB AT NUMBER TEN WITH A HOLLOW UNDER ITS CORNER AND SHE MEANS TO EASE THE PALM FLAT UNDER THAT CORNER WITH FINGERS SPREAD AND HER SISTER IS AT THE YARD GATE FROM THE LANE WITH A STRING BAG ON HER WRIST.**

The path at number ten was put down in a week with slabs of pale stone, and the third from the gate has ridden up at one corner while the rest have kept their seats.

It is a slab about two feet square, with the top smoothed by feet and the corner lifted a finger above its bed, and a hollow of dark soil under it where the draught moves.

She wants that hollow, where the slab stands off the ground and the ants run.

A slab hollow tutors beyond its crown: treads parade above, earth answers below.

So the palm slides flat beneath the lifted slab corner.

The soil under feels cool across the back of the hand, and grit under the lip catches at the skin as the hand turns.

The palm travels in a hand's breadth and meets a bank of earth risen under the slab, black and tight, peppered with grit and a root shorn flat where the edging iron passed in spring.

She combs the bank with fanned fingers side to side, and the soil sinks half an inch under the flat hand and locks again on the way out.

Under the fingers waits a pebble bed. Small washed stones set tight under the bank with sand among them, grey and sharp, locking where they lie.

The corner in the end does not sit on its bed. It sits carried on two rims of stone with earth rammed in the hollow between, and the rammed earth beds the corner against feet crossing.

She slides the palm free of the corner and scrubs the dirt off on the path rim, and stands over the slab as sun clears the fence.

Her sister reaches the yard gate from the lane with a string bag on her wrist and does not hang it on the gate.

"Did the rain get under the frame?"

"It ran off before dinner."

"My beans have come through."

"They come first along the warm wall."

She shifted the bag from one wrist to the other and kept it off the gate where the damp could not reach it, and stood at the gate with the bag on her arm, and then she took the bag on up the path with the gate left shut.

---

A path slab put down for feet has a use far from walls. A slab path is where spare slabs go when they come out of a yard, and the walk to the gate is where they sit till they are wanted for a shed floor.

The slabs at the ends of it are dark and green on one face, the sort that sat through winter under leaves, and the lifted one is pale and drier, the sort that spent winter scoured by the broom.

A slab of pale stone beds into the ground under it, and a slab of dark stone stands on it. Dark stone has a close face that throws water off, and pale stone has an open face that drinks a little and then dries, so it beds down into soil and the dirt around grips it fast.

Trodden stones gone coarse shake the morning dew off in drops, and every drop leaves the crowns a little barer.

The underside of the lifted slab feels rough where the mould left its pits, and the pebble bed below feels rough where the wash left the points bare, and the bank between feels rough where roots have worked it.

Rough stone underfoot cuts a little every step till the top shines, and the shine stands where feet land most, which is the middle of the slab and not its rim.

A trodden-coarse face dries earliest after rain, while a wear-polished one keeps wet along its west lip till supper.

With its coarse crown east and its hollow in shade, the lifted slab dries on the sunrise side first, which keeps the west lip dark till supper.

Drought furs the slab crown with dust, and rain scours it hard again, every scouring taking soft sand and leaving dense stone.

The hollow under the corner runs a hand along and a finger deep, and at the far end the two rims meet the slab face to face with only a cushion of sand between them.

Sand at a face-to-face meeting washes out in a season or two and is brushed back in, while earth in an open hollow binds and is left alone.

The ants in the hollow are the old record of that gap, thick where the earth stands highest under the slab and thin where the pebble bed crowds the mouth.

Ants at the warm end carry grains out by morning and crumbs in by night, and the grains pave the run walls where a fingertip traces them but a nail cannot prize them.

A broken run mends dense at its rims and spare down its middle, and old runs show the dense rims while young ones run even.

---

The moss at the west lip stays green after the walk has paled, and June finds the difference along every slab.

Rain off the fence boards gathers above the lifted corner and falls in one curtain on the pale stone. The curtain breaks on the lip and runs sideways, and the hollow drinks the middle of it while the pebble bed sheds the rest toward the gate.

The east half of the slab sheds its own rain straight off its end, so that half dries from above while the hollow side stays damp underneath through winter.

The slab has shouldered its burden since paint last freshened the gate; its bed stays unshifted to a finger's width.

The lifted corner has fretted its rim by the breadth of a coin over three winters, and the tar fleck that stood flush with the next slab since the fence tarring stands a hair lower now.

Tar melts in sun within one summer and runs, so the mark lingering where the lip shelters it says the slab edge guards the tar.

No other mark sits on these slabs, and the tar says the walk sinks straight down.

The soil below stays dark and close, threaded with pale roots that softened through winter and stir again before July.

A beetle works the dark down there year round, and grains at the bank mouth roll loose under a fingertip.

Beetle traffic leaves pale dust on the shaded pebbles, and the west end shows no dust since the broom passed.

Timbers meet ledges lipless at the gate, stones meet rims lipless out of the wet, soil meets bed bricks the same.

Hazel sticks stacked by the shed lean in a tripod for the beans, barked poles the sun has silvered at the tips. The sticks were cut in winter and driven in spring, and the driven ends have gone dark in the soil while the standing lengths stay pale. Twine loops bind the tripod head, tarred twine that resists rain and tightens as it dries. The loops ride a finger down the poles each season as wind works them, and the beans hide the slippage under leaf by July. Fallen leaves from last autumn still mat the bed corners, oak leaves that rot to lace where worms work them, and the lace keeps morning dew long after the slabs dry.

The joint sand between the slabs grades itself by size, coarse grains perching high where feet kick them and fine grains sifting deep where rain pulls them. A fingertip worked along the joint reads the grade from rough to smooth and back, and the reading alters after every storm. The joint beside the lifted slab runs rough for half its length where the hollow breathes underneath, and the rough grains rasp the skin while the fine stretch powders soft. Pebbles at the path rim sort the same way, thumb-sized stones shouldering outward and pea-sized ones nestling in, the rough shoulders turning grey in sun while the nestled ones stay dark with damp.

Overhead the slabs read straight and level, the lifted one proud by a finger, sunlight pooling on the faces.

A crown roughened by weather sheds morning rain in sheets, and every sheet scrubs the faces cleaner, for loose grit washes away and close stone stands its ground.

The slab keeps the hollow the ants made under it, the corner on its rims with the bank packed close about its heel, and the run will deepen a hair every year under no tread but rain if the sun stands clear of the fence.
