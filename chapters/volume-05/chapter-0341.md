# Chapter 0341 — A Torch of His Own

Wednesday the second of August 2017, from about half past seven in the morning until about ten past two in the afternoon, a kitchen at the back of a parade, and a first-floor corridor in a building by the water, in Tideglass.

**HE BOUGHT A TORCH OFF A MAN IN A MARKET IN JUNE FOR TWO POUNDS AND HIS OWN NAME IS SCRATCHED ON THE BOTTOM OF IT IN A NAIL, AND IT WENT ROUND THAT KITCHEN ALL MORNING AND OUT OF THE BUILDING AND UPSTAIRS AND BACK, AND NOT ONE PERSON WHO HELD IT ASKED HIM ANYTHING AT ALL.**

The kitchen is at the back of the parade, through a passage, off a door that has a wire over the bottom of it because the door is on a slope, and it is about twelve foot by eight and it has a gas cooker, a sink, a bench down one side and a table down the other, and there is a man who cooks in it and there is a lad who washes up in it four mornings a week and has done since the middle of the year before last.

He is about eighteen. Nobody in that kitchen has ever written his name down, and there is no piece of paper in this borough with his name at the top of it, and no one has ever asked him for one.

The torch lives on a shelf on the wall by the door, upside down on a folded tea towel, and it is a black rubber one about the length of a forearm with a fat lens at the front and a switch on the tail, and it has been on that shelf since the second week of June.

It cost him two pounds. He paid it to a man with a table of old electrical things at a market four miles off, and the man told him it worked and it worked, and when he got it home he found his own name scratched on the bottom of the case in the middle of a rectangle where somebody else's name had been, in a nail, three letters and a stroke, and it is not a neat thing and it is not going to come off.

Nobody in that kitchen has asked him where the torch came from.

---

The man who cooks in it came in a little after eight that morning, which is late for him, and took the torch off the shelf and used it for about ten minutes looking in the back of a low cupboard and then said the thing about the bulb in the hall and went out, and handed it back at the door with the lens still warm.

At about nine a woman came in to eat her breakfast sitting down, which she does about twice a week, and she took the torch off the table and carried it through into the cold room at the back to see what was in the cold room, and put it back on the table on her way out with the switch on, and the bulb at the end of that torch is a yellow one now and not a white one and has been since the middle of July.

The man who cooks in it was still at that table at about half past nine and the torch was on the table by his elbow, and he turned it over and looked at the case and said that a man of about eighteen had bought a thing in a market for two pounds, and the woman said that two pounds is two pounds, and neither of them was talking to the lad, and he was at the sink with a cloth in his hand and heard every word of it.

At about midday the man who cooks in it came back for the thing he had said he would come back for, and the torch was standing on the top of the fridge with the switch on and had been standing there since about nine, and it was still there at about half past twelve and still had the switch on, and nobody in that kitchen has said one word to anybody about that either.

At ten past twelve the man who cooks in it said it.

"Take that out of my kitchen," he said. "I am not having a torch on my counter that belongs to a lad. It has been on my fridge since nine. If I go in there in a fortnight and it has been on that fridge since nine I will have it off and it will go in the bin and I will not be the one that has told you twice."

He took the torch off the fridge and the bucket with the water in it and he went out through that door and up the hill.

---

He goes up to that block by the water on Wednesdays as well, and has done since about the middle of June, and nobody in that building has ever asked him why he comes on a day that is not his, and there is no tap in that kitchen that runs and the water goes in a can.

The landing on that first floor is bare concrete underfoot and not much over the length of two doors, and the skirt along the bottom of the wall got one coat of paint on it in the last ten years and has had nothing since, and the window at the end of it is the one nobody has ever cleaned, and a bowl with a towel round the outside of it is standing under a strip of wet paper against the end wall, and he has not once said a word to the woman of about fifty-five about that bowl and she has not once said a word to him about it.

He had the torch in his left hand and the bucket in his right and he emptied the bucket into the outside gully at the bottom of that stair and came back up with the torch.

It lights about nine foot of that corridor in a yellow, and the bulb is going, and he stood in the middle of that landing with it and looked at nine foot of floor and then he did what he came up to do, which was to run the mop down the length of a landing that has had nothing on it since before Christmas.

He has run that mop every time he has been up there since Christmas, on a floor with nothing on it, and the reason he does it is that he does not know how anybody else would know that there was nothing on it, and he has never asked anybody in that building how that is worked, and nobody in that building has ever asked him why he does it, and the mop goes down the same concrete four times a month and comes back up wet and goes into a bucket and goes out again.

The woman of about fifty-five came out of the far room at about half past twelve with a jug in each hand and stopped, because the light from the end of that corridor was a yellow one and not a white one, and she has not seen that torch in that building before.

"That is a lamp," she said.

"It is a torch," said the lad. "I got that off a man in a market in June and it cost me two pound and there is my name scratched on the bottom of the case in a nail. It is not an old man's and it is not off the hall."

"Does it light."

"It lights about nine foot," said the lad, "and the bulb is going and it will not be a white one again."

"Right," said the woman of about fifty-five, and she went past him down that stair with both jugs and pulled the door to behind her at the bottom, and she did not say one word about a market four miles off or about a man with a table of old electrical things or about what a torch has to do with anything at all, and she did not ask him where he had it from, and she did nothing at all with anything he said.

He was back in that kitchen at about ten past one and the torch went on the shelf by the door upside down on the folded tea towel, and at about ten past two the woman of about forty-two came through that kitchen on her way to a hall off that parade with a bag of cups in her hand and said the light in that hall has gone and that she has not got a bulb and that she is not going up a ladder, and he took the torch off the shelf and gave it to her without a word and she took it and went, and she brought it back at about a quarter past two and put it on the table and said it had lit about ten foot of that hall and that the bulb in that hall wanted buying and not borrowing, and then she went.

Nobody asked him a single question about any part of that day.

The three letters and a stroke scratched in a nail on the bottom of that case are the only name there is anywhere on it, and they are not his initials and they are not anybody's he can answer to, and the man that scratched them there was selling sets out of the back of a van at a market in the June and had a nail in his pocket and did it to about nine things that morning.

The man who cooks in that kitchen has seen the bottom of that torch twice. He looked at it the first time it came off the shelf and he has not looked at it since, and if you had asked him in that kitchen in the last fortnight whose it was he would have said it was the lad's, and he would have been right, and that would have been the whole of what that kitchen has ever had to do with it.

**A TORCH WENT OUT OF A KITCHEN AT THE BACK OF A PARADE AND UPSTAIRS IN A BUILDING BY THE WATER AND DOWN A PARADE AND INTO A HALL AND BACK, AND A BULB IN A HALL OFF THAT PARADE IS STILL NOT BOUGHT.**
