# Chapter 0837 — The Kerbstone On Rubber

Thursday the seventeenth of August 2023, along the kerb at the lock-up from after rain until the stone has dried, and a kerbstone settled on rubber is the whole of that Thursday.

**THERE IS A KERBSTONE ON ITS RUBBER AT THE LOCK UP SETTLED AT ONE END AND SHE MEANS TO PACK UNDER IT WITHOUT LIFTING IT, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS IN THE OFFICE WITH THE DOOR SHUT BECAUSE THE DUST BLOWS IN OFF THE YARD.**

That kerb is the row of stones edging the hard ground by the gates, with two flat bars behind it and rubber folded under the low joints to keep the stones off the grit.

One stone has gone down at the far end by half a finger since spring. Lorries turning in catch the corner of it, and the rubber under that corner has squeezed out toward the yard, leaving the end sitting on grit.

She does not lift it. A kerbstone wants two people and bars, and bars under a stone on rubber want the rubber out first, and the rubber out wants the stone up. Packing from the toe will do for a settled end on tired ground.

She starts with a bucket of grit, a trowel, a short lath, and a brush.

The toe of the stone is the face toward the yard, where the ground falls away. She brushes the grit back from it until the rubber shows, black and folded, with the fold pressed flat where the weight has been.

Dust lies over everything from the morning rain and the traffic since, soft on the stone and soft on the bars behind it. She brushes it off the top of the stone and off the rubber lip, so the packing goes to clean surfaces and not to dust.

The office door stays shut through it. The shutter is up over the roller door, and behind the office door the woman who books work goes about her day, with the kettle going once and the drawer going twice, and no word through the wood except a chair pushed back at midday.

The door is shut because the wind off the yard carries dust straight in whenever a lorry turns, and the woman kept it shut in spring for the same reason and keeps it shut now. A shut door on a hot day is not a slight. It is dust.

She thinks about the stones while she packs, how each one takes lorries all day and asks for nothing except to sit true. The yard is tired ground, worked for decades, and the kerb is the edge of it that shows every fault first. Packing the toe is not mending the yard. It is keeping the edge honest while the middle goes on as before.

The mallet hand aches from the lath, and sweat runs off her forehead onto the stone and darkens it in spots that dry pale again within minutes.

She works grit under the low end with the lath, a spoonful at a time, pushing it back under the rubber and not under the stone. The rubber is the bed, and grit under the rubber raises the stone without touching the joint, and the joint stays as it was.

The first spoonfuls go nowhere. Grit pushed under rubber on tired ground spreads and sinks, and the stone takes nothing from it until the ground under the rubber firms.

She keeps at it. Grit in, lath back, thumb on the top of the stone to feel for movement, grit in again.

The test is a bar laid across from the next stone. Where the settled end sits low the bar rocks on the high one, and where it sits true the bar lies without moving.

After four rounds the bar lies with only a faint tick under one end. After six it lies dead.

She runs a finger along the joint between the two stones. The faces meet without a lip now, and the rubber lip at the toe sits tucked and square, with fresh grit packed to it in a neat slope.

She brushes the top of the stone clean and the bars behind it, and sweeps the surplus grit back into the bucket.

---

The bucket goes round the back, and the lath goes on the bench, and the bar goes against the wall where it lives.

She walks the kerb from one end to the other with a foot on every stone. None of the others give. The settled one sits with the rest now, taking its share.

She stands by the gates when the stone has dried and looks at what is there. A kerbstone sitting true on its rubber with fresh packing at its toe, and behind it the bars of the kerb, dark with rain and drying pale at the edges.
