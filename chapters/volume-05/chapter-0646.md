# Chapter 0646 — The Sixth Thing On It

Thursday the tenth of June 2021, in this borough. He is in a kitchen off the parade for about forty minutes in the middle of the afternoon, and there is a piece of paper on that table which he reads in about half a minute and does not ask one question about, and the whole of it is one bulb and a hotplate he has in the van and does not fit.

**A MAN WHO BUILDS THINGS COMES UP THAT PATH ON A WEDNESDAY WITH AN ELEMENT FOR A STOVE IN THE VAN, AND FINDS SIX THINGS WRITTEN ON AN ENVELOPE ON A KITCHEN TABLE FACE UP, AND READS THEM IN AN ORDER HE CAN SEE IS NOT A REPAIR ORDER AND CANNOT WORK OUT WHY, AND THE ONE QUESTION THAT WOULD SORT IT OUT IS THE ONE HE DOES NOT ASK, AND HE DOES ONE THING OFF THE LIST, THE LAST THING ON IT, AND LEAVES THE STOVE HE CAME FOR.**

He is a man in his early forties. She is a woman in her late seventies. They have been in that kitchen together twice.

---

Here is what he has in the van, and it is worth putting down because it is the pivot of the whole afternoon.

He has an element, and he has had it since before he went up that road.

Here is where it came from and it is not where anybody would want it to have come from.

He was in that kitchen on the Wednesday of the nineteenth of May for the best part of an hour. He was in it because a chair had come off its pin, not because anybody asked him about anything else, and he was on his knees under a window sill for a while with his back to that cooker, and when he stood up he looked at it the way he looks at everything in a kitchen, and he clocked the cooker, and the cooker is twenty years old and the left front element on that model is the first thing that goes.

That is all that has happened. No woman has told him about that hotplate. Nobody has rung him about it and nobody is going to.

It is in a box in the back of the van under a stack of offcuts and it was in there for another job that has not happened yet, and he took it out of that job's van in about ten seconds when he came away from that road and he has not thought about it since, which is not true, he has thought about it every day.

He has also got a bulb. It is a normal bulb and it is a five watt one and it has been in the door pocket of the van since the Friday before last, and he did not put it there for her, and he does not know why it is still in the van.

---

He comes up the path at about half past two on the Wednesday.

And here is the first thing about the afternoon and it is the whole of it, and he does not know it is the whole of it until he is on the way back down.

The road does not look like a road he has been on before. Not that anything has changed. What has changed is that he has now been up it four times and he can look at it from the top without stopping, and on this Wednesday he looks at it from the top and thinks, in the plainest way, *that house.*

Not number twelve. The row. The way the light comes off the wet render at about three in the afternoon in June, and the two gates that need easing, and a hedge that has not been done since the spring.

He thinks *that house* and he does not think *that woman*, and that is new, and he does not examine it.

---

He knocks. Two knocks and a pause and one, which is his own knock, and he has never used it before on that road, and there is something about a knock you have not used before that makes a person inside take longer.

She opens the door with her hand on it, because the latch on that door has never been on and has not been on since before Christmas, and he has never noticed and she has never thought about.

And she says, "Come in."

Not *come in, I've not got the table out of the way.* Something shorter. She has said it twice before in this borough and both of them were about the table.

---

She makes tea. He does not sit down at first and then he does, at the table, which is under the window with a cloth on it and a ring on the wood where a cup stood every day until the Thursday.

And the envelope is on the table.

Face up. Between them and the tin. It is a gas envelope and it has got writing on it, in pencil, about a hand's width of it in the middle.

He sees it when he puts his cup down.

---

And here is what happens, and it takes about half a minute, and after that afternoon he cannot get the half minute back.

He picks it up.

He does not ask for it. He does not say *may I*. He puts his hand out and picks a piece of paper off a table in a woman's kitchen, and there is a part of him that knows he should have said something, and he does not say anything, and he reads it.

It is face up. It has been face up the whole time he has been in this kitchen and the pencil is facing him, and he does not have to turn anything over.

And it says, on the side that is facing him, which is the side she wrote on and the side the gas bill is not on:

the gate

the front room

the window in the back room

the hotplate on the left

the bath tap

the passage light

---

And what happens in his head is not a thought. It is a thing that happens to a man who does this for a living, in a kitchen, in about three seconds, and it is that he has read an order that has been written by somebody who cannot do it.

You do not do the passage light before you have looked at the hotplate. You do not put a room that is sound on a list of things at all. And the gate is not a job, the gate is a gate.

He reads the gate twice.

---

And the thing that he does not do, and could have done, and it is the only interesting thing he does all afternoon, is ask her.

*What order do you want these in.*

Four words. It is the question you ask a person who has given you a list. He has asked it perhaps a hundred times. It takes a second. It is the easiest question in his trade.

And he does not ask it.

And the reason he does not ask it is that the answer is going to be about the gate, and he can see from where he is sitting that the gate is not a gate.

---

She watches him read it.

She has her cup in both hands. She does not put it down. She watches a man in a jumper pick up a piece of paper off her table and turn it over and read it twice from the top, and she does not say a word, and there is about half a minute in that kitchen where a woman of seventy-eight is watching a man read six things she wrote in the middle of the morning and she is not saying anything at all, and he is not saying anything at all, and neither of them is going to.

And he puts it back down on the table.

He puts it back where it was, and he squares the edge of it with the edge of the table, and it goes down face up the way it was face up, and he has not turned it over.

---

And then he says, "How long's the blanket been on that window."

And she says, "Which window."

And he says, "The back one. You've a blanket over it. There's a draught coming off that wall you could feel from the door."

And she says, "Since before Christmas."

And he says, "Right."

And he does not say anything else about the window for the rest of that afternoon, and she does not either, and it is the longest either of them goes without saying a word in a kitchen like this.

---

And here is the side of that house, and he is in the doorway of the kitchen when he sees it.

There is no path round the side. There is grass, and it has been walked flat in a line about a foot wide from the kitchen door to the coal shed and then on round to the gate, and it is the first rain on that side in a while that has left the grass standing up again at the edges of it.

He looks at that for longer than he looks at anything else in that house.

And what he does with it is nothing. He is a tradesman and he is not going to say *you've no path* to a woman of seventy-eight who has not asked him, and he is not going to say it because he has just read six things off her table and every one of them was a thing she had decided on by herself, and a man who says *you've no path* is a man who has told her what to do with her own garden.

He puts it away. He will think about it for about a month and it will turn into something he does on a Wednesday, and he will not remember the grass.

---

Here is what he does between about three o'clock and about ten past three.

He does the passage light.

He had the bulb in the door pocket of the van and he gets it, and the holder in the passage is one of the old screw ones and the old one has been gone since before Christmas and has been in a saucer on the window sill in the back room for about a month, and he knows that, because she tells him, and he does not ask how it got there.

He fits the bulb. It takes him about four minutes. It is the sixth thing on the list and he does it in four minutes and it is the only thing he does.

He does not do the hotplate. He has the element in the van, in a box, under a stack of offcuts, and he has had it there since the Tuesday.

And here is the reason, and he is honest about it with himself on the way down the path and he is still not honest about it inside the van.

It is not that the element is difficult. It is that he could not do it in ten minutes and go.

If he fits an element in that stove, then there is a box out of the van in that kitchen, and a man on his knees in that kitchen, and he is there for two hours, and at the end of two hours he is standing in a front room with a woman of seventy-eight and two people who have both been told something different about him.

And on a bench on the parade he agreed with a woman in her late forties that they would not put their knowledge together in that room.

And that agreement was about a chair and a stool.

And the hotplate is not a chair and a stool.

And he does the light and leaves the stove, and what he has done is put a thing off a list so that he has an excuse to have been in the room, and he will not be able to explain that to himself in a straight sentence for about a week.

---

She stands in the doorway of the kitchen while he is in the passage with the steps.

And she says, "You've not looked at the stove."

And he says, "I'll come back."

And she says, "Right."

And he says, "I'll do it when I've got a morning."

And she says, "You don't have to do that."

And he says, "I know."

And there is about four seconds of a kitchen and a passage where two people who have both said something that is not what they meant are standing a door apart.

---

He goes at about ten past three.

And she does not thank him and he does not expect it, and what she says at the door is the thing that she has been building all afternoon and has not decided to say until she opens the door.

And she says, "It's not on there because I couldn't be doing with it."

And he says, "The bulb."

And she says, "The bulb."

And he says, "Right."

And she says, "No. The other thing."

And he looks at her.

And he says, "I wasn't going to ask you."

And she says, "I know you weren't."

And then he is on the path and the gate goes behind him and neither of them says one word about what that was about.

---

He sits in the van at about a quarter past three with the engine off and does not go straight down the road.

The element is where it was, under the offcuts, in a box the size of a shoebox, and he has known it was there all afternoon without looking at it once, and he puts his hand on the box and takes it out and puts it on the passenger seat where he can see it.

And then he does the thing that tells him what kind of man he is, which is that he takes a pencil off the parcel shelf and writes one letter on the box.

*M.*

That is all. No date, because he does not date a box like this and has never dated one, and because a date on that box would be the first thing about it that anybody could use.

And he puts the box back under the offcuts, and starts the engine, and goes down to the parade, and does not turn up that road again until the Wednesday after.

---

And after he has gone she goes back into the kitchen and looks at the table.

The envelope is on the table in the middle, face up, where it was.

It was not square like that when she wrote it. It was not square like that when he picked it up. It is square with the edge of the table now, and she can see from where she is standing that it has been done on purpose by somebody who did not have to.

And there is a passage light on.

---

And she stands in her own kitchen and looks at six lines of pencil on a gas envelope, and one of the six has been done, and nothing has been crossed off.

**A MAN READ SIX THINGS OFF A TABLE IN ABOUT HALF A MINUTE AND COULD HAVE ASKED THE ONE QUESTION THAT SORTS THEM AND DID NOT ASK IT, AND HE STOOD THERE AND DID THE LAST ONE ON THE LIST IN FOUR MINUTES AND LEFT THE ONE HE CAME FOR, AND THE ENVELOPE IS ON THAT TABLE FACE UP AND SQUARE WITH THE EDGE OF IT, AND ONE THING OFF THAT LIST HAS BEEN DONE AND NOTHING HAS BEEN CROSSED OFF.**

(End of chapter 0646)
