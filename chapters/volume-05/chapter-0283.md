# Chapter 0283 — Five Rooms

Tuesday the twenty-seventh of December 2016, from about half past eight in the morning, a parade of shops in Tideglass, a kitchen, a house in Corrance Lane, a hall, and a first-floor room at the end of a parade.

**HE WAS IN FIVE ROOMS THAT DAY AND IN ONE OF THEM SOMEBODY PUT SOMETHING IN HIS HAND, AND HE PUT IT IN THE POCKET OF HIS CARDIGAN, AND HE DID NOT ASK ANYBODY WHAT IT WAS FOR.**

The kitchen at the back of the parade had about nine plates in the sink at half past eight on the Tuesday morning, which is an ordinary morning, and the machine that heats the water had a timer on it that nobody has ever reset, and the clock on that wall said ten to nine when it was about a quarter to nine.

He was there from half past seven. He is on the dishes and that is a thing he does for money and it is the only thing he does.

There was nobody else in that kitchen. The woman he washes up for had gone at six on the Friday and would not be back until the fourth of January, and the machine that heats the water has a timer on it that nobody has ever reset, and the clock on that wall is still nine minutes fast.

He washed the plates. The hot water went off at about nine because of the timer and he finished the last of them in cold.

---

At about ten he was on the floor of the shop that shuts early, because the shop does the breakfast and the room at the back has a door in it that comes through into the shop, and when the breakfast is over the two of them put the chairs up together and he is the one who does the chairs.

There were four people in that shop at that hour and three of them were talking about Christmas and one of them asked him whether he was doing anything on Friday night and he said he did not know yet, and the woman who asked him said there was a do at the Methodist hall on Carrow Lane with a band on, and that they were all going, and he said that was all right.

He carried four chairs through and stacked them against the wall by the door and put the mop in the bucket and carried the bucket out to the passage, and nobody told him what to do next and he went and did the next thing himself, and the next thing was the floor of the passage, and after that it was his own time, and he sat down on an upturned crate in that passage with his cardigan over his knees for the best part of a quarter of an hour.

At about half past one he was in a kitchen in Corrance Lane.

A woman he has washed up for twice had a family in and had asked him at the shop on the Friday whether he would come and do the washing-up on the Sunday and the Monday, and she paid him the same as she paid the woman who usually came, which was not very much and was a real figure, and he had asked for it to be the same as the other one and it was.

There were about fourteen plates and a good deal besides them, and two people at that table asked the woman who he was and she said the boy who does the dishes, and nobody else in that house said one word to him from the time he came in to the time he went.

He had the last of it done by about ten to three and put it away and washed his hands and his hands were cracked across the knuckles and had been since about November, and he stood at that sink for a second with his hands in the hot water and then dried them on his sleeve because the towel in that kitchen is not a towel.

At about half past seven he was in a hall off a parade where there is a meter and about seven people and a jug of tea, and the young woman of about twenty was in it with her coat still on and had come off the bus.

There is a radiator under that window that has been cold since the end of the last century, and nobody was going to be the one who put it on, and they were doing the price of glass and a door at the top of a street and a bank holiday that had been cancelled for a reason nobody could establish.

At about twenty past eight, when she was putting her coat over the back of a chair, the young woman of about twenty took something out of the bottom of her bag and put it on the table by his cup without saying anything at all about it.

They were a pair of gloves, wool, grey, and they had no fingers in them, and the wool had gone a bit shiny at the tips of the thumb and the heel from being in a bag, and there was a paper band still on one of them.

"They're not much use," she said. "You'll not want them."

He looked at them.

"You don't have to say anything about it," said the young woman of about twenty, and went back to her chair.

He picked them up and turned one over and looked at the fingers that were not there, and then he did not put them on, and he did not put them back on the table, and he put them in the pocket of his cardigan, which was over the back of his own chair, and the pocket took them.

"There was a woman in a shop in August," he said, "who wanted a pair of the proper ones, and I said the proper ones were no good for washing up, and she wanted the proper ones anyway."

"What did you do."

"I said no and she went out and got them somewhere else," said the man of about eighteen.

He did not ask her what they were for. He washed the rest of that day's things out of the pocket before he went, because the pocket of that cardigan is not a place a thing can stay in and be dry.

At about half past nine he was in a first-floor room over a shop at the end of a parade, where about six of them play cards on a weeknight, because a warm room in this part of the town is the one that is free, and it is a mile from the hall off the parade and the church wants paying for. A man in his thirties keeps it. He opens the door on a Tuesday and shuts it on a Wednesday, and nothing is written down, and he rings nobody to say which.

He was let in by a man who said come in if you're stopping, and he stopped, and he played one hand and then watched three more, and he was not in the second game because he had not brought anything to put in it, and nobody at that table asked him one single question about himself all evening, and one of them asked him whether the shop was doing a Christmas on and he said it was doing a breakfast and that was all that was happening.

He got out at about half past ten and walked down the parade in the rain past the shutter that had come down at one and past the bank, and he had his hands in the pockets of his cardigan, and the pair of gloves were in the left one, and he did not have them on.

**AND ON THE WEDNESDAY MORNING THERE WERE ABOUT NINE PLATES IN THE SINK AT THE BACK OF THAT PARADE, AND THE PAIR OF GLOVES WERE IN THE LEFT POCKET OF A GREY CARDIGAN THAT WAS OVER THE BACK OF A CHAIR, AND ON THE THURSDAY AFTERNOON HE TOOK THEM OUT OF THAT POCKET AT THE TOP OF A STAIR AND PUT THEM ON FOR THE LENGTH OF A CUP OF TEA AND TOOK THEM OFF AGAIN, AND ON THE FRIDAY THEY WERE IN THE SAME POCKET AND THEY WERE DRY.**
