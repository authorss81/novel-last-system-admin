# Chapter 1033 — The Top Of A Shelf With Nothing On It

Undated in this draft.

**THERE IS A SHELF ACROSS THAT FLOOR AND THE DOOR OF IT STANDS OPEN BEHIND.**

That little store is about six foot by five, it opens off that yard, and nothing in it is glazed at eye level. The high window up in the top corner is a short one, about a foot square, and it lights the middle of that floor and about two foot of that shelf and nothing else. The shelf is put across it on two uprights of four by two with the boards running the length of it, and there is nine inches of clear floor between the end of it and the wall on the left-hand side, and nothing is standing on those boards at all. Not a tin, not a bottle, not a broom, not a coil of anything. The dust along the top of those boards has not been moved since before the summer except at the front edge, where there is a groove about the width of a finger where something heavy was dragged along it once and never again.

He was in there on the rule that a man who has taken money has to go and find somebody to tell, and the one door in this borough standing open on a builder's yard is the door of that store. She was not in that office. He had given his name once in a shut bay at the far end of that ground and it went on nothing whatever and he has not stopped hearing about it since.

He put his hand along the top edge of that shelf and ran his fingers the length of it, and what he got was dust and that one groove where something heavy had been dragged along it, and the question he came in there with came out answered, and the answer is that nowhere in this borough is there a second one of anything to put a name in.

She came through the door of that store on her way across to that office, and she did not stop, and then she did.

"You are stood in my little store."

"I am."

"That is the first room in that whole yard to have had anybody in it since before the summer, and there is a plate and a barrow in one of the other three that a man walks into every week of his life."

"Then I am the first one to be in it since the spring, which is why I am in it."

"Then you have come in here to stand in a room with nobody in it, which is what you did in the bay at the gate end the last time I saw you, and you put something into words to me then that has gone on nothing at all here."

"I have."

"I have not forgotten it either, and you can take it from me that I have not."

"I have started."

"You have not."

"A woman has put money into my hand out of her own. I have made the tray. There is not one stone or one brick of mine in my own ground yet and starting amounts to that at the moment, and I have come to tell you because you are the only person in this borough who keeps anything, and I am not going to have you hear a piece of this from anybody else first."

"You have come into that store to tell me that a woman has paid you out of her own pocket."

"Yes."

"I am not going to have that."

"It is not work."

"I know it is not work. That is exactly why I am not having it." She came the length of that store and put her shoulder against the frame of the open door with her coat still on. "I have turned down four men at that desk since the season shut and I turned a fifth one down in this yard for the same reason, and you are the sixth, and the reason has not altered in a fortnight and it is not going to alter because a woman has paid you. A column takes work that somebody asked a house for. Nobody has asked me for one thing of any of it. You came in here on your own two hands and you are standing in my store telling me about it on your own two hands, and I cannot put that on anything in this building."

"Then put nothing on it."

"I am putting nothing on it and I am not going to and I would like you to go back out of that door now please, because I have four men coming into this yard this week to be asked about a job a house has sent for, and every one of them came because a house asked them to come."

He did not argue with her about any part of it. He had come in there with something in his mouth and she had taken it off him in six sentences and he stood in the dust of that store and did not put one word back, and what he was stood there with was the plainest refusal he has ever been handed in this borough by a woman who has no reason to hand him anything at all, and he has had four refusals off that woman since that season shut, and all four went the same way, which is that a person stood in a room not knowing what she was paying for.

"You have been in this borough long enough and you have never once put a thing in front of me that was not work, and now you have come into a store where there is nothing on the shelf and told me about your own pocket. Do you know what the trouble with that is."

"Tell me."

"That it is decent. That is all of the trouble with it. If you had come up here this morning and asked me for work, I would have had to look at what is on that desk and think about a season, and I would have turned you down and been right to do it. You have brought me something nobody asked for, and I cannot put it anywhere, and there is not one thing in this building I can do about it, and you will walk out of here thinking I turned you down when in fact I have nowhere to put you."

"Then it is the same answer twice."

"It is not, and the part you are not getting out of me is this. Turning a man down for work is the ordinary trade. Turning one down for something nobody asked for is the first I have had to do since you came to this borough, and I have not had to do it to a person who was owed a thing. That is not a favour and I am not going to stand in a doorway and dress it up as one."

"You asked me a question the other morning."

"I did not ask you anything."

"You asked me for my name and I gave it to you in the dark and you would not put it down and you gave me four sentences about why, and I have thought about those four sentences every day since, and I have come here this morning to ask you one thing in return, and I am asking it badly."

"Go on."

"What is it for."

---

She did not answer him straight away and he understood that she had not asked a question of anybody in that yard in a long time and that she was not certain what to do with one now it had come.

"You put a thing in my mouth and expect me to hand you something back for it."

"Yes."

"That is the arrangement you have been arranging against for ten years, and you have just walked into a store and done it."

"That is what I have done."

"Nothing goes onto anything in that building," she told him, "and you are leaving the rest of it alone, because you do not know either, and if you did know you would have brought it in here with a date on it."

Nothing in that store is going onto anything in that building, and she stayed in that doorway with her hand on the frame until he came out past her into the yard, and he went off up that frontage with a question sitting in his mouth that he had no answer to at all, nor has he any idea yet what a man does with an answer he is given for nothing.
