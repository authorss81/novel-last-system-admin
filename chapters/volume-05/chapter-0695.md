# Chapter 0695 — The Bowl Under The Tap

Monday the tenth of January 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number twelve from first light until dark and the whole of the day is a bowl under a tap in the next room and a shut door with a folded ladder behind it.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT EMPTIES A BOWL UNDER THE BATH TAP IN THE MORNING AND AGAIN AT DARK, STANDS IN THE DOORWAY OF A FRONT ROOM SHE HAS NOT SAT IN SINCE JULY AND LOOKS AT A FOLDING STEP LADDER STANDING FOLDED AGAINST THE WALL UNDER THE WINDOW AND DOES NOT GO IN, AND IS SPOKEN TO AT HER OWN BACK DOOR BY A LAD WITH A NOTE ABOUT A METER AND SAYS SHE CANNOT HELP HIM AND ASKS HIM NOTHING.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight. She is on her own all day except for a lad at her own back door in the morning. She speaks to him there.

---

The bowl is under the tap before there is any light in the passage.

She goes through the kitchen in her coat with the apron over it and into the end room where the bath is under the landing and the landing is over the front, and the cloth is folded in the bowl and the bowl is about half full, and she lifts it out with both hands and puts it in the bath and tips it and rinses it and puts the cloth back and sets the bowl down again under the tap.

The tap drips.

It has dripped since before Christmas. It drips off the spout and lands in the cloth and spreads, and the bowl takes what the cloth cannot take, and she empties it at first light and at dark because those are the times she is in that room.

She does not write it down. There is no pencil in the house. The gas envelope is on the right of the kitchen table, face up, square with the edge, pencil side up, six things on the back of it in pencil and nothing crossed off, and the second thing on it is the front room.

---

She goes back to the kitchen and puts her hands round a cup.

The kitchen is at the back. The table is under the window. The green chair is at the table with the cushion on it and she sits on it because it is the chair she sits on, and the table is dead flat where the window-side foot was planed and sawn and shimmed and put back in dry card joint with no glue, and a tradesman could see it at six feet.

On the left of that table there is a taped box the size of two loaves with a cloth over it. The cloth is dry on top and damp under where the mark was. The label on the side of it has gone soft at the corners and the gummed forwarding strip over the first label carries a street she has never been to and a number that is not twelve.

On the right of that table there is the envelope.

She squares the cloth over the box without lifting it. She does not open the box. She has not opened it since it came and she is not opening it on a Monday in January.

---

The passage light is on.

It has been on since June. It runs the whole length of the runner and shows the wire brush in the middle of the runner with the handle across the walking line, unwashed since the first of September. She steps over it, right foot then left, the way she always does, and goes to the front room door.

The front room is between the door and the kitchen. Washing her hands goes through the room she will not sit in. She does not sit in it. She has not sat in it since the sixteenth of July.

She stands in the doorway and does not go in.

---

The folding step ladder is against the wall under the window.

It is folded. It has four treads and a tray on top and a strip of dark tape round the second rail and a chalk mark on the third tread, and it is standing on its folded feet with the tray end up, squared to the edge of the floorboards the way the woman who carried it squared it on the seventeenth of December.

There is a rush chair against the wall by the window and nobody sits in it. There is a stool she stood on in the summer. There is a sideboard with nothing on it. There is a pale patch on the ceiling just inside the door with a defined edge.

She looks at the ladder and at the patch and at the chair.

You cannot paint round a ladder and you cannot move it and you cannot ask anybody to take it away, and those are three facts and they are the same fact. The front room is the only one of the two undone that could now be started and cannot be finished.

She shuts the door. She shuts it every time she is in it.

---

The stove warms and does not boil.

She puts the kettle on it and waits, and while it waits she goes to the back room and looks at the window. The tape is on the blanket and the blanket is on the mattress in the top corner and the tape was re-taped. The room is cold. She does not stay in it.

In the apron pocket there are two printed cards with the same free number on them. The report is with a freeholder who has not arrived. Four of six is still four of six. The front door is not on the latch and has not been since before Christmas.

She takes the kettle off before it sings.

---

The knock at the back is not the front.

It is a knock on the back door frame, short, the way a person knocks who does not want to be heard far. She goes through the kitchen with the cup in her hand and looks through the glass.

There is a lad on the step. He is in his late teens with a bag over his shoulder and a folded note in his hand, and he is cold.

She opens the door a crack. The cold comes in round it.

"Morning," he says.

"Morning."

"I've got a note for the meter," he says. He lifts the note up. "They sent me round."

"What meter."

"The leccy meter. In the house. I've to read it."

She looks at him. The key to the coal shed is on a hook behind her own back door. The padlock hangs open in the hasp on the coal shed door and the shed has been shut since the twenty-second of November and was opened and shut again on the twenty-first of December. The gate stands back against the coal shed wall since the first of September with two drop bolts in her apron pocket.

None of that is a meter.

---

"I've no meter you can read," she says.

"You've got one somewhere," he says. "They all have."

"Mine's not one you read," she says, which is true and not true, and she knows it as she says it. "There's nothing in here for you."

He looks past her into the kitchen. He can see the table and the cloth and the envelope and the window. He cannot see the front room. The front room door is shut.

"Is that the front room," he says, looking at the shut door across the kitchen.

"It is."

"Is there anybody in it."

"No."

He nods at the note in his hand as though the note will do something the talking has not done.

"They said to ask," he says.

"Ask what."

"Ask if I can come in and look."

She stands with her hand on the door. She is seventy-eight years old and she cannot ask anybody for anything since before Christmas, and what that means on a Monday morning is that she cannot ask this lad who he is and cannot ask him for a card and cannot ask him to come back with somebody who knows the house.

And he does not ask her anything that would matter. He does not ask her about the bowl in the next room. He does not ask her about the box under the cloth. He does not ask her about the ladder folded in the front room, because he cannot see it and she has not named it.

---

"You can't come in," she says. "I'm sorry."

"Right," he says.

"It's not that I won't," she says. "I've got nothing you can read and I've nobody to stand with you while you look. That's the whole of it."

He puts the note back in his bag. He is not put out. He has a round of these and this is one door on it.

"Will you be in in the spring," he says.

"In the spring," she says, and puts no day on it, because she has no day to put on it and because the spring is the place where work goes that cannot be done now. "You'll want to try then."

"Right," he says again.

---

He goes down the yard and out through the gateway. The lamp on the bracket over her gate was relamped on the twenty-fourth of December and is still on, though it is morning. He goes under it without looking at it.

She shuts the back door. It shuts true. She stands in the kitchen with her hand flat on it until his steps are gone.

Then she goes back to the table and sits on the green chair. The cloth over the box wants squaring and she squares it. It is a small thing and she does it properly, corner to edge, the way she does everything on that table.

The bowl in the next room drips into the cloth. She can hear it from the table if the house is quiet. It is a small sound and it has been there since before Christmas and it will be there at dark when she empties it again.

---

At the middle of the day she eats at the table.

She eats facing the window with the cushion behind her back. She does not eat in the front room. The stool in there is where she stood in the summer to reach the high corner. The sideboard has nothing on it. The ladder is where the woman of about thirty put it and squared it, and it has not been opened out and it has not been moved and she did not go in after it.

She washes up and puts the cloth on the drainer. The tap in the kitchen runs and stops. The tap in the next room drips and does not stop.

A repair with no date on it is not a repair. She has known that since December. The bath tap wants a washer and the front room wants paint and the ladder wants hands, and none of them has any date because any date would mean asking, and she cannot ask.

---

At dark she goes back to the end room.

The bowl is about half full again. The cloth is sodden. She lifts it out with both hands and tips it into the bath and rinses it and puts it back. The cistern weeps at the far side of a joist in a roof space nobody has entered since the spring, and the reason she cannot go up the fixed four rungs screwed to the wall beside the hatch is not the dark and not the top, it is that there is nobody to keep the frame while she does.

She comes back down the passage and steps over the brush. The passage light is on. The front room door is shut. She puts her hand flat on it for a moment without opening it, the way a person touches a door to know it is shut.

Then she goes to the kitchen and sits until the lamp over the gate shows through the window.

The ladder is still folded against the wall under the window in the front room. The box is still taped on the left of the table under the cloth. The envelope is still square on the right. Four of six is still four of six.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS EMPTIED A BOWL UNDER A DRIPPING TAP AT FIRST LIGHT AND AT DARK, HAS STOOD IN THE DOORWAY OF A FRONT ROOM AND LOOKED AT A FOLDING STEP LADDER STANDING FOLDED AGAINST THE WALL UNDER THE WINDOW AND HAS NOT GONE IN, HAS TOLD A LAD AT HER OWN BACK DOOR THAT HE CANNOT COME IN AND HAS ASKED HIM NOTHING AND BEEN ASKED NOTHING ABOUT THE LADDER, AND HAS SAID IN THE SPRING AND PUT NO DAY ON IT.**
