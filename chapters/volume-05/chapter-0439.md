# Chapter 0439 — Six Lengths Down

Friday the seventh of September 2018, and the whole of it, a boundary along a ditch at the end of a road off that parade, in Tideglass.

**A MAN IN HIS THIRTIES PUT UP SIX LENGTHS OF FENCE THAT HAD BEEN LYING ACROSS A FOOTPATH FOR ABOUT A YEAR, WITH HIS OWN POSTS AND HIS OWN WIRE AND HIS OWN TIME, AND HE ASKED NOBODY FOR ANY OF IT AND HE CHARGED NOBODY FOR IT AND HE HAS NOT MENTIONED IT TO ONE PERSON, AND THE OTHER MAN WALKED PAST IT AT FOUR IN THE AFTERNOON AND WORKED OUT WHO HAD DONE IT AND SAID NOTHING.**

The boundary is at the back of a piece of ground that has been doing nothing since before he came to this town. There is a path along it about four foot wide, grass in the middle and a rut on the left-hand side where people walk when they are not looking down, and beyond the boundary a ditch about two foot deep that has not been cleared since the road was cut.

The fence is a boundary fence. It is not anybody's in particular. Nobody has been on it since April of last year, and what stopped then was a man coming out on a Thursday with a brush and a can, and the six lengths went down in the second week of May and have been lying ever since.

They are down flat in the ditch and across the path. The posts are still standing in the ground with nothing on them. The lengths are galvanised and the galvanising has gone white in patches where the two of them have been lying in wet grass, and there is about a foot of nettles grown through the bottom third of the middle one.

The man in his thirties has been going past that path at about half past six in the morning for three years, in the dark, on the way to a place off Corrance Lane, and he has put his foot in the grass where they lie four or five times a year, and in June he stood still on one of them at about ten past six in the morning and worked out that it was fence under his foot.

That was the whole of the thinking. It took about four seconds.

---

He did it in one day, on that Friday, and he started at about ten past eight in the morning, because a man who does that has to start while it is dry enough to dig.

He has his own materials and they were in his van, and he did not hire them and he did not buy them. Six posts of the same section as the ones already in. Thirty foot of wire. A pack of staples. A claw hammer, a spade, a bar, and a length of wood about four foot long that he used to drive the staples in.

He dug the six holes. He got them down about two foot, which is below where the grass roots stop and into the clay, and he rammed the bottoms of them hard with the length of wood and stood on it. He did not loosen the ground around them and put the posts back in, which is what a man does when he is in a hurry and what makes a fence lean in a few years.

He took the old lengths up out of the ditch one at a time and washed them at the standpipe at the top of the road, which took him longer than anything else he did that day. He cut the heads off the old nails that were standing up out of them with the claw end of the hammer, so that nobody walking that path in the dark puts a hand on a length and opens a hand on a nail end. There were about fifty of them and he cut every one.

He ran the wire along the top and stapled it, and then he set each length back where it had been, and he did not have to guess, because the posts were still in the ground and the marks on them were still there.

By about half past two in the afternoon the six lengths were back on their own posts at their own heights, and the path was clear from end to end.

He put the turf back over each hole in pieces, the right way up, and pressed it down with the back of the spade, and went over the whole line twice and made good where the pieces did not meet.

There is nothing on it. No name, no number, no notice, no paint, nothing tied to a post, and the turf he put back is a slightly different green from the grass either side of it for about six weeks and then it is not.

---

He has been asked nothing about it. Nobody saw him do it, because he started at ten past eight and finished at half past two, and there is nothing on that ground until six in the evening.

He has not told the man he works with, who would say it was a waste of a day. He has not told the woman at the shop, who would say it was kind. He has not told anybody, and he has not gone back over it in a way that would show anybody he had, except that he has gone that way four mornings since and looked at the line from the other side of the ditch. It is straight. The posts are plumb. The wire is tight.

If he is honest about the reason, and he has been, walking it over about four times a day for a fortnight now, the honest answer is that there is no reason he can put into a sentence. It is not his fence. It is not his path. Nobody asked. Nobody who could be told cares. He was the only man who was going to, and he did not want it to still be lying in the grass in December, and that is not a reason, and he knows it is not a reason, and he is not going to go round the ring looking for a better one and give it to anybody.

The man in his forties walked that path at about four in the afternoon, going the other way, with a bag in his hand.

He saw a fence where there had been no fence on the Wednesday.

He stopped. He came back four paces and looked at it properly. He looked at the line of the posts and the depth of the turf over the holes, which had been cut and put back and pressed, and at the tops of the lengths, which did not need a wire at all and had got one anyway.

He put the bag down on the grass and stood there for a while.

He worked out that one man had done it, alone, in one day, with his own money, and that the man had not wanted his name on it, because there is nothing on it. And he worked out which man it was, because there is only one man he knows in this borough who does that sort of thing alone, and he has watched him do two other things of that sort in a year, and neither of them had his name on them either.

And then he picked the bag up and went on down the path to his van.

He has not mentioned it. He and that man have said about a dozen sentences to each other in all of the three years, and every one of them was on the last morning of August and about a stump, and they have not seen each other since, and there is no reason they should start now, and if one of them brought it up the other one would have to decide on the spot what his face was doing, and neither of them has had to decide anything.

**SIX LENGTHS ARE BACK ON SIX POSTS ALONG A DITCH AND TWO MEN KNOW WHAT THAT MEANS AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS PUT IT TO ANYBODY ELSE.**
