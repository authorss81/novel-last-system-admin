# Chapter 0693 — He Could Write His Own Number On The Other Side

Monday the third of January 2022, in this borough. He is at the unit in the morning and in a manky kitchen from late morning until dark, and the whole of the day is a step ladder that has been in a house at the end of a road since the seventeenth of December and a top run of wall units a quarter of an inch out.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES IS ASKED FOR A STEP LADDER BY THE MAN AT THE BENCH OPPOSITE AND SAYS HE HAS LENT HIS OUT AND DOES NOT SAY TO WHOM AND DOES NOT SAY WHEN IT IS COMING BACK, LEAVES A TOP RUN OF KITCHEN UNITS A QUARTER OF AN INCH OUT BECAUSE HE HAS HAD TO SIGHT IT FROM A STOOL, HAS A PIECE OF SCRIBING CARD IN HIS POCKET WITH TWELVE WRITTEN ON ONE SIDE AND ROOM ON THE OTHER AND A PENCIL BEHIND HIS EAR, AND DOES NOT WRITE ON IT.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is on his own all day except for a man at the bench opposite and a woman in her own kitchen.

---

It comes first thing, before the radio goes on, which is how he knows it is going to be the whole of the day.

"You've not got a ladder."

"I've not got a ladder."

"You had one before Christmas."

"I've not got a ladder now."

The man at the bench opposite has a length of oak on the bench in front of him with a plane and a shooting board and a card with a pencil line on it, and he does not look up, and there is nothing in the way he is sitting that is asking anything.

"You've lent it out."

"Aye."

"Right."

That is the exchange. The man at the bench opposite does not ask who and does not ask when, and he goes back to the oak, and there is a radio on low at that bench with the talk on it that neither of them follows, and it is a Monday in January and the man at the bench opposite has been in that building every working day since before the summer came.

---

He gets to the unit before the rest of it and stands in the doorway of his own bay with the strip light on and the extractor going and does not go in yet.

The shoebox is on the top of his own bench where he cannot come in without seeing it, and the lid is on it, and the element is down inside in a bed of screwed-up newspaper, and it has been on that bench since the third of August.

The drawer under the bench is shut. It has been shut since the sixteenth of November and nothing has gone into it and nothing has come out of it, and the new brush is in a plastic bag flat along the back of it and the nine pounds he paid for it in the summer is still the only money he has ever put into that van out of his own pocket for anybody.

The stack of offcuts goes from the front bulkhead back to about the middle of that bay. The leads are along the other side with the dust sheets folded on top of them, the way they have been since June.

The space behind the offcuts is a folded step ladder's wide. It has been that shape since the summer. It is that shape now. There is nothing in it.

---

He stands there for a bit.

He is not working out whether to go and get it. There is no version of Monday morning in which he drives to a builder's merchant on the ring road and asks a man in there whether a woman he spoke to in a street on the twenty-ninth of November left a step ladder at a tip he has a key to, because the answer in there would be no and the answer at the tip would be the merchant saying he does not know a thing about it either.

What he is working out is the other one, which is the one he could actually do.

---

He has a piece of scribing card in the shirt pocket of the coat on the back of the cab door.

It is the corner of a card about the size of a playing card and there is TWELVE on it in his own writing in a scribing pencil, and he wrote it at a tip off the ring road on the sixteenth of November because there was a taped box on the top of that heap with a street on it that he has gone past and a number on it that is a house at the end of a road, and he read a name off the other half of that label and put the box back down the way it was and folded a bit of bag over its top corner with his own tape.

There is room on the other side of that card.

He has got a pencil behind his ear. He could write a number on it and drive it down to the ring road and put it in the merchant's hand and the merchant would put it on a spike by the door, and if a woman in a white van ever went in and asked him about a ladder he would give it to her and she would ring him and he would have his ladder.

That is the whole of the thing, and it is a pencil and a card and a drive and a conversation with a merchant who knows him.

He does not do it.

---

He has a very good reason and he is not going to say it out loud to anybody, and the reason is this:

The moment he writes a number on that card, that card is a thing he has given to somebody, and TWELVE is a thing he is doing something about, and he has not been up that road since the seventeenth of June and has gone past the end of it twice and stopped once and put his hand on a door handle once and taken it off again, and there is a woman at the end of that road who cannot ask anybody for anything, and a gate that stands open against a coal shed wall, and a tank in a roof space that nobody has ever been in, and a woman in a kitchen with her back to the glass.

He has done that twice now. He has put a card in a pocket and left it in a pocket. He has a number and a pocket and neither of them is a plan and both of them are better than a plan would be, because a plan is a thing that has a day on it and this has not got a day on it and can therefore not turn into one of those Sunday nights where a man is sitting in a kitchen wondering whether he was right.

He puts the card back in the pocket and picks up the router.

---

The house is off the ring road and it is a manky kitchen and the woman is about sixty-five and is in and out all day, and the job is a run of wall units with a pelmet over the top of them, and the ceiling in that room is eight foot and the top of that run is at seven foot four.

He has a packing bench, the little steplool he made himself out of two offcuts in the summer, and a stub of a folding steplool that came off a skip in the summer. None of the three is four foot and none of the three is going to be.

He does the bottom run on the bench at about a foot and a half and the middle run standing on the floor, and the top run and the pelmet off the steplool, which puts the top of his head at about six foot and his hands at about six foot eight, and he has to work above his own reach for the last four inches of it, and the only way to do that properly is to have a look at it and then do it, and you cannot have a look at a pelmet from a steplool.

He does it anyway, and it takes him most of the day, and it is not terrible.

---

The woman comes in about the middle of the afternoon with a cup of tea she puts on the worktop.

"Will you be long?"

"I've a bit of the top run to do."

"You're on that stool."

"Aye."

"Is that all you've got?"

"I've got a stool."

She looks at the stool for a bit.

"My son does that ceiling in the hall," she says. "He had a thing off a mate and he put a board on the joists and he did the whole ceiling in a day and a bit."

"Right."

"He said he did it himself."

"Right."

"It's the same board, isn't it. A bit of ply."

---

And there it is, and it is the second time in a month that a woman in this borough has had a bit of ply in her head and a stool under her and nobody has joined the two up, and he does not join them up either.

"That's a bigger bit of ply than that," he says.

"Mm."

"It's for a ceiling. That's a floor."

"It's the same material."

"It's the same material," he says, and goes back to the pelmet, and she takes her tea and goes out and shuts the door, which shuts.

---

He works out the top of that run at about the end of the day and it is a quarter of an inch out.

Not a lot. You would not see it. He gets down off the stool and stands back and looks at the length of it across a manky kitchen and it is a quarter of an inch proud at one end, and it is proud because he set the first one off the ceiling with his eye from a stool and then set the other five off the first one, which is what you do when you can see the whole run at once.

He could take it off. It would take the rest of the afternoon with a spirit level and a scribing block and a chisel, and it would mean a ladder.

He leaves it and packs up and puts the stool and the bench in the van and shuts the tailgate and stands in the road with his hands in his pockets.

---

The oak is stacked in the unit under the strip light and the two gates are made and leaning against the back wall with a number chalked on the back of each one, and they have been made since the summer and the man who wants them wanted them by the spring and wanted the money up front and has the money.

He looks at them.

He put a card in the latch stile on those and not the hinge stile, because on the latch stile it lifts out in a few years, and he said so to a man on a shop telephone in October and the man said *that's a thing, is it*, and he is going up to a cottage in the spring to hang them on a pair of posts that are already in the ground, and hanging a gate on a post that is already in the ground on your own needs a ladder, and you cannot do that from a stool.

He has not got a ladder.

---

He gets in the van.

The drawer is shut and the shoebox is on the bench and the stranger's brush is in the side pocket of the canvas bag in the footwell with the stone in the leather bottom of it, and the piece of scribing card is in the shirt pocket of the coat on the back of the cab door with TWELVE on one side of it and nothing at all on the other.

He has a pencil behind his ear.

He looks at the card for about as long as it takes to put the pencil behind the other ear, and then he starts the van and goes home, and the card is in the pocket, and the space behind the offcuts is a folded step ladder's wide, and the top run of that kitchen is a quarter of an inch proud at one end, and there is a step ladder in this borough standing folded against the wall of a front room at the end of a road that nobody has lent out anything to anybody, as far as anybody in this borough knows.

---

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES HAS SAID *I'VE LENT IT OUT* AND NOT SAID TO WHOM AND NOT SAID WHEN, HAS LEFT A RUN OF WALL UNITS A QUARTER OF AN INCH OUT BECAUSE HE HAD TO SET THE FIRST ONE OFF THE CEILING WITH HIS EYE FROM A STOOL, HAS A PAIR OF OAK GATES LEANING AGAINST A WALL THAT HE CANNOT HANG WITHOUT A LADDER, AND HAS HAD A PIECE OF SCRIBING CARD IN HIS POCKET WITH ROOM ON THE OTHER SIDE OF IT SINCE NOVEMBER AND HAS NOT WRITTEN ON IT.**
