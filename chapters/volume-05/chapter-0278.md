# Chapter 0278 — Frost

Thursday the first of December 2016, from about six in the morning, a street off Marsh Lane in Tideglass, a bus shelter at the top of it, and a parade of shops.

**IT WAS AN ORDINARY COLD WEEK, AND A MAN LIVED EIGHT DOORS UP A STREET WHO DID GAS AND HEATING AND WAS NOT CALLED, AND SOMEBODY STOOD IN THE COLD AT HALF PAST TEN AND SAID IT WAS THE FIRST WINTER SHE COULD REMEMBER IN THIS BOROUGH, AND NOBODY ARGUED WITH HER.**

There was ice on the parade at about ten past six and there had been ice on it since about three, and the gritting lorry came down at about a quarter past six with the blades up, because it had been below freezing and not wet, and it went down the parade and down the lane off Marsh Lane and round the back of the shops and up again the other side, and that was the only time anything came down that parade in the whole of that week.

It did not come back on the Friday and it did not come back on the Saturday.

By half past seven the light was the colour of a wet road, and the ice was still in the middle of the parade where the blades had not reached, because the middle of the parade is where the buses stop and the buses had not been.

The boiler at number six in that lane went on the Thursday at about ten past eight in the morning.

The woman who lives there has been there since the spring and the house is a rented one, and the boiler at the bottom of it is a wall-mounted combi with a clock on it that has run two hours fast since the summer, and the pressure light was off on the Thursday morning and the hot tap ran cold at the kitchen sink and stayed that way.

She rang the firm at about half past nine and the woman on the telephone asked her the postcode and the make off a sticker on the front and then said the earliest they had was Friday afternoon between four and seven, and said there was a figure for coming out after four and a different figure before four, and read the after-four one out, and it was ninety-four pounds.

"That's for a look."

"That's for coming out," said the woman on the telephone. "You'd not want me to tell you the other one."

The woman at number six said she would take the Friday, and then she sat on the arm of the sofa with a fire lit in the grate and a hot water bottle under a blanket and the gas fire going on the other side of the room, and the washing came out of the machine and went on a clothes horse in the front room and dried there.

---

The man at number two came past her door at about half past eight that morning with a bag of tools in one hand and his keys in the other, and he said good morning, and she said good morning, and he went on past her on the far side of the road with his head turned away from her house, and she did not say to him.

She has seen him out there on his Saturdays in a van with a sign on the side of it, and she has seen two other houses on that lane take a sheet off him about a kettle and about a radiator. It had come to her at about a quarter to ten that she could slide a tenner note under his door, and it had come to her and she had not done it, and it was not a thing anybody in that lane discussed, and the door of number two was shut at eight o'clock and again at noon.

The firm came on the Friday at ten past four and stayed until twenty to six, and the woman at number six had hot water and heat and a fire out of the grate and a strip of paper on the machine with a date and a time on it, and nobody rang the man at number two and nobody has said his name since.

The bus from the college comes in at the top of that lane at about a quarter past seven in the morning, and on the Thursday it did not come, and it did not come at a quarter to eight either, and the young woman of about twenty stood at the shelter with a bag of books in it from about ten past seven until about ten to nine with the other two people who were stood there, which was a man in a fleece going to the works and a woman with a shopping bag.

None of the three of them said the word ice out loud. They all said something else about it.

"It's the Cleeve road," said the man in the fleece. "They put salt on the Cleeve road at about six, and then the plough comes through and takes the top of it off again."

"It's not the Cleeve road," said the woman with the shopping bag. "It's the bottom of the parade, that's where it's worst, and they have not been down there since about seven."

"You can stand and wait for it," said the man in the fleece. "I've the works and they'll wait for me."

The bus came at about ten past nine. The woman with the shopping bag got on it and the man in the fleece did not and the young woman of about twenty did, and she was at the college an hour and a half late on the Thursday, and there was nobody at the door of that building when she got to it.

The school on that parade put a sheet in three of the shop windows on the Monday and a sheet on its own railings, and the sheet said the parents' evening was on the Thursday from four until seven because the term was nearly over, and the parade at a quarter past three had more people standing at a bus stop than it had had since the summer, and the youngest of them was in a coat with its hood up and was standing in the shelter.

The cold did not lift all week. The pipes on that parade were going at about half past four every afternoon with a mist off them standing about two foot up off the pavement, and on the Friday a man came round the shops with a bucket and a mop and put a sign out, and on the Saturday there was a standpipe going at the top of the parade outside the bank with two men on it and a queue on the pavement filling bottles out of it, and the parade was quiet by nine every night because it was cold and because it was nearly December.

At about half past ten on the Thursday morning, in the shelter, with the ice gone grey under a sky that had not moved all week, the woman with the shopping bag said it.

She said it to the man in the fleece, who was still there, and not to the young woman of about twenty, who was looking up the parade for a bus.

"It's the first winter I can remember in this borough," she said.

The man in the fleece said, "Ay," and pulled his sleeve down over his hand.

"My dad had one the like of this, but he'd not been on this road a year. He was on the Marshmont side, and that's not the same, is it. That's not the same at all."

"That's right," said the man in the fleece. "That's the Marshmont side."

Nobody argued with her about it and nobody improved on it either. The man in the fleece looked at the ice for a while and said that it was a bit early for it, and the woman said she had not said when it started, and he said he was not saying it had not, and that was the end of it.

**AND ON THE SATURDAY MORNING THE WOMAN AT NUMBER SIX CAME OUT WITH A BASKET AND THE BASKET DID NOT GO DOWN THE LANE, IT WENT DOWN THE SIDE PASSAGE TO THE BIN FIELD BEHIND THE ROW, BECAUSE THE PAVEMENT HAD NOT BEEN DONE, AND ON THE SUNDAY A MAN CAME DOWN THE LANE SELLING LOGS AND SHE BOUGHT ONE OFF HIM IN HER DOORWAY WITH A NEWSPAPER UNDER IT AGAINST THE STEP.**
