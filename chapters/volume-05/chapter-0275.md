# Chapter 0275 — A Letterbox

Tuesday the fifteenth of November 2016, from about a quarter past seven in the morning, the top of Marsh Lane in Tideglass, and a parade of shops off the top of it.

**THE BIN LORRY HAD BEEN AND GONE, AND A PIECE OF POST CAME BACK UP THE LANE FOR A DOOR WITH NOBODY BEHIND IT, AND IT WENT DOWN IN THE DOORWAY, AND NOBODY WENT UP AND NOBODY TOLD ANYBODY.**

The lorry came up Marsh Lane at about a quarter past seven and had gone down it again by twenty to, and it had done what it does, which is take a row of bins off a pavement and put them back on the same pavement, and on the way round it had set one of them a foot too far back, up under the lamp at the top.

The woman who runs the shop that shuts early came out at about ten past eight with a cigarette going and stood and looked at that bin.

"Every week," she said. "Every week it's up there. He doesn't have to put it up there. He's going past it on his way to the next one."

She stood at it a while longer and then went back in and shut the door on the draught, which she does, and the door is the second worst on that parade and it has been since the summer.

---

The post comes up that lane at about a quarter to eight, and the man who brings it has brought it for long enough that the woman who runs the shop can hear his bag before she can see him.

He has a bundle under his arm and a shoulder bag on the other side and he does not have a card, and there is no book on him, and nobody in that lane has ever asked him for anything, because a lane is a lane and the houses on the top of it are numbered and the only things on it that are not houses are a bus shelter and a pillar box that has had the collection plate taped over since about April.

The first three doors he rang twice. At the fourth he rang once and went on without waiting, and at the sixth he put a leaflet through the letterbox standing back off the step so that it went in flat.

At the shop that shuts early there were three cards and an envelope with a window in it and a bill for a shop that no longer had a name over the door, and he put all five through the door and went on.

At the pillar box he put two in and then stood for a second with his hand on the side of it.

At the bus shelter there was a woman with a bag of shopping and her house keys in her hand.

"He's been early all week," she said, to the man with the bag. "He's been early since the rain stopped."

"You'll get the lot of them before the buses," he said.

"That's what I mean," said the woman. "I've stood here six weeks now and not one of them's wanted a thing off anybody. No envelope, no bill, no card. You could stand here all morning and go your whole round and nobody would ask you for anything at all."

"That's the post for you," said the man. "That's the one thing in this town that's got somebody to sign for it. You could leave a bowl of water out on a step and nobody'd come, and a postman comes."

"You'd think the post could knock."

"Post knocks," said the man. "Post doesn't get an answer."

The top flat at the top of Marsh Lane is the one with three bells on a small board by the door, and there are no names beside any of them, and there have not been any since the summer, because the last of the empties went out of that flat in June and the man who took them off went down the middle of the road with a barrow and nobody came with a pen to take a name off the board.

The man with the bag had one item for that flat on the fifteenth. It was a white envelope with a stamp on it and the address typed on the front of it in a small tidy hand, and he looked at it, and then he looked at the board with the three bells on it, and then he put his thumb up against the top bell and did not push it.

There is no answer from a set of three bells with nothing written beside them. He knows that. He has known it since June.

The door has a step in front of it about four inches deep with a tiled recess, and the tiled recess was dry under the overhang and had a leaf blown into the corner of it, and he put the envelope down on the tiles against the jamb, side on, so that if anything came in off the lane it would go over the top of it and not onto it.

Then he wrote nothing, because there is nothing to write it in, and he did not go up, because going up is a knock, and knocking is a thing you do to a door when you want something off whoever is behind it.

He went back down the middle of the road with the bundle still under his arm.

Nobody in that lane saw him do it. The woman at the bus shelter had her back to the top of the hill and was looking down it for a bus. The woman who runs the shop that shuts early had gone back inside and shut the door and was doing the day's bread in there, and neither of them has ever heard about that envelope and neither of them is going to.

The bin came off the kerb at about half past nine when the woman who runs the shop went out and got it herself, because the lorry does not come back.

**AND THE ENVELOPE WAS ON THE TILES IN THE RECESS OF THAT DOOR AT NOON AND AT HALF PAST TWO AND AT DARK, AND IT WAS NOT OPENED, AND IT WAS NOT RAIN ON, AND THE BINS WERE ALL ON THE KERB BY HALF PAST NINE, AND THE POSTMAN HAD BEEN ROUND THE OTHER SIDE OF THE BANK AND DOWN THE PARADE AND IN AND OUT OF SIX MORE DOORS IN THIS BOROUGH, AND HE WAS ASKED NOTHING BY ANYBODY ALL DAY.**
