# Chapter 0977 — Inside One Clay Cell

Undated in this draft.

**A HOLLOW CLAY BLOCK ON ITS SIDE BY THE OFFICE WALL HAS ONE OF ITS FOUR CORNERS OPEN TO THE AIR.**

The block lies on its side on the flags by the office wall at the unit, tipped off the stack it came off when the stack was moved in the summer. It is an ordinary hollow block of the kind used for a cavity wall, eighteen inches long, nine high and four thick, and it has four cavities in it running the length of the block, two in each half.

Lying on its side, one of those cavities stands upright. It is a square hole about four inches across with a wall of about three-eighths round it, and because the block is on its side the cavity has the open end of the block above it, where a masonry nail used to pin the block while it was being laid.

The clay is fired red through the body of it and grey at the edges, and the cavity walls show the marks of the mould on their faces. Where the block was dropped on the flags the arris on one side is chipped back to about an inch and the chip is a raw pale break that has not weathered at all, because it is new and the rest of the block is not.

The inside of the cavity is dry and grey with the dust of the block's own making, and the floor of it stands about an inch above the flags because the block is lying on two of its ribs. The dust in the bottom of it has lain there since the block was tipped and has the print of the drip from the office eaves in the middle of it.

The flags by the office wall were laid in a bed of sand and they have heaved in the winter and settled again in the spring, so that a line of them stands about half an inch proud of the next and the joint between is a dark line rather than a joint. The bed under them was of sand laid on the hardstanding, and it is damp at the bottom of it now and dry at the top, and the flag that stands proud sits half an inch clear of that. The block came to rest against that line, and one end of it has dropped half an inch into the hollow that opened along the joint when the flags lifted.

The store runs along the far side of the yard with its wide door shut and its bench inside it, and between the store and the office wall there is a strip of flags two feet wide which nobody has swept since the spring because there is nothing to sweep off it. The office window looks down that strip, and a person standing at the window can see the block without having to come out into the yard.

Along the top of the office wall runs a lead coping, laid in sections about three feet long, and one section has come loose at a joint and lifts about a quarter of an inch at its near end. That is where the drip in the bottom of the cavity comes from, and it is why the print in the dust has a nose to it and why the dust around the print is a shade darker than the dust at the edges of the floor.

The gate at the yard end of the strip stands open against the post and has done since before the winter, and the latch on the gate is hooked back over its staple. The staple itself was never let into the post, and the latch has therefore been laid over a screw that is not there, which is a thing that is true of half the gates on this side of the road.

Rain off the roof of the office runs across the yard and past the far end of the block, so the block's own top surface is washed every time it rains while the inside of the cavity stands dry. Over a wet week the flags round it go dark to the edge of the chip, and by the middle of the next week they are pale again at the top and still damp at the bottom.
---

One finger goes into the cavity at the open end and comes down inside it on to the dust, and then it is swept round the inside, side after side, so that the pad of it runs along one wall and stops at the corner and goes along the next.

Four sides, taken in that order, and they are not the same. The first side is the width of a whole finger with a little over, and the pad of the finger cannot lie flat against it anywhere along the three feet of its length because the wall has bowed out in the middle. The second side is the same width at the top and a little under it at the bottom, where the clay has shrunk away from the corner mould.

The third side is flat enough to take the whole pad, and the fourth is the same as the second. So the cavity is not quite square: it is wider across the middle than the mould made it, and the corners are not the corners the mould intended because the shrinkage in the clay took the corners in a little and the drying in the kiln took the middle out.

The floor of the cavity is dust, and the dust takes the print of the finger as well as the drip did, and takes it deeper on the side where the block leans and shallower on the side where it does not. The depth of the cavity from the open end to the closed end is about a foot and a half, and at that distance the finger is still comfortably clear of the far wall.

Nothing gives. The walls of the cavity are the same thickness everywhere except at the chipped arris outside, and the block as a whole does not move under the hand at all, being bedded on two of its ribs and not rocking anywhere along its length. The dust is disturbed and the print of the drip survives under the new one, and nothing else about the block is altered by the going in and the coming out.

The block was made the same week as about two hundred others of its size, and the size is the size a whole pallet of them will carry. Its maker's name is pressed into one of the ribs on the outside with a steel die, and the letters are the size of a thumbnail and have gone soft at their edges where the clay is softest.

Where the block has chipped, the break runs through the wall of the cavity and shows the clay inside it as a paler and finer body than the outside face. That inside body is what the two skins of the block are made of, and it is the same colour all the way through, so that a chip on one rib is a chip through the wall behind it as well.

The rib the block stands on at its near end is not flat. It has been ground off at one corner by a barrow wheel, and the flat that came from the grinding stands a few degrees out of true, which is why the block leans the way it does and why the end of it has dropped into the joint of the flags.

The other blocks off that stack are stacked against the store wall in the yard and have been used to keep the ends of a piece of roofing felt out of the rainwater. Their arrises are all sound and their cavities are all clear, and one of them has a skin of old mortar dried on its top rib from years of standing in it, gone hard, and staying where it is.
---

The woman from two doors down comes in at the gate and stops at the corner where the office wall returns into the post, with the post at one shoulder and the wall at the other and both hands hanging by her sides. She does not come further into the yard than the post, and she does not go the other way along the strip of flags, and the block with the open corner in it stays where it lies between the office wall and the gutter.

Not one word of it reaches the other. She looks at the far end of the yard once and at the gate once, and after a while she puts to the other one that the wind has been coming round from the west all week and has brought nothing with it, and the answer to that comes back without any particular care in it.

Her hand lifts in front of her chest about the time the finger comes out of the cavity and goes into the cuff of the other sleeve, and stays there, and the yard is quiet for as long as she is standing at it.

The block lies on its side with one square corner open to the air and the other three of them shut, and the cavity goes in the same depth from the same end of it.
