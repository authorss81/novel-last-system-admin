# Chapter 0329 — The Fifth Thing

Thursday the fifteenth of June 2017, the week of the twelfth of June, a building by the water in Tideglass, and a passage three streets off it.

**A FLAT ROOF OVER A FIRST-FLOOR CORRIDOR HAD A GLAZED FRAME ON IT WITH NOTHING SEALED ROUND THE BASE OF IT, AND WATER CAME IN AND RAN DOWN THE WALL OF A STAIRWELL AND OVER TWO DOOR HEADS AND OFF A WALL IN A CORRIDOR, AND A BOWL WENT UNDER IT ON TUESDAY AND STAYED THERE, AND THREE STREETS OFF A MAN HOOKED A BIN BACK ONTO TWO BRACKETS AND WENT DOWN THE STREET.**

That building is a block of flats on the low side of a road by the water and the front of it faces the water and the back of it faces about nine yards of ground and a wall, and the stair that everybody uses is an outside one on the back, nine treads, straight, with a steel rail screwed to the wall side and nothing at all down the open side of it.

The corridor on the first floor is twelve foot from the fire door to the end wall, with a concrete floor, a painted skirt, four doors off it and a window at the far end. Over the top of that corridor the roof is flat, and about four foot back from the middle of it there is a lantern in it, which is a raised frame of glass on a curb about two foot square and about a foot high, put in when the flat roof was done because a corridor with no light in it is a corridor nobody wants to come down.

There is nothing in this borough that has ever been up on that roof. Not the man who does that building, who does the gutters and the outside and the stair treads and comes on a Wednesday, and not a man in a fleece, and not anybody at all. The parapet round that roof is about two foot nine and the ladder to get at it is behind the tank, and the tank is against the side of the building where nobody goes.

What has gone is the sealing round the base of the lantern. You can see it from the corridor, because the curb is under the inside edge of the glass and the outside edge of the curb is exposed to the roof, and what is round that exposed edge now is not a bead of anything. It is bare metal with a grey skin on it, and it is about a foot of it all the way round, and the kind of grey skin that is on it has been going white and chalky in a wind for a year and has been wet for a week.

---

The woman of about fifty-five went up that outside stair at about a quarter to eight on the Tuesday morning with two empty jugs in her hands, and she stopped on the fifth tread, which is nine, because there was water on the landing.

Not much of it. It was coming down the wall of the stairwell in a run about the width of a thumb from under the ceiling at the end by the window, and it had been going down that wall and along the underside of the corridor ceiling and over the head of the far door, and the head of her own door, and the wall of the corridor was wet in a crescent from the skirting to about the height of a chair back, and the paper had come off it in one strip about two foot long and was hanging off the skirting board.

She went and got a washing-up bowl from her own room and put it under the strip and filled it about a third and went to work.

She emptied it at about ten. She emptied it at about one. She emptied it at about four and she was still emptying it at about half past five when the light under that ceiling began to go, and the day had been the kind of day where at about ten past six there is still a colour in the sky on the water.

Nobody in that building has been on that roof. Nobody in that building has been in the cupboard off the top landing where the old cistern is. Nobody in that building has had a ladder out on the back of it in the month of June. On the Wednesday morning the door of the store at the bottom of that outside stair stood open and the brooms in it were wet at the ends, and the woman in the room next door along, whose own door stands open the width of a hand, saw it and said nothing to anybody about it then or since.

On the Wednesday the woman in the room next door along came out onto that corridor at about ten in the morning and there was a bowl under the strip by the end wall, and the strip of paper was still hanging off the skirting, and the woman of about fifty-five was not in.

She stood in her own doorway for about a minute.

Then the door of that corridor went and the woman of about fifty-five came back from the bin store with a jug in each hand and saw her standing there.

"That's been going since Tuesday," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"There's a smell off that wall in the morning," said the woman in the room next door along.

"I know."

"It's not coming from behind my door."

"It's not," said the woman of about fifty-five. "It's coming down off that ceiling and running over the head of the doors and it comes in about a foot above the floor and it goes along that skirt to the window and it goes out of the window."

The woman in the room next door along said aye, and stood there about another half minute, and then said that the caretaker did nothing about anything in that building between about eight in the morning and about six in the evening, and then said that was not a thing she was saying to anybody, she was saying it to her, and shut her door to the width it always is.

The woman of about fifty-five went in and shut her own door and put her jugs down.

The bowl is under that strip. It is emptied four or five times a day and has been since the Tuesday, and the water in it is not clean and she has a second bowl for the jugs now, and there is a towel round the outside of the first one. There is a wet mark on the ceiling about the size of a tea tray where the run comes off and she has looked at that mark every day since and it has not got bigger and she has not decided whether that is a thing to be glad of.

And the machine is on its side in the corner where that corridor widens by the fire door, with its lead across the top of it and about half a bag of that landing's own grit still in the filter, and the bowl is about five foot from the machine, and the two of them are on the same landing and neither of them is anything to do with the other.

Three streets off that, on the Friday morning at about eight, the man in his forties came along a passage between two lines of houses and a wheelie bin was standing in the middle of it.

It was one of the three that belong to the front houses of the first line and it had been pulled out of its two brackets and left in the middle of that passage, and it was on its side, and the lid was up, and there was nothing in it and there was a puddle under it about the shape of the base of it.

There is nothing in that passage to stop anybody walking round a bin. It is six foot wide. He stepped over the puddle and carried on and got to the second line and turned round and came back, because he had been along it in the first place because a bracket on a downpipe wanted looking at and he had come back down that way.

He stood in the middle of that passage and looked at the bin for a bit.

Then he picked it up. It is a bin with a bracket on the back of it that hooks over two studs in the wall, and the whole weight of the thing is on that bracket, and the bin is not light empty. He got it up to the wall and hooked it on and the top stud took it and the lower one had about a quarter of an inch of play in it.

He went back up the passage and got a key out of his bag and put it on that stud and turned it a quarter, and the play went, and he put his hand on the lid and the bracket and pulled and the bin came off the wall in one piece the way it is meant to.

He did not put his ear to it and he did not look inside it and he did not knock on either of the two doors it belongs to and neither of those two houses knows a thing about it.

He went back down the passage and got into his van and shut the door and went to his next thing.

---

On the Friday afternoon the man in his forties came along the front of that parade about half past four while the woman who works in a shop was putting a card up in the window, and she said good evening to him and he said good evening and went on about ten foot and stopped and came back and put his head round the side of her door.

"Did you see anything to do with a bin in that passage off the top of Marsh Lane."

"I did," said the woman who works in a shop. "It's on its side in the middle of it."

"Somebody's pulled it off them hooks."

"They have."

"And it's back."

"It is," said the woman who works in a shop, "and it's a bin outside two houses that have got nothing to do with anybody, and you put it back on, and you didn't say to either of them that you had, and if you had said to them that you had, one of them would have said don't, because a thing on their own hooks is a thing they can point at."

He looked at her for a second.

"That's not why I didn't."

"You didn't," said the woman who works in a shop. "I said what would have happened, not why it didn't." She put her hands flat on the card in the window and pushed it. "Was it a long bracket."

"Two studs."

"Then it was off its studs and not off its hook," said the woman who works in a shop, "and that means whoever pulled it off did it wrong, and if they come back and pull it off right, it'll be on the ground again in a week. Have you got a spare one of them."

"I've got a strip of angle in the van."

"Then that is a thing you have done and I am not going to say that you did it, because a thing that a person has done and a thing that a person has been given credit for are not the same size in this town, and I have watched one of the second ones turn into the first one inside a month."

He said that was fair and went on down the parade with his bag, and there is a bin on two studs on the back of two houses on that passage, and the man in his forties was at that door in the middle of the afternoon of the following week with a length of angle and a drill and was on that wall for about twenty minutes, and neither of those two houses was in when he did it, and neither of them has said anything about it to anybody.

**A BOWL WENT UNDER A STRIP OF WET PAPER ON A LANDING BY THE WATER ON A TUESDAY IN JUNE AND IS STILL UNDER IT, AND A BIN IS ON TWO STUDS ON A PASSAGE OFF THE TOP OF MARSH LANE, AND THE MAN WHO PUT THAT BIN BACK DID NOT CARRY ANYTHING OUT OF THAT PASSAGE BUT HIS OWN BAG.**
