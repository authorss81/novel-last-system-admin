# Chapter 0995 — The Job He Has Not Priced

Undated in this draft.

**THE RACK OF SAMPLE TIMBER STANDS AGAINST THE WALL AT THE FAR END OF THE STORE AT THE UNIT.**

The far end of the store is about nine feet back from the front of the bench and the roof light does not reach it, so that everything in it keeps the colour of the inside of a lid. The rack is a thing he made out of two lengths of batten and thirty years of somebody else's opinion about what a rack ought to be: one horizontal member at hip height and another at the top, with the ends of the sample stock stood up in the angle between them like pipes in a rack. There is a piece of every thickness the place has ever dealt in, from a shaving to a nine-inch length of oak, and the bottom half of every one of them has gone the colour of tea where the damp comes off the yard wall and stands in the joint between the batten and the brick.

He came in at seven and put the bag under the bench and has not taken it out again. The bag has a sheet of stiff paper in it, creased once and not his, and he knows roughly where in the bag it is without looking, which is a thing about a bag that a man does not decide to know. Across on the bench beneath the roof light is a drawing-board, its corner off square, and the rule, and the pencil, and the folding rule shut. At the other end of the office there are two trestles with a length of floorboard across them that he put there the week before last to plane and has not touched since. That was the morning he set aside. He has had four mornings like it since, and the board is where it was, and the fine shaving on the top of it has gone hard at its edges where the damp got at it.

---

The light off the roof light lies on the bench and stops about where the bench ends, and past that line the store is the same brightness all day, and a man can work at the far end and forget that it is morning. He has been at the far end since the rain stopped because the far end is where the timber is, and the timber is what the shop is, and he has been putting a joint on a length of three-inch stock and taking it off again and putting it back, and the joint is better now than it was, which is everything that has happened in the store this week.

Then he puts both hands flat on the sample rack and takes the weight of it and rocks it a hand's width to see whether the wall behind it has come away from anything. The rack comes a full quarter of an inch at the top and stops dead, because the top batten is screwed into the brick and the brick is not going anywhere, and the lower batten has moved in its fixing by about the thickness of a coin over forty years, which is nothing and is also everything. He takes his hands off it and stands looking at the place where his hands were. In the bag, on the crease, there is a sheet of stiff paper with three lines on it and a word struck through in an old woman's hand, and he is not going to take it out this morning.

What he is going to do instead is go up the frontage and find out what would have to be true for that book to take him at all, and he has been putting it off for two days for a reason he has not looked at directly, which is that the answer is going to be work, and work is the thing he has not got, and there is a smaller reason under that one and it is this. A man at the lock-up wants a wall taken out and a doorway through into the second bay, and he wants it priced, and he wanted it priced by Friday, and Friday was yesterday. He has not done it. He has not put a figure down. He has had four days in which the only piece of work anybody has ever asked him for in this whole street lay on the bench and got in his way every morning, and he has gone round it every morning, and he has been round it this morning as well.

He knows exactly why a man does that, and it is not that the work is difficult. It is that the work is the one piece that was asked for, and everything else he has done this season he offered, and the offering is the only part of it he has been proud of.

Both of them went up the river on the same morning and the shop has been like a shop in a photograph since, because the woman from two doors down comes in the middle of the week for the sand and the keys and stays twenty minutes, and she has not come in at all this week. The book at the lock-up had two lines on it and both of the lines went with them, and there is nothing on that book now that anybody in this street has to do with, and the season is shutting it, which is a thing the woman at number ten told him in a cold yard with the wind off the coal shed and which he has not thought about since because thinking about it produces nothing you can put a spade in.

He could put a figure on that wall now. He has taken a dozen of doors out of places like it and he could be in that room with a tape and be out again before the middle of the morning with a number that would not be wrong by more than a couple of pounds, and the whole of that job is four days of work for a man and two for a boy, and he has put the number in his head four times this week and has put nothing down on paper, and a figure that has only ever been in a man's head is a figure he has not given anybody. [break] He puts the board on the end of the bench square to the edge and takes the folding rule and the pencil and puts them in the bag on top of the sheet without straightening it, and then he stands in the middle of the store with the bag in his hand and finds he does not know what to do with his hands, which has not happened to him since he was fifteen. What he does with them is pick the cap off the pencil, look at the point, and put the cap back on, and his hands do nothing else for the rest of the morning.

He goes out by the side door, across the yard and up the frontage, the bag on his shoulder, and it is not raining, and the flags in the frontage have dried in patches at the high joints, and the wind is coming off the low ground behind the houses with the smell off the cut grass in the field at the back of number six. There is nobody about on the frontage at half past eight on a weekday in the middle of a season when everybody with work is up the river, and there is a use he has not made of it in a fortnight and does not intend to make. The rack stands against the wall in the dark at the end of the store with a hundred ends of timber going brown along their bottom halves, and the shelf under it runs even without a hollow anywhere in it, which is the one thing in that end of the building that has not needed him in ten years, and he is not standing at it now, because he has gone up the frontage to find out what a man has to have before the book will keep him, which is a thing he should have asked about on Tuesday.
