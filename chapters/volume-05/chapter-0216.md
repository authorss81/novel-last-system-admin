# Chapter 0216 — The Week

Tuesday the twelfth of January 2016, from about ten past five in the morning, the intake at Marrowgate.

**HE IS NOT ON A ROTA AND THERE IS NO LIST AND NOTHING HE DOES IS WRONG, AND HE HAS BEEN DOING IT FOR ELEVEN YEARS, AND HE COULD NOT EXPLAIN IT TO ANYBODY IF HE TRIED, BECAUSE BEING UNABLE TO EXPLAIN IT IS THE ONLY WAY HE HAS KEPT IT, AND HE IS NOT RESCUED AND NOBODY IS GOING TO ASK HIM TO STOP.**

He had not told anybody he was going to be there. At ten past five in the morning in the second week of January there is nothing in Marrowgate to do at that hour except stand at the end of a bank and look at a man on four feet of steel.

The intake is a brick chamber with a hatch and a grating over it. The water comes in under a foot of iron and there is a screen across the whole of it, and in January the screen is a foot and a half of river weed and everything else the estuary has ever decided to lose.

There was a rake. It was a long-handled flat rake and it was older than the man holding it and it had been mended twice with wire, and it was worked by hand from a platform, and it takes two men a whole morning, and Walter Sallow did it on his own.

He was eighty-six and thin, in a coat over a jumper and gloves with the fingers cut off at the second joint, and he worked the rake in a rhythm that Jonas Mercer watched for a while and then stopped watching, because a rhythm is not interesting, it is only a man doing the only thing he can do, twice, for a bit over an hour, with a break at about a quarter to seven for a mug of tea out of a flask that had been in a bag on the bank since the night before.

The hatch was shut behind him, and the water going into the town was off, and had been off since a quarter to six, and would be off until he had finished.

"There is a machine for that," Jonas Mercer said, from the bank, after about ten minutes, because he was tired of not saying anything.

"There is," said the man, without stopping.

"Why is somebody raking it."

"Because it's there." He pulled a slab up onto the grating and stood on it to get at the one behind, and it came away in a sheet the size of a door mat and about four inches thick, and he carried it to the bank and tipped it into a skip with a hole in the bottom of it. "There's a machine comes round. It's a trailer with a brush on it and it does four of these in a morning and it comes on a Tuesday and a Friday and it costs about what the town spends on the shelter's gas in a year."

"Then it comes on a Tuesday and a Friday."

"It came on a Tuesday and a Friday," said Walter Sallow. "That was two years ago, near enough. It's a going round. It goes where it's wanted."

"Who wants it."

"Nobody's asked me."

He said it the way you say the name of a street you live on. Then he went back down onto the platform and worked the rake for another twenty minutes and stopped, because the screen was done, and stood with his hands on the rail looking at the water underneath, which had gone green in the last half hour in a way that meant the town was getting its water out of the estuary again.

**AND THE COST OF THAT SCREEN IS A THING THAT HAS HAPPENED EVERY TUESDAY AND EVERY FRIDAY FOR AS LONG AS ANYBODY IN THAT TOWN HAS BEEN ALIVE, AND IT COSTS SOMEBODY A DAY'S WORK, AND NO DOCUMENT IN THAT TOWN RECORDS THAT IT DOES, BECAUSE THE MOMENT IT STOPPED BEING WORTH RAKING WAS A MOMENT NOBODY DECIDED.**

There is no tank big enough to hold the town's supply, so while the intake is shut the whole of Marrowgate is dry. A woman of about eighty takes in washing. She has four machines on a line behind a row of houses at the top of Church Street, fed off the same supply as everybody else's, and she cannot start until the water comes back at about eight, and she has to have four houses' washing back inside by the middle of the afternoon because that is when they want it.

She has solved it. She washes on the four days, and on the two days she does not wash at all, and nobody has ever asked her why, and she has never said, and the two days are the days the water is off.

Jonas Mercer found this out at about half past eight in the morning from a man of about eighty-seven in a kitchen doorway who was complaining about it, and then went and found her, and she told him the whole of it in about nine sentences and did not once say the word problem.

"Do you know what the water's off for?"

"No," she said.

"Tuesdays and Fridays it isn't, normally. That's the two days."

"No."

"Then it's the intake. He rakes it and there's nothing in it to fall on and it's been going a long while." He said it as a fact and not as a favour. "You could get a hose off the river and it wouldn't be the same water and you'd have to boil it."

"I couldn't get a hose off the river," she said, "because he takes the hose."

Walter Sallow, at about nine o'clock that morning, was in the yard with the town's flatbed lorry and a coil of hose on the tailboard, and Jonas Mercer asked him about the hose and was told, exactly, that it was the town's, that it went round on the truck on a Tuesday and a Friday, that anybody in that town who wanted a hose to wash with could come and take it and put it back, and that nobody had ever come.

**AND THAT WAS THE MORNING, AND THE MORNING WAS ONE OF THE FIVE, AND NONE OF THE OTHER FOUR HAD ANYTHING TO DO WITH A WASHING MACHINE OR WITH A DAY THAT DOES NOT GET PAID FOR.**

The clinic is unlocked at eight by him and locked at six by him, the water in it is his to turn on and off, and so is the water in the shelter and the boiler in the shelter, which he lights at about a quarter to six, and the night round is at about ten, when he walks four rooms, looks at four radiators, and signs a book with a column in it that has the day and the time in it and nothing else.

The gate is his. It is up at seven and down at six, both by hand, both because the chain is stiff and the motor was replaced in about 2010 by somebody the town hired for a day and has not been back.

The yard is his to keep. The lorry, the barrow, the hedge, the gutter on the shed, the door on the third bay that does not shut, the pump outside the clinic, and the corner of the yard with small broken things in it that is not a skip and has never been a skip and is not on any list, and that he takes to the tip when it is full and puts in a brown bag.

And the five things are: the works, the clinic, the shelter, the gate, the yard.

He walked round all five with him on the Tuesday and again on the Wednesday, and by the Wednesday afternoon he had the whole of the arrangement without being told it, because the arrangement is written down nowhere except as a name on a sheet, and everything else about it is a man going round a town at the hours at which a man goes round a town.

**AND HERE IS THE THING THAT IS THE WHOLE OF THE SECOND SYSTEM, AND HE HAD FOUND IT OUT BY THE WEDNESDAY AFTERNOON AND HE TOLD NOBODY.**

Walter Sallow was the only person in that town who could read a pump house, strip a stopcock, relight the shelter boiler, work the intake rack, and change the chain on the yard gate.

He was not the only person who did any of it.

The gardener raked the yard, had raked it for nine years, and could not have worked the rack and did not want to. A woman of about seventy-four swept the clinic, could open the door perfectly well, had been asked to once by somebody in an office in a different town, had said no, had been perfectly within her rights, and nobody had since thought to ask her again. A man of about ninety-one let people in at the gate on a Sunday when Walter Sallow was not there, which is the only Sunday in about nine years anybody can remember, and he did it because the gate was open and because he was there.

Everything else in that town was done by somebody. Only the five were not, and the five were not because there was nobody else, and because a place cannot be given to a person, and because the town has said out loud in a room that if somebody can, then the person cannot be replaced, and that has been the position for every day anybody has checked.

He asked the question in the yard at about half past two on the Wednesday, standing at the back of the shed where the broken things are, in the flat grey light, with the lorry behind them.

"Are you all right with this?"

"I'm all right with it."

"That's not what I asked."

"Then I don't know what you asked," said Walter Sallow, and he was not sharp, and he was not offended, and he genuinely did not know, and Jonas Mercer could see that he did not know.

"I am asking whether it is a burden. Whether you would like somebody else to have it. Whether anybody in that room has ever asked you that question, or whether they are afraid to, which is the same thing with better manners."

Walter Sallow thought about it for long enough that the light changed.

"No," he said. "And I want to tell you why I can't give you a better one than no, because you'll think I'm being straight with you and I'm not, I'm telling you the actual arrangement of the thing. I have never worked out how to say it is a weight. If I said it was a weight, then somebody in that room would have to do something about it, and there is nothing that room can do, and there is no version of the room doing something about it. So I'd have said the true thing, and it would be exactly where it is now, and I'd be a man who had said a true thing and got nothing, and there would be two of us at the end of it, and that is worse than not having said it." He put his hand on the rack pole and checked it, which he did not need to do. "And if I said it was fine, that'd be a lie, and I'd rather be stuck with the no."

"Is it fine."

"It's eleven years," said Walter Sallow. "It's eleven years and it's a Tuesday and I got up at five and I'm not tired and there's tea in the bag. I can't tell you the weight of it, because there has never been a morning where I picked it up and put it down, and a man who has never done that does not have a number, and I have thought about it and I have got nothing better than the no."

---

He carried a bag of the town's linen to a house on Church Street and back, and he let a man in at the gate who had been standing outside it for an hour because he had come to see the shed, and he took the brown bag to the tip on the Thursday, and he sat down for a while on a wall in the sun with his hat over his face while Jonas Mercer watched him not do anything at all.

Walter Sallow put the brown bag in the back of the lorry and drove it out of the yard at ten past four and did not switch the yard light on, and Jonas Mercer watched him go and then walked down to the slip with the notebook still in the inside pocket of his coat, and four of the five things in it were the linen and the gate and the tip and the wall, and the fifth one was the thing he had been asked about in the yard at half past two on the Wednesday, and it was not in there, and nobody in that town is going to be told about any of it by anybody.

**AND A MAN OF EIGHTY-SIX ANSWERED A QUESTION HE HAD NEVER BEEN ASKED BEFORE, IN A YARD, IN THE FLAT GREY LIGHT, AND COULD NOT GIVE AN ANSWER THAT WAS ANY USE, AND SAID SO OUT LOUD, AND IT WAS THE MOST STRAIGHT THING ANYBODY HAS SAID TO JONAS MERCER IN ABOUT A YEAR, AND HE WROTE THE REST OF THAT WEEK DOWN ON A TRAIN AND DID NOT WRITE THAT ONE DOWN.**
