# Chapter 0309 — What the Building Did Third

Sunday the twenty-sixth of March 2017, from about half past ten in the morning, a building by the water in Tideglass.

**THE WATER IN THAT BUILDING IS OFF AT THE STOP TAP ON THE SUNDAY, AND A WOMAN WENT DOWN THAT PASSAGE AT HALF PAST TEN WITH A JUG IN BOTH HANDS AND FOUND THAT SOMEBODY HAD GONE UP BEHIND HER DURING THE WEEK AND UNSCREWED THE NOZZLE OFF THE STANDPIPE AND CAPPED IT WITH A CAP THAT WAS NOT THE CAP, AND SHE SAID NOTHING TO ANYBODY ABOUT IT, AND NOBODY IN THAT BOROUGH WAS ANYWHERE NEAR IT WHEN IT HAPPENED, AND NOBODY WILL EVER BE TOLD WHO DID IT.**

The stop tap for that building is on the ground floor in the passage, behind a board that comes off, and it is off from about half past seven on a Saturday until about half past seven on a Monday, which is what it has always been, and on the way back on the Monday morning the first person down that passage puts it on and the building has water in it again.

The standpipe is on the wall of the outside stair about halfway up, coming out of the wall and going up, with a valve on it and a lock handle, and above the valve an outlet with a male coupling on the end of it, and a cap on a short chain, and a length of pipe off the coupling about four foot long with a jet nozzle screwed into the end of it.

There was no nozzle.

What was on the coupling was a brass cap, and it was not the cap that came off that building, because the cap that came off that building is a moulded plastic one with a chain and it has been on that outlet since it went in, and it is kept in the cupboard under the stairs on that first floor, on a shelf with a rolled wool blanket and a short length of flat bar in it, because that is where the caps and the blankets are kept in a building like this and everybody who ever put them there was being tidy.

She came down that passage at about half past ten on the Sunday morning with a two-gallon jug in one hand and the same jug's handle in the other and stopped where she always stops, which is about three foot past the meter, because the stop tap is behind the board that comes off and the board comes off after you lift the flap of the case on the wall.

The board was already off.

The case on the wall that the tap is behind is a wooden box with a little door on a hinge, and that door was standing open and the light was on the strip fitting overhead, and that was the first thing that was wrong, because there is no reason for a light to be on in that passage on a Sunday morning at half past ten when the man who comes on the Monday has not come.

She put the jug down and looked at the standpipe.

The outlet was capped. The cap was brass and it was about an inch and a half across the flats and it was a good cap, a better one than the moulded plastic one, and it had a washer under it and it was wound on tight, and the four foot of pipe and the nozzle were not in the passage, and they are not in that building, and no two people in that borough ever saw a man in a building carrying a length of pipe with a jet nozzle on the end of it.

She stood in that passage for about a minute.

Then she put the board back against the wall so that the case shut over it, and picked up the jug, and carried it up that outside stair, and let herself into the room at the end of the corridor, and put it on the floor by the sink.

The tap over that sink runs off the same supply and the supply was off, and there is no cold water anywhere in that room on a Sunday until about half past seven at night, and the jug was full when she brought it up and the jug was empty when she had drunk and washed in it, and she is on a month in that room and has been since before the winter.

Nobody in that building knew that anybody had been in that passage. Nobody in that borough knows who capped it or where the pipe went. She did not put it in a letter and she did not say it out loud to a soul that day, and she did not say it on the Monday, and she has not said it since.

---

The woman in the room next door along was up at about half past nine and had her own door standing open three inches with a chair against it, and the chair had been put there in the summer because the latch on that door has never caught.

At about two in the afternoon that day she came out onto the landing with a folded blanket that had been rolled up on the arm of that chair for about eight days, and she had asked nobody where it came from and had been in that landing every day in between, and the man who had put it there had not come back up that stair since the Saturday.

She knocked on the door of the room at the end of the corridor and the woman of about fifty-five opened it about two inches.

"I've got a blanket," said the woman in the room next door along. "It's a good one. It's wool, and it's not got a mark on it, and it's a bit too big for the bed you've got but it's warmer than what you've got on."

"I've got one."

"You've got a tea towel and you've got a cardigan over the end of it," said the woman in the room next door along, "and I can see both of them from here, and the radiator in this room has been off since before Christmas and it is not coming on on its own."

She said all of that in one go, standing in the corridor, and she did not come in and she did not ask where the blanket had come from and she was not asked, and she did not mention a man with a bag of tools or a landing or a box.

The woman of about fifty-five took it in both arms, and it was folded, and she did not open it, and she put it over the end of the bed on the side where the cardigan was not.

"That's kind of you."

"Don't do that," said the woman in the room next door along. "I've a spare. I've had a spare since about November and I have not needed it since about November, and there it is, and if you ever want it back you put it outside my door and I will get it when I've got nothing else on."

And she went back to her own room and put her own door to where it always is, and there was nothing said in that corridor by anybody about either of those two things, and nobody on the ground floor of that building knew that either of them had happened.

---

On the Monday morning the man in his forties let himself in at the street door of that building at about ten to eight with a bag of mortar mix under his arm and a trowel, and the step outside the street door had been loose in its concrete since before Christmas, the front left corner of it, so that it moved under your weight and made a noise like somebody knocking twice.

He mixed it on the step with the end of the trowel, knocked the loose corner out and came out about two inches, packed the mix under it, and drew the top up flush, and put his boot on it and then put his weight onto that boot, and it stayed where he had put it and made no noise at all, and the water that comes off that building runs across the top of that step and goes down the side.

He did it in about ten minutes, on his own time, before the first person down that passage had the stop tap open, and he told nobody in that borough that he had done it, and the woman of about fifty-five was in that room with a jug empty on the floor by a sink and a blanket folded over the end of her bed, and the standpipe outside on the wall had a brass cap on it that does not belong to it.

**AND THE STEP OUTSIDE THAT DOOR WENT DOWN WITH A TROWEL AND A BAG OF MORTAR MIX ON A MONDAY MORNING, AND A BRASS CAP IS STILL ON AN OUTLET ON A LANDING, AND A WOOL BLANKET IS STILL ON THE END OF A BED IN A ROOM AT THE END OF A CORRIDOR, AND THE INSIDE OF THAT DOOR CARRIES A HASP THAT SOMEBODY FITTED THERE IN LATE LAST YEAR, AND NOBODY IN THAT BUILDING HAS SPOKEN TO HER ABOUT ANY PART OF THAT DOOR.**
