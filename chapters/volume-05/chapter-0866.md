# Chapter 0866 — A Stone Over A Hand

Sunday the third of December 2023, at the edging heap at number twelve from the middle of the day until the flags have gone the colour of the sky. One stone off that heap and the width of it.

**THERE IS A STONE OFF THE TOP OF THE EDGING HEAP AGAINST THE WALL AT NUMBER TWELVE AND SHE MEANS TO BRING BOTH HANDS TOGETHER ON IT FROM THE TWO ENDS UNTIL THE FINGERS OF ONE HAND MEET THE THUMB OF THE OTHER, AND HER SISTER IS IN AT THE YARD GATE STANDING AT THE CORNER OF THE SHED WITH HER ARMS FOLDED.**

The heap is the six feet of edging that came off the front bed in the spring and has been standing against the wall ever since with the blue string over the top of it.

What she has been putting off since the Saturday is not which of the stones are the five bad ones. That has been done, once, and it stands. It is whether one of them can be laid at all by one person, and that is a different question and it is a question about her hands.

Because a hand is a rule for a flat thing.

That is the whole of it. You can close a hand on a board, or a length of bar, or a pipe, or the side of a kerbstone, and the hand is the same width every time you do it and the answer comes out the same in the morning and at night and in a wet week. A hand is useless on a thing that is not the same width all the way along, and a stone out of a bed is not that.

An edging stone is a piece of the ground.

---

She takes one off the top of the heap and sets it on its side on the flags in front of the wall with the top face up, because the top is the face that shows, and the top is the face that has to be the right width when it is laid.

There is no wear on it anywhere. There has never been any wear on it, because it has not been anywhere, and it is worth saying that out loud in the yard because it is the reason a heap of six feet of stone sits against a wall for nine months without anybody knowing a thing about it that cannot be told by looking.

What she wants to know is a number.

It is not square.

The top of it is a foot and a bit. The bottom of it, where it would sit on the course below, is about four inches, and there is a long slant from one to the other on each side of it, and that slant is not a cut. Nothing cut it. It came out of the bed in a block and it was broken into lengths and the break ran where the stone wanted to run, and what is left of the original shape is still in it.

So the stone is a foot and a bit at the top and four inches at the foot and about six inches in the middle, and it wants two hands at one end and one hand at the other.

She tries it, the whole way, both hands from the two ends until the fingers of one met the thumb of the other, and it is right.

Then she tries it on the next one.

That one is a foot and a half at the top and the same four inches at the foot, and it is a stone that was cut out of a different part of the bed and taken a different way, and the two of them came out of the same six feet of ground within thirty yards of each other.

A third one is narrower than either of them, and its foot is three inches and its top is eleven, and the slant on it is so long that she gets her thumbs and not her fingers round it at the wide end.

A fourth has been under the string since it came out of the bed and there is a line of grit across the top of it where two stones have sat on it, and the grit has kept the rain off one half and not the other and there is a difference in the colour of the two halves that her thumb finds before her eye does.

That is the finding, and it is a small one, and it stops the question she started with.

The stones in that heap are not a size.

A heap of anything that has been dug out of one place has no size in it. It has a range, and the range is the difference between the easiest piece of the bed and the hardest, and no two of them in a barrowload will be the same. Eleven good ones and five bad, and there is no reason on earth for any two of the good ones to match each other, because they were not cut to a size, they were taken out of the ground. A quarrier sells you a size. A bed does not have one.

So a run of that edging laid in the March is going to be laid from the front of the bed backwards.

That is the part she had not thought about. You stand at the front of a bed to lay a run because you want to see the course you have already laid and the course you are laying at the same time, and from the front the top of a stone goes out of your sight the moment you set it. And with these stones, the top is the widest part. Every one of them will want to go on with its wide face out and its narrow face under, and a stone laid the other way up is a stone with a head on it that lets the rain sit in the joint behind it, and in the first winter that stone is the one that moves.

It is also a job that cannot be done in a morning by anybody on her own, because a stone that wants two hands at the top and one at the foot is a stone you set with the heel of one hand and the fingers of the other and then get the arm under it to rock, and the arm is the thing that goes first, and it went last March on a different bed.

---

Her sister comes in at the yard gate in the middle of the afternoon and stands at the corner of the shed with her arms folded and does not come over to the heap.

Word went back along the shed that the coal had not come, and the answer came back that Tuesday was the day, which was what came back in October as well and would come back in about as many weeks as that. The shed door, she wanted to know, was sticking. It was not sticking. It stuck in the summer.

She stood there in the same place until the light had gone off the flags and then went out at the back gate, and the heap was twenty feet away from her on the other side of the wall, and she did not go round to it.

Not one word of it was about the stones. She did not look at them and she did not turn round towards the heap once in the time she stood there, and the wall was between the two of them the whole length of the yard, which is a wall that has been there since the house was built and is the reason two people can stand twenty feet apart in the same yard and be having two entirely different conversations without either of them being dishonest.

The heap is against the wall and the string is over the top of it and the stones are in front of it on the flags, and that is the whole of what the flags show to anybody standing on them at the end of an afternoon.

Four inches at the foot and eleven good ones in that heap under a string that has not moved since the spring, and every one of them wanting two hands.
