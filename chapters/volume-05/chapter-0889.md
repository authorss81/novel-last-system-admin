# Chapter 0889 — What The Hand Stops Against

Thursday the twenty-ninth of February 2024, at the cold frame at the end of the bed at number ten, while the boards are still in the wet. What a hand stops against in a bed of soil.

**SHE HAS A COLD FRAME STANDING AT THE END OF THE BED AT NUMBER TEN WITH NO GLASS IN IT SINCE NOVEMBER, AND SHE MEANS TO LAY ONE HAND FLAT ON THE SOIL OF THAT BED AND PUSH IT FORWARD UNTIL IT STOPS, AND HER SISTER IS AT THE YARD GATE COMING IN AND GOING DOWN THE PATH.**

The frame has been at the end of that bed for three seasons.

It is a timber cold frame about four feet by two, made of a bottom rail on three sides and a sloping side at the south end, so that the glass faces the low sun, and it stands on the soil of that bed with the middle of its bottom rail an inch above the soil and the two ends of it clear.

She has wanted to know why it has not sunk.

A thing like that is supposed to sink. The bottom rail of a cold frame is four inches of softwood with the grain running along it, and a bed is loose soil to the depth of a spade, and two winters of frost and a few watering cans will take a frame like that four inches into the ground without anybody touching it.

This one has gone in about an inch and no more in three seasons, and it is not level in its bed, and the north side is sitting lower than the south.

None of that can be got at from outside.

So the hand goes flat on the soil of the bed and pushes forward.

The soil is in a bed that gets dug every year, so the hand goes in to the first joint without any trouble, and then it keeps going.

What stops it is not the frame.

The hand goes in about two inches and then comes on something hard, and the fingers feel their way round it and find that it is a brick.

A hand pushed forward until it stops is not the same job as a hand pushed forward along a thing, and the difference is in the stopping. A hand that travels keeps its answer moving. A hand that stops has hit something, and what it has hit is underneath it.

A brick, lying flat, under the frame.

It is under the bottom rail on the south side, about two thirds of the way along, and it is bedded in the soil the way a brick is bedded when somebody puts one down without any particular thought about it: on its face, flat, level, and it is the whole of what carries that side of the frame.

Which is a brick, and nothing else. Somebody wanted that frame not to sink, had a brick, and put it down.

Then the hand goes in again on the same line further along, to find out whether there is only the one, and there is only the one.

Then the hand goes to the other side, where the frame has sunk an inch, and the soil there is loose and soft to a good depth and the frame rail goes down into it and stops on nothing at all.

One brick, on one side, at two thirds of the length.

Then the hand goes back to the south side and feels round the brick again at its near edge, and finds that the soil has closed in against the brick and gone into the chip in its corner, which means the brick has gone down a quarter of an inch since it was put there, because a brick does not settle into soil that gets dug twice a year without the soil closing in round it.

Which puts a date on the brick. Nothing about it suggests an old thing buried when the bed was made. It went down in the first year the frame was there, and it has moved a quarter of an inch in the two years since.

---

Her sister comes in through the yard gate from the path side and goes straight down the path towards the shed without stopping at the frame.

She wanted to know whether the shop had been telephoned about the cold frame glass, because she has been thinking about that frame since the day the box came back empty, and she would put the pea sticks in it the first week of March if the glass ever arrived.

She was told the shop had not been telephoned and that the order is on the account.

"Then it will sit on the account until the year ends."

"It is in a book."

"So is the money."

She stood at the end of the path by the shed and looked back down the bed for a moment, and then said the frame was standing at a funny angle and would want doing before the sticks went in, and she went into the shed and came out with a coil of wire over her arm and went back up the path.

---

A cold frame that is going to last has a base under it. Three courses of brick on edge laid round the footprint, or a timber kerb sat on the ground and screwed to it, and the frame then stands on the kerb and the kerb takes the weight and the bed never touches the frame.

The frame at the end of that bed has no base, because it was bought as a kit, and a kit frame comes as four pieces and a set of screws and no base at all, since the base is the part you make yourself and everybody makes it later or not at all.

Which is why it stands at the end of the bed and not along the side. The sloping side wants the south, and a frame along the side of a bed would take a foot off a bed that has to be walked along.

So the frame stands on a brick.

That explains a frame that ought to be in the ground and is not.

A cold frame with no base goes into the bed. The rail settles into the loose soil and the frame sits down and stays sat, and after a winter or two the soil is level with the top of the rail on the outside and the frame is warm partly because it is a frame and partly because the ground all round it has closed on it.

This frame has a brick under one side of it, so the south rail is carried on that brick and has been carried on it for three seasons, and the north rail has gone into the bed as far as loose soil will let it, which is about an inch.

That inch is the whole of the angle in that frame. Four feet of frame standing an inch out of level is a finger's width of lean at the top, which is what her sister saw from the path, and it is not going to get less.

What is under the brick matters too, and it is the part that gives it a date.

The brick is soft, red, with a chip off one corner, and it is nothing like a paving brick. It is a brick out of something, laid on its face in a bed of soil that has been dug over it twice since.

The soil inside the frame is about two inches below the soil outside it. The soil inside has not been dug, because a frame four feet by two at the end of a bed is the one part of that bed nobody walks over with a spade in the way.

The soil outside gets dug every year and turned over and gets a little bigger every time it is turned, and it has come up over the seasons until it is within an inch of the top of the brick.

So the brick is carrying a cold frame off the ground for as long as the soil outside stays below it, and the soil outside will be level with the top of that brick inside two years, and the day it is level with it the brick stops carrying anything at all.

The timber is sound and the brick is sound and the bed under the north rail is sound enough to take a base when one gets made, which is a day's work in April with two people and not a job for one.

Whatever gets made under that frame will be square and level and the frame will sit on it properly, and then the glass will go in and bed on a rebate that runs true, which is the last thing about that frame that is wrong now and the first thing about it that will be right.

One brick is carrying that frame off the ground.
