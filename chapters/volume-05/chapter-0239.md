# Chapter 0239 — Not the Man

Wednesday the eleventh of May 2016, from about eleven in the morning, the first-floor corridor in a building by the water in Tideglass, and a parade of shops.

**THE ASK WAS SAID OUT LOUD BY THE WRONG MAN AND COULD NOT BE TAKEN BACK, AND THE ONLY THING HE COULD DO WITH IT WAS GIVE IT TO SOMEBODY WHO IS BETTER AT IT, AND HE HAD DONE THAT ONCE, AND THE SECOND TIME IS NOT THE SAME AS THE FIRST TIME BECAUSE HE HAS NOT RUN OUT OF ANYTHING AT ALL.**

Nell Ardent was at the end of the first-floor corridor with her bag on the floor and a sheet of A4 in her own handwriting in the other hand, and he said the ask to her in one breath, standing up, without stopping to put it in the right order first, which is the way he has said every true thing since the summer.

"I want this borough to say out loud that if there's a regional emergency, its own crews go out of the borough before anything in the borough gets done."

Nell Ardent did not say anything for about four seconds, and then she said, "No," which was not the answer he had prepared for and was not the answer she gave.

"That's not—"

"No, that's not no. That's hold on." She put the sheet down on the sill. "You can't be the one who says that. Not you, not in that room, not with your face on it. Say it again in my head. You are the man who has seen all three of those pieces of paper. There is nobody in this borough or out of it who has seen all three, and you have seen all three, and every single person in a room on Carrow Lane knows that. So the second that word goes out of your mouth, whatever anybody thinks about the ask, what they will actually have heard is the man who read the papers asking this borough to give up its first call. And in about two years that will be the version. There'll be no paper to put next to it and correct it with, because there is no paper about this at all."

"That's the whole of what I'm worried about."

"That's the whole of it, and you knew it before you said it, and you said it anyway, because you wanted it to be said and you didn't want to be the one holding it afterwards." She picked the sheet up again. "And you cannot stand here and offer me that other thing either. Don't. I know what it is. You've still got more of the one thing than anybody in this borough and there's no shortage of the one thing. The problem isn't that you've run out. The problem is that you're the man, and being the man is the whole of the difficulty."

He did not say it. He had it ready, and it had been the truest thing he had said about himself since the summer, and it was not true today, and he let it go past without slowing down for it.

"Then who," he said.

"Ask Mrs Obi."

"Mrs Obi takes the minutes on a pad she carries home in a handbag."

"Mrs Obi," said Nell Ardent, "is the only person in this borough who has ever asked another person in a room what they were asking for and then waited. That's not taking minutes. That's the other thing, and about nine people in two halls have watched her do it without knowing what it is."

He gave her the notebook. That was the price of it and it was not a small one, because it has been in his inside pocket since the summer and it is the only thing he has got, and there is a page at the back of it with about a hundred and thirty words of a man in a cabin in it that he wrote down on Sunday at the door of that cabin.

"That's the whole of what I know," he said. "You can't carry what I know, so you read what I wrote. There's a bit in the middle about a room in a corridor that I got wrong on the day and should never have written down, and you can leave that out, and I can't, because it's all the same hand."

---

Mrs Obi came out of the hall off the parade at about half past three with her pad under her arm, and Jonas Mercer was on the pavement, and he said the ask to her in the road, standing up, in one breath, the second time in one day.

She did not say no. That was not what she did.

"Say it again and slower," she said, "and leave out the bit about it starting now, because whoever put that in your mouth has been in this borough four months."

He said it again.

"Then tell me why you're asking me."

"Because I've read the papers."

"Because you've read the papers," said Mrs Obi. "That is the first honest thing anybody has said to me about any of this in a year, and it is also the reason you are not the one to ask. If you ask a room for that, the room's answer is about you, and the answer you want is about the low end of Peverell Street, and there's no way of getting from one to the other in a room on a Sunday." She shifted the pad under her arm. "Right. Now go away, and don't tell anybody you asked me, and I'll decide what to do with it on a Sunday when I've got nine people in front of me and not one day before."

"That's not yes."

"That's not no," said Mrs Obi, "and you've about three days to get used to that, and I'll tell you now while it's free, because you'd spend a week turning it over. Most of this borough is going to do exactly this, and then wonder afterwards why nothing happened, and it isn't going to be anybody's fault, and there'll be a version of it put about afterwards that says a man who reads things asked us to give up our first call. I don't want that version. You'll want it less than I do."

After that he was on the parade, where the shutter on the shop goes up and comes down three times in twenty years, and the window card with the shop's own telephone number on it and nothing else on it is still in the window where it has always been.

Hanna Wray asked him about the price of a standpipe, and he told her, and it was a long answer, and she wrote it down on the back of a delivery docket with a pen off her own bag, and neither of them mentioned anything else.

Tomas Renn wanted to know whether a Thursday crew took a first hour for setting up when they'd been in a yard all morning, and Jonas Mercer worked it out standing up against a wall with a biro on the back of his hand and told him, and Renn said that was the figure he had, and went back inside.

A man he did not know asked him, from about ten feet away and without any preamble, whether the low end was going to be cut off the list, and Jonas Mercer said he had not heard of a list, and the man said there was always a list of some sort, and went off up the parade.

After that he was on the parade twice a week for a fortnight or so and nobody asked him anything at all, and the parade was exactly as it had always been, and he found out everything else the way everybody else in this borough finds out about things, which is by standing in a shop when somebody says it.

**AND HE SAID THE ASK OUT LOUD TWICE AND IT WAS WRONG BOTH TIMES AND HE COULD NOT UNSAY IT EITHER TIME, AND HE GAVE THE NOTEBOOK TO A WOMAN WHO CARRIES A BAG, AND ON THE ELEVENTH OF MAY HE DID A SUM AGAINST A WALL THAT CAME TO FIVE HUNDRED AND THIRTY-TWO, AND THE ASK IS GOING TO BE ASKED BY SOMEBODY ELSE, AND HE WILL BE STANDING IN A CORRIDOR ON THE TWENTIETH.**
