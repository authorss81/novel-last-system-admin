# Chapter 0688 — She Gets Up On It Beside Her Own Shed

Monday the thirteenth of December 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house at number ten from first light until dark with nobody in it, and the whole of the day is a bit of ply in a drawer that does not do it and a step ladder she has to carry up a road she has never walked.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES TELLS A MAN WHO COMES DOWN HER BACK WITH A SACK ON A BARROW THAT SHE HAS GOT A LADDER, HE ASKS HER ONCE WHERE SHE GOT IT AND SHE SAYS OFF HER SISTER AND HE DOES NOT ASK THE NEXT QUESTION, AND SAYS HE HAS NOT GOT ONE AND DOES NOT WANT ONE, SHE GETS UP ON IT BESIDE HER OWN SHED AND FINDS THE FELT GONE AT THE FRONT EDGE SO THAT A BIT OF PLY WILL NOT DO IT, LEAVES THE PLY IN THE DRAWER ON TOP OF FOUR INCHES OF TAPE, AND CARRIES THE LADDER UP THE ROAD AND STANDS IT AGAINST A SIDE WALL AND KNOCKS ONCE.**

She is a woman in her early thirties. She is at home all day. A man from the coal yard comes down the back.

---

The ladder is in her hall where the post goes, between the bowl of keys and a bit of card with the window deliveries on it, and it has been there since Wednesday evening when her sister carried it round on a hip and put it down and would not stay for the tea.

Her sister carried it round from number nine on the Wednesday night in the dark and put it down in the hall and would not stay for the tea. She has not been able to tell anybody what she found out about it, and what she found out about it was a strip of tape on an aluminium rail and a chalk mark on a tread and nothing else at all.

There is no number on it. There is no street on it. There is no name on it and no paper of any kind stuck to it and no gummed strip with anything written on it.

That is the whole of what she knows, and she has been carrying it round her own house since Wednesday, on a hip, and putting it down when she has both hands full and picking it up again when she does not.

---

The outside light over her back door is on a bracket and she put it up herself in the spring and it works, and she goes and switches it on every Saturday morning and stands and looks at it and switches it off again.

Today is not Saturday so she does not touch it, and she finds herself not touching it about half way through the morning, which is the sort of thing that happens in a house where a person has one arrangement with a switch and it runs the week.

The bin is out at the kerb with the lid shut. She has paid for a collection on a rota since the spring and the day is on a card she does not have, and she has stopped trying to work the day out of a kitchen calendar because a kitchen calendar in December in a house with one person in it is a thing you stop doing, and the bin stays out.

---

The man from the coal yard comes down the back with a sack on a barrow, which he does about once a month and has done since the spring, and he has not knocked because he has never once knocked.

He puts it outside the back door and she goes out and he is looking over the fence at the shed.

"That shed of yours."

"I know."

"It's still doing your roof in."

"I know it's still doing my roof in."

"You got the ply on it?"

She looks at him.

"I said it was a bit of ply," he says. "That was the whole of it. That was the entire conversation I had with you back in November and I want it on record that I said a bit of ply."

"I haven't got it on."

"Why haven't you got it on?"

And that is the moment, and she could say the drawer, and she could say the tape, and she could say that there is a bit of ply in a drawer in her kitchen that has been on top of a tape roll since the sixth of November.

"I've got a ladder," she says instead.

---

He turns round.

"Where have you got a ladder from?"

"Off my sister."

"Right." He takes his hands out of his pockets and picks up the barrow handle. "Well. If you've got a ladder, that's a bit better than what I said, because what I said was a board across the top and a board across the top of that is no use if the felt's gone at the front. It'll be gone at the front. It's always gone at the front on one of these, they lay them from the back and the front edge is the first thing to go."

"What do I do about the front edge?"

"You pull the front edge up and you put a new bit of felt on it and you go over it with a bit of the same stuff and you do it dry." He says the word dry the way a man says a thing he is picturing. "That's a proper job for anybody who knows and it's nothing to a man on a ladder, and I've not got a ladder, and I'd not go up on one of those roofs, and that's not a thing I want saying out loud to you in your own back door in December."

"No."

"So you'll do it yourself, then."

"I'll do it myself."

"You'll be up on it in the wet, mind."

"I know I'll be up on it in the wet."

---

He does not ask where her sister got the ladder.

That is the whole of it and she waits for it, standing in her own back step with a man and a barrow, and it does not come, and he is not avoiding anything. He asked her where she got it and she said off her sister and that closed it, and what he does not ask is where her sister got it, because a man does not ask a second question about a ladder in a gateway, and if she had offered it he would have said *right* and gone.

She goes in and gets the drawer and leaves it open where it was.

---

The drawer is under the worktop and it has the hammer in it and the screwdriver and the tape roll with about four inches of brown tape left on it and a bit of ply about the width of the worktop, and the ply has been on top of the tape since the sixth of November, and the hammer and the screwdriver have not been out since September.

She does not use any of the three.

She gets the ladder and carries it out through the back door into her own yard, and the back door shuts true behind her and makes the sound it makes, and the yard is about the width of a car and a bit longer, and there is no room at all in it to open a folding step ladder.

She goes to the front instead. She goes out of the front door with it and along the front path and round the side, and the front door she leaves on the latch, which is the only door in her house she can do that with.

---

The shed is a manky shed and it has been a manky shed since before she had it and it is the one thing in the back of that house that is not inside the house.

She opens the ladder out on the front path where there is room, and it comes open all at once the way it came open for her sister, and the second rail has the tape round it and the third tread has the chalk mark on it.

She carries it to the side of the shed and sets it up on the paving.

---

From the top tread she can see the front edge of that roof and it is not a board and it is not a mess. It is a shed roof with felt on it that has been on long enough to have gone the colour of a dirty cloth, and at the front edge, along about a third of the width, the felt has lifted and curled and come away from the wood underneath it, and the wood underneath is grey and the arris has gone round on it, and where the felt has come away there is a black line of dirt in the boards that says exactly how long it has been letting water in.

The bit of ply would have gone across the middle, on the middle, where the felt is still good and where there is nothing at all to fix.

She stands on the top tread and looks at it.

---

Then she comes down and puts the ladder against the side of the house and goes and gets the ply out of the drawer and gets it up against the front edge of that shed from the ground.

It is not wide enough and it is not long enough and the edge of it is not straight, and it would sit about a third of the way back from the front edge, on the part of the roof that does not leak.

She goes in and puts it back in the drawer on top of the tape.

She shuts the drawer, which is the first time she has shut that drawer since she put the ply in it, and the drawer does not have a thing in it of its own, it is just where the tools live, and there is no list in that house and there has never been one.

Here is what she could do about the front edge of that roof and she does not do any of it.

She could go to the man at number eleven with the workshop who had a bit of felt on his bench all summer. Her sister said that on Wednesday and it is a good idea and it is a door and a sentence and a piece of money.

She could go back down the back and ask the man from the coal yard, who is probably still on the road with his barrow, where to buy a roll of the right felt, which he would tell her for nothing, in about four words, and then forget about it.

She could get the ladder back out and put her own hand on the front edge of that roof and find out how bad it is properly, in daylight, with both hands, which is what she has just done from eight foot away and could do from three foot away if she stood on the top tread and leaned.

She does not do any of them, and the reason is that all four of them end at a person, and she has got a sister who will do a thing if she is asked and a man with a barrow who will do a thing if he is asked, and she is on her own all day in a house where the front door is on the latch because it is the only door she can do that with.

The shed keeps doing her roof in.

That is at the end of the back and there is a draught off the top of it, and a man has said so twice now, once in November and once today, and she has a bit of ply in a drawer and a hammer and a screwdriver and four inches of brown tape and a step ladder she cannot give back.

She looks at the ladder for a while.

She cannot give it back because there is nobody to give it back to. It came off a porch. It came off a woman who took it out of a porch because it was raining, and that woman cannot give it back either, and behind her there is a man at a bench opposite somewhere in this borough who lent it to a stranger in a street on a Monday morning and did not say when.

She could put it in that porch. She has not been past that porch since Wednesday. She does not even know the number of that house, because she did not ask, and her sister did not say, and the whole of the arrangement between the two of them on that Wednesday evening was a woman saying *put it back* and a woman saying *I can't put back what I can't give*.

---

What she does with it is this.

She gets it folded, which takes some doing on its own, and she gets it up on her shoulder, and she goes out of her own front door and along her own front path and out of her own gate.

And then she walks up the road.

---

There is a house at the end of that road and she has known about it since the summer. She has seen it from her own back step with a bin out, which is the way anybody on that side sees it, and she has seen the whole front of it dark in the middle of the morning while there is a lamp on all day over a gate at the end, and she has looked at both of those things from her own yard and has not said anything to anybody about either of them.

She has never been up that road.

That is the whole of what she is doing and there is nobody watching her do it and no reason on earth for her to be doing it, because a step ladder is not a parcel and there is no number on it and it is not going to be any use to anybody she has met.

It takes longer than she thinks it will, because the road goes down and then it does not go back up, and she has a step ladder on her shoulder and it is heavier at the top end than she expected, and by the time she gets to the last twenty yards her shoulder has gone from an annoyance into a thing she is aware of every step.

The gate is standing back against the wall of a coal shed and the path is clear and the road has nobody on it and the front door is about six feet back with a step and a light over it that is on in the middle of the day.

She puts the ladder down against the side wall of that house, between the door and the corner, standing on its folded feet so it does not go over.

And she knocks on the front door.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES HAS SAID *I'VE GOT A LADDER* TO A MAN WHO HAD SAID A BIT OF PLY AND HAS FOUND FROM THE TOP TREAD OF IT THAT THE FELT IS GONE AT THE FRONT EDGE SO THAT A BIT OF PLY WILL NOT DO IT, HAS PUT THE PLY BACK IN THE DRAWER ON TOP OF FOUR INCHES OF TAPE, HAS ASKED NOBODY ANYTHING, HAS WALKED UP A ROAD SHE HAS NEVER WALKED IN HER LIFE, AND IS STANDING ON A DOORSTEP WITH NOTHING TO SAY.**
