# Chapter 0328 — The Bin Day

Sunday the eleventh of June 2017, from about a quarter past nine in the morning until about a quarter past eleven, the bottom of the Cleeve road, a parade off it, and a shop on that parade, in Tideglass.

**TWO MEN WHO HAVE NEVER SPOKEN TOLD TWO STRANGERS ON ONE MORNING THAT THE RUBBISH ON THAT STRETCH IS GOING ON A DIFFERENT DAY, AND THE TWO THINGS THEY SAID CANNOT BOTH BE RIGHT, AND A WOMAN WHO HEARD BOTH OF THEM SAID THAT NEITHER OF THEM WAS NOISE.**

The stop at the bottom of the Cleeve road is a pole and a plate and nothing over your head at all, with the printed times going soft behind plastic that somebody has scored with a key, and on a Sunday morning about nine it is used by about four people an hour.

The man in a fleece was standing at it at about a quarter past nine with a cup from the bakery at the corner in his hand, and there was a woman at it with a folded umbrella over her arm and a bag on the pavement beside her, waiting for a number that was about four minutes out.

"There'll be a bit of a change to your bin day," said the man in a fleece. "They're moving it off the Thursday. That parade's going onto a different day, middle of the month, and it's the road works, and nobody's told anybody anything about it, so you'll find out when they don't come."

"How do you know that," said the woman with the folded umbrella.

"Guy at the works."

"What road works."

"The top end, by the junction," said the man in a fleece, and drank his coffee, and did not say more about it, and got a look on his face which is the thing he does when he has come to the end of what he knows.

The woman with the folded umbrella said that was that then, and picked her bag up off the pavement, and neither of them said another word, and the man in a fleece went on up the Cleeve road with his cup.

---

The parade off the Cleeve road has a shutter on the unit at the far end of it and the man who puts shutters up was out on that parade at about ten because he had four to do on a Sunday morning before the weather, and his ladder was standing against the front of the closed shop with the top of it about a foot below the fascia.

There was a woman leaning on the front of the shop next door with her arms folded, waiting on somebody in a car, and she had been standing there when he came down off his ladder.

"Since yesterday," she said.

"Since yesterday," he said.

"They've put a printed thing on my door."

"They've put one on about sixty doors on that parade and about the same up the Cleeve road," said the man who puts shutters up, "and it came round on the Thursday, and I've been up and down that parade for twenty-two years and I can tell you what it says, which is that the collection on that parade is moving from the Thursday to the Tuesday from the middle of the month, and that's the whole of it."

"Is it the whole of it?"

"That's the whole of what's on it."

The man who puts shutters up got his ladder and folded it and stood with it against his leg.

"What I would say is it's not just that parade," he said. "It's the whole of the end of the street. That's how they do it. They take a run of them and they move the whole run, because if you move one street and not the next one, then you've got a street where nothing comes and a street where it always comes, and then somebody rings up about the one where nothing comes. So it's not one parade. It's the top of the Cleeve road and that side and about two hundred yard back, and it's the Tuesday from the middle of the month."

"And how would you know that," said the woman with her arms folded.

"Because I've been on that top of the Cleeve road since about the middle of March, and I go up and down it every day of the week," said the man who puts shutters up. "I've seen two men with a roll of them on the Thursday and they were not stopping at the parade."

The woman with her arms folded said that it did not matter to her either way because the next one was Tuesday anyway, and went in.

---

The woman who works in a shop is on the front of that shop four days a week and she is not on the front of it on a Sunday, and she was in at about a quarter past ten with a mop and a bucket, and the man who puts shutters up came down the parade past her and stopped, and she said good morning, and he said good morning, and then he said, out of nothing, the same thing he had just said to the woman outside the closed shop.

"I've told that woman it's not just that parade."

"You've told her it's the top of the road," said the woman who works in a shop.

"It is the top of the road."

"A woman with a folded umbrella stopped me in the road as I came up from the bus stop and told me that at a quarter past nine this morning a man told her at the bottom of the Cleeve road that it was the Thursday that was moving and it was the road works at the junction, and he said it was that parade," said the woman who works in a shop. "He was in this shop on the Thursday and he said it at the counter then, and the notices had only just come round that day, and I have not been out of this shop this morning and I could not tell you where he got his from, and I am not going to stand here and tell you he made it up, because a man in a fleece goes where he likes on a Sunday. He said the Thursday was moving."

"They move it one day forward or one day back and the men who say so don't know which," said the man who puts shutters up. "The Thursday moving is the same as the Tuesday starting if you count the other way, is it not."

"That's not remotely the same," said the woman who works in a shop, "because one of those is a change and the other one is a date, and one of those is that parade and the other one is two hundred yard, and the two of those are about eight hundred apart on that road, and only one of the two of you was in the road."

He stood in that shop with his ladder against his leg and did not answer that.

"No," he said. "Only one of us was in the road."

The woman who works in a shop got her mop out of the bucket and wrung it.

"It came on the Thursday and I never read it," she said. "It's on the door of that shop and it was on the door of yours and it's a bit of card with a picture of a lorry on it, and about nine out of ten people on that parade would have walked past it, and I'm one of them. If I hadn't been the one you came past, I would have walked past it too."

"I didn't read mine," said the man who puts shutters up.

"You said a day and a date off it."

"I said the day and the date because I had it in my head, and I had it in my head because I'd had it off two men on a roll on the Thursday, and I did not take that bit of card off my door and I did not read it, and I took it off the door because it was under the foot of my ladder and it was in the way and I put it in my pocket, and it was in my pocket on the Friday and in the bin on the Friday night, and that is the whole of what happened to it."

"Well," said the woman who works in a shop.

She carried her mop out into the shop floor and got on with it, and the man who puts shutters up went off down the parade carrying his ladder.

Here is the whole of what is known about how those notices came.

It came on the Thursday in the first week of June on a lorry with nothing on the side of it that anybody in that parade had seen before, and four men got off it at the top of the Cleeve road with a roll of a printed thing, and they put one on every door they came to, and if nobody came to the door they put it on anyway and went on, and they were down that parade in about three quarters of an hour. They were not in the shop. They were not in the bus shelter. Nobody in either of those two places saw a man get out of a lorry, and the man who puts shutters up was on a house about two hundred yard off at the time and the man in a fleece was at the works.

There are about sixty of them on about sixty doors and the ones that are not under a ladder or a bin or somebody's elbow are still on the doors, and the sun has got to the ink on the ones that face the weather and they have gone the colour of the door underneath them.

The man who works in that shop took one off the outside of that shop himself, on the Thursday week, when he brought the bins in, and it came off in his hand and he put it in the bin with the paper because it was in the way of the lid, and he has never read it and there is nobody in that shop who has.

The man in a fleece came into that shop at about half past ten that morning on his way to put his cup in the bin outside, and he had not been in since the Thursday, and the woman who works in a shop was on the floor with a mop.

"You've had a man in with a ladder."

"He came past."

"He'll have told that parade's collection's moving."

"He'll have told them it's the Tuesday and the whole of the top of the road," said the woman who works in a shop. "And you told a woman with a folded umbrella at the bottom of the Cleeve road that it was the Thursday that's moving, and it was the road works at the junction, and it was that parade."

"He didn't ask me where from."

"I know he didn't," said the woman who works in a shop. "That's what I want to say to you and I can say it to you because you can't do anything about it. You said a thing to a stranger on a Sunday morning that is not true, or it isn't the whole of what is true, and you'll not find out which, because the only thing on this earth that could tell you is on sixty doors that nobody has read, and the man that said the other one never read one either."

The man in a fleece put his cup in the bin outside and came back in and stood in the doorway.

"I said what the fella at the works said to me."

"I know you did. And that's the end of it and it's the reason I'm not cross," said the woman who works in a shop, "and I want to say the other half of it as well, and then I'm not saying anything else about it. That is not noise. That is what this street talks about. If there was a bomb under that parade you'd have nine people telling me about it by ten o'clock and I'd have four different accounts of it and I'd be stood in this shop working out which of the four was right. It is not noise, but it is not two things either. It's the weather. You stand in a street long enough and you'll hear about the bin day from nine different men and every one of them will be telling you what somebody else told him, and there is nobody in this town whose job it is to be right about it."

The man in a fleece said that was about the size of it, and went, and the woman who works in a shop went on with the floor.

**A PRINTED THING IS ON SIXTY DOORS IN THAT PARADE AND THE ONLY TWO PEOPLE WHO HAVE SAID ANYTHING AT ALL ABOUT WHAT IS ON THEM HAVE NOT READ ONE, AND A WOMAN HAS STOOD AT A PLATE ON A POLE AT THE BOTTOM OF THE CLEEVE ROAD AND HEARD A THING SHE CANNOT CHECK.**
