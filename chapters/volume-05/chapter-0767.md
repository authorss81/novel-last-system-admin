# Chapter 0767 — The Bricks Off The Wall

Tuesday the twenty-fifth of October 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house and yard at number twelve from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a wall coping rebuilt out of the wall itself and four feet of it left with nothing on top.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS A HEAP OF LOOSE BRICK OFF THE TOP OF HER YARD WALL, HER SISTER PUTS IT BACK AS COPING ON THE WALL AND TAKES THE FOUR SOUND BRICKS OUT OF THE SOUND STRETCH TO DO IT, AND FOUR FEET OF HER OWN WALL IS LEFT BARE ALONGSIDE THE COAL SHED.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight at number twelve on Tuesday. Her sister comes to the front step in the morning and comes round to the back with her and does the second half her own way.

---

There is a fire in the stove and the green chair is up to the table where she sits. Square with the edge of the table on her right is the gas envelope, face up, and the taped box is the other side of the table under its cloth, squared on three sides of it. She looks at neither of them long. Out of the back window the heap of cut grass is against the shed wall where it has stood since the summer, greying on the top, and the dark stripe it brought up the brick behind it has gone the colour of weak tea and is not going any further.

The padlock on the coal shed is open in the hasp and the shed door is shut. The gate is propped back against the wall and the lamp over it burns. A brush lies across the runner in the passage and she steps over it.

The yard wall runs along the lane. It is a low wall, about the height of her hip, and it is capped with bricks set on edge so the rain runs off it instead of going into the courses. About three feet of that coping near the gate end came off in the winter, and the bricks from it lie in the grass at the foot of the wall in a heap with lime mortar in pieces the size of a hand, most of it with grass grown through it.

She has a barrow, a bolster and a sack, and she means to put the lot in the sack and get it out to the front before the middle of the day, because that is what loose brick is for and because the grass is coming up through it and will bind the whole of it together by Christmas.

Her sister comes to the front step in the morning with a tea tray under her arm and puts it on the step and does not come in.

---

"Your gate is off its latch again."

"My gate is propped and it is where it has been since September." She has the sack open at the wall. "You want a cup of tea."

"I want to know what you are doing with that brick."

"Taking it out to the front before the grass takes it."

Her sister leaves the tray and comes down the side passage and out into the yard and stands over the heap and looks at the wall above it.

"That is not brick you are taking out. That is the top of your wall."

"It is loose. It is not gone."

"It came off your wall in February and you have had it in the grass since February and you want it out of the grass." Her sister crouches down and picks up two of the bricks and turns them over in her hand. "These two are whole. These two are whole. This one has let go at a corner and this one has run out along the frog. Now look at the side of that one against the side of that one."

"They are the same."

"They are not the same. That wall is a brick and a half and those two are a brick, and somebody rebuilt part of that wall in a different brick, and that is where your loose coping came from."

"They will break up in a truck."

"Four of them will. The rest will sit in your grass until the grass has a mat on it, and the wall above it will have nothing to be finished with when the winter finishes it."

Then she stands up and goes along the wall and runs her hand along the capping, above the heap and away from it, all the way to the far end by the coal shed, and comes back, and points at the far end.

"How much of mine is up there."

"The lot of it. It has been the lot of it since the wall was built."

"Which bit is loose."

"The bit above the heap."

"Show me the bit above the heap that is sound."

---

She cannot do that. That is the whole of the argument and it goes on for most of the morning.

What is on the wall above the heap is a run of about three feet where the mortar has gone and the bricks have shifted and there is a lip along the top where the rain can get in. What is on the wall away from the heap is tight, laid, and the bricks are straight and the frogs are up and the joints are tight, and that is about seven feet of it, right at the end by the coal shed.

"You can take four off that end."

"I can take four off that end."

"You cannot take the top off the end of a wall."

"I am not taking the top off the end of a wall. I am moving it." She puts the two bricks down on the grass. "You have got a run of three feet up there with a lip on it, and the water is going into it, and if it goes one more winter it will take the face off the wall and you will be looking at a lump. And you have got nothing in that heap you could put back in it that will last, because four of those are the wrong thickness and the rest of them are broken. So you take four off the sound end and you put them in the loose end, and then you have a wall with a lip on one half of it and no cap at all on the other half, and by the spring you will have a lump where the lip was and another one at the end where the cap was."

"That is worse than what I have."

"That is the same as what you have, moved. The lip is not where it was. That is all you have done."

---

They do it. It takes the rest of the morning and it is done well, and she will say that.

Her sister takes the four off the sound end with the bolster and a lump of wood to get under it, and sets them in the loose run one at a time with the frogs up and the joints tight and a bit of lime putty under each so the brick does not rock, and she makes the three feet of coping straight with a line across the top of it and a second one down the face, and by the middle of the day the run above the heap is as good as the run that was left, and better than what was there in February.

The broken bricks go back in the heap under the wall and the lime goes with them. The barrow goes out to the front and the sack comes off and stands on the path, the tools go back on the nails by the shed, and the coal shed is shut with the padlock open in the hasp where it always is.

Then she stands in the middle of her own yard and looks at her own wall.

The three feet above the heap are capped and true and will not take any water at all.

The four feet by the coal shed has got nothing on the top of it but the wall itself, standing open to the sky for the first time since the wall was built, and the courses at the top of it are the oldest mortar in the yard.

---

"That will do it."

"That will do it for a while."

"It has not got anything on it."

"It has not got anything on it and it has not had anything on it since the wall went up, and it took all of it before." Her sister comes and stands beside her at the end of the wall. "And the face of that wall is the face the rain comes down, because there is nothing above it and nothing near it, and that mortar is lime putty your grandfather laid. You have moved your water off the gate end and put it on the shed end, and there is more of it this side than there ever was at the gate."

"Then it will lift."

"It will lift in the hard weather." Her sister puts two fingers on the top course and pushes. "That is a frost wall and there is nothing on the top of it, and it will lift a corner where it is loosest and you will have a gap you can get a finger into at the end of the year, and that is the work I have done for you and it is better than your loose three feet and it is not finished."

She stands at the end of her own wall with her hand on the top course and her thumb in the line of the mortar, and the mortar goes down into the wall in one piece, and there is nothing loose about it to get a finger into today, and there will be.

"You have told me it is not finished."

"I have told you it is not finished and I have not promised I would come back." Her sister goes to the side passage for her tray. "You have your coping and you have your heap. Put a bit of polythene on the top of that four feet if the wind gets up, and you will not get it any better than that until somebody takes that wall down to the footings and starts again, and nobody is going to do that to a wall on a lane."
