# Chapter 0707 — The Kettle With The Furred Spout

Thursday the third of March 2022, in this borough. She is in a house she is clearing from first light until dark and the whole of the day is a kettle lifted and a ladder left where it is.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES LIFTS A KETTLE WITH A FURRED SPOUT IN A HOUSE SHE IS CLEARING, LEAVES A FOLDED STEP LADDER ON A LANDING WHERE IT IS BECAUSE IT IS NOT HERS TO MOVE, AND SPEAKS TO A DAUGHTER WITH KEYS ABOUT WHAT GOES ON A VAN AND ABOUT A BOWL EMPTIED MORNING AND DARK.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is in a house she is clearing all day. A daughter with keys is in and out of the house all day. She speaks to her in the kitchen.

---

The white van is at the kerb before there is any light in the street.

She opens up with her own keys and airs the house room by room, front room and kitchen and back room and landing, windows up, doors wide, cold air moving through. She sets the bags by the door in a row, necks tied, labels out where there are labels. The bolster is on her own board in the van with the drawer under the passenger bench shut with the leads and the screws in it, shut since the middle of November. The box is behind the bench under a bag of rags since the twenty-second of October with a number on its label she read twice and wrote nowhere. Somebody else blanket is in a bag of her own rags since the twelfth of November. She leaves all three where they are and gets the pad out.

The job is four hours unpaid and no invoice, as half her jobs are. She writes the figure for the job on the pad and puts no line for anything else on it. The two cuts on both palms are shut now, plaster on one and not on the other.

---

She works round the kitchen from first light to the middle of the day without filling a bag.

The kettle is on the worktop with a furred spout and a stained lid, white body gone yellow at the base. She lifts it and tips the old water into the sink, watching the brown run, and fills it fresh to the line and sets it back without switching it on, handle square to the wall. The water in this house is hard and leaves a rim round everything. She thinks about the bowl under the bath tap at number twelve while she does it, emptied at first light and at dark, and about how a kettle furs and a bowl fills and both go on whether anybody looks at them or not.

She does not take the kettle. What goes on the van is what the daughter says goes on the van. What stays is what stays.

---

At the middle of the day the daughter with keys comes with sandwiches and puts them down on the worktop.

"What goes," she says, pad open, pencil ready.

"Bags by the door. Chair arm blanket stays. Kettle stays. Curtains stay. Nothing off the walls."

The woman in her late forties lifts the kettle again to show the spout.

"See the fur round it," she says. "It wants descaling and nobody has done it. It pours brown for the first cup."

"Leave it," the daughter says. "Mum would not want it passed on."

They eat standing up with their backs to the cold. The blanket is on the chair arm in the front room, folded square, and she has not taken it off the arm. She sorted a front room once in January without taking a blanket off a chair arm and she does it again here, because a blanket that is folded square is a thing that is finished and she does not finish things that are not hers to finish.

---

They go up to the landing after, to look at what is there.

The folded step ladder is on the landing against the wall, shut true, with no tape on it and no chalk on it. She saw a step ladder with tape and chalk in a back room on the seventh of January and said out loud that she could not tell whether it was the same one as the one she borrowed on the twenty-ninth of November. This one has neither mark on it and she can tell straight off that it is not that one and not the one folded under the window in the front room at number twelve.

"Does that go," the daughter says.

"It stays," she says. "It is not mine to move and not yours to give. Leave it where it is."

The daughter nods and writes nothing down, because there is nothing to write. The ladder stays where it is, folded and shut, and neither of them touches it.

---

They carry the bags down between them, one at each end, stair by stair, and stack them by the van in a row out of the wind. The van doors stand open. The frost melts off the roof while they work.

"Your hands," the daughter says, looking at the plaster.

"Both palms," she says. "Two cuts. Shut now."

"Did you ask about it."

"I did not ask anybody. I put plaster on one and left the other and got on with it."

She thinks about the not-asking while she says it, because the not-asking is the engine in her and not outside her. She could have asked the man she borrowed the ladder from for his number and did not, and the not-asking cost a porch and a rain and a road and a front room. She could have asked about the number on the box behind the bench and did not, and the not-asking costs nothing yet and will cost something when it costs it.

---

She spends the end of the day in the kitchen with the kettle cold on the worktop.

The daughter locks the back room and gives her the keys to keep until the van comes, two keys on a ring, labels tied with string. They stand in the kitchen with the bags gone and the blanket square on the chair arm and the kettle furred on the worktop, and the house looks more like itself than it did at first light, curtains straight, sills wiped, which is all she can do for it.

"There was a bowl," she says. "In a house I was in, in July. Under a bath tap. White china. With a cloth beside it, folded square."

"What for."

"To catch the drip. The tap drips since before Christmas and the bowl is emptied at first light and at dark. I saw it in July and I think about it whenever I lift a kettle."

The daughter looks at the kettle.

"Could nobody mend it."

"Somebody could. Mending it would mean asking for hands, and asking would mean saying the job out loud, and she cannot ask since before Christmas. So the water keeps coming and the bowl keeps filling, round and white, waiting where it was not meant to stay."

---

She sorts the back room after the bags go down.

The back room is square and cold with a chair in it and a heap of soft things in the corner, curtains thin at the glass, light grey on the bare boards. She lifts each thing and sets it square, folded or bagged, smoothing each fold with her palm before she sets it down, and writes nothing down. The blanket on the chair arm in the front room stays square, corners true, fringe straight. The blanket on the chair arm in the front room stays square. She does not take it off the arm to shake it. Shaking it would mean finding where to lay it, and the floor is dusty, and the line is out, so she leaves it.

She finds a cup under the chair with a rim of brown in it and washes it at the sink and sets it upside down on the drainer. The water runs brown for a moment and then clear. She thinks about the kettle with the furred spout while it runs. Fur round the lip. Brown for the first cup. Left where it is because passing it on would mean saying it is fit, and nobody says that.

---

The daughter comes back with the van keys and a list on a scrap.

"Read it," the daughter says.

The woman in her late forties reads it. Bags. Chair. Kettle stays. Ladder stays. She hands it back.

"Who wrote this," she says.

"I did," the daughter says. "Mum would want it plain."

They stand in the kitchen with the list between them. The kettle sits cold on the worktop with the fur round its spout. The ladder sits shut on the landing. The blanket sits square on the chair arm. All three stay.

"There was a bowl," she says again, because saying it once did not lay it down. "White china. Under a bath tap. I think about it whenever I run a tap in a house that is not mine."

The daughter folds the list and puts it in her pocket.

"Leave the tap," the daughter says. "We have a van to fill."

---

She sweeps the kitchen at the end of the day with a broom from under the stair.

She sweeps round the table legs and under the worktop and into the corners, pushing the broom flat along the skirting where the dust sits thick, and gathers the dust in a pan and tips it in a bag, tying the neck twice. The floor comes clean where she sweeps and shows the worn lino round the sink where the water has stood, pale ring on dark ground that no sweeping lifts. She sets the broom back under the stair. The house looks more like itself.

She stands at the sink with her hands in the cold water for a moment, palms down, feeling the two cuts pull. Both palms. Plaster on one and not on the other. Shut now. She dries her hands on her trousers and picks up the pad and looks at the figure for the job. Four hours. No invoice. Put down plain.

---

She wipes the sills at the end of the day with a damp rag from the sink.

She wrings it out in both hands and runs it along the kitchen sill and the front room sill in one pass each, pressing into the corners where the dark sits. The sills come clean where she wipes and show the dark at the corners where the water has sat, black line in white paint that stays through every wipe. She rinses the rag twice and hangs it over the tap, ends square. The daughter watches from the door with the list in her pocket.

"That is done," the daughter says.

"That is done," she says. "Table legs swept. Corners swept. Sills wiped. Kettle left. Ladder left. Blanket left square."

She picks up the pad and looks at the figure for the job once more before she puts it away. Four hours. No invoice. Put down plain. The two cuts pull when she closes the pad. Both palms. Shut now.

---

At dark she locks up and gets in the van.

She sits in the cab with the engine off and looks at the shut drawer under the passenger bench without opening it. The bolster is on the leads. The box is behind the bench under the rags. The bag with somebody else blanket in it is set to one side where she put it. The shut drawer stays shut and the box stays shut and the bag stays shut, and the kettle stays on the worktop in the empty house behind her, furred and cold, and the ladder stays on the landing, folded and shut, and the bowl fills under the tap in a bathroom under a landing on the other side of the borough, and all of them wait.
