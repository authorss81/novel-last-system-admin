# Chapter 0956 — Under The Leaning Wall

Friday the twenty-second of November 2024, at the kerb along the foot of the store wall with the rain gone off the roof an hour back. Under a leaning wall.

**THE SHEET HAS COME OFF TRUE AT ONE BATTEN AND NOW STANDS OUT OVER THE KERB AT THE FOOT OF IT, WHICH SHUTS A RUN OF AIR BEHIND THE SHEET AND IN FRONT OF THE KERB, AND THE HAND GOES INTO THAT RUN WITH THE FOUR FINGERS OPENED OUT ONTO THE STONE AND DRIVEN AT IT, TO FIND OUT HOW DEEP THE RUN IS AND WHAT IS AGAINST THE FACE OF THAT STONE.**

The store is one of a pair and its back wall is corrugated sheet on sawn battens over a kerb of old concrete about nine inches high, cast along the foot of the wall when the store was built and gone hard and grey now, with the arris along its top broken away on about a foot in every four.

The sheet above that kerb is bolted to the battens, one bolt in each trough, and the battens themselves cross the wall at intervals of two feet and a half, and each of them is screwed to the wall behind with three plugs. Halfway along that run one of the battens has come off true. The middle fixing has worked out of its plug by most of its length, which a screw in a plug does when the plug was put in a hole that was cut short, and the batten has turned about its two end fixings and taken the sheet with it.

A batten that turns takes the sheet out over the kerb by the same amount, and what that opens is a run of shut air between the back of the sheet and the face of the kerb, the length of the bay, three inches deep at the middle of it and nothing at either end where the sheet comes back onto the stone. At the end of that run the space is about as deep as a thumbnail.

No eye has ever been inside that run at any point along its length, and the only way in is at one end of it, where the sheet bears down onto the top of the kerb and stands off the stone by a finger's width.

---

The hand goes in at that end palm upwards with the fingers together, and then they are opened out sideways against the stone, and then they are driven at it.

The grip goes first at the tips and then along the middle of the fingers, because a finger pressed forward onto a stone face at that angle slides on the pad of it and does not stay where it was put. In about the time it takes to draw one breath the four of them are all sliding, and the hand is in the run and against the kerb and not on it.

Let off, and the fingers find the kerb again in the same place. Not in a different place. The stone has not moved and neither has the hand, and the four pads come back down onto the same four square inches of the same face and know it again exactly as well as they knew it the first time. Doing it twice is the whole of it. Once is a guess about how far away a thing is. Twice, with the grip broken in between and then made again, is a thing being found at the same depth twice over and not one inch further in than it was.

What the second find says is that the run is the same depth under the tips and an inch deeper over the knuckles, because the sheet comes out further at the middle of the bay than it does a foot in from the end of it, and a finger is a lever, and the far end of that lever is where the sheet is.

The kerb was cast in one length the whole way along that wall and finished at the far end against the office wall, and the line of it has never once been broken, because there is nothing on this side of it that wants to be got under. A rainwater pipe comes down the face of the sheet at the far end of that run and discharges over the kerb onto a piece of paving, which is the reason the top edge of the stone is knocked along the whole of that foot and not only where something has been wheeled into it.

The water has been going over the same four inches of stone for as long as the store has been there, and it goes over it and not into anything, because the run is shut at its near end by the sheet bearing on the arris and a sheet bearing on stone does not let water past it.

What is under the kerb is not part of the hand's business and nothing about that line has been dug out, and the ground on the far side of the kerb is the same ground the whole of that yard stands on. The face of the kerb itself is cold and rough and dry under the pads of the fingers, and along the top of it the arris has gone entirely over that last foot, because the edge was broken off by something wheeled into it, and what stands in place of an arris is a curve.

Under the run, on the flags, there is nothing. The kerb stands straight along that run and has not been lifted and the flags beside it have not been dug out, and the space between the flags and the ground under them is not part of what the hand is in. It was poured as a single length and not laid in separate blocks, and nothing on that wall has been moved since the store went up. Its top face is about four inches wide and it was struck off level with a float while it was going off, and the float left the surface with enough sand in the top of it to take a knock without chipping, which is why the edge survives where nothing has hit it.

A kerb like that is bedded the way the stanchion plate is bedded four feet along the same wall, and it is the reason a sheet of that wall stands nine inches clear of the ground and not nothing. The battens above it are stiff, which is the whole of what such a piece is for, and the sheet above those is stiffer, and the run is closed top and bottom and at the two ends by the sheet bearing down on the stone, which makes it the one place on the whole of that wall where air is shut in, and the only place a hand could go and come back out of.

The fixings that came out of that batten are not new and are not loose in their holes. A plug of the kind that sets hard and a screw turned into it do that once in ten years, and the plug comes out with the screw still in it and whole, and the screw comes out with the plug still in the wall. None of that is a fixing letting go, and it is nothing like a batten being loose in the wall, which is what a person looking at that sheet out on the drive would take it for. What has come loose is nothing. The batten is screwed at two ends into plugs that have never moved in their holes, and in the middle it has turned about those two fixings, and a batten that turns is still fixed and only not straight.

A bay of sheet on three-inch battens at two and a half feet has about two and a half feet of batten to flex across and four inches of its own depth, and it is stiff across that and nothing like stiff along it, which is why it has kept the set it has taken and has not shaken itself straight in a season of wind.

---

The woman from two doors down is standing at the foot of the rainwater goods further along the same wall, with her back to the channel and her face to the yard, and she stops well short of it.

She stands there for a while looking up at the roof edge of the store, and once she puts her weight onto one foot and takes it off again without moving any other part of herself, and beyond her the yard carries on with nobody else in it. She leaves again the way she arrived, and the flags under the kerb are as dry as they were, because the rain went off the store roof an hour ago along the channel and past the foot of that wall without going into anything at all. That run under the leaning sheet is three inches deep in the middle of the bay and shut at both ends, and its floor is four inches of the face of an old kerb with the top of it broken away over the last foot.

The face of that kerb is four inches wide and three of those inches are colder than the rest of it, and a person can find the same square inch of it twice over, the moment the grip goes off it and the moment it comes back.
