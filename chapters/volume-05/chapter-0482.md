# Chapter 0482 — Fourteen Yard

Monday the first of April 2019, from about ten past eight in the morning until about a quarter past ten. A passage between two houses, in Tideglass.

**IN THE MIDDLE OF FEBRUARY A WOMAN SAID ONE TRUE THING TO ANOTHER WOMAN IN A MARKET AND WENT HOME AND DID NOT THINK ABOUT IT AGAIN, AND ON THE FIRST OF APRIL A MAN CAME OUT OF A HOUSE AT THE TOP OF THAT PASSAGE AND WENT DOWN IT AND DID NOT GO BACK UP, AND THE WOMAN WHO HAD SAID THE TRUE THING WAS STANDING FOURTEEN YARD AWAY WITH HER OWN HOUSE OPEN BEHIND HER AND SAW ALL OF IT.**

The passage is about nine foot wide and about thirty yard long and it runs off a street between number four and number twelve, and there is no way out of it at the bottom except back the way you came in, and nobody has ever made one.

Number twelve is at the top of it on the right. Number four is on the left, about halfway down.

That is the whole of the geography, and it is the only reason any of this was watchable.

---

He is in his fifties and he lives at number nine, which is not in that passage at all, and he has been going into number twelve four mornings a week at ten past eight in the morning for about six years.

He has never given anybody a reason for it, including himself, and there is nothing in that hour that could be described if he tried.

He goes in at ten past eight. There is a radio on, low, most days. He makes her tea and she makes his, and they sit, and sometimes she talks and sometimes she does not, and at ten past nine he goes out again and goes to work. He has never stayed the night. He has never brought anybody. He has not asked her for anything in six years and she has not asked him for anything, and once, in about 2016, she said *you needn't have come in the rain* before he had said a word about the weather, and he said *right* and sat down, and neither of them ever went near it again.

What she has out of it is a person in a room with the radio on, low.

What he has out of it is a room, and a person in it, and the best part of an hour, and he has never once asked himself what it is for, and the reason he has never asked himself is that the answer would be about himself.

The woman at number four has lived there about thirty-one years and has been on her own in it for the last nineteen, and she comes out of her own door at ten past eight most mornings for a bit of air, and has done for as long as anybody can remember.

That is the whole reason. The road is one side and the houses are the other and by ten past eight the delivery engines have gone and the street is a street to stand in. She stands on her own step with her back to her own door for about ten minutes and then goes in and does something.

What she has been doing since about November last year is watching the top of that passage.

Not the man. The top of the passage, and the man going into it, and coming out of it, four days a week, for six years.

And what she has been doing since November is the thing she would not put a name on if anybody asked her, and it is this: she has been waiting for him to stop.

Not because of him. Because of what it does to the woman at number twelve, who has been getting that hour, and who did not know she was waiting for it to stop, and who has never once in nineteen years asked anybody in that row for anything at all.

The woman at number four had worked out by about December that she was waiting for a man she had spoken to four times in six years to give up the only company either of them got. And she went on standing on her own step.

What she did about it was nothing, for about two months.

And then in the third week of February she was in the market with the woman from number twelve and the conversation turned, the way it does, and the woman from number twelve said that her daughter was in New Zealand and had not been in the room since Easter.

And the woman at number four said, "She isn't."

And the woman at number twelve looked at her.

And the woman at number four said, "She has been at the hospital up at Cleeve since about 2017. She is on the list there on two mornings a week and I have seen her on the bus at the top of this parade about four times, and I have never said anything, and if you want me not to tell you that I will not tell you and I have not said it to anybody else in the world."

That is the whole exchange, it lasted about as long as it takes to put a bag down, and out of twenty years of knowing each other neither of them has ever opened that subject, and neither has tried.

What the woman at number twelve did with it was nothing, which is what the woman at number four had expected and had been dreading for three weeks before she said it.

She did not stop speaking to her. She was not angry. She was not even quiet for long. She was, from that Wednesday, *no different at all*, and the woman at number four went home from that market and sat down and understood that she had put the one thing she had in her hand into a room where it was going to sit for nineteen years, and that nobody in that room was going to pick it up.

What she had not been ready for was the other half of it, which is that the man kept coming. He came on the Thursday and the day after and the Monday and every weekday after that for six weeks, and the woman at number twelve had her radio on the whole time he was in the house, and the hour went on exactly as it had for six years.

And the woman at number four stood on her own step at ten past eight and watched a man go down a passage and knew that she had put something into a house and that it was going to come out somewhere, and that it was not going to come out in the house.

She did not enjoy those six weeks. She has been through it and what is at the bottom of it is not enjoyment. What is at the bottom is that she wanted it to stop, and it went on for six weeks after she had done the thing that made it stop in somebody else, and she had to stand on her own step and watch a man be kind in a house she had put a true sentence into.

And underneath that is the part she does not look at directly. She is not a person who would have said it if she had not thought it through, and she did think it through, and she said it anyway, and the reason she said it is that the woman at number twelve had asked her in a market whether she thought the daughter would ever come and see her properly.

And the woman at number four had an answer to that question and it was true and she gave it.

---

On the Monday, at about ten past eight, the woman at number four opened her own door and went out on to her own step.

She had her own cup in her hand and she was in her dressing gown and she was going to stand there about ten minutes and go in again, and that is the whole of what she was doing and there was nothing about it she would have called doing anything.

The man came out of number twelve at about ten past nine carrying nothing, which was the first thing that was wrong with the picture, because in six years he had never come out of that door with nothing in his hands. He came down the passage. He got to about the middle of it, which is about fourteen yard from where she was standing.

And he stopped.

He stood there for about a minute and a half, with his hands at his sides, and he turned and looked back up it, at the top of the passage, at that step he had gone up about twelve hundred times.

And then he did not go back up it. He carried on down and out of the mouth of it and up the street, and he stopped at the corner for about another minute, and then went off up the street at his ordinary pace.

She did not move for about two minutes. There is nowhere on that step to go back to without turning round, and she did not turn round, and she stood there with the cup in her hand and the house open behind her and watched a man walk to the corner.

Here is what she has got, and she has been over it every day since and she gets to the same place every time.

She cannot ask him why. Not because he would not tell her. He would tell her. He has said *right* to her four times in six years and he would have said it a fifth time and given her whatever she asked next. That is not the difficulty.

The difficulty is that there are two answers and she has been able to see both of them since about ten past eight on the Monday morning.

One of them is that a man in his fifties got tired of a cup of tea four times a week.

The other one is a woman in a market on a Wednesday in February.

And she cannot put the second one anywhere, and she cannot rule it out, and it will be in her head for the rest of her life, and there is no asking him that does not put the market in that room as well.

And underneath that, which is the part she does not look at directly, is that if the second one is the right one then she did it. Not in February, in the market, in nine seconds. In February she said a true thing. Whatever he has done since the first of April, she is the one who made the room it happened in.

And there is no undoing a thing said in a market in nine seconds. She could go up that passage tomorrow and tell the woman at number twelve the whole of what her daughter does and where, and it would change nothing at all, because the true thing is still true and has been for two years and the woman at number twelve knew it and had chosen it.

And she is not going to say anything.

He has not spoken to her, and she is not waiting for him to. There has never been anything between the two of them worth starting, and in six years the one time he ever addressed her on his own account was a branch that had come down in the street in 2016, and there has been nothing since, and that is all of him and always was.

What he has done since the Monday is gone to work at the same hour and come home at the same hour and gone into his own house, and on the Thursday she went out for her air at half past eight instead of ten past, and she saw him standing in his own front room with the radio on, low.

She has noticed that. He has a radio. It had not occurred to her until the Thursday that a man with a radio in his own front room did not need to be in somebody else's for the best part of an hour, and she has not been able to put that down since and she is not going to try.

And the woman at number twelve is at the top of that passage and does not have anybody in that house on a weekday morning any more, and has told nobody, and has not come down the passage to number four, and has not shut anybody out of anything.

Which is the part that has made the woman at number four worse off than she was in February, and she has not told anybody that either, and she is not going to.

**ON THE SEVENTH OF APRIL A MAN CAME OUT OF HIS OWN HOUSE AT NUMBER NINE AT ABOUT HALF PAST EIGHT IN THE EVENING AND SHUT THE DOOR BEHIND HIM AND WENT UP THE STREET AS FAR AS THE BEND, AND FOURTEEN YARD DOWN A PASSAGE A WOMAN PUT A CUP SHE HAD NOT USED BACK IN THE CUPBOARD WHERE IT LIVES AND LOOKED AT IT, AND NEITHER OF THEM SAID ANYTHING TO ANYBODY.**
