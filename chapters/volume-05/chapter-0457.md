# Chapter 0457 — The Way Round

Thursday the twenty-ninth of November 2018, from about half past seven in the morning until about half past eight. A street off a parade, and the end of it, in Tideglass.

**FOR A FORTNIGHT A WOMAN HAS WALKED THE LONG WAY ROUND TO WORK EVERY MORNING AND NOBODY ON THAT STREET HAS OPENED THEIR MOUTH ABOUT IT, AND SHE IS NOT GOING TO GIVE ANY OF THEM THE CHANCE TO.**

There is a woman who has walked up that street to work every weekday morning for about nine years, and she has walked up the same side of it, on the same line, at the same speed, for so long that it is a thing about that street the way a crack in a pavement is.

She goes to a building about three quarters of the way along, where she works behind a counter from about nine until about half past five, and she comes back down the same street at about half past five and does her shopping in a place at the bottom and is home by about ten.

The whole of that is nine years of the same walk, and the only thing that has ever varied in it is the weather.

---

What she has changed is the end of the street.

The end of that street, about twenty yard short of where it meets the bigger road, is where a wall goes across the end of it. There is a gap in that wall about five foot wide with a swing gate on it that has not been shut since somebody's dog went through it, and on the far side of that wall are about nine houses and a row of garages and the end of that street, properly finished, with a pavement and a bus stop and a shop that sells fish.

She has stood at that gap at about half past seven in the morning a great many times in nine years. Everybody who has ever lived in those nine houses has stood at that gap. It is the way into that street and there is nothing else anybody calls it.

For about a fortnight she has not gone through it. She goes to the corner, turns right along the bigger road, goes about a hundred and thirty yard, and comes into her own street from the other end, which is about six minutes longer, and which brings her past a row of houses she has never had any reason to walk past.

She started doing it on the Thursday of the fifteenth of November and she has not missed a weekday since.

Nobody has said anything.

That is the whole of what is doing to her.

There are between four and nine people on that street between half past seven and half past eight in the morning in this weather, and she knows most of them by the shape of the coat, and every single one of them has watched her walk the length of it for nine years and not one of them has ever said one word to her about anything at all, least of all about the fact that she no longer goes through the gap.

Not one. Not a question, not a joke, not a "lost, are you." A man who has been standing outside a house with a smoke since about 2010 walked past her on the twenty-second and said "morning" and nothing else, and she said morning, and both of them let it go by.

She has been waiting for about a fortnight for one of them to say something and not one of them has and she has not given any of them the chance, and that is the second half of it, and she is not going to give anybody the chance.

Here is the reason, and she has not said it to a single living soul and is not going to.

There is a woman in one of those nine houses who is in her sixties and has been for some time, and that woman used to stand at that gap in the mornings and used to be in it by about half past seven, and about four months ago she stopped being in it, and the standing at the gap has got later and later, and about a fortnight before the fifteenth of November it stopped being there at half past seven altogether.

She did not work it out on the fifteenth of November. She worked it out about three weeks before that, on a morning when she was at the gap at about a quarter to eight and the gate was pushed back against the wall and there was nobody at it.

And she is not a person who can put a question to anybody. She has never in her life been able to stand in a doorway and ask somebody how they are, and she manages it at work about thirty times a day and it costs her something every time.

So she did the other thing. She went round the other way.

That is the whole of what has happened, and here is why it is not going to be undone.

She has now gone past that row of houses about ten times, which is every weekday since the fifteenth of November, and about a quarter of the people in it have come out while she was going past, and about half of them looked at her, and not one of them has any idea who she is and not one of them would have any idea what to make of her.

If she starts going through that gap again tomorrow, then in four weeks' time nobody on that street will remember that she ever did not, and in six weeks nobody on that street will remember that she does, because a woman in a dark coat going to work in the morning is not a thing anybody on a street keeps in their head.

There is nothing to undo. That is the trouble with it. There was never anything there that anybody else was holding.

---

On the Thursday morning of the twenty-ninth of November it rained from about twenty to seven and the six minutes were the six minutes.

She went to the corner, turned right, went the hundred and thirty yard, and came into her own street at the fish shop end, and the whole way along it she was aware of the gate on her left at the end and she was aware of it the whole way and she did not turn into it.

At about eight she was at her own door and she was two minutes wetter across the shoulders than the woman who goes through that gap, and she has worked out that this is the whole of what it costs and has decided it is a cost she can live inside.

She is not going to tell them. She is aware that not telling them is a decision she has made ten times in a fortnight, and she is aware that a decision made ten times is a habit, and she is aware that on about the twentieth of December it will not be a decision any more.

**SHE PUT HER COAT ON THE STAND AT THE BACK OF THE PREMISES AT EIGHT O'CLOCK AND WENT BEHIND THE COUNTER, AND THE GATE AT THE END OF THAT STREET STOOD OPEN AGAINST THE WALL UNTIL THE MAN WHO PUTS SHUTTERS UP WENT PAST IT AT TEN PAST SIX IN THE EVENING.**
