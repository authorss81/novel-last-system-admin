# Chapter 0423 — The Front Room

Saturday the thirtieth of June 2018, from about half past nine in the morning until half past nine at night, a block of four flats off a side street, and the same block on three evenings in July, in Tideglass.

**A MAN IN HIS SIXTIES TOOK THE PAPER OFF THE LONG WALL OF HIS OWN FRONT ROOM IN JUNE AND FILLED ABOUT NINE FOOT OF IT AND STOPPED AT THE CORNER, AND A MAN IN HIS THIRTIES CAME ALONG SEVEN WEEKS LATER AND DID THE WHOLE WALL IN THREE EVENINGS IN HIS OWN TIME AND TOOK HIS OWN THINGS AWAY WITH HIM EVERY NIGHT, AND THE MAN IN HIS SIXTIES FOUND IT DONE AND SAID NOTHING AND DID NOT ASK.**

Four flats, built about 1960, entered from the street by a passage barely wider than a man, the meter cupboard on the left, the stair at the far end of it, and the door on the latch most mornings.

The ground floor has been his since about 2009. The front room off that passage has a bay. It is about thirteen foot by twelve including the bay, and the bay is a canted bay about five foot wide and it goes out over the pavement, and there is a fireplace on the wall opposite the bay that has been out of use since before 1968 and has a cast iron insert in it and a coal fire that was not lit in this house after about 1970.

The wall he is doing is the wall on the left as you come in, running from that fireplace to the door of the room. It is about twelve foot long and it is the only wall in that room with anything on it, and the ceiling is about ten foot and the cornice is original and good and he is not touching it.

---

He started it on the eighth of June.

He is seventy-one and he gave the trade up about 2014 and handed what he had to a man who stopped working, and he has not filled anything since about 2008, and he can fill and he can float and he can get a corner right about half the time, and he knows both of those things about himself and has never argued with either of them.

He got the paper off that wall in two days, which is how long it takes one man who is not trying, and the plaster under it was sound in about two-thirds of it and had blown in about four foot of it at the bottom by the fireplace, and he filled those four foot first, and then he filled about five foot more going up, and he got as far as a corner.

That corner is where that wall meets the fireplace side, and getting a square corner where a flat wall runs into a chimney breast is the hardest thing in that trade and everybody in it knows it, and he can do about one in two.

He did not do that one.

He stopped on the twelfth of June at about half past four in the afternoon. There is about five foot of that wall bare from the corner, and a ladder is against the middle of the run, and there is a bucket under the edge of the fill, and a lump of plaster in the bottom of it, and a trowel on the ledge of the bay, and about half a bag of finishing plaster in the corner with the top folded over, and a piece of hardboard about two foot square laid flat on the floor where he mixes, and a scrap of paper over that.

He has not touched it since the twelfth of June and he is not going to.

He has not told anybody. There is no man in this borough he would tell. His own reasoning, which he has never put to a living soul, is that a man does not leave a wall half done and does not start one, and that between those two things there is a third thing that a man of seventy-one does on his own on a midweek afternoon, and nobody finds out about that afternoon.

And the room is like that: the bay is clean, the floor is bare boards, the gas fire is off because it is the summer, and one wall is half papered and eight foot of it is finished and sanded and the rest of the room is the same distemper it has been since 1974.

---

The man in his thirties came into that block on the Saturday of that week at about half past ten in the morning for a job on the first floor, and he let himself in through that passage door because he is let himself in, and he went up the stair, and he came down it again at about half past ten because he wanted a length of pipe off the floor of that back room, which the man who lives upstairs is not expecting anybody to want, and that is all the reason there is. The man in his sixties was out, as he is on those mornings.

The man in his thirties went through the ground floor to the front room because the light was on and the door was open four inch, which it is not.

He stood in that doorway and looked at it for about a second.

There is a room with a bay and a fireplace and a ladder in the middle of it and a bucket under the edge of a wall and a trowel on a ledge and a piece of hardboard on the floor, and about five foot of finished plaster on that wall meeting about five foot of old paper at a line five foot off the floor.

He went in. He is not a man who can walk past that and go back down a stair, and he has not been able to since he was about nineteen.

He looked at the fill for about ten minutes.

He could see from the doorway that it had gone. It had not come off the wall, and it was not a bad job in the way that a bad job comes off; it had come away from the background in long hollow blisters about the size of his hand, and it was hollow under about half of it, and it was soft about a sixteenth of an inch in the middle of the blisters.

It is the ordinary outcome. A wall filled in the third week of June in this borough, with no wetted background and no mesh, in three weeks of dry weather with an east wind, will do exactly that. It has done it in every house in this borough that has been done in the last thirty years, and the plasterer who did it knew it would, and everybody in the trade knows it, and nobody has ever put a right date on it because a plasterer in this borough will not come back to a wall for nothing.

He got a knife and went round the edge of it and took about two square yards of it off the wall in four pieces, back to the old plaster, in about twenty minutes, and swept it up, and put the boards and the bucket outside on the flags.

And then he stopped, and he went out and shut that door and went up the street and did his own job on the first floor, and he came back down at about half past four and shut that block's passage door and went home.

He came back to that room on the evening of the second of July, and again on the fourth, and once more in the second week of that month.

He came in through that passage door and let himself in the way he has always let himself in, and came down that stair at about half past seven each of those three evenings, and he worked that wall from about half past seven until about nine, and he went up at about nine, and he took his own bucket and his own tools and his own boards with him every one of those three nights.

He did not use anything that was in that room. He did not use that bucket and he did not use that trowel and he did not open that bag of finishing plaster, and it is still in the corner of that room with the top folded over and it is the same amount as it was on the twelfth of June, and nobody in that house has ever counted it and nobody ever will.

What he did, in three evenings, about five and a half hours in all, is the whole of that wall.

He wetted the old plaster, properly, with a brush and a bucket, the night, and again in the morning of the next session, because a man who does this in this borough in July does it at night and wets it twice.

He cut the edges of the old plaster back about two inch where the new fill was going onto it, and he scratched the back of the old plaster with a nail, and he filled the four foot by the fireplace first, in two coats, because that is where the blown area was.

He did the corner. He cut the chimney breast side back about four inch with a multitool and took the arris off it and feathered it, and got a square corner on the first go, and there is a man in this borough who has watched about two hundred men try that corner and did it once himself in about ten minutes.

He then did the five foot of new plaster over the old line in a single coat about a quarter inch thick, and floated it, and trowelled it, and left it about the same as the eight foot that was already there.

On the last of the three evenings he went over the whole twelve foot with a sanding block, and all the joins, and the arris by the door, and the cornice line, and he wiped the whole of it with a damp cloth, and he took his boards and his bucket and his tools and went up that stair and out through that passage door.

He did not fill the crack above the door that has been there since 1974. He did not touch the cornice. He did not touch the fireplace and did not touch the bay. He did exactly the twelve foot of wall and nothing else in that room.

And nobody in that house knew he was in it on any of those three nights. The man who lives on the first floor was in until about nine and the man who lives on the second was away, and the man in his sixties is in bed by about ten and does not hear a staircase.

The man in his forties came through that passage door at about ten past seven in the evening on the Saturday of the fourth of July, which was the end of the second of those three evenings.

He has had a key to that passage door for about six years and nobody in that block has ever asked him for it and nobody has ever mentioned it.

He had been doing a job two doors up that street and had left his own long ladder in a hallway and had come back for it, and he came into that passage and the front room door was open about two foot and there was a light on in that room and a board across the doorway.

He stopped in that passage.

The man in his thirties was in that room about nine foot in with his back to the door and a trowel in his hand and a hawk against his hip and a bucket at his feet, and he was doing the join where the new plaster met the old line, and he had a good light on that wall off a bulb hanging off the cornice on a flex, and they saw each other.

The man in his thirties turned his head. The man in his forties was in that doorway with a ladder over his shoulder.

Neither of them said one word about it.

The man in his forties went back down that passage to the front door with the ladder over his shoulder, and out and up the street, and he has not been in that block since and is not going to until the end of the year for a completely different job, and he does not know whose wall that was or whose trowel that was, and put to him he would say he saw somebody working and got out of the way.

The man in his thirties stood there with a trowel in his hand for about two seconds and then carried on with the join. He does not know that the other one was ever in that building, and he has never asked and is not going to.

The man in his sixties came in on the Sunday morning at about half past ten, four days after the third of those evenings.

He came in at the front and went through the passage to his own front room, and the light was off and the door was shut.

He opened the door and put the light on.

The room was as he left it. The bay was clean and the floor was bare boards and the gas fire was off and the fireplace was the same and the ladder was against the middle of the run where it had been since the twelfth of June, and the bucket was under the edge of the wall, and the trowel was on the ledge of the bay, and the bag of finishing plaster was in the corner with the top folded over, and the piece of hardboard was on the floor.

And the whole twelve foot of that wall was filled.

He stood in the doorway and looked at it.

He did not go over it and put his hand on it. He did not look at the corner by the fireplace, which is the corner he did not do and which is the only part of that wall in the whole borough that a man might look at twice.

He looked at it from the doorway for about ten seconds, then he shut the light off and went out and shut that door and made his tea.

He has not asked. He is not going to ask. Nobody in this borough has been told a word about that wall and there is nobody he would say it to, and the man in his thirties was in there on three evenings in July without knowing that he was watched through a doorway on the second of them, and the other one was in there that same Saturday evening, and neither of those two men has ever said anything to the other one about anything.

He does not paint it. There is a pot of emulsion in the hall cupboard that he bought at the start of this year for the back room, and it will be there in the September and in the March.

**HE LOOKED AT IT FOR ABOUT TEN SECONDS FROM THE DOOR AND WENT OUT AND SHUT THE DOOR AND MADE HIS TEA, AND A TROWEL IS STILL ON THE LEDGE OF THAT BAY AND A BAG OF PLASTER IS STILL IN THE CORNER OF THAT ROOM WITH THE TOP FOLDED OVER, AND NOBODY HAS ASKED HIM ABOUT EITHER.**
