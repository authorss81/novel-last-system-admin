# Chapter 0659 — Four Hours At The Back Of A Frame

Saturday the seventh of August 2021, in this borough. She is at the end of that road from about half past eight in the morning until about ten past four in the afternoon, and the whole of it is a gate, and it is four hours, and it is not ten minutes.

**A WOMAN WHO SAID IN A KITCHEN THAT SHE WOULD DO A GATE WENT UP A ROAD ON A SATURDAY MORNING WITH A CHISEL AND A BAD BRUSH IN A BAG AND CUT TWO DROP BOLTS OUT OF A LUMP OF OLD GLOSS IN FOUR HOURS AND NOT TEN MINUTES AND GOT BOTH HER PALMS CUT INSIDE, AND THE GATE LIFTS AND SWINGS AND DOES NOT COME IN ON THE PATH FOR THE FIRST TIME SINCE IT WENT ON, AND NOBODY IS STANDING AT IT, AND THE BRUSH SHE USED FOR IT IS ON A SIDEBOARD IN A FRONT ROOM THAT HAS NOT BEEN PAINTED SINCE BEFORE CHRISTMAS.**

She is a woman in her late forties. There is a woman in her late seventies in the same house, at a kitchen window and then out on the grass with a mug, for about four minutes of the afternoon. There is nobody else on that road.

---

She is at the end of the road at about half past eight in a white van with the tailgate up and the bag in the footwell where it has been since the Monday.

And she does not go up the path for about half an hour.

And here is what those half an hour are, because it is not fear and it is not a decision and it is the third time this summer she has sat in a van outside a house she is going into.

On the Monday she wrote a Monday on a pad in her own kitchen and did not cross it out.

On the Friday of the sixteenth of July a woman in her late forties said *I'll do your gate* in a kitchen with a man in it, and it is now the Saturday, and it has got to be today or it is not going to be at all, and nobody has said that out loud to anybody.

And the last thing she does before she gets out of the van is take her own coat off, because a woman who knocks on a door at about nine in the morning on a Saturday in a clearance jacket is a woman who is here about something else.

---

She knocks at about nine.

Two knocks and a pause and one, and she has not knocked on that door in her life.

And the door is off the latch, and it comes away from the frame under her knuckles before she has finished, and swings about an inch and a half on a dry hinge, and the woman in her late seventies is standing in the passage behind it with her hand off the door.

And there is no version of this in which the woman says no, and they both know it, and that is the whole of the standing in that passage.

And this time she goes in.

---

And here is what she can see in about nine seconds of that house, and it is worth setting down because two of the three things are the reason the morning works and the third one is the reason the afternoon is going to be what it is.

The first thing is that the front room is straight ahead and it is dark and it is cold and there is a sideboard against the far wall with nothing on it and its top about waist high.

Nothing on it. Not a card, not a bowl, not a photograph, not an ashtray, not a cloth. It is a long low sideboard in oak with a top about two inches deep in plan and a lip round three sides, and it has been there longer than the carpet, and there is nothing on it at all.

She looks at it for about two seconds. She does not think anything about it. She is on her way to a kitchen and there is a gate to do.

The second thing is that she goes through it into the kitchen, and the kitchen is at the back, and there is a table under the window with a cloth on it and a green chair at it with a cushion on the chair.

The third thing is that the woman in her late seventies says, "Which one," and she says, "The gate," and the woman in her late seventies goes out of the room.

---

And here is what that means, and it means it in about two seconds and neither of them is ever going to say it.

*Which one.*

Not *what for*. Not *who sent you*. Not *what do you want me to do*.

*Which one.*

As though there were a list, and as though the woman in her late forties were the only other person on that road who had seen it, and as though the correct and expected answer to a knock on that door in the first place was an ordinal number.

And the woman in her late forties has a chisel in her bag and the list is in a kitchen in her own house under a fridge in a pad with the first line crossed off, and she has crossed the first line off herself, and she wrote a Monday on it on the Monday, and she is about to do the first line properly for the first time since before Christmas.

---

She is out of the kitchen door at about ten with the bag.

And the gate is over on that side of the house, the way it is, with the line of grass walked flat from the kitchen door to the coal shed and then on round to it, and she goes along that line because it is the line a person would go.

And she goes round to the back of the frame, because the lump is on the back of the frame, and she is now on the same side of the gate as the house.

---

And here is the fault, and it is worse than it looked in a kitchen with a man in it.

The top drop goes into a socket in the bottom of the gate and the bottom drop goes into a socket in the frame, and both of them are covered — not painted, covered — by about four inches of gloss that has gone on and on and then gone on again over wet, and it is not a layer and it is not two layers.

It is a lump. It is a lump of old gloss about the size of a hand, and it is one solid piece, and it runs in under the top of the drop and round the back of it and out along the timber about four inches either side, and where it has run into the grain of the oak it has taken a small amount of oak with it and dried hard.

And it is about the thickness of a thumb and a half and it does not come off in pieces.

---

She puts the chisel to it at about ten past ten.

And the first thing she learns, in about a minute, is that you cannot go in at the front of it, because the front of it is a lip and the lip is where it is thinnest and the lip is what holds the whole lump on.

And the second thing she learns is that a bolster chisel is the wrong tool for it, and she has a bolster chisel because it is the one in the van, and a bolster has a straight edge and no blade and you cannot get a bolster into the side of a lump like that at all.

She works at it with the corner of the bolster for about half an hour and gets a piece out of the middle of it about the size of a thumbnail, and she looks at the hole she has made and it is a hole and it is useless.

---

And then she does the thing that takes her the rest of the morning, and it is the only thing she has ever been good at in this trade, and it is not chiselling.

She gets down on the grass on the side where the light is and she looks at the lump from underneath, from about eight inches away, on her knees, with her cheek nearly on the grass, and she does not touch it for about four minutes.

And what she can see from underneath is that the lump is not stuck to the timber. It is stuck to itself.

There is a join in it. It is where the last coat of the last lot of gloss stopped at about half past six on an evening in somebody's life and the next coat went on over it wet, and the two have gone into one another and made a skin about the thickness of a coin, and the skin is over the whole of it.

And under the skin, on the underside, at the back, there is a place where the skin has not gone down onto the timber at all. It is about half an inch of it and it is on the underside of the lump where it crosses the back of the drop.

---

So she goes at it from underneath.

And it comes off.

It comes off in about ten pieces over about two hours, and it comes off in a way that is nothing like a chisel and everything like a person who has spent a long time looking at other people's old paint, and the pieces are about the size of a thumbnail and a half and they are pinky-coloured on the underside where it never saw the light.

And she works from the bottom up, because it is a lump and lumps come off from the bottom, and she does the top drop first because it is in the gate and the gate is what moves.

---

And her hands get cut twice inside the palm.

And that is a real thing and it is not a small thing, and it happens on a lump with a skin on it in July with the sun on that side of a house, and what happens is that the skin breaks under the corner of the bolster and puts a corner of its own edge into the fleshy part of the hand below the little finger, and she wipes it on her jeans and goes on, and it does the same thing again an hour later in the same place because she has not changed what she is doing, and the second one is worse and she does not wipe that one on her jeans.

---

And about twelve o'clock the woman in her late seventies comes out of the kitchen door with a mug.

And she comes out across the grass and she does not go near the gate. She stops about two arms off, on the walked-flat line, and she holds the mug out, and she does not hand it over and she does not put it down, and the woman in her late forties takes it out of her hand.

And that is the whole of what happens between those two women on that side of that house on that Saturday, and it takes about ten seconds, and neither of them manages the other thing.

And then they stand there.

---

And here is the part that is not in the two seconds, and it goes on for a good deal longer than two seconds, and neither of them will ever refer to it, and it is the reason the woman in her late forties is at a gate at all instead of in a van.

The woman in her late seventies is trying to say thank you.

She cannot do it. She has a mug in her hand and she has had since before Christmas of not being able to ask anybody for anything, and the words for what she feels about a woman on her knees on her grass with a chisel and a cut hand are not words she has got, because she has not practised them on anybody since before Christmas.

What she does instead is say:

"You'll want to put them back after."

And she means the drops. She means the two drop bolts, and she means that when a gate is off its drops there is nothing keeping it there, and she is telling the woman in her late forties something about her own trade that she does not know she is saying, and it is the closest thing to a thank you that has been said in that house since before Christmas.

And the woman in her late forties says, "Aye. I'll put them in the sockets. They don't want a hole, they want a socket."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Right."

And that is the entire conversation.

---

And the woman in her late forties works on until about half past two, and the second drop is out of its socket by then, and both drops are on the grass.

And the frame is bare oak with the black of the old paint still in the tooling of it, and the two sockets are cut by a man who has been dead longer than either of them has been alive, and there is no rot in it and no movement in it and nothing wrong with that frame at all.

---

And then she does the brushing, and this is where the brush she bought out of a bucket on the Monday earns its four pounds.

She has got a chisel and a bad brush and a lump of old gloss off a gate frame, and the frame has four inches of loose dust and skin and pinky-coloured grit on it, and if she hangs that gate back on that she has done the same thing a man would have done, which is the whole of the trouble.

And she has got a wall brush.

And it does it. That is the surprise of the morning and she does not know it until about twenty past two. A wire brush with a hard deep crimp cannot get into a socket, and this one goes into the socket and comes back out with the bottom of the socket full of grey dust, and it is the soft short crimp that does it, because the short crimp is the thing that can get into a hole about two inches deep.

And she does both sockets and the length of both drops and the back of the frame, and it takes her about half an hour, and the grey dust goes into the grass and not into her bag.

---

And then she puts the gate back.

And she does it on her own, and it takes her about a minute, and it is the easiest minute of the morning.

It goes on the top drop, then the bottom one goes into the socket with the heel of her hand, and the gate comes to the frame, and she takes her hands off it.

And she gives it a push with the flat of her hand.

And it swings.

---

And here is what she has done, and she stands on the grass about two feet off it and looks at it for a while.

That gate has not shut on that path since it went on.

The drops are painted in and the drops are what the drops are for, and a gate on drops with the drops locked will swing four inches and stop against its own frame, and it has been stopping against its own frame since before Christmas, and it has been banging at about four in the morning when there is a wind from the west, and a woman has lain in a house and listened to it.

And it does not come in on the path now.

It goes back about three quarters of the way on the grass and it comes and stops where the other end of it stops, and it is not touching anything, and there is a gap at the hinge side you could get a hand into.

And it is the first time since it was put on that it has done that.

---

And nobody is standing at it.

That is the sentence. She stands on the grass at about half past two in the afternoon of the seventh of August 2021 and looks at a gate that swings, and nobody is standing at it, because the only person in that house is at a kitchen window about twenty feet away behind a curtain that has not been taken down since before Christmas, and the only other person is in her late forties with a chisel in one hand and a cut palm on the other.

---

And at about half past three she goes in.

Not to be thanked. To wash her hands. That is the whole of the reason and it is a real one, because there is blood in the crease of both palms and a grey dust in the creases of her fingers and she cannot get at either of them in a cold tap outside a coal shed.

She goes in the front door, which is off the latch, and it is open behind her.

---

And she has to go through the front room to get to the kitchen, because the kitchen is at the back of that house and the sink is in the kitchen, and she has been in that front room twice since before Christmas and both times she was asked in.

And it is dark and it is cold and there is a sideboard against the far wall.

And she is carrying the brush.

She has been carrying it since about half past two. It is in her left hand, along with the chisel, and she carries her left hand the way a person carries a hand that has a cut in it, which is with the brush and the chisel in the crook of the fingers and the palm turned up and away from everything.

And there is a sideboard, and the top of it is flat, and it is the first flat thing in that room at about half past three in the afternoon.

---

And she puts the brush down on it.

That is all. There is no decision in it. There is no moment. She goes through a dark room towards a tap and there is a table about waist high against the wall in the dark and her hand goes down on it and leaves the brush.

And the sideboard has nothing else on it, and it has had nothing else on it since she first came in in June, and the brush is on it, and it is face up with the handle towards the door.

---

And she goes through to the kitchen and washes her hands in a cold tap with a bar of soap on a string, and she dries them on a tea towel off the rail by the cooker, and she puts the towel back on the rail, and neither of them says one word about the towel.

And she goes out the front door and down the path and gets in the van and shuts the tailgate.

And at about ten past four she is at the end of a road with a chisel and no brush in a bag in the footwell, and the van is very light.

---

**A WOMAN CUT TWO DROP BOLTS OUT OF A LUMP OF OLD GLOSS IN FOUR HOURS, AND THE FIGURE A MAN GAVE A WOMAN IN A KITCHEN IN JULY WAS NOT TRUE, AND NOBODY WAS WATCHING ANY PART OF IT, AND THE GATE NOW SWINGS AND DOES NOT COME IN ON THE PATH AND THERE IS A PIECE OF BRUSH ON A SIDEBOARD IN A FRONT ROOM IN A HOUSE ON THAT ROAD, AND THE SIDEBOARD HAD NOTHING ELSE ON IT, AND THE BRUSH WAS PUT THERE BY A WOMAN WHO WAS CARRYING IT PAST AND DID NOT PUT IT DOWN ON PURPOSE AND DID NOT KNOW SHE HAD DONE IT, AND A WOMAN IN HER LATE SEVENTIES WAS AT A KITCHEN WINDOW THE WHOLE TIME AND CAME OUT WITH A MUG AND COULD NOT SAY THE OTHER THING, AND THE FRONT ROOM IS THE ONLY ROOM ANYBODY SITS IN AND SHE WROTE IT SECOND ON A GAS ENVELOPE IN APRIL AND NOBODY HAS ASKED HER.**

(End of chapter 0659)
