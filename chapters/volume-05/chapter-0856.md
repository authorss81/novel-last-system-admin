# Chapter 0856 — The Blunt Front Of The Trowel

Saturday the twenty-eighth of October 2023, at the edging heap at number twelve from the middle of the day until the light has gone off the flags. One trowel, and an inch of it that has stopped cutting.

**THE FRONT INCH OF HER OWN TROWEL HAS GONE BLUNT AND SHE MEANS TO TURN IT IN HER HAND AND READ IT ON ONE OF THE STONES IN THE EDGING HEAP AND PUT IT BACK ON ITS NAIL, AND HER SISTER CALLS ONCE THROUGH THE SHUT KITCHEN DOOR AND DOES NOT COME OUT.**

The heap is on the house side of the gate, laid down in the spring when the edging came off the bed and was never got to. It runs about six feet along the wall and it is as high as her knee at the middle and tapers to nothing at both ends, and the stones in it are all lying the wrong way up, which is the way they came off the bed and the way they have been since.

Six feet of edging is about eleven good stones and five bad ones. The good ones have a dressed arris along the top, an inch and a bit across, straight, with the tooling still on it. The five bad ones have no arris at all and are only fit for the bottom course or for the ends where nobody looks.

They cannot be told apart with the eye while they are piled. That is the whole reason the heap exists and the whole reason it has not been done. Turned over one at a time, the arris comes to the top and the stone says what it is. Turned over in a heap all at once, which is what a shovel does, they go back to being a heap and nothing is gained and about six inches of the good arris is lost against the inside of the heap.

So the stones have to be turned by hand, one at a time, with something thin put under the edge of each and lifted.

The trowel is the thing in the shed that is thin enough.

---

She gets it off the nail in the frame of the shed door, where it has hung since the spring with the blade down and the handle up, and the wooden handle has gone the colour of a bit of old rope and there is a grey bloom on it in the hollows of the grain where it has hung with its own weight.

The grain of a handle is the one thing about a trowel that tells you how old it is and how it is used. It stands open where a thumb has been and it is closed where the hand has not, and the wear follows the handle and not the blade, because the hand carries on doing exactly what it was doing when the steel goes blunt.

She does not open the shed further than that. She stands at the heap with it in her hand.

The first stone she tries is about a third of the way down from the top end, a long one, and it is lying with its bed face up, which means its arris is underneath it somewhere and it is going to have to come out and go back the other way round.

She puts the front inch of the blade under the near edge of it, flat, the steel lying along the stone and not standing on it, and lifts.

The stone does not come. It slides about half an inch along the stones underneath and then stops, with the trowel still under it, and she can feel through the steel that it is going to go on sliding rather than lifting. What the front inch of the blade has found is not an edge to lift against. It is a face lying flat on other faces.

She takes the trowel out and lays it flat across the top of the stone and draws it toward herself, edge down, with a hand that is not pressing much.

The stone comes up polished.

That is the test, and it is the whole of the test, and she could have done it at the bed or in the road for all the difference it makes. An edge that is still an edge bites into a stone. It goes in about a hundredth of an inch and then the stone says no, and the trowel stops with a jump in the hand, and there is a bright line behind it about the width of the inch. An edge that has gone blunt runs the whole length of the stone and does nothing at all except scrape it, and what is left is a dull grey band the width of the inch and no mark in the middle of it at all.

There is no mark in the middle of it at all.

She turns the trowel in her hand so that what is left of the low sun comes along the edge instead of across it, and she looks down the edge with her face about eight inches off it, which is how you look at an edge and not at a blade.

An edge is a line. You do not see a line. You see where it stops and where it starts.

This one is not a line. It is a shallow trough with a curve in it, lowest about the middle of the inch, and at each side it curves away into the wide part of the blade and does not come to anything. At the point it rounds off entirely, and the rounding is about as wide as her thumbnail.

She puts the pad of her thumb along it, from the shoulder towards the point, and there is nothing under the thumb but smooth steel with the faint ripple of the grinding in it. The grinding ripple runs across the edge, not along it, and where a new edge is put on a blade the ripple goes along, because that is the direction the stone travels.

She knows the end of it. There is no argument left. A trowel front does not go blunt down its length, it goes blunt at the front, and the front is the inch that goes into the joint first and takes the knock of whatever is under it, and the back three inches of the same blade have been nothing but a handle for turning things round for twenty years and are as good as they were in the spring.

There is nothing else in that shed that will do what the front inch of that trowel does. A screwdriver blade is round and will roll. A chisel is a quarter of an inch thick where it widens and will not go under a stone at all. A bar is for prising with and takes up more room than the gap there is. Nothing bought would be better, because the trouble is not the tool, it is that one inch of one tool has been in the ground since April and has had the ground itself taken off it.

She puts it back on its nail and shuts the shed door on it.

Her sister comes to the kitchen door in the middle of the afternoon and does not open it, and asks through the door whether she has had her tea. The answer that goes back through the door is no. Her sister tells her the kettle is on the hob and goes away down the passage, and the door is shut again and there is nothing else said through it.

What she does instead, for the rest of the afternoon, is go along the heap with her hands and nothing else in them.

She puts the flat of her hand flat down on the top of the heap and feels down the sides of the stones with the ends of her fingers, the way you feel a row of books on a shelf for the one that is out of line. It is slower than a trowel and it tells her less. But it tells her that the heap has been standing in six months of rain against a wall and that the stones at the bottom of it are wet through in the middle and dry at the edges, and that means the heap has drained downwards and settled, and that the top of it is not the same as it was when it was built.

Which is the other thing. A heap that has settled cannot be picked over with a trowel from the top, because every stone that comes off the top makes the ones below it move, and half of them turn as they move and half of them do not, and the ones that do not are lying against the ones that do and there is no picking at that afterwards.

It wants a heap taken down a course at a time and set out in rows on the ground. That is a morning's work with a barrow and a bar and two hands, and it is not a Saturday with one trowel in it, and she is seventy-eight and she knows which of those two things she is.

So the stones stay where they are.

---

The heap stands where it has stood since the spring, six feet of it against the wall with every good arris in it lying face down against something else, and the loop of blue string still over the top of it where it was put back in the summer. Nothing has moved in it today and nothing was going to.

The front inch of that trowel will not take a grip again this year.
