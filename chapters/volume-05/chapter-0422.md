# Chapter 0422 — Coming Back

Monday the twenty-fifth of June 2018, from about five to nine in the morning until about ten past six that evening, a shop off that parade, and its front door, in Tideglass.

**A DOOR CAME BACK OFF ITS TOP HINGE ON A WIND AND HIT THE MAN IN HIS FORTIES IN THE SHOULDER AT ABOUT HALF PAST TEN, AND THE SAME DOOR CAME BACK FOUR HOURS LATER AND HIT THE WOMAN WHO WORKS IN A SHOP ABOVE THE WRIST, AND ONE MAN SAW BOTH HALVES OF IT AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS SAID A WORD ABOUT IT AND NEITHER OF THEM WILL.**

Sixty yard back from that parade she has a shop of her own and shuts it herself, and she is at that counter from about ten and on her feet until she goes, and she has been on her feet at that counter about two years. The shop next door, the one that shuts early, is not hers and neither it nor the woman who runs it is in this chapter.

The door of that shop is a hardwood door about three foot by seven foot with a lever handle on the outside and a push plate on the inside, hung on three butts on the left-hand side as you come in, opening outward, with a fanlight over it about a foot high and a quarter of an inch of glass in it that has been loose since before Christmas and does not rattle because there is nothing behind it.

The thing on the floor under that door worked until about the March. It has not worked since, and there is a folded strip of stiff brown paper wedged under the door on the inside to stop it coming back on itself, and somebody jammed it in there with the heel of a hand, and the door has not closed properly since.

So the door does not come back to its own frame. It goes past it. It goes out to about a hundred and ten degrees, it stands there in the wind, and then the wind goes and the door comes back, and because there is nothing stopping it and because it is a heavy door on three butts it comes back further than it went out, and the top hinge does about an inch and a half and then the door is coming back with its own weight behind it.

It has been doing that since the March. It has done it about four hundred times. It has hit a man carrying a bag of washers in the April and a woman with a child in the May and it has not broken anything and nobody has ever reported it and nobody in that shop is liable for it and nobody has ever rung anybody.

---

The wind got up on that parade at about a quarter to noon.

It is a west wind and the shop faces west and it comes straight down the length of that parade and into that door, and on that Monday it blew at about thirty mile an hour for about an hour and it did the whole of that parade.

The man in his forties went out of that shop at about half past ten.

He had been in it for about six minutes buying a coil of wire and a roll of tape and he had his bag in his left hand and his right hand on the push plate, and he went out backwards the way anybody goes out of a shop with a bag, and the wind took that door off its hinge and stopped it out at about a hundred and ten degrees, and stood it there.

He stood in the doorway with the bag against his chest and the door standing out into the street and the parade going past in front of him, and he was not in any hurry about it.

And then the wind dropped about four mile an hour, and the door came back.

It came back off the top hinge and hit him on the top of the right shoulder, hard, corner first, and he got his right hand up and caught the edge of it and stopped it about three inch off his own face, and it was going to take his hand with it if he had not moved his feet back, and he did.

The woman who works in a shop was behind that counter about nine foot back and she saw all of that.

She did not say anything then and she has not said anything since.

He got out into the street, shut that door, and walked up that parade to his own van, and he did not look back at that door, and he has not mentioned it to anybody in this borough, and nobody in this borough has asked him.

She was on that counter from about nine until about a quarter to six that Monday.

That Monday is the worst Monday of that year for standing, and the reason is not mysterious and nobody has ever said so: the shop is nine foot deep and the counter is five foot long, and there is one aisle, and a person who works that counter cannot sit down, because in this borough a counter person who sits is a counter person who is not at her counter, and that is not a rule anybody wrote down and it does not need to be one. She had eaten standing up against the end of that counter at about half past one with a box of something out of the back and she had been on her feet for over four hours by then.

At about half past two in the afternoon there was a lorry outside that shop on the parade with about twenty foot of conduit in three lengths on a sack truck, and she went out to the front step to give them a hand to get it in, because there was nobody else in the shop and because she has done that about a hundred times.

She went out backwards with the first length in her arms across her chest, ten foot long, held on the crook of her left arm and under the elbow with her right hand, and the wind was still on that parade and it came out of the west about twenty mile an hour.

That door went out to its hundred and ten degrees and stood there in it, and the woman stood on the step with a length of conduit across her chest and waited for it to drop, the way everybody who has ever done that has learned to wait for it to drop.

And it dropped about four mile an hour rather than the wind's own, because that is what the fitting under that door does when it has been packed with paper and the door is out past its stop.

It came back off the top hinge and hit her on the outside of the left forearm about two inch above the wrist, and it was not the corner this time, it was the edge of the stile, and it went across the bone.

She did not drop the conduit. That is what she thinks about afterwards. She has no memory of deciding to keep hold of it and she has a clear memory of the edge of that door going across the bone of her arm, and then the conduit being on the step and her own two hands on it and her left arm held against her chest with the right hand, and her back against the wall of the front of that shop.

She stood there like that for about two minutes.

It was a bright Monday afternoon and there were people on that parade. Nobody stopped. There is a shop front on that parade with a rule of its own that people go past a person stood at the front of it and nobody stops and nobody says anything and that is not unkindness, it is what everybody in this borough does, and she is one of them and she has never said so in her life.

Nobody rang anybody. Nobody rang the shop. Nobody went into the shop to see whether the woman who works there was all right, because there is nobody else in that shop, and she was outside it and the door was open behind her and about nine foot of that shop was visible from the parade, and there was nothing in it to see. At about half past two the man in his thirties was inside that shop.

He had come through the yard from his own van with a length of quadrant he had been waiting two days for, and the door was open and he came in through it with his shoulder round the edge of it, because everybody comes in through that door that way.

He was four foot inside the door with the quadrant under his arm when he heard the noise outside, which was the noise of ten foot of conduit going down onto the front step, and then nothing.

He went back out and stood in the doorway.

He saw the whole of it in about three seconds. He saw her against the wall with her right hand over her left forearm, and he saw the conduit on the step in two pieces, and he saw the door standing out into the street in the wind.

She had picked the conduit up again by the time he got to the step. She straightened up and put the two lengths back on the step and stood looking at the parade and not at him.

He did not ask her what happened. He did not ask her if she was all right. He did not say a word about the door, then or afterwards, and he has not asked her about that arm once in the fortnight since, and it has not come up between them at all.

Here is exactly what he knows and it is more than either of the other two people in this chapter knows.

He was in there at about twenty-five to noon that morning, and he is in there most weeks, and on that occasion he was a third of the way down that counter with a box of plugs he had been in for on the Thursday before, and he saw the man in his forties go out of that shop with a bag in his left hand and the door go out and stand there and the door come back and catch that man on the shoulder and that man get his hand up and stop it and walk away up the parade.

He did not say anything to the man in his forties about that then or since and is not going to. He did not think about it for about a day and a half, and then at about half past two on that Monday he watched the same door come off the same hinge and hit the woman who works in that shop on the same arm, and he stood in the doorway of that shop and worked out in about two seconds that it was the same door and the same hinge and that it had been doing that to people all summer.

And he said nothing, and he went in and put the quadrant on the counter and paid for it and she rang it through and he said nothing else, and he carried it out to his own van in that yard.

Nobody else in this borough has watched that door do it twice, and nobody else ever will.

---

He was back at that counter at about five that evening for a small box of screws.

She was on that counter with the light on and the door shut and she had her left forearm out of the sleeve of what she was wearing, which she does about three days in ten in the summer and which has nothing to do with anything.

He did not look at the arm. She did not mention the arm. He said what he wanted and she said what it was and he paid and he went out through that door with the box under his arm, and the wind had gone, and the door behaved itself perfectly, and neither of them referred in any way to the time that it had not.

That is the whole of it, and in a fortnight neither of them has raised it, and she is not going to, and the strip of stiff brown paper is still jammed under that door and the top hinge still comes about an inch and a half and the fanlight over it is still loose in about an inch of its putty, and the door is not anybody's to fix and it is not anybody's to report and it will be there in the cold.

**SHE STOOD AGAINST THE WALL OF THE FRONT OF THAT SHOP WITH HER RIGHT HAND OVER HER LEFT ARM FOR ABOUT TWO MINUTES, AND HE WENT BACK INSIDE AND PUT THE QUADRANT ON THE COUNTER, AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS SAID ONE WORD ABOUT IT AND THE DOOR IS STILL ON THAT HINGE.**
