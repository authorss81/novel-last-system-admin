# Chapter 0709 — The Washing Round The Shed

Thursday the tenth of March 2022, in this borough. She is in her own yard and kitchen from first light until dark and the whole of the day is washing pegged round a shed that lets water in.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES PEGS WASHING ROUND A SHED WITH FELT GONE AT THE FRONT EDGE, LEAVES A DRAWER SHUT WITH PLY AND TAPE AND A PENCIL AND A FOLDED PAPER IN IT, SPEAKS IN HER OWN YARD TO A COAL MAN ABOUT THE DRAUGHT AND ABOUT A BOWL UNDER A TAP, AND SPEAKS ON HER OWN STEP TO HER SISTER ABOUT THE DRAWER.**

She is a woman in her early thirties. She is at home all day. A coal man is in her own yard at the middle of the day. She speaks to him in the yard.

---

She starts with the washing before there is any light in the yard.

The basket is on the kitchen table and the pegs are in a bag on top of it, wood pegs worn smooth. She carries it out in both hands, hip to the door to open it, and pegs the washing round the shed in a square, shirts to the wind, sheets where the wind will take them, socks in a row along the line. The shed stands dark at the end of the yard with the felt gone along the whole front edge and the pale line where it lifts and the dark underneath where the water gets in. The ply will not do it. The tape with four inches left will not go round it. The draught comes off the top of it and lifts the sheets and sets them flapping.

She pegs round the draught without looking at it straight, the way she has pegged round it since May, learning where the wind takes and where it lets alone. The sheets know the line better than she does, finding the same belly every time. The tape roll came off a shelf in a house at the end of that road in May and neither she nor her sister has ever said where from to the other. There is four inches left on the roll. Four inches would not get round a parcel, let alone a shed edge.

---

She comes in and puts the kettle on, flame blue, and stands at the kitchen window with the cup in both hands, warming them, breath fogging the glass. She wipes the fog with her thumb and looks through the clear line.

From where she stands she can pick out the washing square round the shed and the shed dark inside the square, felt edge lifting white at the front, water dark under it. The drawer under the worktop stays shut, oak front, brass cup gone dark, with the bit of ply in it on top of the tape roll, edges square, and the hammer and the screwdriver that have not been out since September lying side by side. and the pencil on top of the ply, and the folded piece of paper with three lines on it that name what a felt edge would take and not what any of the three things is. She does not open it. She has not opened it since the twenty-eighth of December, when she put the paper in and shut it.

She thinks about the bowl under the bath tap at number twelve while she stands there, emptied at first light and at dark. She walked up that road once on the seventeenth of December with a step ladder on her shoulder and knocked once and put it folded under the window in the front room and squared it and did not go in after it. The ladder is folded there still, tape round the second rail, chalk on the third tread. The bowl fills there still, round and white. Both wait where they were not meant to stay.

---

The coal man comes at the middle of the day with the sack on the barrow down the back.

He tips the coal in the bunker, black shining, dust rising, and stands back and looks at the shed with the washing flapping round it, sheets bellying, shirts snapping. He shades his eyes with one glove.

"That edge wants doing," he says. "Felt gone the whole front. Water in under it."

"Aye," she says.

"I told you in November and on the thirteenth of December and in January. It will rot the boards you cannot see."

"I know it."

He looks at the washing and at the shed and at her.

"What would it take."

She could say the three lines on the bit of paper in the drawer. A roll and a tin and a lip of timber under a new edge. She does not say them, because saying them would mean asking for hands, and asking would mean saying the job out loud in a cold yard with the washing flapping, and the not-asking is the engine in her and not outside her, and it has run this way since May without breaking.

---

"What did the lass at number twelve do about her drip," he says.

She looks at him.

"What drip."

"Bath tap. Bowl under it. White china. I was in that yard in January and heard it through the wall, bead and ping, bead and ping."

She turns a peg over in her pocket.

"She empties it at first light and at dark," she says. "Folded cloth beside it. She has done it since before Christmas."

"Could nobody mend it."

"Somebody could. Mending it would mean two pairs of hands and nobody to keep the frame while she goes up. So the water keeps coming and the bowl keeps filling."

The coal man nods and puts his gloves on.

"Same as this," he says, nodding at the shed. "Water keeps coming and nobody to keep the ladder while it goes on."

---

He goes off down the back with the empty barrow, wheels rattling on the setts, and she takes the washing in before it gets the damp in it, armful by armful, cold cotton to her chest.

She folds it on the kitchen table in squares and sets the pegs back in the bag, counting them into her palm, wood smooth, spring stiff. The outside light over her own back door is off, small glass dark, as it is on every morning that is not Saturday. She switched it on every Saturday morning since the spring, coat over nightdress, bare feet in shoes, on and look and off again, and did not switch it on on Saturday the twenty-fifth of December and stood in her own dark yard looking for a lamp at the end of that road instead, and there was one.

She looks for the lamp now in daylight and cannot see it, as she has never been able to from this yard. From the main street at the bottom you can catch it, a small amber point at the top of the hill if you are looking for it. She knows it is on because it was on when she looked in December and because lamps left on stay on when nobody can find the switch that turns them off, like the passage light that has been on since June.

---

She mends a hem at the middle of the day with the drawer shut beside her.

The shirt is on the kitchen table with the hem turned, pins in a tomato, cotton white. She threads a needle from the tin on the sill and sews it with small stitches, thimble on, tongue between teeth, hem flat under her palm. The drawer under the worktop stays shut while she sews, with the ply and the tape and the pencil and the paper in it. She does not open it to square anything. She sews round the thought of it.

She thinks about the ladder while she sews, folded under a window in a front room at number twelve. She squared it there on the seventeenth of December. Tape round the second rail. Chalk on the third tread. She told her sister the whole of the visit on the twenty-eighth, in this kitchen, and telling it once did not lay it down. The ladder waits. The paper waits. Both stay where they were not meant to stay.

---

She takes the rubbish down the back after, to the bins by the wall.

The bins are full and the lids will not sit, rubbish mounded, string and peel. She lifts the lids and presses the rubbish down with both hands, feeling it give, and sets the lids square, rims true. The back is narrow with walls either side and the sky grey over it. The coal man barrow marks show in the damp where he came with the sack. She looks at the marks while she stands there.

The outside light over her own back door is off. She looks at the small glass where it sits over the door. Switched on and off every Saturday morning since the spring, coat over nightdress, bare feet in shoes. Missed the twenty-fifth of December. Switched out of turn on the seventeenth of February. Off now. Off is how it sits when nobody needs it.

---

Her sister comes at the end of the day off the front path without knocking and lets herself in off the yard, and the back door shuts true behind her the way it always does.

They stay in the kitchen. The front room at number ten gets no more of a Thursday in it than it got of a Tuesday in December.

"Coal man been," her sister says.

"Aye. About the edge."

"What did you say."

"What I always say."

Her sister looks at the drawer under the worktop and then does not look at it, and puts her cup down instead.

---

She scrubs the sink at the end of the day with the brush from under it.

She runs the tap and scrubs round the rim and the plug hole and rinses. The sink comes white where she scrubs and shows the stain round the overflow where the water sits. She dries her hands on the towel by the cooker. The drawer stays shut with the paper in it. The kettle ticks on the worktop going cold.

Her sister watches from the table with her cup in both hands.

"Clean," her sister says.

"Clean," she says. "Hem sewn. Rubbish down. Sink scrubbed. That is the day. The roof waits and the drawer waits and the bowl waits."

---

"You have not opened that drawer," her sister says.

"No."

"Why not."

"Because it is shut and it has been shut since the twenty-eighth of December, and every time it stays shut is a time I have not had to look at three lines that name what a thing would be and not what it is." She turns her cup round on the table. "And you have not opened it either, and you have been in this kitchen every time since Christmas, so you know exactly how easy that is."

Her sister lets that sit.

"There is a bowl," she says. "Under a bath tap. At number twelve."

"I know the bowl."

"Emptied at first light and at dark. Full again between."

"Aye."

At dark she goes out into the yard with her arms wrapped round her and looks at the shed with the felt gone at the front edge standing dark at the end. The draught comes off the top of it and goes through her coat. The drawer stays shut in the warm kitchen behind her. The bowl fills in a cold bathroom on the other side of the borough. All three wait.
