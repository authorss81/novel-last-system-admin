# Chapter 0686 — It Will Not Go On The Lorry

Friday the third of December 2021, in this borough. She is in her van and in three houses from first light until dark, and the whole of the day is a step ladder she cannot get back to the man she borrowed it off and a house she empties where a ladder is not rubbish and is not furniture.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES HAS A STEP LADDER ACROSS THE LOAD OF HER VAN AND A TAPED BOX THAT IS NOT HERS UNDER A BAG OF RAGS BEHIND THE PASSENGER BENCH, USES IT ON A WARDROBE THAT SITS AGAINST A CHIMNEY BREAST, OFFERS HALF A JOB INSTEAD OF A WHOLE ONE BECAUSE OF THE SPACE THE LADDER IS TAKING, AND LEAVES IT STANDING IN THE OPEN PORCH OF A HOUSE SHE HAS EMPTIED WITH THE DOOR OFF ITS HINGES AND NO NOTE ON IT.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is on her own all day except for a man of about sixty-five who has not touched his stairs, a woman of about sixty, a woman on the telephone about a job, and the man whose house she is emptying and a daughter at his door.

---

The ladder is across the load.

That is the first decision she made on Monday morning and she has not looked at it since, and what it means is that the box is behind the passenger bench on the floor against the bulkhead with a bag of rags on top of it, and the drawer under that same bench is shut with her own bolster on the leads and the tin of screws, and the ladder is laid along the left of the load bay at an angle with its tray end against the nearside wall and its feet against the back of the bench.

It is a good ladder. She has had it since Monday morning and she has used it once. Four treads, a tray in the top, a strip of dark tape round the second rail where a hinge has been, and a chalk mark on the third tread.

The chalk mark is not hers and the tape is not hers and she has never asked about either of them and is not going to.

It is four foot of aluminium along the left of that load bay and the load bay of that van is about seven foot, so it is not a fact about space. It is a fact that the ladder is in the way of the thing she does all day, which is getting a bag of rags out from behind the bench without moving a thing first.

She has moved it four times. She has got down on one knee and moved it and got back up and that is what the four times were.

---

The first house is a man of about sixty-five who has done the front room himself and has not touched the stairs and has a mattress in a bag at the bottom of them with the strapping still on it.

"You'll want to go up."

"I know."

"You can get the mattress down on your own, can't you?"

"I can get it down on my own."

"Only the head end's any weight. Once it's turned it'll go."

He has not asked her to take an end of it. She has not offered. That is the whole of the arrangement and they have both got it exactly where they want it.

At the top there is a wardrobe carcass with the doors off and the back off, and a box of tools with no lid, and a lamp with a flex out of it that somebody has cut the plug off and left the flex hanging.

She goes down.

---

The second house is the one she told the man in the street about on Monday, and the woman of about sixty is in her own front room and the front room is clear apart from a chair, a small table and a blanket over the arm of the chair.

"You've brought a ladder."

"I have."

"Well." The woman looks at it. "That's a nice one."

"It is."

"Are you using it in here?"

"I'm using it in the back."

"You could put it down in the front if it was in your way." She says it and then hears herself and puts her hand on the table. "No. You'll bring it out after. You'll bring it out the same way it came in."

"I'll bring it out the same way it came in."

The rule about the arm of the chair is not any different. There is a thing that has to be lifted off and set down in the room and a reason behind it that she started to give in November and did not finish giving, and the woman in her late forties has not asked her since, and has not asked her this morning either, and this morning she has gone further and has asked about the ladder, which is a different question, and the answer to it is nothing to do with the blanket.

The blanket goes in a bag of her own rags in the back when the room is done. It is the second one.

---

The back of that house is a small room off the kitchen with a chimney breast coming out into it about a foot, and a wardrobe on the wall beside it with its back to the plaster, and the wardrobe is bigger than the gap.

The ladder goes up the second time. She puts the tray against the wall at shoulder height and stands on the third tread, which is the one with the chalk mark on it, and she can get her right arm over the top of the wardrobe and she can get her left arm as far as the chimney breast and no further, and she can feel the two inch of air between the back of the carcass and the plaster with her fingers, and there is no way on this earth of getting a wardrobe down off a wall when your left hand is a chimney breast.

She comes down.

She gets a length of poly off the roll in the door and puts it on the floor and puts the wardrobe over on its face onto the poly, and unpicks the two back panels and takes them off and then lifts the carcass off the wall entirely, and the wall behind it is not a good colour, and she does not offer an opinion about the colour and the woman does not ask for one.

It is the most awkward thing she has done this month and she does it in the way she does everything, which is badly and without stopping.

---

She is outside in the middle of the afternoon with the tailgate up and the ladder in the way and the winter coming, and she gets the pad out of the glove box with the band round it, and it is shut, and the band is tight, and on the page inside there are two houses and a garage and a figure against each in her own writing.

She looks at the line for the second house of the day and she cannot get a figure for the back room, and the reason she cannot get one is that she spent most of the day on a wardrobe she could not have shifted off that wall with a tower, and there is nobody in that house to have the back room in that figure, and she is not going to put a number down that she has not spent the day on.

She puts the pen back in the door pocket next to the roll of tape and shuts the pad.

That is the whole of it. There is a box behind the bench with a number on its label that she read twice off a grey smear on the twenty-second of October and has not written down, and there is a pad in the glove box that is a professional instrument and has a rule about money in it, and neither of them has ever been the same kind of object to her and both of them are the same kind of object to somebody else.

---

The third thing the day does is the one she will turn over.

A woman rings her at about the end of the afternoon and it is a job she wants, and it is a good one, and it is up the far side of the ring road, and it is a full clear of a two-bed terrace with nothing in it that anybody has died in, which is the kind she likes.

"How much for the lot?"

"There's stairs in it."

"There's stairs in it. It's a two-bed. There's a back room and a front room and a landing and a bathroom and a kitchen and there'll be a garage."

"There's a garage. What's in the garage."

"There's a fridge in the garage."

"Right."

"The fridge is the one that does that. The one that goes. It has to go or I can't leave it and the vendor wants it out because of the smell."

"The fridge can go on the lorry."

"The fridge can go on the lorry. The rest of it can't."

"Meaning what."

"Meaning I can't do it with what's in the van."

She stands in the road outside a house off the ring road with the tailgate up and a folding step ladder lying along the left of her load bay.

"What do you need."

"A van with nothing in it."

"Right."

"That's not a no."

"That's not a yes."

---

And that is what the not-asking costs, and it costs it in a currency she understands exactly, and she stands in the road and works out that a two-bed terrace with a garage and a fridge in it is worth more to her than anything else on that list, and that what is in the way of it is four foot of aluminium lent to her on a Monday morning by a man who did not say when it was coming back.

She could ring him. She has not got his number.

That is the fact and she stands with it for a bit.

She has got a pad with a band round it that lives on the passenger seat when the van is shut and there is no line on any page of it that says anything about a man she spoke to in a street on Monday morning for as long as it took to say what she had come to say, and there was no reason for there to be, and she wrote nothing down then and she is not going to write anything down now, and the number she has got is a mobile and it was on a card she has never had and she gave it nothing to be written on.

"Right," she says. "I'll do the front and the landing and the bathroom. I'll leave the back room and the garage and I'll leave the fridge and I'll tell her why."

"That's not going to please her."

"No," she says. "It's going to give her a fridge out of it, though, and there's nobody in that house who's going to be able to tell you why you've left a room."

---

The house she empties is a man's, and he is in his sixties and he has moved out in the summer and has put everything in the garage and has been getting rid of it in bits since, and there is a man with a horse van and a daughter at the door and the man will not have it in his garage another week.

His name is on a set of papers on the kitchen table and she reads the surname once and does not use it, and she has never in this work found out anything about a man from his papers that she wanted, and that is not virtue, it is that there is no part of this job where a name would do her any good.

It takes most of a day and she does the back room last because she always does the back room last, and the horse van goes with the last load and the man signs and pays and says it's a favour and she says it is not a favour and takes the money, and the van is empty inside by the time the tailgate is up.

And the ladder will not go on it.

She tries it twice. It does not go on the front because there is nothing in the front left, and it does not go in the middle because a man with a horse van does not want a ladder across his load and does not say so and she can see it on him, and the back is full of the man's own things to the height of her chest because a man who moves out in the summer and does not finish does not have a rule about it.

The horse van goes. The man says he'll be in on Friday and not before, and she says she will ring him, and she will not ring him.

She stands in the porch of that man's empty house with a folding step ladder in her hand.

---

It is a step ladder. It is not rubbish. It is not furniture. There is no third place, and she has said that sentence to herself about a blanket in a bag of rags, and she has thought it before that about a good deal else, and it is the sentence her whole trade is made out of and it has never once been any use to her.

There are four things she could do with it and she stands in the porch and does all four in her head.

She could put it back on the offcuts behind the bench, which is where it was on Monday morning, and which is a space in a van that belongs to a man she has spoken to once.

She could leave it in the porch for whoever gets the house, which is a thing people do and which is how a skip works, and which would be gone in a week, and which is the same as putting it on the top of a heap, and she has a rule about a tip that says everything on a tip is already somebody's.

She could ring the merchant off the ring road and leave it at his tip, which is on her way home most evenings.

Or she could take it home and put it in her own garage, which she has not got, and which is the reason there is a third thing in that van behind the bench at all.

She leans it against the wall of the porch.

The door of that house is off its hinges and has been since August and is standing against the inside wall, and the porch is open to the road, and it is going to rain in the night, and there is a tape in her door pocket and she does not take it out.

She does not write a note on it and she does not knock on anything.

She goes and gets her pad off the passenger seat and looks at the line for that house, and what she writes is the figure she said out loud at the door, and she writes it and puts the pen back and shuts the pad, and there is nothing on any page of that pad about a ladder, and there is nothing on any page of that pad about a box either, and both of those things came out of the same van this autumn and she has a rule that stops at rubbish.

---
