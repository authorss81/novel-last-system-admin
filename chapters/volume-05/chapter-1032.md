# Chapter 1032 — A Square Of Paper Kept Flat

Undated in this draft.

**THE WINDOW IN THAT ROOM AT TWELVE IS SHUT DOWN TO A SLOT AND THE CHAIR BESIDE IT FACES THE WALL.**

Upstairs at the front of that house there is a room with a sash window in it that has not been up since before the summer because nobody in the house goes up there except the daughter, and the daughter is on that book and is at that yard today on a day when that yard is working. The sash is down about four inches off the bottom rail and the light that comes in under it lies along the floor about three inches deep, and the chair beside the bed stands with its back to the window because a chair that has been moved once for a woman to sit down in and then pushed back has usually been pushed back too far. The bed is against the wall opposite and nothing is on it.

She was in that chair with her coat still on when he came up, and she had been in it long enough to have taken her coat off over her arm, which is what a woman does in a room that is not hers when she has decided she is going to be in it a while. No fire has been lit in that room since the spring and there is not one in the house either, and the chair faces the wall because the strip of light under the sash does not reach far enough into the room to sit in, so a woman in that chair is sitting with the wall in front of her and the window behind her and about as much of the day as she can get.

"You have not come about my range."

"No."

"Then you have come up to a room with nothing in it to do, and I have not had that since the Tuesday I came down the river, so sit down if you can find somewhere and do not touch that bed."

He put his hands behind his back and left them there, and that was not the first thing he had decided to do in that house.

---

"You have had your hands behind you since you came through that door."

"I have."

"Whatever it is you are wanting, you are not going to reach for it."

"Not while it is in your hand."

She had it flat on her own right hand, open, and it was a square of paper about four inches by four, folded in three and opened out again so many times that every fold had gone through to a soft grey. A name is on the middle of it in a hand that is not hers, and the name is not hers, and nothing else on that square at all, no date, no figure, no mark of any kind, and she has never once wondered where a piece of paper that size comes from, because she has been carrying one like it since she was a girl in that house. She has had it on her since before he came up that frontage the first time and she did not ask for it and cannot make anything of it.

"I have never shown that to anybody in this house."

"I know that."

"Then you know what it is and you know I am not going to be told what it is by anybody who has seen it, so I am going to ask you the only thing I can think of asking anybody." She did not look at the paper. "Is her name in that book."

He stood in the middle of that room with his hands behind his back and got it out of him in about as long as it takes a man to decide.

"No."

"You have not had the book open in front of you this week."

"I have stood at that desk this week with the book open in front of me. I put the whole of a thing into words there that I have never put into words to anybody, and the woman who keeps that book told me what a column takes, and it takes work somebody asked for."

"Then she cannot put it."

"No. She cannot put it and she cannot go and ask you for it either, and the rest of that is not mine to put in your room and I am leaving it alone."

"You are not going to ask me to send for anything."

"I am not going to ask you anything at all, which is the only thing I have managed in four months on this frontage."

---

She turned her hand over so that the square of paper lay face down on her palm, and she did not fold it again, and she kept her hand open.

"I want you to understand what you have just put in front of me. My mother stood on that book before I did and before my grandmother, and I have never asked anybody what became of her week, and I have never asked anybody to put a line in against her name, because I did not know that anybody would. And now I know that the only way that name gets anywhere is for a house to send for something, and I am not a house that wants anything, and I have not been one since before the summer."

"That is not on me."

"No. I know it is not." She looked up at him then.

"That is the reason for this room and not the one below. My daughter ran this house for six weeks while I was up the river and she came back and put the whole of it on the table in front of me at that table, and a man stood at the end of it with his hands in his pockets and not one thing went down about him, and every woman in that room let him stand there. I have thought about which of us was the worst of it every day since that Tuesday. And a woman who has thought about that for a fortnight does not come downstairs and ask a man for a wall. She comes upstairs and asks him for the other thing, and not one brick of the wall is in any part of it."

"Is there anything at all you can do about it."

"There is one thing and it is not worth doing. She has told you in her own words what she will take, and what she will take is work somebody asked a house for, and a house does not have to want anything out of it. It only has to ask. If you ever wanted a man in that kitchen for a day and a half, and you went in there and asked her for it, she would enter a line and my name would go against it, and every woman in that yard would hear of it inside a week. That is all of what is open to me, and it is a thing you would have to want for yourself, and you have not told me you want anything out of that kitchen except a wall that does not come off it."

"Then you can leave that alone as well," she told him. "I have had a wall asked for by a man in a passage and I turned him down, and I asked him for it in a pantry instead, and I am not going to be talked into anything else in that house before the wall is done."

He went down that stair and out into the passage with two names in his head that had no business in one head together, and both of them are on nothing at all, and one of them belongs to a man who is standing in a kitchen this afternoon with nothing in his hands and the other to a woman who has been dead longer than he has been in this borough, and he is the only person in this borough keeping either of them. He had taken on the first thing for anybody that was neither work nor money and that cannot be done.

The strip of light under that sash comes in about the width of three inches, which is short of a hand laid flat, and it will be a hand's breadth by the time that window is up.

She kept the square of paper flat on her own open hand until he had gone down the stairs and out into the passage, then folded it in three along the folds it already had and put it away where it had been living for however long. Nobody in that house saw her do it.
