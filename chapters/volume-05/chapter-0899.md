# Chapter 0899 — Down To The Dish

Thursday the fourth of April 2024, at the square hole in the apron at the lock-up from the time the yard has dried until the light has left the concrete. Down to the dish in a square hole.

**SHE HAS A SQUARE HOLE IN THE APRON AT THE LOCK-UP WITH A DISH IN THE BOTTOM OF IT AND SHE MEANS TO SET THE HEEL OF THE HAND INTO THE MOUTH OF IT AND PUSH UNTIL THE WRIST MEETS THE RIM AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS AT THE MESS ROOM DOOR FROM INSIDE WITH THE DOOR OPEN AND HER STANDING IN IT.**

That opening has marked the apron since the pour set, and brooms have passed it weekly as it kept its outline.

It is a hole about six inches square, with the sides going straight down about four inches to a dish of earth in the bottom.

She wants how far down the dish lies and what it is, and whether the sides go straight or belly out underneath.

A hole in an apron rewards a hand better than a look, because feet wear the rim while earth settles the bottom out of sight.

So the heel of the hand goes into the mouth of the hole and pushes down until the wrist meets the rim.

The rim is sharp on two sides where the concrete broke clean, and rounded on the other two where feet have worn it smooth.

The hand goes down past the wrist a little and stops on something firm. Not concrete. Earth, seated firm below in a shallow saucer, dark and moist, carrying grit plus a nail head rusted level at its centre.

She turns the heel a little and the dish gives about half an inch under it and then stops firm again, because earth in a hole packs down the more it is pressed.

What the dish sits on is more earth, going down further than the hand can reach, with the sides of the hole going straight down to it without any footing under the concrete.

The woman who books work appears at the mess room door from within, door wide, arms folded as she leans.

"Will the hole take a post?"

"It will take a post for a summer."

"Will it stand the winter?"

"It will stand till winter lifts it."

She unfolded her arms and folded them again, and stood in the doorway a while with the sun behind her, so that the hole could not be seen from where she was standing, and then she went back to her work and left the door open.

---

A hole like that is left when a post is taken out and not put back, because the concrete is cut round the post and the post is lifted, and the earth underneath is left to settle.

The bar leaves its mark where it bites, and the concrete keeps the mark the way timber keeps the mark of a saw.

The sides show the marks of the iron bar that lifted it, with two gouges on the north side where the bar slipped twice.

Concrete breaks clean where it has gone hard and crumbles where it is lean, and this apron broke clean on the cut sides and crumbled a little on the worn ones.

That is why the rim is sharp where the saw cut it and rounded where feet have passed over it for years.

Earth in the bottom of a hole dishes down in the middle because rain sits in it and presses it, and each rain presses a little more in the middle where the water stands longest.

That is why the dish is shallow at the rim and deep in the middle, with the nail head sitting in the deepest part where the water threw it.

The nail head is from the post that stood there, with the shank rusted away and the head left in the earth where it fell.

Iron rusts fast in earth that stays damp, because water and air meet on the iron and eat it, and a nail in a dish like that goes in three years.

That is why the head is flat and thin and the shank is gone, while nails in dry timber last twenty years and more.

A nail in dry timber keeps its shank because oil from the timber coats the iron and keeps water off, and each winter adds another coat as the timber sweats.

A nail in a dish keeps nothing, because water sits on it every rain and never dries right off, and the rust flakes away each time a foot shakes the ground.

The head left here is from a rose nail with a broad head made for fencing, and the broad head is what kept it from going down deeper when the shank gave way.

Rose nails spread their load into soft timber the way a washer does, and a fence post hung on rose nails pulls out of its rail before the nails pull out of the post.

That is why the post came out with its nails still in it and left only this one head behind in the earth.

She sets the heel in again deeper, to feel how the sides run below the rim.

They run straight for about three inches and then belly out a little all round, where the earth has washed out from under the concrete and left a lip overhanging it.

A lip like that will take a post for a summer, because the post wedges under the lip and the lip keeps it upright.

It will not stand the winter, because winter rain gets under the lip and lifts the concrete round the hole, and the post rocks loose by spring.

A hole like that is filled with old grout already gone hard, rammed round a new post, and the top sloped off so the water goes off it instead of standing in it.

Grout slopes throw water the way slates do, and a sloped top keeps the foot of a post dry while a flat one ponds it.

A flat top ponds because the rim of concrete round it stands proud and keeps the water in, and each ponding softens the earth a little more under the post.

A sloped top throws it out over the apron where the broom takes it, and the foot stays firm through winter.

The concrete of the apron is solid to the knuckle all round the hole, with no cracks running off it and no hollow drumming under it.

Concrete drums hollow where water has got under it and washed the earth away, and the drum sounds dull when tapped with a knuckle.

This apron rings true right up to the rim, which says the earth under it is still firm and the lip under the rim is only a local wash and not a general scour.

The earth in the dish is firm at the rim where feet press it and soft in the middle where water sits, and the nail head lies firm in the soft part the way a stone lies in mud.

She takes the hand out of the hole and brushes the earth off the heel on the rim, and reads the hole from the yard for what the square says.

Rain off the lock-up roof runs across the apron in a sheet and drops into the hole, and the sheet brings grit that settles round the rim and sharpens it underfoot. The sheet thins where broom strokes have polished the pour and thickens where moss rims the far corner. Moss rims only the shaded corner by the downpipe elbow, where drizzle lingers past noon. The elbow drips long after rain stops, feeding that green rim through dry weeks.

From above the hole looks like a dark square in pale concrete, with the dish showing through as a darker spot in the middle.

From the side the lip shows as a shadow all round under the rim, where the earth has gone back from the concrete.

A dark square in pale pour with a darker saucer at the middle of it, and a shadow going round the whole of the rim where the lip stands clear of the earth beneath it, and both of them belong to the same hole. The palm had the measure of that and took it away again, and the dish of earth stayed where it was put with the nail head flat in the middle of it, and the lip kept keen all round beneath it.
