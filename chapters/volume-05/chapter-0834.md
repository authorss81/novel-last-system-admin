# Chapter 0834 — Three Holes Where The Lilac Was

Monday the seventh of August 2023, in that side passage at number twelve from the time the dew is off the grass until the light has gone off the lilac, and a lilac that is going with a drain is the whole of that Monday.

**THERE IS A LILAC IN THE SIDE PASSAGE AT NUMBER TWELVE AND SHE MEANS TO TAKE THE STOOL OUT OF THE GROUND, AND HER SISTER COMES INTO THAT PASSAGE ONCE ABOUT HALF WAY THROUGH AND IS TOLD TO STAND CLEAR IN THE DOORWAY AND DOES NOT COME IN.**

That lilac is against the house wall in a strip about two feet wide, cut back in May about two feet off the passage with a spade, square, and left to go grey where the cut was.

It has put out about a foot since May and it has put it out thick, which is what a lilac does when you take the top off it and is the whole reason she took the top off it. In May she worked out from the way the bar went in where the rest of it was going, and you take the top off a lilac and you have three. She told her sister so at the time over the back wall, that a lilac cut back puts out more than was there, and she is now doing the thing that follows from it.

The rule she has for a lilac is that it is not a bush, it is a stool with shoots off it, and a stool is buried and the shoots are the plant. Whatever the shoots have become, the stool under them is the same size as it was the day it went in, and it goes on being the size of it for twenty years and it does not get smaller whatever you do to the top. So the only part of a lilac that anybody wants is the part that gets bigger, and everything else is in the way of a drain.

She starts with a fork and a spade and a wrecking bar, and a sheet on the paving to keep the soil off the flags laid in May, which are not to be lifted in any of it.

The top is already off, so what is left is the stool, the thick base where the stems meet the roots, with three strong shoots up from it about as thick as her wrist.

Three, and she is glad of the three, because a stool with three shoots on it has three strong roots to key it in and a stool with one shoot on it has to be fought out sideways against whatever is next to it. A lilac wants a wide stool with room round it and this one has never had any room, being two feet off a house wall.

She cuts the shoots first, low, with the saw, flat across, and stacks them on the sheet for the barrow. Each cut face is pale and wet in the middle on a dry day, with a dark ring round it where the bark was.

The bark ring is where the bark was and not a stain. A lilac's bark is loose and grey and comes off the wood in a ring, and if the cut is made above the ring the face is white all the way out and if it is made through the ring the face has a black rim about an eighth of an inch wide, and she cannot make that go differently and does not know that it should.

She puts the shoots on the sheet and not on the barrow, because a cut face lying on the barrow with fifty other things in it will be scratched to nothing, and these three are firewood and there is no hurry with them.

Then the stool itself. She forks round it in a circle about a foot out, loosening the soil, and puts the bar in under it from three sides to feel where the roots run.

The first time the bar goes in it stops at three inches. The second time, on the other side, it goes past the handle. She takes it out and goes along the run of it with the bar flat and her hand on the top of it, and the bar tells her exactly how far round the stool the roots go, because a bar laid flat over roots goes over a hump and into a hollow and a bar laid flat over soil goes straight.

The stool goes further than she expected. The bar goes down a full blade and then another half under the middle, into ground that is firm on both sides of it, and the roots run out under the paving on one side and under the house wall on the other without giving.

The house wall side is the one that stops her. The bar gets under about half of it and then there is the wall and the bar cannot go into a wall, and a root running into a house foundation has to be cut and there is only one place to cut it.

She works it an inch at a time, fork and bar by turns, cutting the side roots with the spade where they show, square, the way she cut the root in May, and leaving the cut ends clean.

Square is the whole of it and she cut the other one the same way in May with the same spade for the same reason. A root cut with a spade gets a face at an angle of forty-five degrees and the soil closes on that face the first time it gets wet and the root does not call out from it and does not go anywhere. A root cut with a saw gets a face as flat as a shelf and it stays dry under the soil and the root uses it as a face to grow along, and a year later there is a hard edge under the paving where the root has been. She has seen both. The paving goes down square on a root cut with a spade and it goes down hard and wavy on a root cut with a saw, and the wavy one is what everybody means by paving that has settled.

So the spade goes in at about a hand's width out from the stool and comes out at the stool, and every cut is a face and not a shave, and she goes round it four times before it will lift.

Her sister comes into that side passage once, about half way through, with a basket for the back garden, and is told to stand clear, and stands in the doorway of the side door and does not come in past it. She does not touch a shoot and nothing is asked about the lilac.

“Mind where you put your feet,” she tells her.

“I am in the doorway and no further,” her sister tells her, and she goes back out with the basket empty, and that is all between them on it.

The basket is in the doorway of a side passage four feet wide with a barrow and a sheet and a stool and a bar in it, and a person standing in the only doorway in the middle of it for as long as it takes somebody to turn round and say two sentences.

---

When the light has gone off the lilac the stool is out.

It comes up with a sucking pop out of the hole, whole, with soil on it and roots hanging, and she lays it on the sheet and knocks the soil off back into the hole with the fork.

It is about as big as two fists together and it is not a ball of roots at all, which is what she had expected. It is a knuckle of old wood with three short stubs on it where the shoots were and a fringe of roots an inch long coming out of the bottom of it, and the fringe is the whole of what it had left to grip the ground with, and that is a thing about lilacs that nobody tells you.

The hole it came out of is deeper than the stool and about the size of the stool, and the sides of it are clean where the spade went and ragged where the roots tore, and the bottom of it is where the bar was in the morning.

The soil goes back in and it does not go back the same, because soil with the root fringe torn out of it is not the same as soil with the root fringe still in it. She puts the fork in and turns it over twice and puts it in flat and does not pack it, because packed soil over a broken root collar is a hard pan, and a hard pan in a passage is where the water stands.

The paving is still down in one piece on both sides of the strip, and the root she cut in May under the middle of it is not touched, and the heap of edge stones by the gate is not touched.

She stands at the back end of the passage with the sheet full and the barrow beside it and looks down the strip where the work was. The stool is out of the ground and there are three holes where it was with the passage open in between them, and the cut faces on the stacked shoots are already going pale at the edges.
