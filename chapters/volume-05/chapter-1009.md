# Chapter 1009 — A Name Comes Off At The End Of A Season

Undated in this draft.

**THE SMALL STORE AT THE LOCK-UP HAS A SHELF PUT ACROSS IT AND ONE HIGH WINDOW.**

The small store off the yard at the lock-up is a room about six feet by eight with a door on the yard side that has a hasp on it and no lock on the hasp, and one high window of small panes in the far wall that has not been cleaned inside since before the summer and lets in about as much light as a sheet of paper does. There is a sack of old packing in the corner, a broom against the wall, and a yard brush behind the door with its bristles flat. Somebody put a plank shelf across the middle of the room on two uprights set into the floor, and it is not square to the window above it, so that the shelf comes out about three inches further on the yard side than it does at the back, and everything that gets put on that shelf slides to the front of it. There is nothing on the shelf and nothing under it either. The door stands open because he came in through it.

A store off a yard like that is not a room anybody works in. It is where things go in on a Friday and come out of on a Monday, and it is swept out on the Monday by whoever is nearest, and nobody in that yard has opened the door of it in daylight for a year. He had not been in it since the spring either, and he had been in the office four times since the summer, and it is worth putting those two facts side by side.

She came across the yard from the office rather than send him, which told him before she was in the room that she had understood what he had come with and had decided not to have that conversation at a desk. She stood in the doorway with her coat still on and her hands in her pockets and she looked at him for about as long as it takes a man to decide whether he is going to tell the truth or the other thing.

"You have somebody else's ask.

"Yes."

"Then you will not have it off me in this room with the door open, and I will not have you tell me whose it is first, because you will not be able to put that into words without telling me." She came the whole way in and pulled the door to behind her by the edge of it, so that the yard noise stopped all at once. "Whose is it."

"A woman who has been on your book since she was seventeen."

"Away six weeks and come back and the first thing she can think of is to come off."

"She has four days."

"You have four days, because I have told you the date in this room whether you came for it or not." She took her hands out of her pockets. "Then here is the price and it is three sentences and I am going to give you all three before you have got a word out. A name comes off that book at the end of a season and not one day before. The six weeks she has just done up the water go on it, and if she comes off before the season shuts then that six weeks do not go on it either, and there is a man on the yard now who will tell you himself what that is worth to him in a fortnight's time. And the book shuts on Friday."

He put his hand on the shelf while she was on the third of the three, because he had to put it somewhere, and the shelf moved about a quarter of an inch on the yard side and stopped dead on the far side, which told him the two uprights are in different states of mind about what they are carrying.

"Can a name go on outside a season?"

"No."

"Can it go on after Friday?"

"It can go on in the spring and it will be worth exactly what it is worth then." She did not move away from the door. "And before you go out of that door with a face on you, hear the rest of it. She is not the first. Two men came back up the water in the autumn with six weeks between them and asked me to take them off, and I could not do it, and one of them is still on that book this morning and does not know that I tried. So she has asked the right question at the wrong time, and there is nobody in this borough she could have asked who could have done it sooner, which is the shape of this thing and it always has been."

---

That is what she could put down and it is what she has got one column for, and she put him in it. He came out of that store knowing more about the arrangement he had been living in for ten years than he has ever known about anything, and knowing it about three weeks too late to do anything at all with it, and he stood in the middle of that yard working out what a person is when the only thing that is on any paper anywhere in their life is their name against somebody else's work.

He stood in that store for a while and let it be the shape of the thing. The four sentences she had given him covered the whole of what he had come up that frontage for in three weeks of walking, and he had asked her for the price of a line in the summer and been refused, and here was the price of a removal and it was larger than the other one, and the reason it was larger was that the woman asking could not lose anything by getting it. A man cannot come off a book and take his work off it with him. A woman can, and she can only do it by putting six weeks of somebody else's back down so that her own six weeks are worth what she has been told they are worth, and this is the first arithmetic in the whole business that has a person's life in it and not a man's pride.

He asked her whether she would put down that a man had come with it. She would put down anything he asked her to put down except the part she could not put down, and what could not be got onto it was whether he had come because a woman sent him or because he wanted something out of it, and from where she stood she had seen him unable to tell which of the two it was, and a book with two columns would have had him in one of them and she has only got the one. He agreed with the whole of it. It is the only time in his life he has agreed with the woman who keeps a book about the exact shape of the thing he came to get at it.

Then he thanked her, which she waved at him before it was half done.

---

He went out into the yard, and he did not go straight down the frontage, and he did not go to the unit, and he stood the better part of an hour in the open middle of that yard where the lorries turn with his hands in his pockets and the water running off the middle bay roof in a line at the edge of the road, working out how a woman at number nine is going to feel on Friday morning when she finds out that what she asked him for costs her the only six weeks she has ever been able to point at.

Out of true by about that much, and everything that goes onto that shelf stops at the front edge and waits there, and it kept him there for the length of one conversation without either of them noticing.
