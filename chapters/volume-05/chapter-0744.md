# Chapter 0744 — The Paper In The Drawer

Saturday the twenty-third of July 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house and her own yard at number ten from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a piece of ply leaned against a shed and three lines of paper put away in a drawer with the tools that would do them.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES CARRIES A PLY OFFCUT OFF THE WALL IN HER OWN YARD AND LEANS IT AGAINST THE FRONT EDGE OF HER SHED WHERE THE FELT HAS GONE AND DOES NOT NAIL IT, IS OFFERED HER OWN THREE LINES OF PAPER FOR HER SISTER'S TABLE AND SAYS NO ON THE GROUND THAT IT WOULD MAKE IT HER SISTER'S JOB, AND PUTS THE PAPER INTO THE DRAWER UNDER HER OWN WORKTOP WITH THE HAMMER AND THE SCREWDRIVER IN IT AND SHUTS THE DRAWER.**

She is a woman in her early thirties in her own house and yard at number ten on Saturday. Her sister comes to the gate in the middle of the day, comes up the yard, and speaks to her in the yard and then at the kitchen table.

---

She carries the ply offcut out at about the middle of the morning.

It is a strip of ply about a foot and a half and four inches wide with a square end where it came off a sheet, and it has been against the wall of the yard since the end of May because it is not rubbish and it is not use, and the wall is where such a thing lives until it is one or the other.

She takes it up in both hands and walks to the shed with it and gets it against the front edge of the roof where the felt has come off and where the corner lifts and comes down and lifts again.

She stands it there at the angle of the lift and lets go of it.

It stands.

That is the whole of what it does. It does not stop the corner lifting, because the corner is over on the inside edge of the shed and a strip of ply standing on the outside of it takes the weight of the water and not the weight of the flap. What it does is stand there under the piece that is loose, all day, every day, for as long as nobody else puts a hand on it.

She does not nail it. She has a hammer and there are nails in an old tin on the worktop and there is a three-inch gap between the ply and the shed face at the bottom because the felt is not there for it to nail to.

She looks at it for a while from the back step and then she goes in.

---

Her sister comes to the gate in the middle of the day with her bag on her arm and looks down the yard past her at the shed and the strip of ply standing against the edge of the roof.

"That is not fixed."

"That is not fixed."

"It will come off in the first wind that gets under it."

"It will." She put her thumb and finger against the ply where it met the shed. "And when it does I will put it back, and it will do the same job again, and it is not costing me anything to have a thing doing a job badly and free instead of not doing it at all."

"You have not had that thing up since the end of May." Her sister came further down the yard to look at it.

"No."

She came in through the gate and shut it behind her and came up the yard to the back door, which shuts true, and they went through to the kitchen together. The washing was out and pegged across the draught with the peg at the shed end pushed a finger further than it wanted, where it has been since the end of May, and she has not loosened it and is not going to.

The table in the middle of the kitchen had the paper on it with the three lines on it, folded in half and folded in half again, face down, with the folded edge towards the chair she sits on.

"You have had it face down." Her sister stood at the table without touching it.

"It has been face down since Tuesday."

Her sister put her bag down by the leg of the table and took her cardigan off over her arm and hung it on the newel, and looked at the back of the paper and did not touch it.

"Give it me." "And I will put it on my own table and I will get somebody who knows about roofing to look at it, and it will be somebody else's job, and you will not have to say the sentences at all."

She did not answer for a moment.

"That is not a thing about roofing." She did not sit down either.

"It is three lines about a shed."

"It is three lines and they do not say what any of them weigh or cost," she says, "and there is nobody in this town I can stand in front of with those sentences, and if I gave you that paper then within a month it would be a job on your table and not mine, and I would have done that to you on a Saturday in July without asking you, and I am not doing that."

"You have asked nobody for anything since December."

"Yes."

---

Her sister sat down at the table at the other side, which she never does, and put her forearms on it.

"Then what are you going to do with it?" Her sister turned her forearms over on the wood. "It has been on that table since the end of April. It has been folded and unfolded and folded and it has not moved and it has not gone anywhere, and every week it is the same piece of paper and every week it is three lines and none of them have got a single word under them."

"I am going to put it in the drawer."

Her sister looked up.

"That drawer has been shut since the Thursday and you have not opened it." Her sister nodded at the worktop as if she could see through it.

"I know what is in that drawer." She put two fingers on the drawer front. "There is a hammer and a screwdriver and after this there will be three lines of paper in it as well, and I am not doing that because I have run out of places to put paper."

"Is that better?"

"No. That is worse, and it is worse on purpose, and I want it in there so that anybody who opens that drawer has the means and the job in front of them at the same time and can see that the two do not fit."

---

She took the paper off the table and unfolded it out flat on the wood and looked at the three lines with her sister looking at them over her shoulder.

"A roll of something. A tin. A lip of timber under a new edge."

"That is what it has always read."

"It is what it has always read and it is the only sentence in this house that is finished," she says, "and it was finished one December night, and finishing it is not the same as being able to start it."

She folded it in half and folded it in half again and put her hand flat on it for a second, and did not get up.

"You are not doing it now," her sister said.

"I am doing it when you have gone."

"Why not now."

"Because you would watch," she says. "And because you asked me for it four minutes ago."

Her sister sat where she was.

"You are going to shut a job in with your tools," her sister says.

"Yes."

"And you are not going to take it out again?"

"Not this week."

---

She carried the washing in at the middle of the afternoon and folded it on the table beside the paper without covering it, and then folded the paper and put it where it had been on the table until her sister was at the gate.

Her sister waited at the gate with her bag on her arm and looked once more down the yard at the strip of ply standing against the shed at the angle of the loose felt, not nailed, doing nothing.

"I am at nine tomorrow if you want me," her sister said.

"I will want you," she said, and did not say when, and her sister went off up the lane and did not wait at the gate.

She came in and washed the two cups and put them away on the shelf and went over the table with the cloth, and there was a clean square of wood in the middle of it that had not been a colour for a while and matched the rest of it now.

Then she pulled the drawer under the worktop out with her elbow and put the folded paper down on the hammer and the screwdriver and pushed it shut, and stood with her hand flat on the drawer front.

Three lines of paper in there now with the tools that would do them, in one place, on purpose, put in after the person who had asked for them had gone out of the gate, and she had said out loud at the table that it was worse that way.

She took her hand off the drawer and shut the back door, which shuts true.
