# Chapter 0888 — Nails Standing On End

Monday the twenty-sixth of February 2024, at the pipe standing in the corner of the yard at the lock-up, from the time the office glass has cleared until the shutter has gone down for the day. What is standing in the bottom of a pipe.

**SHE HAS A LENGTH OF PIPE STOOD ON END IN THE CORNER OF THAT YARD WITH NAILS IN IT, AND SHE MEANS TO PUT ONE HAND DOWN INSIDE THAT PIPE AND THEN CLOSE THE OTHER HAND ROUND THE WRIST OF THE FIRST AND PUSH, SO THAT THE FORCE COMES OUT OF THE ARM AND NOT OUT OF THE FINGERS, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS AT THE MESS ROOM DOOR WITH HER HEEL AGAINST IT.**

It has stood in that corner since the autumn and it was put there by whoever emptied the bin the first time.

It is a length of about four feet, set up on end against the brickwork with the sawn end uppermost, and it is filled from the top with the nails that come out of the job.

There is a bin of nails in the office with a label on it, and the bin gets emptied into that pipe when the bin fills up, and the pipe has been filled four times since it was stood there, and the last time it came to within an inch of the mouth.

The nails are the ones out of pallets. Cut nails and clouts and the short twelves that come out of a board, all mixed, and they are mixed because they go in a bin mixed.

She has never put her hand in it.

A hand cannot go into a pipe full of nails, and the first thing anybody finds out about a pipe full of nails is that it cannot be got at. The bore is four inches and the fingers get about an inch before the first nail is against them, and inside a pipe of nails a hand has no purchase at all, because a hand pushing in a pipe full of nails pushes on a nail, and the nail pushes on another nail, and the pipe takes the lot.

So the second hand closes over the wrist of the first, from the underside, and pushes.

That is all of it, and nothing else will do it.

---

The woman who books work turns up at the mess room door about half past ten with her heel against it and the door shut behind her, and stands in the doorway rather than coming out.

She wanted to know whether the small hand truck was still out at the unit, because a job came in on Thursday for a gate post and the hand truck would want collecting first.

She was told the hand truck was out and would be back on the Wednesday.

"That'll be the day."

"It was back on the Tuesday the time before."

"Then I'll get the gate post delivered."

"You can get the gate post delivered."

She stood in the doorway a moment longer, and then said the thermometer on the office wall has read the same mark since Christmas, and that somebody ought to put it right before somebody believes it, and she went back in and the door shut behind her.

The hand goes down about an inch and comes on nails.

They are lying at all angles, the way loose nails lie, and the first layer of them is across the top of what was tipped in last. The surface of it is round, because loose material tipped into a round pipe stands up in the middle and falls away at the sides, and it is a hand's width below the mouth in the middle and level with it at the edges.

She pushes, and the other hand pushes on her wrist, and the top layer of nails moves about half an inch and stops.

The first question is whether that pipe is empty under the nails, and it is full. A nail lying across other nails and pushed from above does not move, because the load goes straight down the pipe and the pipe is standing on hard ground, and the whole column of nails underneath has to go somewhere, and it has nowhere to go.

She pushes harder, and gets the hand in another inch, and the fingers come on something that is not a nail.

Wood.

A batten, about the width of a thumb and an inch and a half through, lying across the bore an inch and a half under the nail surface, with its two ends jammed into the wall of the pipe at the sides and its middle carrying an inch and a half of loose nails.

And that batten is why this pipe has never been emptied.

A pipe of nails cannot be emptied by tipping it out, because a batten lying across the pipe catches everything above it and lets everything below it out, so a tip empties the bottom of the pipe and leaves the top of it in.

It cannot be emptied by hand either, because an inch and a half of nails is a lid and a hand does not get through a lid of nails.

And it cannot be lifted, because a four-foot length of pipe with two feet of nails in the bottom of it takes two people and a clear patch of apron, and it is standing in a corner with brick behind it.

What that batten is doing there is evident once a hand is on it. Somebody laid that pipe down on the ground, part-filled it from the end, laid the batten in on top of the nails to stop them being knocked out along its length, and then stood the pipe up in the corner with the batten in it.

The two ends of that batten have been in the rust of the pipe long enough to have gone into the wall of it, so the batten is not coming out either, and whoever wants the nails out of that pipe is not going to get them by tipping it.

And the way that pipe got filled explains why the batten is not a thing anybody has ever had cause to think about. The batten is in the middle of it, so the pipe was laid down to be filled. A pipe filled standing up has its nails tipped in from the top and settled, and the top of the fill ends up round and hard, packed by its own weight as it goes on. A pipe filled lying down has a piece of wood laid across it on purpose, by somebody thinking about getting the nails out again later and expecting somebody else in mind to help lift it.

Which is how that pipe came to be standing in a corner at all: down on the ground, part-filled, a batten across it, and up on its end again against the brickwork. Whoever was going to help lift it never came.

One more thing about that pipe, and it is the reason the nails in it are worth knowing about.

The mouth of it stands a foot and a bit above the apron, open, with no lid on it and nothing over it.

So the inch and a half of nails above that batten has been in the open since it was stood there, and it gets the rain that comes on that corner, and the ones in the middle of the pipe stay dry because the round of the pipe keeps the rain off them.

The result is a pipe with bright nails in it and rusty nails in it at the same time, and the rusty ones are the ones nearest the mouth, and they are the ones that will come out first.

Nothing is done about any of it, and nothing wants doing, and the pipe and the batten are sound.

The pipe is sound steel with a little rust on the outside and a film of it inside. The nails under the batten are bright where they rub one another and about two and a half inches long, which is what goes into a shutter or a gate stile. The ones above it have gone dull and orange in the rain, and the ones above those are still bright.

That pipe stands in the corner of that yard with the batten in it and the nails above it, and whoever empties it will take them out in handfuls with a bin on the ground, rusty ones first, although two handfuls an afternoon is not a rate anybody will call progress.
