# Chapter 0667 — The Open Drawer

Monday the thirteenth of September 2021, in this borough. He is in a unit off the industrial estate from first light until midday with a shoebox on his bench, and the whole of the morning is a drawer he does not shut and a road he does not drive up.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES WORKS ALL MORNING ROUND AN OPEN DRAWER UNDER A BENCH IN HIS VAN WITH A NEW BRUSH IN A PLASTIC BAG IN IT, TELLS A MAN ON THE TELEPHONE A FIGURE HE KNOWS IS WRONG, LEAVES THE DRAWER OPEN, AND DOES NOT DRIVE UP THAT ROAD.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is on his own all morning. Two men speak to him and neither of them is the one who should have.

---

The unit is cold when he lifts the shutter, and his bench is where he left it on Friday, with the shoebox on top of it where he cannot come in without seeing it, and inside the shoebox is a stove element he has carried twice in car parks for no reason.

He puts the kettle on the ring at the back and stands with his hands in his pockets while it heats, and he looks at the box without opening it. He knows what is in it without looking, which is why it is on top and not underneath.

He wants to get a kitchen frame ready for a house on the estate without touching the van drawer, because the drawer is open and has been open since the third of August, and he wants to prove to himself he can work round it.

He gets his hand as far as the edge of the shoebox twice in the first hour. The second time he puts two fingers on the lid and lifts it about an inch, and the element is down inside in a bed of screwed-up newspaper, and the ceramic is the colour of a tooth, and there is a hairline crack going round the top of it that he has known about since he bought it off a man in a car park in June and could not see in daylight.

He puts the lid down. It is not cracked. He has been telling himself it is not cracked since June, and a thing in a shoebox on a bench is the easiest kind of thing in the world to leave alone, and that is the entire reason it is on top of the bench and not in a cupboard in the unit where it would be out of his sight and also out of his reach.

He thinks about ringing the man he bought it off. The number is on a card with the money still written on the back of it, and it is on the telephone before he has decided to look at the card, and he decides to look at the card, and then he puts the kettle down on the ring and goes to saw.

He lays the frame on trestles and marks the rail where the new bead will go, and he saws outside the line and planes to it, and the shavings curl down onto his boots. The radio at the bench opposite is on low with talk he does not follow, and a man in his mid thirties at that bench nods to him and gets on with his own work.

At midmorning he needs a short stiff brush to clear dust out of a mortise before he glues. The brush he reaches for in his canvas bag is not there, and he stands with his hand in the empty side pocket for a moment.

The stranger's brush with a curl of old egg-coloured gloss in the crimp is in that side pocket, on a leather bottom with a stone in it, in a footwell, since the twentieth of July, and it is not the brush he wants.

He blows the mortise out with his mouth instead and wipes it with his finger, and the joint fits dry and true, and he has a scrape on the inside of his wrist from doing it, and the dust is down inside the tenon where the glue is going, and he does it again with a bent piece of wire because the wire is in the van, and the van is twenty yards away with the drawer open in it.

He leaves the wire. He is not going to the van for a wire and he is not going to the van for a brush either, and those are two different not-goings and he keeps them apart.

---

He eats bread at the bench with the frame clamped, and while the glue goes off he goes out to the van with the tailgate open to fetch screws, and the drawer under the bench on the passenger side is open as he left it.

Inside it flat along the back in a plastic bag is the second wire brush, nine pounds of his own money out of a rack by the window in a shop with a galvanised bucket by the door.

He looks at the plastic bag and does not touch it, because touching it would make it a thing he was deciding about, and he has already decided.

The brush in the bag is for a job he is not going to do, because the woman who clears houses put her hand over her mouth in a kitchen on the sixteenth of July and said she would do the gate, and if he goes first he takes that off her and there is no way to put it back.

He has not driven up that road since the seventeenth of June. He has gone past the end of it once, in the last week of August, without slowing down, and he saw the pale line of the gate against the dark wall of the coal shed from the driver's seat and he took his eyes back to the road because the road was there. He has not been told anything and does not need to be.

He shuts the tailgate with the drawer still open inside and goes back to the bench.

---

The shop telephone rings about a cupboard. He takes it standing up, with the frame still on the trestles and the filler going off behind him.

"Smith."

"Is that the joiner? I'm after a cupboard door. My son's got one off a door and it's driving me mad, I can't shut it."

"Right. What is it, a flat panel or a shaker?"

"I don't know what that is."

"Has it got a lip round it, or is it just a slab of a door?"

"There's a lip on it. You can see where it's been, there's a lighter line where it's been."

"That's a shaker. What size is it?"

"I couldn't tell you that."

"Are you working off the door, or have you got the old one?"

"I'm working off the door. He's took the doors off for a bank holiday and he's put the panels in a skip already."

"Then you'll have to measure it before you go into the bank holiday." He gets the tape off the bench hook. "Measure the width, measure the height, and measure the lip, and if the lip's not the same all round about it, it's going to fight you. Take the measurement to the joinery place on the corner and ask them for a four-millimetre lipped panel to order and say it's for a bank holiday on the Monday, and tell them it's the Thursday before or they won't have it."

"Right. And what'll it be for the fitting?"

He opens his mouth, and the figure is there before he has heard himself get to it, the way a figure is when you have said one to a stranger before and your mouth has learned the shape of it.

"There's about ten minutes on a door like that."

"What's ten minutes? A quarter of an hour?"

"About ten minutes."

"No, that's not a quarter of an hour, is it."

"No."

"Right." He hears the woman on the other end decide to be pleased rather than to argue, and he waits while she does it. "Well. That's not bad, is it."

"It's not a bad number to be told."

"Right you are. I'll ring the bank holiday Monday, then."

"You ring the Thursday before and ask for the Monday. They'll be shut on the Monday."

"Right. The Thursday before." The line goes down.

He puts the receiver back on its cradle, and stands there, and does the thing he has done every time since July, which is to run it back in his head and decide whether it was the right thing. It takes about four seconds. It was the right thing. A woman working out her own costs with a tape measure and a form on a knee does not benefit from a man in a unit telling her the truth about an hour, and neither does a man who is going to have to eat the difference out of a frame that took him the best part of a week.

The voice at the bench opposite comes across the sawdust.

"Who was that?"

"Bank holiday job. Cabinet door."

"Did you quote him?"

"Yeah."

"How much?"

"Ten minutes."

The man at the bench looks at him for a second and then goes back to his own work, and neither of them says anything else about it, and that is the arrangement they have without ever having made it.

---

He fits the bead and pins it and fills the holes, and he sands the frame with the grain and not across it, and the wood comes up pale and clean under his hand. He looks at the shoebox while the filler dries, and he thinks about the kitchen at number twelve, where he said nothing on the sixteenth of July to a woman who asked him how much is that, and decided it in about four seconds while he was still looking at the drops, and still thinks it was the right thing.

He said about ten minutes for a job that is not ten minutes. He said fifty pound to an old man at a gate in July, a figure he has never charged anybody, and did not correct it.

Both sentences are still true in his head and neither of them needs another sentence after it.

He is glad of work that has a beginning and an end and a price, because the shoebox on top of his bench has none of those now.

---

He packs the tools at midday and sweeps the shavings into a pile with a brush that is his own and lives on the bench, and he puts the frame on the rack with its face to the wall.

He does not open the van drawer to put anything away. He puts the canvas bag in the footwell with the stranger's brush still in the side pocket, and he leaves the under-bench drawer open under the bench.

A drawer that is open is a thing he has not finished with, even when he has finished with it, and he knows that is not sense, and he leaves it as it is.

He drives out of the estate with the windows down and goes the long way round so he does not pass the end of that road, because passing the end would make it a thing he did on purpose.

He stops for fuel on the ring road, and while the tank fills he looks at the shop with the bucket by the door across the way, and he does not go in, because there is nothing in there he needs.

The woman who clears houses bought hers out of a bucket by that door on the twenty-sixth of July, and he bought his out of the same bucket, and neither of them knows the other did, and that is how it should stay.

He gets back to the unit and locks the van with the drawer still open inside it, and he puts the shoebox back on the top of his own bench where he cannot come in without seeing it.

The table in a kitchen at number twelve stands dead flat on a shim planed and sawn flush under the window-side foot, and a tradesman can see that from six feet away, and he is the tradesman who can see it, and he does not go to look.

No Wednesday has been booked. Nobody has asked why the second line is what it is. The gate at number twelve is standing open against the wall of a coal shed with both bolts in an apron pocket.

---
