# Chapter 0828 — Eleven Boards And A Pair Of Bands

Saturday the fifteenth of July 2023, out on that frontage from the middle of the morning until the shadow of that office cabin is across the whole of it, and eleven boards that are going with the frontage is the whole of that Saturday.

ELEVEN BOARDS ARE DOWN ALONG THE FRONTAGE AT THE UNIT AND HE MEANS TO TAKE THEM UP AND PUT THEM ON EDGE AGAINST A WALL, AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN IS AT THAT FRONTAGE FOR A QUARTER OF AN HOUR IN THE MIDDLE OF THE MORNING BECAUSE THE BOARDS ARE IN HER WAY.

That frontage is scaffold boards laid down the front of the yard to walk on, eleven of them end to end, grey with use, with sand under them and weeds through the joints.

Nobody chose to make a path there. These are boards that have been moved from one place to another all summer because boards get reused, and eleven of them laid end to end along the front of a yard is a way of keeping the feet of the office cabin off the mud and off the standing water at the end of the frontage, and it does that job about as well as anything laid flat on sand does it, which is not very well, and it does it with no more sense than that.

He put most of them down himself in June with another pair of hands in the middle of a run, two people to a board at the middle joint where the eight inches is over two steel bands underneath. The bands were there already, flat on the sand under the joint, and they went back under when the boards went down.

That is the part he has come back to since. They were there already. He did not ask, and he cannot remember not asking, and what he can remember is that he was given the end of a board and told to put it down there, and the two of them got the joint in without either of them saying anything about what was underneath it.

He starts at the near end with gloves and a bar, lifting each board at the end and walking it back to the wall.

The first three come up clean. Sand under them, dry, with the print of the board in it and nothing else. The fourth has a nail through it into a sleeper and he drives it out from below and lays it aside.

The end of a board is the handle. A scaffold board is about nine feet long and thirteen inches wide with a lifting hole at each end, and the end is the only part of it that a hand and a knee can take without the board folding down on top of itself. He takes the end, lifts it until the sand lets go of it, and walks it back with the far end off the ground, and the board stays flat and does not twist because he is not carrying it by the middle.

At the middle joint he finds the bands.

They are steel bands about two inches wide, dark with oil, banded at both ends round the eight inches where the two boards meet over them, tight, with the ends folded under. They were put there to tie the joint, and they have been under there since June, out of sight, with the boards on top of them.

A band is a thing with a name and a use and a way of getting on. It is put round a pallet, or round a bundle of boards, or round anything that wants to be treated as one thing instead of a heap of things. A band round an eight-inch joint between two boards is not keeping those two boards together, because two boards laid end to end are not trying to come apart at that line. It is keeping something underneath them. That is what a band does in a corner. It goes round a thing that wants to stay a corner.

He sits back on his heels and looks at them.

He does not know what they are. They are no part of the boards and no part of the sand, and they do not belong to the yard. They look like packing off something heavy, cut off and laid flat to keep the joint from rocking, and whoever put them there knew what he was doing and left no sign of why.

There is one thing he can say about them and it is only about size. They came off something. The ends are folded under and not cut off flush, and a fold under means the band was pulled tight and the tail was pushed back on itself, and the band has to be long enough to go round whatever it was on and come back on itself, and two inches wide and folded at both ends is a band off something about the width of a foot and a half across the corner.

He does not know what it was. It is not his yard, and the frontage is going with the frontage, and a thing that somebody put under a frontage in June with no sign of why is not his business on a Saturday in July.

That is the stop, and it comes in the middle of the morning with his hands on the fourth board.

Whether he opens them. A banded joint asks to be cut to see what is under it, and one left shut asks to stay shut. He runs a thumb along the edge of one band and feels the fold under it, tight as a drum, with grit in the fold that has been there for weeks.

The grit is the other half of it. There is grit in the fold of both bands, and the grit is the sand of that frontage, and it has got in there by being sat on through a wet June and a dry July. So the bands were put down before the boards, and the boards went over them at once, and neither of them has moved since. That is not a guess. He puts his thumbnail in the grit at the fold and it goes in to the depth of the nail, which is what happens to sand that has been trapped and rained on and dried and rained on again with nothing let out.

He does not open them. He slides the bar under the board above the bands and lifts it clear, and lays the bands back flat on the sand where they were, in the same place, with the folds under. What was put down by two people in the middle of a run stays as it was put down, and his work goes round it and not through it.

If he opens them he is not finding out whose it was. He is finding out only that there is something there, and the something will be a lump of old something that somebody cleared out of a building, and it will have a week in which he has had it in his hand and turned it over and put it back. And it will not be any more the fact it was this morning, which was that two people had put two steel bands under the middle of a run of boards for a reason that was good enough for them at the time.

The woman from two doors down is at that frontage for about a quarter of an hour in the middle of the morning because the boards are in her way along the path. She stands clear by the cabin while he carries, and she does not touch a board and does not ask about them, and she does not look at them twice.

“I can get round by the wall,” she tells him.

“Give me a minute and the way will be clear,” he tells her. She waits until the way is clear and then she is gone up the path.

There is a quarter of an hour of that frontage with a woman in it who cannot get past it, and he did not think about the frontage at all until a person standing at the end of it needed a way round, and that is the whole of what the boards were for.

---

The rest of the day is lifting and carrying and laying on edge.

Eleven boards go up against the wall at the back of the yard, on edge, with battens under them out of the damp, in the order they came up, and the fourth with the nail in it at the end of the run where the sleeper was.

On edge is the only way eleven boards go against a wall. Flat, they are eleven boards on the damp with the wet coming up through them from the ground and no air on the top face at all, and in a month the bottoms of them are grey. On edge they are a raft of air with the boards touching each other on their edges, and the air goes up between them and out of the top, and they dry at the rate the weather dries them, which is the only rate there is.

The battens under them are offcuts of the same stock and they are set a foot apart along the wall, and the boards stand on them with the air under them and not one of them leaning, because eleven boards on edge against a wall will lean if there is nothing at the top to stop them and he has not got anything at the top.

He sweeps the sand where they were into the hollows and leaves the ground level, and he coils the string he used to tie the stack and hangs it on a nail.

The weeds that were through the joints stay where they are, because pulling them is no part of taking eleven boards up and because they will come up with a fork in a fortnight when the frontage is gone.

When the shadow of the office cabin is across the whole of the frontage he stands at the near end and looks down it to the wall. Eleven boards are on edge against the wall and the middle of the frontage is eleven feet of sand with the print of them in it, and in the middle of that sand, under the place where the joint was, the two steel bands are lying flat with the folds under and the grit packed into them, exactly where they were this morning, and he is not going to know what they went round before he does know what went round them.
