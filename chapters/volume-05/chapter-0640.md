# Chapter 0640 — The Leg On The Stool

Friday the fourteenth of May 2021, in this borough. She is on number twelve's step at about ten past ten in the morning with a spanner in her coat and she is off that road again by about half past ten, and the whole of it is one kitchen, four steps of tile, and a thing she was not asked to do.

**THE WOMAN WHO BROUGHT THE FOLDING CHAIR COMES BACK WITH A SPANNER BECAUSE THE CHAIR IS NOT SAFE, WHICH IS TRUE, AND SHE FINDS THE STOOL, AND SHE SAYS NOTHING ABOUT IT, AND THE WOMAN SHE BROUGHT THE CHAIR TO TELLS HER, BEFORE SHE CAN ASK, THAT THE STOOL CAME OFF A MAN ON THE ROAD, WHICH IS A LIE TOLD TO HER FACE TO PROTECT A THIRD PERSON SHE HAS NEVER MET.**

She is a woman in her late forties. There is a woman in her late seventies at number twelve, and there is a man in his early forties in this borough who made a stool and whom she does not know exists.

---

Here is why she is on that road on a Friday morning, and it is a good reason, and that is the difficulty.

On the Sunday she sat in it, and it did not take long. She had gone up with a roll of tape because the woman at number twelve lets her stand in the passage and she had run out, and she went into the kitchen and the woman at number twelve was at the window and did not turn round and said, "There's a chair, sit down a minute," and she sat down.

And the chair went.

Not all the way. It went about two inches to the left and stopped with a noise, a dry knock in the timber floor, and the woman at number twelve turned round, and the woman in her late forties said, "Right, that leg's not right," and got up.

And the woman at number twelve said, "It was all right on Saturday."

And the woman in her late forties said, "It's had a good go at you."

That was a joke and the woman at number seventy-eight did not make it and the woman in her late forties did not explain it.

And on the Friday she was in her own van and the spanner was in her hand and she thought: it is going to go again, and if it goes again with a woman in it, that is a thing that happens in a kitchen and then it is in the paper, and then it is a thing about a chair that a woman in her late forties took out of a garage on a Monday in April.

So she came.

She is at the gate at about ten past ten and she has the spanner in her coat pocket and a piece of flat bar in the other pocket and she has thought about this for a while.

And she stands there for about two minutes without knocking, and what she does in the two minutes is go over the thing she has come to say, and it is this.

It is not safe, that chair. I put a bit of iron in it in a hurry and I should not have done that to a thing going in a woman's kitchen, and I am sorry, and I will take it away and bring you a proper one.

That is a good speech. There is a fault in it and an apology in it and an offer in it, and it is the speech of a woman who does this for a living.

And in the middle of it, in the middle, is a thing she is not going to get round, which is *I took it out of a garage and it was not on the list*, and she cannot get round that on a doorstep in daylight, because the garage belongs to a woman who is at her sister's and who will be back, and because the woman on the other end of that arrangement asked her to say the skip and she said the skip.

So the speech has a hole in the middle of it and she is going to have to knock and say it anyway.

---

And she says, "It's the chair."

And the woman says, "I know it's the chair. I heard the spanner through the door."

And she says, "You've heard a spanner through a door?"

And the woman says, "Twice. Thursday and Friday. You've had it in your pocket since about half past nine."

That is the woman in her late seventies being funny at her own front door, and the woman in her late forties has never seen her do that before, and it takes the legs out of her.

And she says, "Right."

And the woman says, "Come in."

The stool is against the wall by the sink on its side, on top of nothing, exactly where it was left on the Wednesday night.

And the woman in her late forties sees it at about the fourth step.

And the chair is by the dresser with the cushion on it, and she goes to the chair.

And she does not look at the stool again for about nine minutes.

She gets the chair on its side on the kitchen tiles and it feels heavier than it did when she brought it, which is not possible, and is only true because she is nervous.

And the leg is out again, all of it. The pin has walked out of the tube and the bolt she put through the cheeks of the hinge is still there but is only doing the work of one now, and there is about a quarter of an inch of play in the whole back leg and she can get a finger in the gap at the top.

She puts a thumb and a finger on it and moves it, and it moves.

And she says, "That's the pin. That's not my iron, that's the pin. That's been walking out since Saturday and it's walked right out."

And the woman at number twelve says, "You put it back in."

And she says, "I put it back in a third of the way. I know I put it back in a third of the way. I do that so it beds in. It's bedding in too well, that's the problem, that's what bedding in is."

And the woman at number twelve says, "I've not used it a great deal."

And she says, "I know you haven't. That's why I'm here."

And then she stops, because she has just said *that's why I've come*, and *I know you haven't* is not a thing you say to a woman about a chair you are repairing in her own kitchen unless you have been in this kitchen before, and she has, twice, and one of the two times she put this chair down.

And the woman at number twelve does not point that out.

That is a kindness, and it is a deliberate one, and it is one of about six things that happen in that kitchen that neither of them will ever mention.

She works on it for about ten minutes with a spanner and a piece of flat bar and a screwdriver and nothing else, and she gets the pin out and she puts a new washer under it and she puts the iron back and tightens the bolt, and she does the thing she does on her own bench of putting the pin back a third of the way, and she is aware of her own hands the entire time.

And the woman at number twelve sits on the settee in the front room with the door open and comes in twice, once for a cloth and once to ask whether it wants doing again before long, and the answer is no, and both of them know it wants doing again sooner than that.

And then it is about a quarter past ten and she is on the tiles with her back to the sink and the stool is behind her, and she cannot help it.

She gets up and she looks at it.

That is all. She gets up off the floor and she looks at a stool, for four or five seconds, with nothing in her hands.

And it is ash. It is a hand's breadth off the floor on its side, and it is a beautiful thing, and it is not hers, and she knows that inside about two seconds. A thing you have made yourself is a thing you can pick up and turn over. She cannot lift this one with one hand and she knows it without trying, because the shape of a stool that is a shade too heavy is a shape she has read a hundred times off other people's benches.

And it has been under somebody's hand a lot. The seat is smoother at the front edge than it is at the back.

And she looks at it, and she puts her hand flat on the seat for a second the way a person touches a banister in the dark, and she takes her hand off it, and she does not say a word.

---

And the woman at number twelve, who has been in the doorway with the cloth since about a minute before this, says, "That came off a man on the road."

And she says, "What?"

And the woman at number twelve says, "The stool. It came off a man on the road. Before the bank holiday, that. He was round the doors and I said I didn't want anything and he left it."

And she says, "He left it."

And the woman at number twelve says, "He left it on the step and I went out and brought it in. He's not a bad man, he's about your age, and he does the gardens round here as well as the other work, and I know it's a cheek but you can't keep a thing like that off a doorstep."

And she says, "Right."

And the woman at number twelve says, "I've not told anybody I'd got it, because you'd think I was taking things off people, and I'm seventy-eight and I'm not having that."

Here is what happens in the woman in her late forties, and it happens in about two seconds and it is the whole chapter.

The sentence is a lie. She knows it is a lie inside about one and a half seconds of the start of it, and she knows it the way you know a thing that has been got ready — not by the words, by the shape. There is a man, and there is a road, and there is a doorstep, and there is a refusal, and all four are in place before the sentence has got to the end. A lie that has been got ready goes like that. The truth does not.

And the woman at number seventy-eight has told it to the person who gave her the chair.

And the woman in her late forties stands on the tiles of that kitchen at about a quarter past ten on a Friday morning and understands the whole arrangement in about three seconds, and it is this: she is not being lied to. She is being *managed*. She has been put in her place, correctly, gently, and for her own good, by a woman with a better hand at this than she has, and the woman has used the one move that could not possibly be refused, which is to make the lie a fact about herself, so that objecting to the lie means objecting to the woman being spoken about.

And what the woman at number twelve is protecting is not the chair and is not herself. It is a man in a white van who made a stool, and who is somewhere in this borough this morning, and whom the woman in her late forties has never met and does not know exists.

And the woman in her late forties decides, in the same three seconds, that she is not going to say one word about it.

And then she does the thing.

She says, "Do you not want it looked at."

And the woman at number twelve says, "It's a stool."

And she says, "It's a stool with a leg on it, same as the chair."

And the woman at number twelve says, "Well."

And she says, "Give me the spanner off the side and turn the gas on low under the sink, because I'm going to take that hinge off and if I can't get it off I'm not leaving it."

And the woman at number twelve says, "You don't have to—"

And she says, "I know I don't have to."

It takes her about twelve minutes.

And she does it on her knees on the tiles of another woman's kitchen, with the gas on low under the sink because she does not trust herself to be right about the other one, and she takes the whole hinge off the stool and finds that whoever built it had used a bolt and a washer and had put the pin back a third of the way through, exactly as she does it, exactly as she did it on the chair, and she sits back on her heels about halfway through and says nothing at all out loud for about a minute.

And the woman at number twelve stands in the doorway and watches a stranger mend a stool that a man she has not met left for her, and the stranger is the same woman who left the chair, and she does not understand one bit of it and does not ask.

And at about half past ten the woman in her late forties gets up off the kitchen floor and puts the stool back against the wall by the sink on its side, on top of nothing, where it was, and wipes her hands on her own pocket, and picks up her spanner and the flat bar and puts them back in her coat.

And she says, "That's it now. That's it for good."

And the woman at number twelve says, "Well."

And she says, "And the chair's had a new washer as well, so that one's not going anywhere either."

And the woman at number twelve says, "Right."

---

Here is what she is carrying down that road at about half past ten, and it is the only thing she has ever taken out of a house that was not furniture.

She has a job.

And she cannot un-say it. She has been in that kitchen and mended a thing in it that is not hers, and a woman of seventy-eight has told her a story to keep her quiet about it, and there is now a stool in that kitchen that two people have touched and that neither of them will mention.

And the chair will want doing again. And the stool will want doing again. And she will be the one who comes, because there is nobody else on that road who could come, and she knows that, and so does the woman at number twelve, and neither of them has said it.

**SHE WENT UP THAT ROAD WITH A SPANNER BECAUSE THE CHAIR WAS GOING TO GO OVER WITH A WOMAN IN IT, AND SHE FOUND THE STOOL AND SAID NOTHING, AND THE WOMAN AT NUMBER TWELVE TOLD HER BEFORE SHE COULD ASK THAT A MAN ON THE ROAD HAD LEFT IT, WHICH WAS A LIE TOLD TO HER FACE TO PROTECT A THIRD PERSON SHE HAS NEVER MET, AND SHE SAID NOTHING BACK, AND THEN SHE MENDED THE STOOL AS WELL, AND ON THE WAY DOWN SHE WORKED OUT THAT NOBODY HAD ASKED HER FOR ANYTHING AND THAT SHE HAD BEEN GIVEN A JOB.**

(End of chapter 0640)
