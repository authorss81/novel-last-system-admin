# Chapter 0387 — On the Ledge

Saturday the twenty-seventh of January 2018, from about half past eight in the morning until about half past nine at night, a back passage behind a row, in Tideglass.

**A GALVANISED CHURN HAS STOOD ON A BRICK LEDGE IN A BACK PASSAGE SINCE A SATURDAY IN NOVEMBER, AND ON THE MORNING OF THE TWENTY-SEVENTH OF JANUARY A MAN IN HIS THIRTIES WHEELED A BICYCLE AS FAR AS IT AND COULD NOT GET PAST IT, AND A MAN IN HIS SIXTIES CAME OUT OF HIS OWN GATE ABOUT TEN FOOT AWAY WITH A CUP IN HIS HAND, AND NEITHER OF THEM SAID ONE WORD TO THE OTHER ABOUT IT, AND IT IS ON THAT LEDGE.**

The passage is behind a row of eight houses on a street that does not go anywhere, and it runs about seventy yard from one gate to the other and it is about five foot wide with a high brick wall down both sides and nothing overhead, and four of the eight houses have a back gate into it and the other four are joined to their neighbours.

The brick runs nine courses high each side and at about the height of a man's hip there is a ledge, about ten inch deep, where the courses are set back a course and a half. It is not a thing anybody built to put anything on. It is what happens when a boundary is bricked to a height and left there.

The man in his sixties has the first house on the left at the top end. He has been in it since about 1998 and he goes in and out of that gate perhaps six times a day, and the gate is on a hinge that drops about an inch and comes up, and it is a gate he can lift with two fingers.

---

He brought the churn out in the last week of November.

It is a proper churn, the old kind, galvanised, about fifteen inch tall and about ten inch across the base, with a dent in one side about a third of the way down and a rim round the top and no lid on it and a rope handle on a loop of wire at the back of the neck. It came out of an outbuilding at the bottom of his own garden with him and it is not heavy for a man of his age for about four steps.

He put it on that ledge on the Saturday because it was raining hard and he was going to take the lid off it and wire brush it and put three coats of something on it, and he could not do that in the rain in a garden with no outbuilding big enough, and the ledge under the porch of his own back gate is the only dry place in that passage with enough room.

And then it rained for another three days and then he was busy and then it was the turn of the year, and it has been on that ledge since the last week of November, and it has been rained on and frozen on and rained on again, and there is a film of green at the bottom of it.

What he has not done about it is move it.

It is his and it is on a wall that is not his, and it is above the water that comes off a row of roofs, and every time he has come out of that gate since November he has had to go round it, and that is a thing you do.

He has thought about it four or five times. He has thought, standing in his own yard with his back to the gate, that the thing to do is walk out and put it in the corner by the coal. And then he has put his hand on the gate to go out and gone round it.

He is not going to do that, and the reason is not pride. It is that a churn on a ledge in a shared passage is a thing that somebody has to be the one to pick up, and he is not going to be a man who bends in the wet in a passage four times a day for a thing that can be carried in about four seconds, and he does not want there to be one occasion when the man who keeps that passage, if it is a man and not a woman, comes along and watches him do it.

---

The man in his thirties found that passage in October.

He parks at the top of that street because he has been parking at the top of that street since about the middle of last year and it is a Sunday and it is where there is room. And the top of that street has no way out at the top end, and the only way out of it on foot is through a gap in a fence into that passage and out of the gate at the bottom onto the parade.

He had learned that in about October and has used it perhaps twenty times since. He is not a man who is a passenger in his own arrangements. He would rather be at the bottom of the street than take a bicycle through there.

But on the morning of the twenty-seventh of January he was on a bicycle.

It is not a fast bicycle. It is a roadster of about twenty years with a carrier at the back and a skirt guard on one side and a chain that he has been meaning to look at. He was taking it out to a house on the Cleeve road to be got at by a man who has been after him about it since the beginning of the year, and he had it at the top of that street at about ten past ten and it was the only way he wanted to take it.

He got it through the gap in the fence and about nine foot down that passage and he could see the churn.

The ledge is on the right-hand side going down and the churn is at about the top third of it.

It is exactly the width of the passage with the churn on that ledge. There is about two foot of clear ground the other side of it, which is enough for a man on foot and not enough for a bicycle with bars about twenty-two inch across.

The front wheel would go up. He stood there and looked at it and worked that out the way anybody works out that sort of thing, by looking.

He could have lifted the churn. It is a churn with nothing in it and it weighs about seven pounds empty and he could have picked it up with one hand, put it on the ground, gone past, and put it back on the ledge where it had been.

The man in his sixties came out of his own gate at about ten past ten with a cup in his hand.

He came out and shut the gate to behind him and turned round to face down the passage, which is what a man does when he comes out of a gate in a passage, and he was about ten foot away from the churn and about eighteen foot from the man with the bicycle, and he had a cup in his right hand and the gate on its hinge behind him.

The two of them looked at the churn.

Neither of them said anything.

The man in his sixties looked at it and then at the bicycle and then at the man, and the man in his thirties looked at the churn and then at the man in his sixties, and there was a stretch of about two seconds in which either of them could have said one sentence and neither of them said a sentence.

The man in his sixties did not say to himself that the churn was in the way, because it was not in his way, it was on a ledge he walks past. The man in his thirties did not say to himself that the churn belonged to the other one, because he does not know whose churn it is and never has and has not once wondered.

There is nothing in the passage at ten past ten on that morning except a churn on a ledge and a bicycle and two men who are both looking at the same object for the same two seconds and for reasons that are not the same at all.

What the man in his thirties did was put a foot on the pedal and ride back up the passage and out through the gap in the fence.

It took him a quarter of an hour longer. He went back along the parade past that shop, down past the place that is shut, and round by the front of the parade and out to the Cleeve road. He was at the house at about twenty past ten instead of at about ten past ten, and the man there had gone out, and he waited twenty minutes on the step, and the man was not back until about a quarter past ten, and he did not get the bicycle looked at until the Wednesday.

The man in his sixties drank his tea at the top of the passage for about twenty minutes and then went back in and shut his gate.

Neither of them has mentioned it to anybody and neither is going to.

The man in his thirties has come down that passage about six times since the twenty-seventh of January and the churn is on that ledge every one of the six times, and he has gone round it every one of the six times, and he has not once said to himself that he is being let down by a man in his sixties over a churn. It is a churn on a ledge. It would be a churn on a ledge if he were the last man in England.

The man in his sixties has come out of that gate perhaps three hundred times since the twenty-seventh of January and has gone round that churn every one of them, and he has not once said to himself that a man on a bicycle is being put to it by a churn that is his.

There is no arrangement between them and there has never been any contact between them beyond good morning. They do not know each other. He does not know the man on the bicycle is a man he would recognise in another street, and the man on the bicycle does not know that the man with the cup comes out of the first gate on the left.

**HE PUT A FOOT ON THE PEDAL AND RID BACK UP THAT PASSAGE AND OUT THROUGH THE GAP IN THE FENCE AND WENT ROUND BY THE PARADE INSTEAD, AND HE CAME DOWN THAT PASSAGE SIX MORE TIMES SINCE AND WENT ROUND IT EVERY ONE, AND THE MAN IN HIS SIXTIES HAS GONE ROUND IT ABOUT THREE HUNDRED TIMES.**
