# Chapter 0649 — The Back Of It

Tuesday the twenty-second of June 2021, in this borough, and not in this borough, because the house is on the far side of the ring road in a street she has driven through twice in six years. She is in it from about half past eight in the morning until about four in the afternoon and the whole of it is a clearance she has already lost a quarter of and a gas envelope she has been carrying since Saturday.

**A WOMAN WHO CLEARS HOUSES RUNS OUT OF PAPER IN AN EMPTY HOUSE AND TAKES THE ONLY PIECE IN HER COAT, WHICH HAS SIX THINGS WRITTEN ON THE SIDE SHE IS FACING DOWN, AND WRITES A ROOM-BY-ROOM JOB LIST ON THE OTHER SIDE WITHOUT TURNING IT OVER, AND TAPES IT INSIDE A CUPBOARD DOOR THAT IS OFF ITS HINGES AND GOING TO BE REPLACED, AND THE PROOF THAT IT CANNOT COME BACK IS THAT SHE STOPS AT THE GATE AND WORKS OUT THAT SHE CANNOT GO BACK FOR IT, AND THAT SHE CANNOT WRITE ON IT AGAIN WITHOUT RUINING THE SIX THINGS ON THE OTHER SIDE.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is in a house by herself from about half past eight. There is nobody in it.

---

The job is a full clear.

It is the second one this month that has come in as a full clear and it has come in as a full clear because the woman who booked it said the words *up and out* on the telephone and then said *I just want it gone*, and those are two different sentences and a woman who does this for a living can hear the difference.

She has been at it since about half past eight on her own with a barrow and forty boxes.

---

Here is what is wrong with it, and it is wrong by about a quarter.

At about midday the woman who booked it rings and says her brother has come in with a key and is in the back room and would she leave it, and she says yes, and she says it about a quarter of an hour before she can do anything about it, and she says it without one word of the tone in her voice that she is thinking about the money.

There are eight boxes of that back room already stacked in the hall, and she carried every one of them down herself on her own hip that morning, and she puts them back in the room, one at a time, in about fifteen minutes, and it takes until about half past twelve.

And the woman who booked it says on the telephone at half past twelve, "You're doing that for nothing, love."

And the woman in her late forties says, "It'll be on the bill as a re-charge."

And the woman who booked it says, "Don't be daft."

---

Here is what a clearance actually is, and it is worth putting down because it is the whole of the eight hours.

It is not carrying. Everybody imagines carrying.

It is standing in a room that somebody has lived in and working out what a person would keep if they knew they were keeping it, and there is no correct answer and every single object is wrong.

The photograph on the mantel is wrong. The photograph is always wrong. And so is the one in the drawer with the other ones in it, and so is the wedding list in a drawer that should have been binned in a different decade.

She keeps all three photographs. She puts them in a box with the good stuff and writes the room on the lid in marker, and if anybody ever rings her about it, which nobody has ever done, she could go and get them out.

---

And at about half past two on the Tuesday she has about an hour left and she needs to write something down, because there are two sacks to go into the loft and a set of stairs to strip and she does not do stairs on her own, and she has to write it down or she will do the whole of it twice.

She has a pen. She has not got any paper.

That is the whole of it. A woman who does a job for a living every day in a borough this size and she has no paper, because she uses the client's paper, because paper is heavy and paper in a van is a thing she has never once needed.

She looks at the kitchen worktop, which is bare, because she has cleared it.

She looks at a drawer in the kitchen, which is empty.

She looks at the workbench in the garage.

---

And then she puts her hand in the inside pocket of her coat.

And the gas envelope is in there, and it has been in there since the Saturday morning of a table with one bad leg, and she has driven to three places with it against her ribs and she has not taken it out once.

---

Here is what happens in her hands.

She does not look at it. She takes it out and turns it over and puts it face down on the workbench, which puts the six lines down against the wood and her own side of the paper up, because she is about to write on the back of a thing and the writing is the last thing you do to a piece of paper.

And she puts her handbag on the floor and gets the pen off the top of it and she writes.

---

And what she writes is this, and it takes about four minutes, and it is the second list on that piece of paper and neither of them knows about the other one.

back room — eight boxes, put back, nothing taken

hall — four boxes, loft

box room — two boxes, curtains off the rail

bathroom — one box, keep the green tiles

landing — four boxes, strip the stair

And then she writes, underneath:

hoarding, thurs, eight

And then she puts the pen down.

---

And here is the thing, and it happens to her about four seconds after she puts the pen down, and it is the only part of that Tuesday that is not a job.

She has not read the front of it since Saturday morning.

She has read it four times in a kitchen off the parade with a clamp standing up in the middle of the table.

And it has been in the inside pocket of her coat against her ribs since the Saturday and she has not once looked at the side she was not interested in, because there was no reason to.

And now there is, because she has just written on the side that was blank, and she is about to lose it down a garage, and she is not going to lose it down a garage, and so she turns it over.

---

And she reads it upside down.

It takes her about a second. She is sitting on a step in a garage with the pen in her hand and the envelope in the other one and she is looking at a woman's handwriting from the wrong end, and for about a second and a bit she cannot tell which way up it goes.

And when it comes the right way up she reads the fourth line.

*the hotplate on the left*

And then she reads the first.

*the gate*

And then she reads all six, upside down and then right way up, with the pen still in her hand.

---

And here is what she thinks about, and she does not have anybody to think it with, which is the whole of the shape of this woman's life and has been for a long time.

She thinks: *a woman of seventy-eight wrote six things in an order that makes no sense and gave the fourth one to a stranger.*

And she thinks: *the fourth one is the only one I could do anything about and I've done a table leg with a wedge in it and I can't do a hotplate.*

And she thinks: *and I wrote a list on the other side of hers.*

And she does not think, anywhere in that garage, even once, about the gate.

---

And then she puts the envelope down on the workbench, face up, on a bench in a garage on the far side of the ring road, and it is the last surface in that garage with anything on it.

And what she does next is the ordinary thing, and it is the thing that puts it where it goes.

---

She is going to leave the garage last, and before she leaves it she does what she does in every garage.

She wipes the workbench down.

She stacks the sacks by the door. She sweeps the floor out into the bin — a workman's bin, which she borrows from a man on another road and gives back on a Friday.

And there is a cupboard in that garage with a door on it, and the door has come off its hinges and it is leaning against the wall beside the frame where it came off, and it is going to be replaced, because she has priced a door and told the woman who booked it.

And the door is going to a skip.

---

And she looks at the envelope on the bench.

And she thinks, quite plainly, and it is the only sensible thought available: if I leave that on this bench it is going in a skip with the rest of the house.

And she does not want it in a skip. She cannot say why she does not want it in a skip, and she has looked at the four possibilities.

She could put it in her pocket again. She could take it home. She could put it on top of the sacks for the lorry.

And she does none of those three, and what she does instead is what anybody does when a piece of paper has to stay somewhere and there is nowhere.

She takes the roll of brown tape off the bench.

She opens the garage cupboard and looks in it, which is empty, and she turns the door round and puts the envelope flat against the inside of the door, face up, with her own room list underneath it and the six things on top, in pencil, in a garage, on the far side of the ring road.

And she tapes the four edges of it with about two inches of tape and tears the tape off with her teeth.

---

And that is the last time that piece of paper is touched by anybody who has met the woman who wrote it.

And the proof is not a count. The proof is a door.

A gas envelope with a woman's handwriting on it has ended up taped inside the inside of a cupboard door in a garage on the far side of the ring road, on a door that is off its hinges and is going in a skip and is being replaced, in a house where the only other thing in this borough that knows what is written on it is a shoebox in a van on an industrial estate, and a man in a van who has never seen her.

---

And she goes at about four o'clock.

She puts the keys back through the letterbox because the client is out and has been out all day, and she ties the sacks, and she shuts the front door and puts the keys in an envelope and pushes it through, which is a thing she has done about four hundred times and has never once thought about.

And she stands in the empty hallway of a house she will not see again with an empty barrow and her bag on her shoulder.

And the whole of the front room is open to the road and there is nothing in it and there is a smell of cold tea and a carpet with clean squares on it where the furniture was.

---

And here is the last thing she does, and she does it at the gate, and it is the whole of the rest of it.

She stops.

She stops because she has worked out, standing at the front gate of a house on the far side of the ring road, that she cannot go back for it.

And that is not a problem about paper. She has got a hundred and forty of those in the van.

It is that if she goes back and opens that cupboard then she is a person who goes back. And she has never in her life been a person who goes back, and she has never in her life wanted to be one until a woman of seventy-eight put a stool on her step on a Thursday and stood on her path for a minute afterwards with nothing in her hands.

---

And then she thinks, and it is not the sad one:

*it's a door. It's a door going in a skip. Somebody'll buy that door off a skip for a couple of quid if they've got a van and a Saturday.*

And she gets in the van.

**SHE RAN OUT OF PAPER IN AN EMPTY HOUSE AND WROTE A SECOND LIST ON THE OTHER SIDE OF A FIRST ONE AND DID NOT KNOW WHAT SHE WAS DOING, AND READ THE FIRST ONE UPSIDE DOWN BEFORE SHE KNEW WHETHER SHE WAS RIGHT WAY UP, AND TAPED IT TO THE INSIDE OF A DOOR THAT IS OFF ITS HINGES AND GOING IN A SKIP, AND STOPPED AT THE GATE AND WORKED OUT THAT SHE COULD NOT COME BACK FOR IT, AND WENT.**

(End of chapter 0649)
