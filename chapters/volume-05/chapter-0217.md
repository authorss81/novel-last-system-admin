# Chapter 0217 — The Fourth Thing

Sunday the seventeenth of January 2016, from about half past two in the afternoon, a square in Marrowgate.

**TWO SMALL CORRECT THINGS WERE DONE IN ONE AFTERNOON IN A TOWN THAT OWNS EVERYTHING, BY TWO PEOPLE WHO WERE ON NOTHING, FOUR HUNDRED YARDS APART, AND NEITHER OF THEM KNEW ANYTHING ABOUT THE OTHER, AND NOBODY FOUND OUT.**

There was nothing for him to do on the Sunday. The room did not sit. The three of them had come up on the Monday and had been in a room and a yard and a bank for six days, and Nell Ardent had read four documents and had not liked three of them, and Tomas Renn had been to a hardware shop and come back with a coil of wire, and Sanaa Iqbal had spent two days in a clinic with a waiting list on the wall and had not been asked to take any of it, which she had said was the correct arrangement and had then gone and looked at the water.

So Jonas Mercer walked. It was flat and grey and not cold and about four degrees and Marrowgate on a Sunday is a square, a church, two pubs, a post office counter shut, and a great many separate reasons for nobody to be in a hurry.

The man of about ninety was on the bench on the south side of the square at about twenty past two with a shopping bag and a folded paper, and he was the only other person sitting on that side of it, and he had been looking at the parish notice board for a good while before Jonas Mercer came across the square.

The board is a wooden board on two posts and it is the oldest thing in Marrowgate that anybody looks at. The bench is about four feet from it and it used to be six, and it had been put where it was in about 2008 for a reason that had to do with a funeral, and the funeral was nine years ago.

"They've put it over the lot," said the man of about ninety, to nobody. "You can read the top of it and that's the lot."

**AND THE ONLY LATTICE NOTICE ON THAT BOARD THAT AFTERNOON, printed and pinned up at about the height of a person of average height, and it defines nothing, and nothing on it defines *holder*:**

> **A BENCH FACES WHAT A BENCH FACES.**
> **A NOTICE BOARD IS NOT SEATING.**
> **NEITHER OF THOSE IS AN INSTRUCTION.**

Jonas Mercer read the notice, which was about a planning meeting, and looked at the bench, and saw the whole of it, and said nothing, because there is nothing to say to a man of about ninety about a bench, and the man had not asked him for anything and was not going to.

At about a quarter to three a man of about eighty-eight came across the square with a barrow and a sack of something and stopped, because a man of about ninety had got up off the bench and was standing in the middle of the paved bit in front of it pointing at where the bench was and then at where it should be, and then sat down again.

"You'll have to shift it a foot and a half," the man of about ninety said.

The man with the barrow said, "Go on, then," in the way of a man who has been asked to carry something all his life, and put the barrow down and came back and got hold of one end of the bench and walked it round about a foot and a half and set it down, and looked at it, and the man of about ninety said "that'll do," and the man with the barrow picked the barrow up and went on to the pub, and neither of them asked the other for anything and neither of them knew the other's name.

The man of about ninety folded his paper and put it in his bag and went home at about twenty to three, and nobody in that square saw it happen except a man who had come over to read the notice.

---

Jonas Mercer went down the side street to buy a paper he was not going to read, and came back along it about half an hour later, and that is how he saw the second of the two, which was on the other side of the square and half an hour later and had nothing whatever to do with it.

The shop was a hardware shop with a green front and a shutter half down and an A-board on the pavement, and the woman of about eighty-three was at the counter with a list on the top of it, and there was nobody else in the shop.

"It says half past eight on the door," she said.

"It does."

"It opens at a quarter to eight."

"That's earlier."

"It's been a quarter to eight for about a year," she said, "because the woman who opens it has a bus to get and the bus is at a quarter to eight, and I have been in here about twice a week for a year and I have watched the door, and so has that dog." She put the list down. "And the door says half past eight and it has said half past eight since before the old man died, and the old man is the one who put that sign up, and he is not here any more, and there is nobody in this town who is frightened of that sign."

The man behind the counter had a bit of a receipt roll in his hand because that is what he had in his hand.

"I can't paint it," he said. "I haven't got the paint."

"You've got a marker," said the woman of about eighty-three. "That'll do for a bit. And then when you've got the paint you do it properly and the sign's right, and nobody's late and nobody's stood in the rain."

He took the marker out from behind the till and went to the door and rubbed out half past and wrote quarter to, and it did not look very good, and he put the cap back on, and the woman of about eighty-three looked at it and said, "That's better than it was," and paid for her list and went out.

Jonas Mercer stood about a yard to the side of the A-board and did not say anything to either of them, and nobody in that shop has any idea he was there.

**AND THAT IS THE FOURTH TIME SINCE THE PUMP AT THE BOTTOM OF PEVERELL STREET, AND THE COUNT IS BEING KEPT IN HIS OWN HEAD BECAUSE NOBODY ELSE IN THE WORLD IS KEEPING IT.**

The first was in September, in this borough, a man of about fifty-seven with a hired pump and a hose keeping one doorway dry through a wet week, and nobody asked him to and nobody told him where to put the discharge pipe and he worked it out. The second was on the tenth of November, in this borough, outside the third door at the low end of Peverell Street, a man of about fifty-four with a handcart and a shovel and a woman of about eighty-five with a stiff broom and a bucket, about an hour, side by side, and neither of them asked the other for anything, and the whole of what was said between them was that a bar is not a brush.

The third was four weeks ago, in a city that is not this one: a man of about eighty-four with a basket, third in a queue outside a chemist's on a Sunday afternoon, who was wrong about the bottle and right about the label, and a woman of about sixty-nine, four hundred yards further up the same street at about ten to three, who was right about a timetable of the wrong year, and neither of them knew that the other one existed, and the only person who knows both of those happened is the man who walked down that street twice.

And the fourth was a bench and a door, and the bench and the door are two streets apart, and the man of about eighty-eight with the barrow and the man of about ninety and the woman of about eighty-three and the man behind the counter are four people who have not been introduced to each other and never will be.

**AND THAT IS FOUR, AND NONE OF THE FOUR IS ON ANYTHING, AND NONE OF THE FOUR IS IN A FIELD, AND NOBODY IN THIS BOROUGH OR IN THAT CITY HAS FOUND OUT ABOUT ANY OF THEM, AND THE ONLY PERSON WHO KNOWS ALL FOUR IS A MAN WHO STANDS IN CORRIDORS, AND HE HAS NOT WRITTEN IT DOWN AND IS NOT GOING TO.**

Nobody in Marrowgate came to that square about the bench. The marker stayed on the door of the hardware shop until it rained, which was on the Thursday, and after that half of the quarter to came off and half past was not on it either, because he had rubbed the middle out and had not been able to finish. He came back to it with a stencil on the Tuesday and it took him twenty minutes and it is right, and he did not tell anybody he was doing it, and the woman of about eighty-three has not mentioned it.
