# Chapter 0525 — Out to the Corner

The Thursday of the twenty-fourth of October 2019, from about ten past seven in the morning until about half past eight, in two streets and at a corner between them.

**TWO SISTERS HAVE WALKED OUT OF THEIR OWN FRONTS AT THE SAME TIME EVERY MORNING SINCE 1989 AND AS FAR AS THE CORNER, AND BACK AGAIN AT NIGHT. THE YOUNGER SISTER CARRIES A SCARF OUT IN HER HAND AND THE ELDER PUTS IT ON, AND AT NIGHT THE ELDER CARRIES IT BACK AND THE YOUNGER SISTER PUTS IT ON. IT WENT THAT WAY FOR THIRTY YEARS AND ON THE THURSDAY OF THE FIFTH OF SEPTEMBER THE YOUNGER SISTER CAME OUT WITH HER HANDS SHUT AND NOTHING IN THEM.**

They are a woman of about sixty-eight and a woman of about sixty-two, they have been in the same town since they were born, the elder is at number four and the younger sister is at number thirty-one in the same street, and between the two front gardens and the corner there is a walk of some hundreds of yards that goes past nine gardens and the end of a row of trees.

The walk began because they went to the same school and then because they worked in the same place for years, and by the time either of them noticed that they were doing it together every morning it had been going for three years, and neither of them can remember starting.

What the walk is, in practice, is this. The younger woman is at work at eight. The older woman is not out until half past nine. So at ten past seven both of them come out, and they walk to the corner together, and her sister gets the bus that comes at a quarter to, and the elder goes on to the end of the row and comes back the other way. In the evening it is the same in reverse, and on a Saturday they do it once in the middle of the day, and both of them have walked that walk in the rain and once in a fall of snow about nine years ago that neither of them has ever mentioned.

Nobody in those two streets has ever asked them about it. It is not a thing anybody sees. It is two women coming out of two separate fronts at the same hour and going the same way, and quite a lot of this town could pass them in a week and never know there was a walk.

The scarf came into it in the first cold months and it is a long wool one, dark, and it is not new, and there is a run in it about a third of the way from the end that has been there since the nineties. It belongs to the older woman in the sense anybody would say so, which is that the elder wears it in the cold and the younger sister has it in her hand in the cold, and neither of them has ever said whose it is.

From October the elder wears it to the corner and hands it back at the corner and her sister puts it on there and goes to work in it, and the same happens at half past five in the evening in the other direction, and the scarf goes up and down this street twice a day, every day, all the way through the cold, for thirty years.

---

What happened is that her sister stopped.

Her sister stopped on Thursday the fifth of September, which was an ordinary Thursday, and there was nothing wrong with her and nothing wrong with the bus and nothing wrong with the corner. The elder has gone over that morning a great many times in seven weeks and has not found a thing in it.

On the morning of the fifth she came out at ten past seven as she has come out since 1989, and she walked about two hundred yards, and then she stopped and turned round and went back in, and she came out again at ten past and went to work on her own, from the place near the end of the row.

She has done that every morning since. She has not done it earlier and she has not done it later, and she has not been at the corner at the middle of a Saturday since the fifth of September either, and the elder has not gone to look.

The elder has not asked her. Seven weeks is what she has done about it, and it has taken the whole of them for her to be able to put it that way in her own head, and she has put it that way to herself many times and to nobody else.

What she does instead is go out at ten past seven and walk to the corner on her own and stand there.

That is seven weeks now, and this morning is the end of the seventh of them, and she got to the corner about seven minutes sooner than she usually does because she was in a mood to get it over with.

She does the whole thing in about three quarters of an hour. She goes out at ten past, walks to the corner, stands with her back against the trunk of the tree there, watches the street, and at about twenty to walks back the other way and comes in at half past eight.

She is not after a bus. She has not been near the place at the end of the row since the summer, and there is nothing at the end of the row that she wants.

What has happened to her in seven weeks is her right arm. It began in the second week, which was the first week it was properly cold, and it was nothing at first, and then in the third week she could not get the scarf over her head with that hand, and she worked out that she can manage it with the left in four seconds longer, and she has been doing it single-handed every morning since. This morning it took her the best part of ten seconds, because the wind had the loose end while she was working at it.

She has never once considered not taking the scarf.

She was at the corner from about ten past seven with the scarf over her head and her right arm down her side when her sister's front went about eighty yards back up the street.

There is nowhere on that walk not to see a person coming. She was coming at her usual hour, and the elder decided in about the length of the two gardens at the end of the row that she was going to keep her face the way it was, and she kept her face the way it was.

Her sister had her collar up and nothing in her hands.

That is the thing she has not been able to get past in seven weeks. Her sister came out with her hands shut, the way she has come out since the fifth of September, and she came out with them shut this morning too, and she walked about two hundred yards past nine gardens at the hour she has walked them every morning since 1989, and then she came alongside the elder about sixty yards short of the corner, and for the length of a pavement the two of them were walking together, which is not a thing that has happened between them since the fifth of September.

The sister's left hand came up about four inches and stopped in the air and went back down by her side. The elder had the loose end of the scarf in her own left hand and she did not move it either.

Neither of them said anything whatever for the whole of those sixty yards.

---

At the corner her sister turned left towards the end of the row and the elder turned and put her back against the tree.

Her sister got about four paces and then stopped and turned round, and the elder watched her come back, and there was a woman of about sixty-two walking towards her with the scarf round her neck and the loose end over her shoulder and her hands in her pockets.

"What a wind," her sister said.

"Ay," the elder said.

And her sister turned and went on towards the end of the row, and that was all of it. It was the first time either of them had put a sound out since the fifth of September, and not one question was in it.

The elder stood there for another half hour.

A man of about seventy comes and stands at that corner most mornings. They have been at it in the same weather for some years and he has never once asked her what she is doing there and neither has she, and this was the first morning in seven weeks that he spoke, and he spoke about the weather.

"There's one at a quarter past ten," he said.

"I'm not after a bus," she said.

He said nothing back and went on standing where he was, and that is every word that has gone between the two of them since the beginning of September, and nobody in this borough is going to hear about it from her.

At about twenty to she pushed off the tree and walked the other way. The wind was still off the water and it was stronger than it had been when she came out, and by the end of the row it had gone round onto the east side where the houses are, and it took the loose end out from under her chin, and she caught it with her left hand and held it.

She got in at half past eight and stood just inside her own front with the scarf still round her neck and cold on the side the wind had come round to, and she took it off with the hand that works and rolled it loose and pushed it into the pocket of her jacket, which is where it has gone every morning since the fifth of September and where it had not gone on any morning in the previous thirty years.

**SHE PUSHED IT INTO THE POCKET OF HER JACKET AT HALF PAST EIGHT AND IT SAT THERE UNTIL QUARTER PAST NINE, AND HER SISTER GOT ON THE BUS AT A QUARTER TO EIGHT AND GOT OFF WHERE SHE ALWAYS GETS OFF, AND THE CORNER GOT THE TWO OF THEM BACK TO WHERE THEY WERE.**
