# Chapter 0933 — Behind The Gate Strap

Sunday the eighteenth of August 2024, at the lower end of the yard gate strap at number nine from first light. Behind iron that has drawn off.

**SHE HAS THE LOWER END OF A GATE STRAP AT NUMBER NINE DRAWN OFF THE TIMBER IT IS SCREWED TO AND SHE MEANS TO PUT THE HAND INTO THE GAP AT THAT END WITH THE THUMB LAID ACROSS THE STRAP ON ITS NEAR FACE AND THE FINGERS PRESSING THE TIMBER BACK AGAINST THE THUMB, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE STRAP HAS DRAWN OFF.**

The strap was fitted when the gate was hung on its third hinge, and the hinge is now on its second fixing while the strap has kept its screws. It is a strap for a five-bar gate and it came with the hinge, and it was put on the heel stile because the heel stile is where the maker's list put it.

It is a strap of wrought iron about two feet long and an inch and a half wide, screwed flat across the face of the hinge stile, and at its lower end it has drawn away from the wood by about a sixteenth. She wants the space between the strap and the timber, and the width of that space is the only thing the strap has to say.

Iron fixed flat to timber keeps its seat only as long as the timber is there. So the hand goes into the gap at the lower end. The iron is cool and slick on its face and pitted all over underneath, and behind it the timber is dry and warm and quite unlike the iron.

The thumb bears on the strap and the fingers push the wood back against it, and between the two of them the gap opens to about a sixteenth and a half, which is as far as it has ever been. The strap itself does not move under the thumb, because the whole of it is bedded at its upper end and only the foot of it has anything to give.

Behind that sixteenth and a half the timber is not scarred. It is pale, unbroken and untouched, and the paint on the frame round the strap is the same paint and is crazed all the way down.

---

The strap was drawn off at its lower end and not at its upper, and the difference between the two ends is the difference between the screws at them. The upper screw has turned a quarter of a turn in thirty years and the lower screw has turned three quarters, and a quarter turn of an oak screw is about the thickness of the paper it is packed in.

A screw turns because the timber under its head has crushed, and timber crushes where the head bears hardest. The lower screw bears hardest because the strap below it carries the whole of the swing of that gate, and the swing is heavier than the gate because the gate is swung on its own weight every time somebody walks through it. Every swing of that gate puts the gate's weight on one strap and not on the other, and it puts it on the lower strap while the gate is open and on the upper one while it is shut.

The one nearest the hinge carries least and the one furthest from it carries most, and the further a strap is from the hinge the more of the swing it is asked to take. Nothing beyond that enters into the arithmetic of a hung gate, and it is the reason a five-bar gate hung well has its straps in one diagonal line and not in a pair of parallel pairs.

A strap fixed with three screws does not take the load on all three. It takes it on the first one along and leaves the rest as so much iron for decoration, and the two further screws are there because two screws cannot be spaced far enough apart to stop a strap twisting on one. The first one along on this strap is the upper screw, and it has moved a quarter turn, and the other two have not moved enough to see. Thirty years is a long time for an iron strap to stay on a gate stile with no washer under its head, and the washer that was never fitted is the whole of the fault. With no washer the head bears on a circle the size of the head itself, and oak crushes under a circle like that in about a decade, and once it has crushed the head beds down and the strap draws off the wood.

A washer gives the head a face twice its own diameter and the oak underneath goes on doing the same job for another twenty years after the head would have crushed it by itself. A strap drawn off a gate that has never had a washer under it is the commonest fault on any gate in a yard of gates, and it goes unseen because a strap a sixteenth off its timber still closes the gate.

The crush under that head is no thicker than a coin and it is worst at the middle, where the iron bears hardest, and it falls away sharply at the edge. The whole of the crushing is inside the circle of the head, which is why the crescent of open paint stops where it stops and does not wander off along the strap. The paint over that crush has opened into a crescent about the size of a thumbnail and the crescent is full of the grey dust that comes off the head every time the gate works.

Where a strap draws off its timber it shows a band of wood that has never seen the weather. The band on this one is a sixteenth and a half wide and about two inches long, and inside the band the grain is as pale as a sawn face.

A band like that is the cleanest dated record a gate has, because it can be read to a width. A sixteenth and a half is the difference between the head sitting flat and the head sitting on the rim of a crush.

Weather does the same job slower and in patches. Three years of a shutter that has worked loose leaves a stripe of paint standing proud behind the part that has moved, and the stripe is never straight, and it is wider at one end than at the other. The stripe behind this strap is straight on three sides and follows the head exactly, which is the difference between iron moving off wood and paint moving off iron.

The pale band is not going to grey at the edges for a long while, because iron over wood keeps the band dry and the rain has to find the edge of the strap to get at it. It will find it eventually at the bottom corner, where the strap throws its water off sideways, and it will find it at about the rate it found the paint on the rest of the gate.

The stile this strap is fixed to is oak, and the oak has been on that hinge line for the whole life of the gate. It is sound from the hinge bolt to the bottom and dark with a film of iron where the strap lies against it. Under the strap the film is bright and under the washer-less head the film is rust, and the rust runs down from the head in a fine line and stops where the strap throws the rain off.

Nothing in that band of wood has been touched since the strap came off it, and the crescent of paint round the head has been open since the last winter and will open a little more this one. The band and the crescent are two records of the same movement written at two different scales, and they agree.

Her handbag comes with the woman who does her shopping, up to the back step at nine from the yard, and she does not knock.

Handbag in one hand, she came up the step and looked at the strap, then at the hinge, then along the strap once more, and then she set the handbag down on the step beside her and stood there a moment with her other hand on the rail. She did not touch the iron and she did not go through the gate behind it.

"Your wash is going white."

"It has been since the spring."

"Then I will take it in before Sunday is out."

"It will not come down on that."

She picked the handbag up again and went on down the yard with it, and the strap on the gate stayed a sixteenth and a half off the timber with the pale band behind it showing the width of the gap. What went into words there was a wash and a Sunday and nothing else, and not one of the three had anything to do with iron.

Now that the crush under that head has taken its coin, the strap will not go back to the timber it was screwed against, and it will keep the sixteenth and a half of daylight under its lower end for as long as the gate is hung on that hinge. The head cannot bite again because nothing is left under it to bite into, and it cannot rise because the shank is packed tight in the hole it made for itself.

The strap keeps the sixteenth it drew off the oak stile, the paint round its head open in a crescent, and now that the head has crushed the timber under it the pale band of wood behind it shows where the iron used to lie.
