# Chapter 0755 — The Yard At Number Twelve

Saturday the third of September 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house and yard at number twelve from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a heap of cut grass standing in the wrong place.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT TAKES THE RAKE INTO HER OWN YARD AND HER SISTER TAKES THE SECOND HALF, AND THE TWO OF THEM WALK ROUND THE HEAP AND FIND IT IS IN THE WRONG PLACE AND NEITHER OF THEM WILL MOVE IT AGAIN.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight at number twelve on Saturday. Her sister comes into the yard through the gate in the morning, works the second half with the rake, and walks round the heap with her at the end of it.

---

She is in the kitchen from early with the stove warm and the green chair drawn up to the table. On the right the gas envelope lies face up and square with the edge, pencil side up. On the left the taped box sits under its cloth squared on three sides. She looks at neither long. Out of the back window the yard is dry and pale, the grass gone over to seed, and where she cut it short in the wet the clippings have lain in a heap against the coal shed wall since the beginning of August and gone to a grey brown mat with the undersides of them still green.

She goes out at the back with the rake and the barrow. The coal shed door is shut with the padlock open in the hasp and the key on its hook behind the back door where it has always hung. The gate stands propped back against the wall and the lamp burns above it. She drags the barrow out into the middle of the yard, puts the teeth into the mat, and gets about as far as the path before the hip tells her how the morning is going to be.

She rakes in short pulls. The mat breaks up into the lighter stuff on top and the older stuff underneath, and the older stuff has gone to a mat of its own that the teeth lift in pieces rather than sheets. She gets a barrowful and then cannot lift it. She tries the handles twice and stands with her shoulder against them and the load sitting in the tray, and the second time she lets it down again into the grass where it stood.

Her sister comes through the gate with her coat over her arm and stops inside the gate with her hand on it.

"You have filled that and not moved it."

"I have filled it and not lifted it." She stands with her hand on the barrow handle. "You can take the second half if you are minded, and I will do the corner where the rakes are too wide."

"I will take the second half." Her sister comes up the yard and looks at the ground her sister has been over. "You have started at the far end. That is the wrong end. It goes damp there and the grass will be down by noon and stuck to itself, and then you are lifting it off in sheets with the bottom of your boot."

"Then start at this end and I will come to you."

---

Her sister takes the rake off her and stands looking down the yard the way she had been told not to look at it.

"The heap goes under the eave." She points with the rake. "The corner by the step is soft since the rain came off it and that barrow will go down to the axle every time you push at it. Under the eave it is a brick floor and it will take the lot."

"It will take the lot." She agrees with that much. "And then it will sit against the wall with the dew off the brick coming down on it, and by the middle of next month there will be a black stripe up that wall as long as your arm and the whole heap will be a mat again inside a fortnight."

"Then where."

"Somewhere with air through it." Her sister looks along the wall. "Under the eave and a foot out from the brick, and no higher than my knee, and I will not have it against the door."

"That door has not been opened since the spring and it is not going to be opened by a heap." She takes the rake back off her. "I want it out of the rain and away from the wall both, and there is nowhere in this yard that is both."

"There is under the eave with a foot out." Her sister takes the rake back again. "You make the gap or the heap goes where I put it, and it will be my heap, and I will not come back for it."

She looks at her sister standing in the middle of her own yard with the rake in both hands.

"You will not come back for it."

"I will not come back for it." Her sister turns the rake to face the wall. "You know what I am like about a thing I have made. Make the gap or let it lie."

She does not make the gap. She stands about a minute with her hand on the gate, watching the sun come over the shed roof and find the wall, and then she picks up the other end of the rake and they work the heap out together.

It takes the best part of the morning. The sister barrows it in four goes to the corner under the eave and tips it each time without shaking the tray, so the heap comes out laid down and neat, and walks backwards with the handles up under her shoulder every time. The woman at number twelve watches the tray from the side and counts nothing. At the last load the sister takes a handful of the clippings out of the tray and puts them on the top of the heap, because she is that kind of woman and could not leave a tray.

The heap is done by the middle of the day. It sits under the eave, black underneath where the shade has kept the bottom wet, greying on the top where the sun is on it, and it comes up the wall on the brick in a stripe a hand's width deep along the whole length of the shed.

Her sister puts the rake down and stands looking at it.

"That is a good heap." She takes the barrow's handles and turns it about. "That will go on the fire in a week and it will burn hot."

"That will go on the fire in a week." Her sister stands on the other side of it. "And the stripe on that wall will be there at Christmas, and I will not scrub it because scrubbing a wall is a thing I do not do."

"The stripe is nothing."

"The stripe is not the point." Her sister walks round the heap the way she has walked round everything in this yard for years, unhurried, without touching it. "The point is the door. That door has not been opened since the spring. The day anybody opens it they will put their toe in my grass and come round it and shut it and think nothing of it, and I am not having a heap across a door."

She walks round the heap on the other side without touching it either.

"Then it goes by the step."

"The step is where the barrow went down last time." Her sister looks at her. "You told me the corner was soft."

"The corner was soft in the wet and it is not soft now."

The corner has not moved for two months and has not been walked on either, and there is no mark in it, and it will go down to the axle the first time somebody pushes at it after rain.

"Then you have told me and I have heard it." Her sister wipes her hands down her coat. "It is a heap of grass. You have had it two months. I have come and made it into a heap out of four barrowfuls instead of a mat with a footpath through it, and that is the whole of what a Saturday gets you. Move it if you want it moved. I am not coming back for it."

She looks at the heap under the eave with the wall stripe coming up it and the door shut beyond.

"It stays."

"It stays." Her sister picks up her coat from the gate. "And you will not shift it, and I know you, and that is the end of the arrangement and it is a bad one."

---

Her sister goes out through the gate and up the lane. She stands with her hand on the gate and watches the coat go round the corner, and then she goes back into the yard and stands over the heap with her hands behind her, looking at the shade side of it where the bottom is black.

Then she goes up the yard past it without touching it, past the path and the barrow standing tipped where the last load came out, in at the back door, and through the passage with her hand on the wall and her foot over the brush on the runner, into the kitchen where the stove is still warm and the green chair is where she left it.

She stands at the back window and looks down at the heap under the eave.

It is in the wrong place and it is a good heap, and she is not going to move it.

She puts the barrow's handles up against the shed wall and hangs the rake on its two nails by the back door, and stands a while at the kitchen sink with her hands in the water from the cold tap and nothing drawn warm, looking out at the shed wall where the dark stripe has started and will go on without her.
