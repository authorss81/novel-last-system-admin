# Chapter 0673 — Up The Stairs With A Torch

Tuesday the twelfth of October 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from dark until late with a torch, and the whole of the night is a hatch she does not open, a sound she listens for, and a parcel she does not open.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT GOES UP HER OWN STAIRS WITH A TORCH TO LISTEN FOR A CISTERN WEEPING AT THE FAR SIDE OF A JOIST WITHOUT GOING UP THE LADDER, LETS A STRANGER INTO HER PASSAGE OVER THE BRUSH, PUTS HIS PARCEL ON HER KITCHEN TABLE, DOES NOT WRITE A TELEPHONE NUMBER ON IT, AND COMES DOWN AND SITS AT HER OWN TABLE.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is on her own all night. One man speaks to her and he does not ask her one question she could have answered.

---

She empties the bowl under the bath tap after tea, with the folded cloth in the bowl and the tap dripping onto it while she watches, and she puts the bowl back with the cloth in it.

The brown mark on the bathroom ceiling is still there with a hard edge, and in the evening light it looks wider by a finger's width at the front edge, towards the landing and the front room beyond it.

She wants to hear whether the cistern is still weeping without going up the ladder, because going up would make it a thing she had tried and failed at, and listening from below is a thing she can do.

She gets the torch from the kitchen drawer where the string and the fuses and the tea bags live, and she tests it against her palm and it is bright.

---

She goes up the stairs of her own house in her slippers with her hand on the rail, past the half landing where the rail turns, to the top landing in the dark.

The hatch with the fixed ladder is shut above her, and the paint round it shows the line she saw in September, faint and dark at the edge nearest the front.

She stands on the top landing with the torch off and listens, and the house ticks round her the way houses do at night, and the tap drips below through two floors.

Then she hears it, thin and high and steady, a cistern weeping at the far side of a joist, the same sound she heard in August standing a foot and a half higher on a stool from the front room.

She does not go up the ladder.

She cannot turn the supply off from downstairs because she does not know where the pipe comes up and never has, and she cannot ask anybody to get at it and has not since before Christmas.

---

She comes down backwards one step at a time with the torch on for the treads, and at the half landing she stops and looks at the ceiling where the landing meets the front of the house.

There is now a damp patch showing on the front room ceiling, small and pale round the edge with a darker middle, just inside the door where she stands in the doorway to look.

The rush-seated chair is against the wall by the window. The sideboard has nothing on it. The brush is not in here. The brush is on the passage floor in the middle of the runner with the handle across the walking line.

She stands in the front room doorway for about a minute with the torch off, looking at the patch, and she does not go in.

She has not sat in there since the sixteenth of July because there is now a question in that room, and she has never in her life been able to be a person being asked about a thing, and water coming through the ceiling does not make it a room she can sit in.

She stands there long enough to be sure of the shape of it, which is a thing she is now able to do and is not proud of. It is not a straight line down from the corner and it is not a circle. It is a cloud about the width of a tea tray, and it is paler round the outside, and the middle of it is the colour of the back of a spoon, and the outside edge of the pale part is where the white paint has started to lift and come away in a fine flake, and she does not touch that either.

There is a man in this borough who could tell her in a quarter of an hour what that is. He came into this kitchen in the summer and put a card joint in the leg of the table and planed a piece of ash to a quarter of an inch and sawn it flush, and he did it in about forty minutes, and he did not charge her and he did not tell her what it would have cost, and she did not ask him, and she has had him in the house and lost him.

He is the same sort of man as the woman on the answerphone, and that is the whole of the difficulty. They are the same sort of man. They have vans. They will come on a Monday and they will be kind about it and they will not force anybody, and they will not go until the job is done, and a woman who cannot be a person being asked about a thing cannot start that in her own front room, and cannot end it either once it is started.

So the patch goes on and she goes on not having asked.

She stands in the doorway until the torch is cold in her hand and then she goes back up the half landing and stands under the hatch with the light on it, and the paint round the frame is lifting along the same line, and there is a small bead of water standing in the corner of the frame where the hatch meets the joist, and it is not moving.

She puts a finger under it and it comes away on the end of her finger, and it is not dirty, and it is not anything much, and it is the first time she has ever touched the water in that house.

---

The knocker goes a little after eleven, and it is not a letter through the box, it is a knocker.

She is still on the stairs with the torch in her hand.

"Who is it?"

"It's the lad from the top of the road. I've got your parcel."

She takes the parcel off the step and he is standing on the path in a hood with his hands in his pockets, and he is about twenty-five and he has not been in this house in his life, and there is a man in the road behind him walking a dog who does not stop.

"Who's it for?"

"I don't know. It came for number twelve and I'm number fourteen."

"Then it's for me."

"Right." He waits, and does not go, and it is the waiting that makes her say the next thing. "It's got a smell to it. Like a box of fish."

"That'll be the fish."

"You haven't ordered fish."

"No," she says. "I haven't ordered fish."

"Van man brought it. Big yellow box off the van, said it was a parcel for number twelve and I was in."

"Then your van man knows more than either of us."

He laughs at that, and it is a young man's laugh and it is not unkind, and she finds she has liked him for it, for laughing at the right thing, which is a thing she has not had to do about anybody since before Christmas.

She looks past him at the dark end of the road and at the coal shed wall and at the gate standing back against it, and then she says, "You'd better not stand about, it's cold."

"I'm not stood about, I'm waiting for you to say thank you."

"Well. Thank you."

"There's no need. Genuinely. It was on the van and I was going past."

He goes back up the path after the man with the dog, and she shuts the front door on the latch that is not a latch, and stands in her own passage with a parcel in her hands.

---

The brush is where it is. She has to get to the kitchen and the brush is in the middle of the runner with the handle across, and he had to get past it too, and he did not say anything about it, and neither did she, and there is now a person outside who has stepped over it in the dark.

She steps over it herself and goes through to the kitchen and puts the parcel on the table, on the left-hand side, where the cloth usually is, and the gas envelope is on the right of the table face up square with the edge, pencil side up, and the envelope does not move.

The parcel is about the size of two loaves of bread and it is cold and it is a smell of ice and newspaper.

She gets a pen out of the drawer, which is the drawer with the string and the fuses and the tea bags, and the pen is a ballpoint that works, and she stands at her own table with the parcel in front of her and the envelope eight inches away in its square with the edge.

And she knows to the syllable what she would write. It is a number. It is a number in the middle of a boy in a hoodie, and she would put it on the side of a fish box in her own hand and push it through her own front door, and on Monday or Tuesday a woman with a bright voice and a van would come into a front room where a rush-seated chair stands against a wall that nobody sits on, and she would be standing in the room with it, and she would be a person being asked about a thing.

So she puts the pen back in the drawer, and the drawer shuts, and the parcel stays on the left of the table, and the envelope stays square with the edge on the right, and nothing has been written on anything by anybody in that house since before Christmas.

She does not open the parcel either, because a box of fish is a thing for tomorrow, and tomorrow she will not have anybody in, and there is nobody to give it to.

She does not put it in the bin either, and she is aware that not putting it in the bin is a decision she has made twice, once in the morning with the tape and once now with the fish, and that a woman who cannot be a person being asked about a thing is also a woman who cannot throw a thing away, and that between the two of those there is not very much room left for anything else to happen to her.

---

She puts the torch back in the drawer and sits at the table under the window on the green chair with the cushion on it, with a cup she does not drink from.

She listens to the message a third time before bed, and she listens to it with the volume up, and when the woman says *we need to be sure you're happy for us to look* she says out loud, to the empty kitchen, "I know you do," and the machine says *for the freeholder, not for you*, which is the sentence that undoes her, and has undone her twice before.

The gate is first because it is the only one she actually wants. The second line is a room with a chair in it and nobody has asked her what is wrong with it.

There is a card in the hall, in the pocket of the apron, and it says a free call, and she has not decided about it, and she is aware that not deciding about it is a decision, and that she made it in about a second and a half on a Friday afternoon with a man standing on her path.

Four of six are done and nothing has been crossed off, because there is no pencil in that house.

The front door is not on the latch. The passage light is on. The gate is standing open against the wall of the coal shed with both bolts in an apron pocket.

She sits until the cup goes cold, and then she washes it and puts it on the drainer and wipes the cloth flat.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS HEARD WATER SHE CANNOT STOP AND HAS SEEN IT COME THROUGH INTO THE ROOM SHE WILL NOT SIT IN, AND HAS COME DOWN WITHOUT OPENING A HATCH, AND HAS HAD A TELEPHONE NUMBER IN HER HAND WITH A PEN AND SOMETHING TO WRITE IT ON AND HAS NOT WRITTEN IT, AND THE ENVELOPE ON HER TABLE IS STILL FACE UP SQUARE WITH THE EDGE.**
