# Chapter 0939 — Behind The Airbrick

Monday the sixteenth of September 2024, at the airbrick in the boundary wall at number nine with the day shut out of the wind. Behind a vent.

**A CAST VENT SITS IN THE BOUNDARY WALL AT NUMBER NINE AND SHE MEANS TO GET THE HAND IN BEHIND IT THROUGH THE OPENING IN ITS FRONT, THE FOUR FINGERS SPLAYED ACROSS THE BACK OF IT AND THE THUMB KEPT OUTSIDE ON THE STONE BESIDE IT, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR BACK THAT SPACE GOES AND WHAT SHUTS IT OFF.**

The boundary wall went up with the house and the airbrick was set into it in the same week, about two feet off the ground on the lane side, and both of those are the same age. Nobody has been at the airbrick since and there is nothing to be at.

It is a cast iron airbrick about the size of two bricks laid flat, with a frame and a curved louvre in the front of it and a back without anything on it, built into the leaf of the wall with its own bed of lime. The space behind it is the space the wall was built to let air through, and nobody has looked into it since the week it was put in.

---

Every vent has a shape on the outside of it and the hole is behind that, and this one is no different. In at the open front it goes, the fingers spread, the thumb left out on the stone beside them. The louvre is blunt along its lower edge, and it is blunt because it was cast in a mould and not ground, and the iron of it is cold and hard and about the thickness of two coins with a hollow behind it.

Behind the louvre the iron goes back about two inches to the closed back of the airbrick, and that closed back is the far wall of everything there is. Between that back and the leaf of the wall behind there is nothing but air and about two inches of it, closed at the top and the bottom by the wall's own bed joints.

On the lower ledge of the vent, inside, where the louvre's curve comes down to the frame, there are three grains of grit and a skin of grey dust about the thickness of a leaf. Nothing else. The hand's four fingers spread out across the back of the airbrick and cannot get past it anywhere.

---

An airbrick of that pattern is a two-part casting and the two parts are bolted or riveted through the thickness of it, so the whole thing is a box about two inches deep and not a plate with a slot cut in it. Behind it is the cavity of the wall, which in a wall of that age is a space of loose rubble and lime, and the rubble does not reach the back of the airbrick because the fitter left it clear.

A fitter always leaves it clear, and it is the one thing about the fitting of a wall that never varies. Lime that squeezes out of a bed joint and goes behind a vent does it once, in the first winter, and it does it because the wall is settling and the bed joints are closing a little, and after that first winter the space behind the vent is as clear as it was on the day.

What differs behind one vent and another is the dust. Air comes in through a louvre and turns a corner, and what it carries goes in with it and settles on the ledge inside, and there is nowhere for it to go afterwards. A vent in a sheltered wall collects a leaf's thickness of dust in twenty years and a vent on a road side collects it in five, and either way it collects it inside and not out, because an opening that has a hood over it keeps what comes in.

That wall has a hood over it. The coping above the airbrick oversails by about two inches and throws the run off the top of the brick, and an airbrick under a coping is the difference between a vent that breathes and a vent that chokes on its own grit. The three grains and the skin on that ledge are what got past the hood, and most of what arrives at that face arrives from above and is thrown by the coping before it gets to the louvre.

The iron is in good order for ninety-odd years. A cast airbrick in a wall goes at the louvre first, because the louvre is the only part of it with a curve in it and the curve is where the water stands and where the rust starts, and the frame and the box behind it go on in a lump long after the louvre has gone soft.

A wall of that age is rubble and lime in the leaf and the vents are set into it before the wall is finished, because a vent cannot be built into a rubble wall afterwards without taking half the leaf out. The rubble is broken stone bedded in lime that has gone hard and pale, and the stones in it are the same stone as the wall's coping and out of the same quarry a mile off.

The louvre on this one has not gone soft. It has lost a little at the bottom corner, which is where a puddle from the coping would sit if the coping were a foot higher, and it has lost it in the shape of a thumb-nail, and the rest of the curve stands as it came out of the mould.

The woman who does her shopping comes to the door of the outbuilding from the front path with the door standing open behind her and does not go in. She stands at it a while and then goes away up the path towards the gate with nothing in her hands.

The wall gets nothing out of her, and she does not go round to the lane either, and the outbuilding door is still standing open behind her when she reaches the gate. The wall is on the far side of that path from where she stood and the path does not run along the face of it, and nobody has any reason to walk that way on the way to a gate.

There is a second vent in that wall about nine feet along, and it is a newer pattern, and it was put in when the wall was repointed and the fitter chose a pressed louvre instead of a cast one because pressed is cheaper and lighter.
A pressed vent has no box behind it at all. It is a louvre and a frame in a sleeve, and the sleeve goes straight through the leaf and stops at the face of the inner leaf, and the space behind a pressed vent is a length of leaf rather than a box.

Two vents in one wall nine feet apart, one cast and one pressed, tell you the whole history of the wall in about as long as it takes to look at them. The cast one is the original and the pressed one is the repair, and the repair is not a repair of anything: the original was never blocked and there was nothing to repair, and somebody put in a second vent because a second vent was on a list and the list did not say why.

The inside of that ledge is the flattest part of the whole wall and the only part of it that anybody could ever have cleaned, because it is the only part a cloth could be got into. In ninety years nobody has been inside one of these vents with a cloth, and and no wall ever built has offered anybody a reason to go in there with a cloth.

The three grains are not the same as the dust. A grain is a grain and it comes from the mortar of the coping above, where the lime has been taken out of the face by the weather and the sand is left standing in it. The dust is from the run itself and it is a different colour from the grains, and on a ledge nine inches wide the two lie side by side and do not mix, because neither of them moves.

The airbrick keeps its two inches and its box and its ledge and its three grains, and the hood over it goes on throwing the run clear of the louvre, so that the ledge fills at the rate it fills and no faster and a hand put in through that face to-day finds a shallow box with a dusty floor and a dust of its own on the ledge, and comes out again with nothing on it but grey.
