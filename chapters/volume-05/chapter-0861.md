# Chapter 0861 — The Nail Shaken Over The Sheet

Thursday the sixteenth of November 2023, at the gate end of the yard at the lock-up from the time the dew has come off the steel until the last of the light goes. Nails, and a sheet, and about two minutes.

**THERE IS A BENT NAIL LOOSE IN THE YARD AT THE LOCK-UP AND A PILE OF OLD NAILS BY THE GATE, AND SHE MEANS TO SHAKE THE BENT ONE ONCE BY THE HEAD OVER A SHEET SO THAT THE LOOSE RUST COMES OFF IT AND PUT IT DOWN BY THE GATE WHERE NAILS GO, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK COMES IN THROUGH THE NEAR ROLLER DOOR WITH THE SHUTTER UP AND GOES OUT THROUGH IT AGAIN.**

The nail was in the grit at the gate end of the yard, on the edge of the yard where the grit off the bays goes and the water goes and everything else goes.

It is about three inches long and made of wrought iron and it is bent at the neck, about an inch back from the head, right over to about a right angle. Not a twist. A bend, clean, where the iron gave and stayed given.

She takes it up off the grit where it was lying, between her fingers by the head, which is the only place on a nail you can get purchase without feeling nothing at all, and lays a sheet of polythene on the flags under her hands.

Then she shakes it. Once, hard, with the hand going up and down about eight inches and the head pointing at the sheet.

---

The rust comes off in dust.

That is the finding and it takes about two seconds to arrive at. Rusted iron comes off a nail in one of two ways. If the rust has eaten into the metal it comes off in flakes, and the flakes are the shape of the rust underneath, and where a flake comes off there is a hole left in the iron and the nail is finished.

If it has not, it comes off as dust, and there is dust on the sheet and the iron under the dust is pitted all over and dark and in one piece.

There is dust on the sheet. It is a good deal of it for two seconds of shaking and it is the colour of a wet cigarette, and where the nail has been over the sheet there is a mark on the polythene the width of the shank and about two feet long.

She shakes it a second time over a clean part of the sheet to see whether there is more, and there is not much more, and that is the other half of the finding. A nail that has been lying in a wet yard for a season and then has two seasons left in it comes off for about a month and then goes quiet, because whatever was loose in the rust has gone and what is left is bonded into the pitting. Two shakes and not thirty is a nail that is finished letting go of what it was carrying.

She looks at the nail again without shaking it. Under the dust the iron is pitted, deep in places, and the pits run the length of it and follow the grain of the wrought iron from end to end, and none of the pits go through.

So the metal is sound and the surface is not, and those are two different questions, and a nail with only the second of them is a nail that has been out in this yard since the spring and will be out in it for a couple of winters more.

The bend is the other half of it.

The bend is about an inch back from the head, and it is bent the same way all the way round rather than twisted, which means the nail did not get bent by being trodden on or run over by a barrow wheel. A nail run over twists first and flattens second. This one went over and stayed.

She turns it in the light and looks at the claw.

The claw is splayed, both sides, the way a claw opens when it is pulled out of a hole it has been in a long time. And on one side of it, the left, the end of the iron is turned up and standing about the thickness of a coin above the other side, and the standing part is dark and it is worn smooth on the top of it.

That is the mark of a nail used as a punch.

You drive a nail into old hard oak to open a split, or into a hinge that will not swing, or into the head of another nail, and you turn it. You put a bar across the head and you lean on it, and the neck of the nail goes over, and the claw spreads and one side lifts and gets rubbed smooth by whatever you turned it against. And when the job was done somebody pulled the nail out again, which is what the claw is for, and the claw opened on the way out.

A nail bent in a barrow wheel is rubbish. A nail bent over on itself like that has been doing a job, and the job is finished, and the nail has kept its temper in the bend and it is the hardest piece of iron in that yard.

She does not know where it came from and there is no way of knowing. A nail like that has come out of a pallet, or a bench leg, or the lid of something, or out of the roof of a bay nobody has been inside since the spring. It has been on the ground at that gate end since at least the spring, because there is grit packed into both sides of the bend and grit packs in about a season.

The shutter on the near bay is up on the hook this morning, the curtain wound up and the bottom bar clear of the floor, and it came down again after the woman who books work has been in and out through it. It has not been taken out of its guides and it is not going to be.

She takes the sheet up by the corners and the dust on it goes into the bin and the sheet goes in with it.

Then the nail goes down by the gate on top of the pile.

The pile is a short length of pipe offcut stood up against the wall by the gate and about two-thirds full. It is the nails heap. There are no good nails in it and there have not been any for about four years, because nails are bought by the box and used off the box, and the heap is what comes off other jobs.

Which is why the wire nails are still in it.

A cut wire nail that has been in a joint and drawn out is rusted solid from the head down to about an inch, and the rust has swollen the shank until it is thicker than the hole it came out of, and the head tears away in your hand before the shank will move. Those cannot be drawn and they cannot be used. There are two dozen of them in the pipe and they are down to about half a round shank under two inches of rust, and they will be in that pipe when the yard is paved.

Straightening one of those would not help, because it is not straight that is wanted. It is thin.

A bent nail driven into a hole that is already there goes in, because the bend finds the hole and the rest of the shank passes through a hole it was made for. A straight nail driven into the same hole splits the wood out from around it, because the shank is the same thickness all the way and the hole is not, and what you get is a nail in a split. There is not much difference between those two nails and there is everything between them.

The wire nails are in the pipe where they have been, and the bent one is down on top of them, and a bent nail in an old hole is worth more than a straight one cut in the same hole, and that is the whole use of a pile of old nails outside a gate.
