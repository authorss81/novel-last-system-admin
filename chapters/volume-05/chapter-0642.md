# Chapter 0642 — The Bench On The Parade

Monday the twenty-fourth of May 2021, in this borough. He is on a bench on the parade from about half past two in the afternoon until about ten past three, and she is on the other end of it, and there is a foot and a half of cold iron between them for the whole of it.

**THE WOMAN WHO BROUGHT THE CHAIR RINGS THE NUMBER ON A CARD THE MAN LEFT ON A TABLE, AND THE TWO OF THEM SIT ON A BENCH ON THE PARADE FOR THE BEST PART OF AN HOUR AND EACH LEARNS THAT THE OTHER WAS TOLD SOMETHING FALSE BY THE SAME WOMAN IN THE SAME FRONT ROOM, AND NEITHER OF THEM USES IT, AND THEY AGREE ON EXACTLY ONE THING, AND THE ONE THING THEY AGREE ON IS THAT THEY WILL BOTH KEEP ON COMING AND WILL NOT TELL HER THEY HAVE MET, WHICH IS THE WRONG THING AND THEY BOTH KNOW IT IS THE WRONG THING WITHIN FOUR MINUTES AND AGREE IT ANYWAY.**

He is a man in his early forties. She is a woman in her late forties. They have never been in a room with each other and they were in one for fifty minutes on the Wednesday and neither of them spoke to the other.

---

She rings him on the Friday afternoon at about half past two from her own kitchen, and she has got the card on the table in front of her and she has been looking at it for about ten minutes.

It is a bad card. It is a card a man gets printed when he has decided he is a tradesman and not before, and it has got a mobile number and a landline and an address off an industrial estate and a man's name, and it has got KITCHENS AND JOINERY on it in big black capitals with a telephone symbol, and the symbol is on a slight angle, and she has looked at the symbol for about four minutes.

She rings it because of the table.

Not the stool. The stool she has been thinking about since the Wednesday and she has not got a single usable thing to say about the stool. The table. There is a table against a wall in a kitchen at number twelve with two marks on the floorboards underneath where its legs have been, and that table is going to stay against that wall, because a woman of seventy-eight is not going to move a table back under a window, and the woman in her late forties cannot have a table in a kitchen on the off chance that it falls down.

So she rings the number on a card a man in joinery has given out, about a table.

And that is the reason she gives herself, and it is even mostly true, and it took her ten minutes to get to it and she has not noticed what the other nine of them were.


He says he has nothing on until Thursday and she says she is not ringing about work, and he says *right*, and she says nothing for about four seconds, and he says, "Go on."

And she says, "I'll say it quick because I've not said it quick to anybody in my life and I'm saying it quick."

And he says, "Right."

---

He picks the parade because it is on the way and because it is the only place in this borough where two people can stand or sit a foot and a half apart in public and not be anybody's business.

She is there at about half past two and he is there at about ten to three and they do not sit together and they do not speak for about a minute, and then he says, "You all right."

And she says, "Did you get a card in with the blanket."

And he says, "It was in the van. It's on the bench there."

And she says, "That's not mine, that's yours, I've had it on my table since Friday."

And he says, "Right. I'll take it Monday."

And that is the whole of the small talk, and then it does not get any better, and it goes on.


Here is how it turns, and it turns about eight minutes in, and neither of them planned it.

She says, "That woman is frightened of something."

And he says, "Is she."

And she says, "She's seventy-eight and she's got a bad wrist and she got out of a chair on her own and she's frightened of the story, and I know what that looks like because I've seen it on about six jobs and it's never once been about the job."

And he says, "Right."

And she says, "And she told you I was the council."

And he says, "About the damp."

And she says, "There is no council woman about the damp behind that house. There's no council about the damp behind any house on that road. You said it yourself, you'd not seen one van or one clipboard since February."

And he says, "I did."

And she says, "And she told me you did her daughter's kitchen."

And he says, "Ay."

And she says, "There is no daughter."

And he says, "No."

---

And then they both stop, and the parade does what it does, and neither of them says the next thing for about a minute and a half.

Here is what the next thing is.

She has a hand on a bag on her knees and she looks at the parade and says, "I've not been in a council office since I was sixteen and I've never been anything to do with a council in my life, and she said it to a man she'd given a stool to a couple of Saturdays before, and I sat in her front room and took it."

And he says, "I didn't take mine."

And she says, "No."

And he says, "I heard it and I knew it was not true and I did not use it."

And she says, "No. You didn't."

And he says, "I've been working out why I didn't use it and I have not got a reason, and the reason I have got is the one I'd be ashamed of, which is that I have been wrong about her since the Saturday and being wrong suited me."


And here is the thing they agree on, and it is the wrong thing, and it takes them about four minutes and both of them know inside the four minutes.

And he says, "What I am not going to do is go up there and say where that chair came from."

And she says, "No."

And he says, "Because it isn't mine to say. She hasn't asked me and you haven't asked me and there's a third person in it who I haven't met and doesn't know I'm in it."

And she says, "Right."

And he says, "And what I am going to do is go on bringing her things and let her get on with it."

And she says, "So am I."

And he says, "And we don't tell her we've met."

And she says, "No."

And he says, "She'd want to know. She'd make tea and sit us both down in that front room and she'd have it out of one of us inside two minutes, and then it stops being hers."

And she says, "It'd stop being hers."

And he says, "Right."

And she says, "Then that's what we'll do."

And he says, "Right."

---

Here is why that is the wrong thing, and both of them know it, and neither of them says it.

Wrong because the two of them are now the only two people in this borough who know anything, and they have agreed not to put their knowledge together in the same room as the woman it is about.

Wrong because the one thing a woman in her late seventies has been doing since the Saturday of the twenty-fourth of April is protecting two people from finding out about each other, and the two of them have just agreed to make that protection permanent.

Wrong because there are now two chairs in that kitchen and each of them has promised to go on supplying one of them, and neither of them has asked her whether she can stand the two, and she has not told either of them that she cannot.

Wrong because there will be a Friday and a Wednesday close together, and it will not be a coincidence the second time.

And he knows that one. He has said it out loud, on a bench, as a thing he is not going to worry about, and he has not noticed that it is the same arithmetic the woman at number twelve has been doing at her own window since the fifth of May, and that it is the same four sentences in a different order, and that the only difference is which side of it he is standing on.


They sit there after that for about twenty minutes and they say almost nothing.

And there is one more thing, and it is the last thing, and it is the only one of the two true things either of them will repeat afterwards.

And she says, "You put a stool in that kitchen and you never said where it came from."

And he says, "No."

And she says, "And I put a chair in that kitchen and I never said where it came from, and mine came out of a garage on a Monday in April and it was not on the list, and I took it out of a woman's garage while that woman was at her sister's and I said the skip to her on the telephone, and I have not said that to anybody in this borough in about twenty minutes and I am saying it to you on a bench."

And he says, "Right."

And she says, "That's the only reason I'm ringing you and I have been pretending all afternoon that it is about a table."

And he says, "It is not about a table."

And she says, "No."

And he says, "Mine's not a skip, mine's worse than a skip. I made it. I made it on a Monday and I got it wrong and I took the weight out of three rails because I knew I had, and I lied to a man on my own bench about who it was for, and I have had a green folding chair through a kitchen window in my head ever since and I have not told a living soul that it was there and I put the stool in the middle of the floor of a woman who was standing at a sill."

And he says, "I have not said that to anybody either."

And she says, "No."

And he says, "So there are two of us on this bench with a thing in our pocket."

And she says, "Two."


Here is what she gets up and says at about ten past three, and she says it standing, on the parade, with her bag over her shoulder, and it is the sentence she has had ready since the Friday and it is not the sentence she expected to say.

And she says, "She told me you did her daughter's kitchen and told you I was the council, and the damp is not real and she has not got a daughter, and she is doing it because she thinks one of us is going to be all right and the other one is not, and she is doing it to us both at once so that whichever of us she is right about, the other one can carry on coming."

And he says, "That's a very old woman."

And she says, "She's seventy-eight."

And he says, "Right."

And she says, "If you go up there on Wednesday do not ask her about either of them. Promise me that."

And he says, "I won't."

And she says, "And if she says something to me on Friday I will bring it back to you and you can decide what to do with it, and that is the only thing I am asking for."

And he says, "All right."

And she says, "And that is us agreeing to something again and I have just spent the best part of an hour agreeing to something with you and I did not like it the first time either, so I am telling you I do not like it this time."

And he says, "I do not like it either."

And she says, "Good."

And she goes, and he stays on the bench, and the parade is doing what a parade does at ten past three on a Monday at the end of May.

---

Here is what he works out after she has gone, and he works it out on the bench, and it takes him about ten minutes, and it is the only thinking he does in this borough that he gets wrong in a way that matters.

He thinks: she has just told me that a woman of seventy-eight is managing two people for the sake of one of them, and that she is right, and that he has been the one being managed for, and that the stool was not the thing that was wrong with him.

And then he thinks: but she said the damp, and the damp is not real, and the woman in her late forties is not the council, and the woman at number seventy-eight did not say either of them to be kind.

And then he thinks, and this is the one that is wrong: she said the damp to me and the daughter to her because she was keeping the two of them apart, and being kept apart is what she wants, and the price of it is a table against a wall and a woman on the floor of a kitchen with nobody told.

And he cannot see from a bench on the parade that a woman in her late seventies has been standing at a kitchen window at half past six every morning since the fifth of May working out who she would look at.

And he has been right about almost everything on this bench and wrong about the one thing that is in the room.

**SHE RANG HIM BECAUSE OF A TABLE AND SAID SOMETHING ELSE, AND HE SAID SOMETHING ELSE BACK, AND NEITHER OF THEM USED THE ONE TRUE THING THE OTHER WAS GIVEN, AND THEY AGREED ON ONE THING AND IT WAS THAT THEY WOULD BOTH KEEP ON COMING AND WOULD NOT TELL HER THEY HAD MET, AND ON THE BENCH HE WORKED OUT THAT SHE WAS BEING KEPT APART FOR HER OWN GOOD AND COULD NOT SEE FROM THERE THAT KEEPING HER APART IS THE THING SHE HAS BEEN DOING BY HERSELF SINCE THE FIFTH OF MAY.**

(End of chapter 0642)
