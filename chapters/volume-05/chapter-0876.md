# Chapter 0876 — Two Inches Under A Step

Friday the twelfth of January 2024, at the top step at number nine from first light until the street has gone quiet. The space beneath a step, and what it is standing on.

**THERE IS A STONE STEP ACROSS THE TOP OF THAT FRONT PATH AND SHE MEANS TO TURN A HAND ON ITS SIDE AND SLIDE IT IN UNDER THE FACE OF THE STEP, PALM DOWN, ALONG THE WHOLE RUN OF IT, AND FIND OUT WHAT THE STEP IS BEDGED ON, AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING STANDS AT THE FRONT GATE WITH HER BAG ON THE PAVEMENT SIDE OF IT.**

The top step is one piece, and it is a piece, not a flag. It is about three feet by one foot and it is the thickness of a paving slab with a bit taken off it, and it is dressed on the top and part of the way down the front, and it has been the top step of that house for longer than she has lived in it.

It is the only step anybody ever looks at, and it is looked at from above by everybody who crosses that door, and what is under it has not been looked at by anybody at all.

That is the ordinary state of things about a step. A step is a thing you stand on. The whole of a step is above you and in front of you, and the ground under a step is a place that only exists for the step to be on.

And the sand is not an unusual thing to find under one. A front path at this house is flags on sand, and the path was laid by somebody who had a barrowload of sand come with the flags and used it under the flags and then used what was left over for the step, because a step wants the same level as the path it comes off and sand is the quickest way to get a stone to a level.

That is the whole of how it was done and it was not done badly. It was done the way a path is done when it is done properly, which is flags on a hundred millimetres of sand, and the sand under the flags is still under the flags and firm, and the sand under the step is not, because it is at the end of the fall instead of in the middle of it.

Water goes along a path. It does not go across one. Everything that comes off that front path goes down the path and off the end of it, and the step is at the end of the path, and it is the only part of the whole of that path where there is any distance for the water to run at all.

What she wants to know is whether it is on anything.

A stone step has three ways of sitting on the ground. It can be laid on flags. It can be laid on a bed of sand with its own weight bedding it. Or it can be laid on a wall — a brick pier, a course of blocks, a slab of its own laid over a hole.

The third one is the good one and it is the one you can put a hand in under, because a step on a wall has air under it by design and you can find out how much air and how it is arranged.

She is going to find out which of the three this is, and she cannot do it by looking, because from outside you see the top and the front and nothing else, and the front of a dressed stone is the face that was made to be looked at.

So she goes down on the path and puts the hand in.

On its side. Palm down. Thumb along the top of it and the fingers under the front, so that the whole hand is a plate about a quarter of an inch thick and the hand takes up the whole height of the gap and nothing about the arrangement of the hand can be read as clearance.

Then she pushes.

The hand goes in about two inches along the front of that step and stops, and the stopping is not the step and it is not her arm. It is the ground coming up.

Then she takes it out and starts again at the other end, because a thing measured from one end is not a thing measured.

Same answer. Two inches at that end.

Then she goes back to the middle and puts the hand in with the fingers leading and pushes further, and this time it goes in a hand's width deeper than it did at either end, and the fingers go down into sand and the sand goes over the top of them and comes up on the knuckle side.

The step is on sand.

Sand, and about two inches of it, and about four in the middle.

That is not a bed. A sand bed that gives at the middle and does not give at the ends is not a bed at all; it is a stone lying on a hollow, because the sand has been washed out from the middle of the front and gone down the path, and what is left is the sand at the two ends carrying the step up on two points and nothing at all in between them.

Which is why the step gives.

She stands on the front of it with her own weight and there is about a quarter of an inch of movement in it, all of it in the middle, and none of it at either end, and it comes back up when she takes her weight off.

That is the fault. Not a bad step. A step that is not on anything in the middle.

The underside of the slab is dry.

That is the other thing and it is worth as much as the first. It is a bedded stone and the underside of it has never been wet and has never been damp, and a stone that has never been wet under it has never had water go through it, which means every bit of rain that has ever come off that front path has gone round the step and not through it, because there is no joint under it to come through.

She can get her hand flat up under the middle of it and turn her fingers over and feel the whole of the underside, and it is flat, and it is stone, and it has a film of sand on it that has been there since it was laid and has never been disturbed.

There is no mortar. There is no joint. There is no bedding of any sort at the bottom of that step except sand, and the sand at the middle is gone.

There is one thing on the top of that step that anybody has ever done to it.

A drip groove, about an inch back from the front edge, running the width of the stone, and about a quarter of an inch deep.

It is cut, not faulted, and it is cut in the same dressing as the front, so it was there when the stone was made and before it was laid. It is the only work that has ever been done to that step and it is a piece of work somebody did four hundred miles away to no purpose except that water running off a step runs down a groove and off the edge, and where it went down the face of the stone it ran back under the nosing and stood in the joint.

The groove works. The groove has worked for whatever the stone is old.

So the water off that step goes down the groove and off the front edge and lands on the path, and where it lands it goes into the path and along the path, and it comes out at the front edge and runs across the front of the step on its way to the gutter.

And every winter it takes a little more of the sand out from under the middle of that step and puts it down the path, and the step comes down a quarter of an inch further in the middle each year, and there is no way of putting anything back under it.

---

The woman who does her shopping comes to the front gate in the afternoon and stands on the pavement side of it with her bag at her feet.

She stays there about ten minutes looking at the road. At some point she turns round and looks back up the front of the house, all the way up to the roof and down again, the way a person does when they are waiting for somebody in a house.

Then she goes off up the pavement with the bag. The gate stayed where it was the whole time.

Given two inches of sand at the two ends and nothing whatever in the middle, and a groove cut in the top doing the one job it was ever cut for, that step will be standing at that door longer than the door above it lasts.
