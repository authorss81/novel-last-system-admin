# Chapter 0264 — The Seventeenth

Saturday the seventeenth of September 2016, from about eleven in the morning, a building by the water in Tideglass, a parade of shops, the low end of Peverell Street, and a hall off the parade.

**NOTHING HAPPENED IN THIS BOROUGH ON THE SEVENTEENTH OF SEPTEMBER, AND IT WAS THE SAME BOROUGH AS THE SIXTEENTH, AND THE THING THAT IS STILL A QUESTION WAS STILL A QUESTION AT HALF PAST FOUR IN THE AFTERNOON AND NOBODY IN THAT ROOM ANSWERED IT.**

Nina Calder was in the records room with a cup of tea on the sill and a list of what was left in a store, and the door stuck.

"Open it," she said. "You've never opened it and there's no reason you shouldn't today, it's Saturday and I'm here on my own and I'd like the tea to be worth the walk up."

He opened it. The register is at nineteen. The nineteenth is a yard light with a bulb in a maintenance column and the bulb is out, and underneath that a line goes down the page and stops about two inches short of the bottom of a book that is about two inches deep.

He did the sum standing at the table with a biro on the back of his hand, out of a habit, and it came to six hundred and sixty-one, and he wrote it on the piece of paper and put the piece of paper back in his coat.

"That's the same as it was in July," said Nina Calder, not looking up, "and it will be the same in a month, and I have said that to you every month since about February, and I would like it noted that I have said it and that neither of us has ever done anything about it."

"You said it was the same as every month in July."

"I said it was the same as every month in July because it was on my list in July, and now I have said it without the list, and that is progress, and it is the only kind there has been." She turned a page of her own. "Close it up again if you like. It's that book. It has been that book since the nineteenth of November two years ago and it is going to be that book in March and nobody in this building is going to write in the margin, and I include him, and I have been the one looking at that margin most weeks for nineteen years." He was out of the building at twenty past eleven and down the parade by twelve, which is where he goes on a Saturday.

The parade was the same parade.

The shutter on the shop went up at the minute it always goes up, and the window card with the shop's own telephone number on it and nothing else on it was in the window where it has always been, and the woman of about sixty-five was behind that counter, and nobody went in and told her anything.

There was a van on the forecourt at the end of the road that had come off the bridge at about a quarter past ten with a scabbed corner on the offside wing, and a man in the office by the Marchmont gate read a card out to nobody in particular and put it straight afterwards.

Down at the end of the parade a man on a ladder was taking down a sign that had been up since about April, and a second man was holding the ladder, and neither of them would be able to tell you afterwards who had asked whom, and it made no difference to either of them and it was the only thing on that parade that anybody had agreed to do.

At the bottom of Peverell Street there is a pump in a doorway and it is not plumbed to anything and the stopcock is off.

The wall beside it is bare. There is a clean strip of newer paint where a length of hose used to lie in one place, and the grey line of dirt above it, and the hose is in another man's hands four doors up the street and it has been since the twenty-first of June, and nobody in this borough has said one word about a hose to anybody since the summer came in.

The door of number forty-one is open a hand's width, the way it always is, and there is a radio on inside it very faintly, and a woman went up it with a bucket and came back down it with a bucket and did not stop at the door, and the street is three houses and a wall and a bank.

---

The hall off the parade is free on a Saturday afternoon because nobody rents it, and the woman who has had the key to it for about nine years is in it, and the gas is on a meter, and she had put money in it before anybody came because there is no reason on earth to stop now there are about nine of them.

About nine people came, the same as most Saturdays, and every one of them was in that room because it was a Saturday afternoon and the room was warm.

Mrs Obi was at the front with the pad shut on her knee and did not open it. The man in his sixties, who is a building estimator, was sitting down, which is unusual. The young woman of about twenty. The man of about eighteen, who washes up in a kitchen at the back of the parade, four nights and a Friday. The woman of about fifty-five, whose corridor was emptied on Saturday morning at eight and who had spent the morning moving two bags and a box of books into a room over a shop at the end of the parade and would spend the next five nights there. The woman who has been in that hall about two years. The man from the parade, who had not brought anything. And a man in his forties who came for the last hour of it and had not brought anything and said so when he sat down, on the grounds that it was the last time anybody was going to be told that.

They talked about a sign coming down, a price of glass, a boiler in the Methodist hall on Carrow Lane that anybody could have had for nothing since about February, and a man in the third house in Corrance Lane whose roof had come off in a wind in April and was still off, and which had now been off for five months and had become, in the words of the man from the parade, "not a roof any more, it's a hobby."

At about twenty past four the woman of about fifty-five said, to the woman she was sitting beside, in the voice she uses, and not to the room:

"It's the register, isn't it. That's still the one that'll get proved."

"Course it is," said the woman beside her. "I heard it off a fella off a lorry in July and I've heard it since. Old one. Borough's own."

"That's the one. Yes."

She said it the way you confirm something you have had from somebody else and not thought about since. Nobody at that table heard either sentence, or heard both and did not join in, and the man in his sixties was six feet away with a cup of tea and did not look up, and the room went straight back to the price of glass, and that is how a wrong thing stays ordinary.

---

At about half past four the man from the parade put his hand up and asked the room, in good faith, whether anybody had ever found out who prints them.

Nobody had.

"Ask him," said the man from the parade, and nodded at the man of about eighteen, who was against the wall with a cup he had not drunk from. "He asked that in June. It went over the water. I heard about it after."

"You asked it."

"I asked it on the back of a docket and a boatman took it across for me in a wallet and he put it in his pocket," said the man of about eighteen, "and I haven't got a copy of it and I haven't got the docket and I haven't got a number and I haven't got a name and I haven't got an address, and it went out in a brown card wallet with a great many dockets in it and it is on the other side of that water in a box, and if you threw that wallet in the river in October the question would be gone, and it isn't answered, and it is not going to be answered by anybody in this room, and that's not a complaint, that's just the position of it."

The young woman of about twenty said, quietly, "There's still nothing."

"There's still nothing," he said.

At about twenty to five most of it went out into the parade the ordinary way and about four stayed, and the woman of about fifty-five went first because she had a bag and a key to a room over a shop, and the man in his sixties went at five with the tube under his arm, and the hall was empty by ten to six except for the radiator in that corridor, which has been out since about 1998 and was still out.

**AND THE WOMAN WITH THE KEY TURNED THE GAS OFF AT THE METER BY THE DOOR AND LOCKED UP, AND A MAN WALKED UP PEVERELL STREET AT HALF PAST SIX WITH A JUMPER OVER HIS ARM AND A PIECE OF PAPER IN HIS COAT THAT NOBODY HAD ASKED HIM FOR, AND THE PUMP STOOD IN THE DOORWAY OF NUMBER FORTY-ONE WITH THE STOPCOCK OFF, AND AT THE TOP OF THE STREET A WOMAN CARRYING A BAG WALKED PAST THE ONLY SHOP THAT WAS STILL OPEN AND THE ONLY THING HE ASKED HER WAS WHETHER IT WAS RAINING.**
