# Chapter 0711 — The Gates Tried Against The Posts

Saturday the nineteenth of March 2022, in this borough. He is at a cottage lane with posts in the ground from late morning until dark and the whole of the day is a pair of gates tried and wanted hands.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES TRIES A PAIR OF OAK GATES AGAINST POSTS ALREADY IN THE GROUND AT A COTTAGE, FINDS THE LATCH STILE WANTS A PAIR OF HANDS AND A LADDER, AND SPEAKS TO THE MAN WHO HAS PAID ABOUT WATER FROM A TAP AND ABOUT WAITING.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is at a cottage lane all day. The man who has paid is at the cottage at the middle of the day. He speaks to him in the lane.

---

He gets to the cottage lane in the van in late morning with the gates on the back.

The posts are already in the ground where he set them in the summer, true and square, concrete firm, caps bedded, with the caps on. The pair of oak gates are on the back of the van with a number chalked on the back of each, pale figures on dark grain. He unloads them on his own, one at a time, back straight, knees bent, and leans them against the low wall, sacking between. The shoebox with the element in it is on the top of the bench in the unit behind him. The drawer under the bench is shut with the brush in the bag inside it. The stranger brush is in the footwell. He has left all three where they are and brought the rule and the square and the pencil behind his ear.

The scrap of scribing card is in his shirt pocket with twelve on one side in his own writing and room on the other side. The letting agent card is in the same pocket with the boy on the shirt on it since the sixth of October, not rung. He takes the scrap out and looks at the twelve and puts it back. He has not written on the other side of it.

---

He tries the gates against the posts from late morning to the middle of the day without hanging a thing.

He lifts the first gate to the posts on his own and props it with his knee and sights it, shoulder in, breath slow. Post to post, latch stile to drop bolt, it sits true enough to show what it would be, shadow line straight from post to gate. He tries the second the same way, knee in, eye along the top. Both want a pair of hands, one to keep the gate true and one to mark the hinges, hands that know oak and keep still. The latch stile wants a pair of hands on a ladder, because the top hinge cannot be marked from a stool and the stool he built will not do it, legs short, top low.

He puts the gates back against the wall and wipes his hands on his trousers, oak dust on denim, and sights along the wall coping to rest his eye. The top run in his own unit sits in his head, a quarter of an inch out at the top over six foot, shadow line at the cornice. Packing it wants hands he does not have. Hanging these wants hands he does not have. Both wait where they were not meant to stay.

---

The man who has paid comes at the middle of the day with a dog on a lead.

He looks at the gates against the wall and at the posts in the ground.

"Not hung," he says.

"Not hung," he says. "Tried and not hung. They sit true enough to show. They want hands to hang."

The man nods and lets the dog sniff round the posts.

"You said the spring," he says.

"Aye," he says, and puts no day on it, because naming one would mean asking for hands, and asking would mean saying the job out loud in a cold lane.

---

They stand in the lane with their hands in their pockets and look at the gates.

"That one is longer," the man says, nodding at the chalked numbers.

"By a hair," he says. "Posts true. Gates true. Lane slopes. It will sit when it hangs."

He stands with the rule against his leg and thinks about water. A bowl under a bath tap at number twelve, emptied at first light and at dark, and he saw it in July when he was in that house and said nothing about it then. A cistern that weeps at the far side of a joist in a roof space nobody has entered since the spring. A kettle with a furred spout in a house he has never been in. Three houses, three small leaks, and he is standing in a lane with a rule in his hand and a gate that will not go up. None of them is his to stop.

---

"What would you do about a drip," he says.

"What drip."

"Bath tap. At number twelve. Drips into a white bowl since before Christmas. Bowl emptied at first light and at dark."

The man looks at him.

"Mend it."

"Aye. And who keeps the frame while she goes up the fixed four rungs beside the hatch. And who sits with the bowl while the tap is off. There is nobody. So the water keeps coming."

The man lets that sit.

"Same as this," he says at last, nodding at the gates. "Hands wanted and nobody to ask."

---

He walks the lane at the end of the day with the rule in his hand.

The lane is narrow with a low wall either side and the posts standing true in the ground where he set them. He walks post to post, counting his paces heel to toe, and sights along the tops, eye low. True and square with the caps on, shadow straight. The gates would sit well if hands hung them, weight true on hinges, latch meeting keep. He lays his palm flat on the top of one post and feels the cap cold. The dog marks show in the damp where the payer dog went round. He wipes a smear of mud off the cap with his thumb.

He thinks about the ladder while he walks, folded under a window in a front room. Four treads. Tray on top. Tape round the second rail. Chalk on the third tread. Lent on the twenty-ninth of November without taking a number. Left in a porch on the third of December with no note. Now squared under a window since the seventeenth of December. He could have asked for the number. He did not. The not-asking cost a porch and a rain and a road and a room.

---

He eats in the cab with the doors open to the lane.

Bread and cheese on his knee with the scrap of scribing card on the dash, twelve up. He looks at the twelve while he eats, his own hand plain in pencil. The letting agent card sits with it, boy on a shirt, since the sixth of October, corners soft, not rung. He has said nothing about how much since the sixteenth of July. What he decided in four seconds is still right. He puts the scrap back in his pocket and the pencil behind his ear and does not write.

The man who has paid comes back down the lane with the empty lead in his hand.

"Hung soon," he says. It is not a question.

"Hung when hands hang them," he says. "Gates true. Posts true. Lane slopes. It will sit."

---

He loads the gates back on the van at the end of the day on his own, one at a time.

The man who has paid watches without offering, because offering would mean saying a price, and the price was fixed in the summer and paid and wants the gates by the spring. He props the gates true on the back with sacking between them and ties them down.

"You lent your ladder out," the man says.

"Aye. Twenty-ninth of November. Left in an open porch on the third of December with no note on it. Now folded under a window in a front room. Tape round the second rail. Chalk on the third tread."

"Ask for it back."

"I cannot ask whose it is without saying I lent it without taking his number. I cannot put back what I cannot ask for. Same as I cannot write his number on the other side of my card without knowing it."

He takes the pencil from behind his ear and puts it back behind his ear. He does not write on the scrap.

---

He checks the van at the end of the day with the gates tied.

The sacking sits square between the gates, rope tight, knots flat. He lays his palm on the top gate and feels the oak steady under it. The stranger brush sits in the footwell in the canvas bag, stone and leather at the bottom. He does not move it. The bolster sits under the bench in the unit with the leads and the screws. The drawer sits shut under the bench with the brush in the bag in it. All three wait.

He takes the scrap out once more in the lane light. Twelve on one side. Room on the other. The pencil comes from behind his ear and goes back behind his ear. Nothing written. Writing nothing is a thing he has done since October, and doing it keeps the twelve true.

---

He walks the cottage garden at the end of the day with the payer dog at his heel.

The garden is square with a low wall and a bed dug along it. He walks the bed line, boot to boot, looking at the soil. The dog sniffs round the posts and sits. The gates lie tied on the van in the lane. The posts stand true in the ground. All three wait for hands.

"Good soil," he says.

"Good soil," the man says. "Gates first. Soil after."

They laugh at that, short and low, breath showing. The pencil sits behind his ear. The scrap sits in his pocket. The rule sits in his hand. Nothing written. The ladder sits folded under a window on the other side of the borough. The bowl fills beside it. Both wait.

---

At dark he drives back to the unit with the gates on the back and unloads them on his own and leans them against the back wall where they were.

The run along the back wall is a quarter of an inch out at the top and will be a quarter of an inch out at first light. The gates are leaning and will be leaning. The bowl is filling and will be full. He sits in the cab with the engine off and takes the scrap out and looks at the twelve on it. There is room on the other side of it for a number and a street. He puts it back and does not write on it, and the water keeps coming, round and white in a cold bathroom, waiting where it was not meant to stay.
