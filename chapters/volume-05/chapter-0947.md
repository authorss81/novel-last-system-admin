# Chapter 0947 — Behind The Standing Slate

Friday the eighteenth of October 2024, at the lean-to roof at number twelve with the light off the wall below it. Behind a standing slate.

**THE LOWEST COURSE ON THE LEAN-TO AT NUMBER TWELVE HAS COME AWAY FROM ITS BATTEN AT THE HEAD AND STANDS A HALF INCH OFF IT ALONG MOST OF ITS LENGTH, AND THE TIPS OF TWO FINGERS GO INTO THE OPENING BEHIND THAT SLATE AND WRING AGAINST ONE ANOTHER, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE OPENING GOES AND WHAT IS BEHIND IT.**

The lean-to is four feet of roof over the back door, laid on battens and slates about forty years ago by a man who did it in a day and a half and used slates off a roof that had come down in the street. The outer edge of it is at the height of a woman's head, so the lowest course can be reached from the flags without anything to stand on.

The course has not been lifted or reset. Two of the slates along it sit true where their nails are fast, and between them and along the rest of it the heads have come away from the battens, and the batten behind each head is bare along the whole run as a strip of uncovered timber.

There is a line of pale grit along the flags under the outer edge where the rain comes off the slates and drops. And the grit has been swept into the corner at the left-hand end and left in a ridge all the way along the front.

A slate is laid head up the slope and tail down it, and the head of it lies over the front face of the batten while the tail of the slate below hangs down behind that batten. The two do not touch, because the tail of the slate below is hung on the batten beneath and there is a finger of air between the head of one slate and the tail of the one under it, which is what lets a roof breathe.

Two fingertips go into the opening and wring against one another, the first against the second. And the space opens along its own length rather than across it, because a slate's head lifts where the nail is loose and stays down where the nail is still in.

The opening is half an inch at the middle of the course. It closes to nothing at the third slate from the left, where the nail is fast and the head of the slate lies flat on the batten as it was struck. It opens again a foot further along and stays open for the rest of the run to the far corner of the lean-to.

Behind the slate's head is the batten, which is sawn softwood about an inch and a half by three, laid flat against the sarking and nailed through into it at its own intervals. Above the batten and behind it are the sarking boards, board laid on board with a layer of old felt between the two.

The whole of that assembly is a space of about an inch between the back of the batten and the front of the sarking. And it runs the four feet of the roof and then turns up the slope at the far corner.

Where a piece of lead has been dressed behind a double lap of slate and has gone dull and hard as lead does. A batten is not fixed to the sarking over its whole length. It is fixed at a few points along it, and between those points it stands off by the thickness of a nail head and no more, and the two lines of gap under a batten run parallel the whole way and meet nowhere.

---

A slate is kept on by one nail through its head and nothing else, and the nail is the whole of the fix. It goes in through slate two hundredths of an inch thick and into softwood, and it rusts at the shank where it is inside the slate, and the rust works the hole out rather than working the nail out.

Once a nail has begun to move in a hole it does not stop moving, because every time it moves a little the hole gets a little wider and the head of the nail moves with it. A slate with a loose head does not fall off a roof. It lifts about the thickness of a coin and stays there, catching the wind at the overlap below it and letting water in behind itself.

That is exactly what is happening here, and it has happened long enough for the wind at the head of the lowest course to have put a polish on the top of the battens along the whole of that run. A batten with a shine along its top is a batten that has had something moving over it in the weather for a long time.

The slates are not old ones. They are a hard blue and they are brittle through their whole thickness in a way that timber is not, and the slate at the far corner has lost a piece off its own arris where it was turned round that corner and back again and cut to fit.

They are second-hand slates, every one of them, taken off a roof that had come down, and each carries the two nail holes of its own length plus a third where a fitter went through in a place that had already cracked. Those holes are why the new nail has so little to bite on, and they are not a fault in this roof but in the one it came from.

The nails in the course are of the older kind, cut from a length of rod and square in the shank rather than round. They are brown along their whole length, and one of them has lost so much of its diameter that there is daylight between its head and the slate, and that head crumbles under the pad of a finger like something made of sand.

---

What carries all of that is a wall plate at each end and three rafters between them, and the feet of the rafters are cut and nailed onto the plate where the plate lies on the course of the wall. Nothing about the run of battens along the eave is fixed to anything except the sarking, and the sarking is fixed to the rafters, and the rafters have not moved in forty years and do not move now.

There is a wasps' nest in the far corner of the void behind the battens, made of paper and going grey. And it is as old as the roof over it and no newer, and it is fixed to the sarking and not to anything that can be shaken, and it has been there long enough for the paper to have lost its colour entirely.

Her sister comes out of the back door onto the step and away across the yard towards the back alley. With her sleeves down and her hands empty, and she goes past the end of the lean-to without lifting her head for it.

They get as far as the gate before either of them says anything, and what they say is whether the school has broken up for the week, and whether the buses run to the earlier time now that it has, and whether the earlier time is any earlier than it was in the summer. Then she goes on out of the gate and the yard is empty again, and the fingers had come out of the opening before the back door was open behind her.

The opening along the head of that course is where it was, half an inch for six feet of it and shut at the third slate from the left. Behind it the battens are bare along the top where the wind has been at them, smooth there and not brittle anywhere.

Along that low course the heads have come off their battens for six feet and shut again at the third slate, and what is behind the loose ones is sawn timber and then a foot of nothing with a wasps' nest going grey in the far corner of it and no light reaching it since the spring, granting that nobody comes back to it with a hammer in a pocket.
