# Chapter 0650 — A Gate On Main Street

Monday the twenty-eighth of June 2021, in this borough. He is at a bench in a unit off the industrial estate at about eight in the morning and in a house off the parade at about twenty to five in the afternoon, and he is at the end of a road off the parade at about a quarter past six, and the whole of it is a shoebox under a stack of offcuts and ninety minutes easing two gates off their pins for nothing.

**A MAN WHO HAS HAD AN ELEMENT IN THE BACK OF HIS VAN SINCE A WEDNESDAY IN JUNE DRIVES PAST THE END OF THAT ROAD AT A QUARTER PAST SIX IN THE EVENING AND DOES NOT STOP, AND SPENDS NINETY MINUTES BEFORE THAT EASING TWO GATES OFF THEIR PINS FOR TWO STRANGERS WITHOUT CHARGING FOR IT, AND CANNOT WORK OUT WHY ONE OF THOSE IS EASIER THAN THE OTHER, AND DOES NOT RING THE ONE WOMAN IN THIS BOROUGH WHO HE COULD RING.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is on his own all day. There is nobody on that Monday he talks to for more than four minutes.

---

Here is the shoebox, and it has been under a stack of offcuts in the back of that van since the Wednesday in June, and the reason it is there is that he was in that kitchen for the best part of an hour on a Wednesday in May with his back to that cooker, and he clocked the cooker, and the cooker is twenty years old and the left front element on that model is the first thing that goes.

Nobody has told him anything. Nobody has rung him. He went up that road that Wednesday with a shoebox that was in the van for a job that has not happened yet, and he took it out of that job about ten seconds after he came away, and he has not thought about it since.

That last part is not true.

He has taken it out twice.

Both times were in a car park and both times were without any reason at all, and both times he looked at the box and read the one letter he has written on the side of it and put it back.

---

And here is the morning.

He is at the bench at about eight and he is doing a run of four and the fourth one is the run that pays for the other three, and this is a thing he has worked out over about a decade and it is the only thing anybody has taught him that is worth anything.

The first one is a cupboard. The second is a worktop. The third is a man who wants a staircase moved and has not moved it and will not move it and wants him to look at it.

He does not do anything for the third one. He spends about ten minutes on it, drinks the tea, says two things that are true, tells the man what it will cost and what it will not achieve, and takes the money for the visit, and drives away.

And that is a good morning and he is in a good mood at about ten past twelve and he has never once in about ten years thought about doing the first two for nothing in order to be in a house on the third.

---

And then he says out loud in the unit, at about twelve, to nobody, the sentence that decides what kind of Monday it is going to be.

He says, "Get on with it."

He means the element. He has been saying that to himself since the Tuesday and he says it about forty times a day about a dozen different things, and it is the way he works, and it works.

And it does not work on this.

---

Because the thing he has to do is not the element. The element is in a box and he can ring that woman any day he likes — except he cannot, because of a bench on a parade in May, and he has not let himself think about that bench since the Monday he was sitting on it, and a man who lets himself not think about something for five weeks has not stopped thinking about it, he has got it in a box of his own.

And the thing he has to do is a different thing entirely and he cannot even name it, and here it is.

Somebody in that house has written down six things and put them in the wrong order.

---

He knows what a wrong order looks like. He has been on about fifteen hundred kitchens in this borough and a great many of them have had a list on the fridge with a magnet and a date on it and the wrong order on it, and the wrong order is always the same wrong order. It is frightened order. Cheap thing first, expensive thing last, and the expensive thing that is going to hurt you is written in the middle where it looks like it is being taken care of.

He has seen that list on about four hundred fridges.

He has never once seen it written in pencil on the back of a gas envelope with the gate at the top.

---

Here is the afternoon, and it is the fourth job, and the fourth job is a gate.

A pair of gates, wrought iron, heavy, hung on two piers on pins, and one leaf has dropped on its pin and is dragging on the cobbles, and the man wants it up because his wife has said she is not going out through that again.

And he says yes before he has looked at it properly, which he does not do, and then he looks, and the pins have rusted to the piers and the mortar round one of them has gone soft at the top, and he puts a screwdriver blade in the joint and it goes in about two inches, which is the answer to everything.

And he says, "That's not two hours. Easing them on the pins is two hours and I'll do it now for nothing. The piers are going, though, and one day a pin is going to pull straight out of the mortar and you'll have a gate in the middle of your road. That's a day and a half and a Saturday and four new plates, and you'll want it doing before the winter."

And the man says, "Can you not just put them back."

And he says, "I can take the weight off them this afternoon and they'll swing for a year. What I can't do in a morning is make the piers good, and if I let it lie I'd be doing you a disservice, so I'll write it down for you and you can ring me about it."

And the man says, "Go on then."

And he says, "Right."

---

And it takes him ninety minutes and it is the best work he does all week.

And here is what goes on in his head for ninety minutes, and he is not able to stop it, and this is a man who does not talk to himself while he works and has not since he was twenty-two.

Somebody wrote *the gate* at the top of a list in pencil and did not ask anybody to do it and rubbed it out and wrote it again.

And he is in a house easing two gates off their pins for a man whose wife will not go out through the door, and he is not being paid for it, and he has not told them he is not, and they have not asked.

And that is not the same. He knows that is not the same, in the middle of a job, with a piece of chalk on his thumb, at about twenty to five in the afternoon.

---

He drives off at about half past five.

And the road off the parade is on the way. It is not on the way home. It is half a mile out of his way and he takes the half mile, and he does not decide to take it, which is the thing he will not be able to explain to anybody.

---

Here is what that road looks like at about six in the evening on the twenty-eighth of June, and he has now seen it three times from the top of it.

The render goes warm at that hour and the two gates that need easing throw long shadows across the path, and there is a light on in a kitchen at number nine at half past six in the evening, and there is nothing else happening anywhere on it.

He slows down.

He does not slow down. He comes off the ring road at the junction at about a quarter past six and he goes along the row past the parade and he goes straight on, and there is no moment in that in which anybody could say he nearly stopped.

---

And the reason he does not go up there, and he knows this while he is doing it, and he has known it since the Wednesday of the tenth of June.

It is not the promise. The promise is a chair and a stool and a front room and it has got nothing to do with a hotplate.

It is that he has nothing in his hands.

If he goes up that road with a shoebox under his arm then he has admitted that he read six things off a kitchen table, and he will be asked why he read them, and he does not have an answer that is not about a gate.

And if he goes up that road with nothing in his hands, then he is a man in a van who has driven half a mile out of his way to look at a house, and there is no such job.

And he cannot do that to a woman of seventy-eight. That is the thing. He cannot arrive at number twelve with nothing in his hands and let her spend the rest of the evening working out why a man came.

---

So he does not go up there, and there is a pair of gates on the far side of this borough hanging properly, and there is a shoebox under a stack of offcuts in a van that has been standing in a yard since about eight o'clock.

---

And here is the second thing he does not do on the Monday the twenty-eighth of June, and it is the one that matters.

He does not ring the woman in her late forties.

He has her number. He has had it since the Friday in May when he gave her a card out of the blanket at number twelve, and she rang him off it, and they sat on a bench on the parade for the best part of an hour and agreed not to put their two heads together in that woman's front room.

He has thought about ringing her about four times since the Wednesday.

Once about the element. Once about a bench. Once about a bulb that took four minutes. Once, in a car park on the Thursday before last, about nothing at all, when he had the phone in his hand before he had got the number up.

---

And what stops him is not the promise.

What stops him is this.

If she has been up there, then he is a man who got a piece of paper off a table and did nothing about it, and he will have to say so, to her, on the telephone, in a car park.

And if she has not been up there, then there is nothing to ring about.

And he cannot ask which, because asking which is asking her to tell him where she stands, and that is a bench thing, and a bench thing is a thing the two of them agreed to.

---

He gets in at about ten past seven and he does his tea and then he goes back down the yard to the unit to get the last of the offcuts out of the machine and stop it running for the night.

And the shoebox is under them.

---

And here is the last thing about the Monday the twenty-eighth of June, and it is small, and it is the only part of that Monday that would cost anybody anything if it were said out loud.

He stands in the doorway of that unit a few minutes later with the machine off and the light off and the box in his hand, and he thinks a sentence.

He thinks: *if I do not put it in on Wednesday I am not putting it in.*

And he knows while he is thinking it that the next one is not tomorrow, and that it is a Wednesday, and that a man who counts his own Wednesdays is a man who has already decided and is doing something else.

And he puts the box on the top of the bench, in the clear, at the end where the light comes in the door in the morning, where he cannot come in without seeing it.

---

**A MAN HAD AN ELEMENT IN HIS VAN FOR THE BEST PART OF A MONTH AND EASED TWO GATES FOR TWO STRANGERS FOR NINETY MINUTES FOR NOTHING AND DROVE PAST THE END OF THAT ROAD AT A QUARTER PAST SIX WITHOUT SLOWING DOWN, AND COULD NOT SAY WHY A GATE NOBODY ASKED HIM FOR WAS EASIER THAN A GATE SOMEONE DID, AND DID NOT RING THE ONE PERSON WHO WOULD HAVE ANSWERED EITHER QUESTION, AND PUT A BOX ON THE TOP OF A BENCH WHERE HE WILL HAVE TO LOOK AT IT IN THE MORNING.**

(End of chapter 0650)
