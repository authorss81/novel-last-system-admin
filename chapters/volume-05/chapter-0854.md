# Chapter 0854 — The Channel Under The Sash

Sunday the twenty-second of October 2023, at the front window of number twelve from the first of the light until the light has gone off the flags, and the sash channels brushed out from outside is the whole of that Sunday.

**THE SASH CHANNELS OF THE FRONT WINDOW AT NUMBER TWELVE ARE PACKED WITH THE DUST AND GRIT OF THE SUMMER AND SHE MEANS TO RAISE THE LOWER SASH AND BRUSH THEM OUT FROM OUTSIDE WITHOUT REMOVING THE SASH, AND HER SISTER COMES OUT OF THE FRONT DOOR AND STANDS ON THE STEP LOOKING UP AT THE WINDOW AND GOES BACK IN.**

That window is the front window at number twelve, on the wall away from the frost wall, ground floor, and it is a wooden sash with two sashes and a weather bar across the bottom of the upper sash where it comes down to sit on the lower one.

The channels are the grooves the sashes run in. Two down the outside faces of the frame, a channel each side, and a third across the bottom above the lower sash where the upper sash drops into, and the fourth is the shallow trough behind the weather bar at the foot of that upper sash, and it is where the water of a whole winter has to go.

Every one of them is full.

They have packed through a summer with the dust off the path and the grit off the gate, and the grit in them is the sort that gets ground along the channel by a sash coming down two hundred times, and a channel with grit in it does not let a sash past, it just takes the paint off it on the way.

She does not take a sash out.

Taking a sash out of a frame is the cords and the weights and the whole of the weight of the thing coming down into the room, and it is two pairs of hands, and it is that window open and unglazed from November to March. Nobody takes a sash out in October for dust.

What you do is raise the lower sash on its two nails and work at the channels from outside, and the lower sash of this one weighs about as much as a full bucket and goes up on the nails until they take it and stops there.

She ties it up with the loop of blue string off the heap of edging stones at the gate, round the rail and out to the eye in the frame, and the knots she does are the knots she has done for fifty years and take about four seconds.

With the lower sash up she can reach everything on that window from the ground. Two channels down the sides, the trough behind the weather bar, and the whole of the groove the upper sash drops into.

She starts on the outside faces, a soft brush down each from the top, so the loose dust comes off the paint and not into the groove. It comes off in a thin grey film and floats, and she wipes it away with a cloth as she goes because there is a sill under that window and the sill belongs to nobody.

Then the brush goes into the channels.

On the sides it comes away as dust and a run of small grey stones that have been sitting in the bottom of the groove since the summer, and each one is the size of a pin head and is harder than the wood it lies in and has been in there since June, going up and down twice a day.

She works down the full height of each one and the dirt that has welded itself to the wood in a place comes out in a piece, and she leaves the piece on the sill rather than put it back where it was.

The trough behind the weather bar is the worst of them. It is where the water of the last rain went and stood and dried, and what is in it has the grey of silt with the grit through it and is hard as a biscuit right along the back of the bar. The corner of a card gets under it and it lifts off in slabs about the size of a postage stamp, and behind it the groove is clean and pale and the whole length of the bar stands on it again.

She does not touch the weather bar itself. She cleans under it and along it and leaves the paint on it exactly as it is, because the bar's job is to throw the water down the outside of the lower sash rather than back into the room, and that job is done by the paint on it and not by any amount of brushing.

Her sister comes out of the front door in the middle of the day and stands on the step and looks up at the window for as long as it takes to work out what is being done to it, and then goes back in, and the door is shut behind her.

There is one place on that window she does not go near.

On the bottom rail of the lower sash, in the middle, where the hands of everybody who has ever opened this window have gone, the paint is gone and the wood is bare and smooth. It is not a scuff and it is not damage. It is the only part of that window anybody has touched, and it will be there for as long as there is a window to open, and if anybody sands it back to the paint line on it and paints over it, then the one record anybody keeps of who has used this house is gone.

She leaves it. She does not touch it with a cloth either, and she does not put anything on it to make it look like the rest of the rail.

She brings the sash down off its nails when the trough is done, onto the channels she has cleaned, and it comes down without catching anywhere along the way, which is not how it has gone down since the spring.

She puts it up again and lets it down again twice, to be sure of it, and then unties the string and puts the sash on its nails to stay where it is.

She thinks about the sash while she is tying it up, about how a sash has three sets of moving edges and each of them runs in a groove about a quarter of an inch wide, and that everything which has ever gone wrong with a wooden window has gone wrong in one of those three grooves, and none of it has ever been the timber.

---

The brush and the cloth go in the shed, and the loop of blue string goes back over the heap of edging stones at the gate.

She stands on the flags at the end when the light has gone off them and looks up at what is there. A sash down in its old place with the channels clean on both sides and behind the weather bar, and the middle of the bottom rail worn back to the wood.
