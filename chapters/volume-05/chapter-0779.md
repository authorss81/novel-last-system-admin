# Chapter 0779 — The Bay In The Side Passage

Friday the sixteenth of December 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house and yard at number twelve from the morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is four stones of a bay relaid on a thicker bed, and the bay standing a hand's width proud of the passage.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS A PAVING STONE ROCKING IN HER SIDE PASSAGE AND MEANS TO LIFT IT AND RELAY IT ON SAND, HER SISTER TAKES THE WHOLE BAY UP AND RELAYS IT ON A THICKER BED, AND THE BAY STANDS A HAND'S WIDTH PROUD OF THE PASSAGE AND EVERYTHING THAT USED TO RUN ACROSS IT STOPS AT IT.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight at number twelve on Friday. Her sister knocks at the front door in the morning, and they go out through the house into the passage.

---

There is a fire in the stove and she has the green chair up to the table. Square with the edge of the table on her right is the gas envelope, face up, and she does not go near it. The taped box is on the far side of the table under its cloth. The padlock on the coal shed is open in the hasp and the shed door is shut, and the gate stands propped back against the wall. The kitchen sash is a hand's width down in its own frame and she does not go into the kitchen this morning. Out of the back window the heap of cut grass is banked against the shed wall with the stripe it brought up the brick gone the colour of a cold cup of tea. The coping on the yard wall is capped for three feet above the heap and the four feet at the coal shed end has nothing on it. The brush across the runner is the only thing in the passage she walks over.

She goes in and out past the brush lying across the runner and steps over it, and it has lain there since before she came. The passage runs from the yard door to the front of the house, with the wall of the house down one side and the yard wall down the other, paved with six old hard blue stones walked on for longer than she has lived in the house.

The fourth one from the yard door end rocks. There is a dark line round three sides of it where it has moved, and the grit that comes out of the yard door sits against its yard door end, and it lifts a coin's thickness at the near corner when she puts her heel on it.

Her plan is the stone. She means to get a bar in under it, take out whatever is under it, and put it back on a bed of sand kept in a heap by the coal shed for this, and it will stop rocking, because a stone on sand and a stone on nothing do not rock.

Her sister knocks at the front door in the morning.

---

"There is one of them rocking."

"There is one of them rocking and I am going to lift it and put it back on sand."

"On sand." Her sister stands in the passage in her coat. "Which one."

"The fourth one."

"And how deep is the sand."

"About as deep as my hand."

Her sister looks at the four stones from the near end without touching any of them.

"Put your hand on that one."

---

She puts her hand on the fifth stone and her sister puts her hand on the sixth, and she goes back to the fourth, and all three go down about the same when you lean on them.

"You cannot feel that with your heel. You can only feel it with the side of your hand, because it is not the stone that is rocking. It is the bed under it."

"It rocks."

"It rocks because there is nothing under that stone to rock on. It has been bedded on nothing since it went down and it has had forty years of boots and a barrow across it." Her sister takes her hand off and rubs her fingers together. "Now put your hand on the third one."

The third one is different. It does not go down at all. It is high and hard, and about as deep in the passage as the stones either side of it.

"That is the bed, and it is about twice as deep as a hand. And you have got a stone with no bed at all, a stone on a bed twice as deep as a hand, and two stones in between that are somewhere in between." Her sister looks towards the yard door. "Put the fourth one back on a hand's depth of sand and it will sit up above the third and the fifth, and in a year its edge will go and it will rock again, and this time you will blame the sand."

---

They take the whole bay up. That is four stones, the third, the fourth, the fifth and the sixth from the yard door end, and her sister means it, and it is argued for ten minutes at the house end with the two of them looking at the bay and nothing touching.

"You are taking up four."

"I am taking up the bay. A bay is the ground under one patch of it, and if you take up one stone you will be back with this in four years." Her sister kneels and puts the back of her thumbnail into the joint between the third and the fourth. "And the bed under that bay is a bed. It does not go down to the foundations and never did. It is rubble and lime put the thickness of your hand, and it has washed out from under one stone and then the next, because that is what a bed does when it is in the path of the water out of a yard door."

"Then how deep do we go."

"As deep as the good stone and no further, because that is the floor of this passage and the floor everything in this house stands on."

---

It takes the best part of the morning. They get the stones up with a bar and a lump of rubber and a piece of wood. Underneath there is a bed about the depth of a hand, rubble and lime putty gone to sand, washed out from the middle, and the hollow under the fourth stone is as deep as her wrist with a lip on the near side of it where the rubble stopped moving. Her sister gets it out with a bar and her hands until she is down to hard stuff.

Then they put the bed back thicker.

She has stone rubble off the coal shed gutter in a heap and sand by the shed and the gravel that came out of the same bucket as the topsoil last spring. Her sister mixes the rubble, the gravel and the sand on the flags and puts it in with a shovel and packs it down with the back of the handle until the bed stands a hand and a bit proud of the bed the bay was on.

That is the whole of it. It is a better bed than the one the bay had when it was laid and there is no mortar in it anywhere. They go back down it with the back of the shovel and then with a piece of wood and put the four stones back in the order they came out in.

---

It looks like a passage. The four stones are level with each other and level with the two stones beyond the bay, there is not a hollow under any of them, and the joints are tight.

Her sister gets the yard door open wide, stands at that end of the passage with her arms folded and looks down the length of it.

"That is a good bay."

"And you can walk down it."

She walks down it. There is nothing wrong with it and it is a passage between two walls with a yard door at one end and the front of the house at the other.

Then her sister steps aside so she can see the whole length at once, and she sees it.

---

The bay stands proud.

It is not a step anybody would trip over. You would walk over it. But from the yard door end the whole four stones stand a hand's width higher than the two stones before them, the joints are a hand's width deeper, and it goes across the passage like a low sill.

Her sister stands behind her and says nothing.

"It was not meant to be up."

"No. But you made the bed thicker."

"I made the bed thicker and did not take enough out of the ground under it to put the top back where it was." Her sister comes and stands beside her. "That is my doing and I will not argue it. I thought I was putting a deeper bed in under the same top. A deeper bed under the same top is a step. You cannot put more under a thing and have the thing stay where it was."

"You could have taken more out."

"I could have, and then I would have been under the floor of this passage, and you do not dig that out with a shovel."

---

They stand at the yard door end and watch it do what it does.

There is grit coming out of that yard door every time the door moves and every time the wind gets up in the yard, and it came out across the bay, because that was the low place. Now the bay is the high place.

The grit comes out of the yard door, runs forward about a foot and a half and stops. It lies against the face of the bay along its whole length in a line about as thick as your finger, and it does not go over it and it does not go round it.

She gets the yard door and moves it again, and the grit goes the same place and stops in the same place, and it builds into a ridge along the foot of the bay that is two days old in the making and will be the whole length of the passage by the spring.

Her sister does not suggest taking any of it up.
