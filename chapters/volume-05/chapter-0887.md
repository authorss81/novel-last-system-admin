# Chapter 0887 — What Is Under The Joint

Thursday the twenty-second of February 2024, along the front boundary of number nine, from first light until the sun has been on the coping for an hour. The joint where two coping stones meet.

**SHE HAS A BOUNDARY WALL ALONG THE FRONT OF THE GARDEN AT NUMBER NINE WITH A COPING OF LONG STONES BEDDED ALONG THE TOP OF IT, AND SHE MEANS TO LAY ONE HAND ALONG THE JOINT WHERE TWO OF THOSE COPING STONES MEET WITH THE NAILS TURNED DOWN AND FOLLOWING THE JOINT THE WHOLE WAY, AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING PUTS HER BAG DOWN ON THE SILL OF THE FRONT WINDOW.**

The wall runs the whole width of the front and it is a brick wall under a row of coping stones, and the coping is not one length. It is a run of separate stones, each about four feet long, and between them there is a joint every four feet.

There are nine joints along that coping and eight of them are the width of a hair.

She has looked at those eight a good many times in thirty years of looking at that wall, and none of them has ever given her a reason to think about it. They are tight, they are mortared, and they are doing what a joint in a coping is for.

That is worth setting out, because it is why the ninth one is easy to miss. A coping joint that is mortared is carrying its stones. The mortar under the two stones and the mortar in the joint between them are one piece of work, and both were done at the same time by the same pair of hands, and a joint like that behaves like a solid piece of the coping with a crack ruled through it.

So a person walking along that wall from the gate end sees eight joints that are plainly finished and one that is plainly not, and stops at the one, and puts it down as a joint that wants pointing.

The ninth one is a quarter of an inch.

From the path you can see that much from about six feet away, and you can see more than that: the stone above it has a chip out of its top edge on one side, and the chip has been there long enough to have gone the colour of the rest of it.

What a gap like that is made of cannot be had by looking down into it.

The hand goes along the coping with the fingers laid flat and the nails turned down, and the nail of the first finger goes into the joint and follows it down.

The whole of the technique is one sentence. A joint in a coping is narrow at the top and does not get narrower, and it is about five inches deep from the top of the stone to the top of the wall, and a nail of a hand will go down the whole of that if the joint is empty and will stop dead if it is not.

The nail goes in.

It goes down the full width of the joint without touching either side, which says the joint is a clean parallel gap the whole way down, and it keeps going, and after about an inch it comes on something that is not a side and not the bottom.

Cement. Old cement, hard, and the nail rides over it rather than digging into it. The cement goes on for two inches, from an inch down to three inches down, and then the nail drops off it into open joint again and goes on until it comes on stone at about three and a half inches.

So the joint is mortared through the middle of its depth and open for the top inch and open for the bottom inch and a half.

That is the wrong way round, and it took somebody putting a hand on that coping to see it, because from the path a joint like that looks exactly like a joint that has never been pointed.

At the bottom of that joint there is a chock.

It is a piece of stone about three inches long and a finger thick, lying flat in the bottom of the gap, jammed in from one side and tight enough that the two coping stones above it cannot come together without moving it.

And that piece of stone is doing the work of the joint.

The coping along that wall was laid on a wall that has since gone down about half an inch at that point, which is what happens under a joint that sits where two things meet, and the coping was taken up and re-bedded to follow the wall.

But not everywhere. Somebody re-bedded the stones either side of that joint and put the two stones of the joint back with the old height, and then found the two stones standing a finger's width proud of their neighbours at the bottom, with a hole under them.

The chock is what they put in that hole.

It is a piece of the coping itself. The chip out of the top edge of the stone above the joint is the same stone and the same bed, and the chock is what came off it when the stones at the joint were lifted to be re-bedded.

That was not careful work and it was not careless work either. It was a piece of coping done on a cold day by somebody who had a wall that had dropped half an inch and a row of stones that had to come back up to it, and the middle of the run was the easy part because the wall there was still where it was, and the ends were where the wall had moved.

At a third of the way along, the wall under that joint has gone down about half an inch. The brickwork under the joint can be seen to be the oldest work on that wall, and the brickwork a foot either side of it has been repointed twice and this has not, and the line of the coping over the joint runs a very little higher than the line of the coping either side of it, which is how a re-bedded stone shows itself from the path.

So the ninth joint along that coping is doing three things at once. The chock at the bottom carries the two stones of the joint. The mortar in the middle is what is left of the original bedding. And the top inch of that joint is open to the sky and has been since the wall dropped and the coping came back up.

---

The woman who does her shopping comes to the front window from outside in the middle of the morning, puts her bag down on the sill, and stands there.

She did not come for anything in particular. She looked at the bed along the front wall for a while and then she tipped her head back and looked at the coping, and then at the wall under it, and then at the joint where the stone is chipped, and she stood with her hand on the bag on the sill for a bit longer, and then she picked the bag up again and went.

---

That joint will be there for as long as that coping is on that wall.

It cannot widen, because the chock under it is sound stone jammed tight between two stones that are themselves sound, and a chock like that will carry what is above it for as long as the wall carries the coping.

What the open inch and a half at the top of it will do is take rain, and rain into the top of a wall joint in a wall that has a dry side and a garden side runs down the joint until it comes on the chock, and then it stops.

The wall on the garden side of that joint will be wet in that one place in a wet month and dry everywhere else within a foot of it, and a joint that takes water shows itself that way.

The sign is there. About a foot along from the joint, at the foot of the wall on the garden side, there is a piece of brickwork a shade darker than the brick beside it, and it darkens in a wet February and greys off by June, and a line of green comes up in the mortar joint above it and goes away again. The water going into the top of the joint comes down inside the wall on the path it takes through brick, and it comes out at the lowest place it can find, and the lowest place is that foot of the wall a foot along.

Four feet from where a person would be standing to look at it.

That chock will be under that coping long after the chip in the stone above it has weathered away, with the rain getting into the wall above it and stopping at it.
