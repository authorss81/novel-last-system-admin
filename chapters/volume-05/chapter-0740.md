# Chapter 0740 — Seven Things

Friday the eighth of July 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number twelve from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a gas envelope with a list on each side of it and a sister in the kitchen before the day is properly on.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS HER SISTER IN THE KITCHEN OVER A GAS ENVELOPE THAT CARRIES SIX THINGS IN HER OWN PENCIL ON THE BACK OF IT AND SOMEBODY ELSE'S LIST OF ROOMS ON THE FRONT WITH THE TOP CORNER GONE, TAKEN OFF BY TAPE, REFUSES HER SISTER'S OFFER TO COPY THE SIX ONTO A FRESH SHEET WITH A PENCIL BROUGHT FROM HER OWN HOUSE ON THE GROUND THAT A COPY OF SIX THINGS IS SEVEN THINGS AND SHE HAS NEVER BEEN ABLE TO SAY WHICH SIX, REFUSES HER THE SECOND THING AS WELL, AND DOES NOT TURN THE ENVELOPE OVER.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight at number twelve on Friday. Her sister comes to her front door in the morning and is let in and sits with her in the kitchen until the middle of the day.

---

The sister is let in at the front door, which is not on the latch and never has been, and stands in the passage a moment looking at the brush across the runner and steps over it the way everybody in this house steps over it.

In the kitchen the window is up and the green chair is out from the table, and the gas envelope is lying face up on the right-hand side of the table, squared with the edge of it, the pencil side up. On the left is the box with tape round every edge under a cloth that is folded square.

The sister sits down on the plain chair at the other side of the table and puts her handbag down by her feet.

"It is a nice morning," she says.

"It is," she says, and does not go on with it, and her sister does not press it, and they have the kitchen.

---

She reads the six out loud because her sister is sitting at the table and it is a thing you do.

"Gate," she says. "That is done. Front room. That is not done. Window. That is done. Hotplate. That is done. Bath tap. That is done since Monday. Passage. That is done."

"That is five."

"That is five," she says, "and there is not a line through one of them, because there is no pencil in this house and there has not been since before Christmas. The pen in the drawer went in May."

Her sister looks at the envelope lying face up on the table.

"What is the other side," she says.

"I have not looked," she says.

"You have not looked at a piece of paper that lives in your own kitchen."

"I have not looked at the other side of it," she says, "and that is not the same and you will not make it the same. It is face up and the pencil side up and it has been on the right-hand side of this table for months, and everything on this side of that paper is mine."

"Then what is on the other side?"

"Somebody else's list of rooms," she says. "What went in each of them and how much of it, and the top corner of it has gone, taken off with the tape somebody put round the edge of the envelope afterwards. I know there is a list on it and I know what kind of a list it is because a woman told me in this kitchen in the spring when she had it turned over, and I have not put my own eyes on it since, and I am not going to."

"Whose list."

"I do not know," she says. "And I have not asked, and I am not going to start at eight in the morning on a Friday."

---

She put her hand flat on the envelope without moving it, the way you put a hand on a door.

"Why is it on the same paper."

"Because it came that way and because it is not worth a piece of paper of its own," she says. "It was a list of a house. It is not a list any more. The six on this side is a list of things to do and the other side is a list of things that were in a house in a year I do not want to name, and they are not the same list and they never were, and if I turn that over I will have somebody else's handwriting lying next to mine on my own kitchen table and I have managed not to do that for years."

Her sister looked at her for a moment.

"You have turned it over before."

"I have not turned it over," she says. "Not once. And I have had a woman in this kitchen with a pencil in her hand asking me a fortnight ago and it did not help, and I have had a woman in this kitchen without a pencil in her hand telling me the front of it is not mine, and I am going to say the same thing to you that I said to her and you can take it the way you like."

---

Her sister took a folded sheet out of her handbag and a pencil off the top of it and laid them both on the table.

"Then I will copy the six," she says. "Onto a clean sheet. With the pencil. You will have the six in front of you in something you can put a line through."

"No."

"It would take a minute."

"It would take a minute and it would make seven things," she says. "Six on the envelope and six on your sheet and not one line through any of them, and then I would have to say which six are the six, and I have never been able to say which six, because the second one is the front room and it has been the second one since June, and I do not know yet whether it is second because it is the one I cannot start or because it is the one I do not want to start, and the two of those are not the same reason and I cannot tell them apart on a piece of paper."

She stopped there.

"That is a lot of words for a list of six," her sister says.

"It is."

---

Her sister put the pencil down on the table beside the folded sheet.

"Then I will take the second one," she says. "The front room. I will go and get somebody and he will do the front room and it will not be on your envelope and it will not be on my list either, it will just be a room somebody has painted."

"No," she says. "You will not do that."

"I have a man who—"

"You have a man who has done two rooms for me and one of them was a cupboard," she says. "And there is a step ladder folded against the wall under the front room window with tape round the second rail, and it has been there since the December before last, and it is not going anywhere, and there is nowhere in this borough to put a step ladder that is not somebody's outside, and I will not ask anybody to take one away off me."

"That is four things."

"That is four things and they are all true," she says.

---

Her sister picked the pencil up again and put it back in her cardigan pocket and folded the sheet into it.

"They will come back on Thursday," she says, "unless you say different."

"They will come back on Thursday."

"You have never said the second one out loud," her sister says. "You have just said it twice now and you did not know you were doing it."

"No," she says. "I did not."

---

They had the kitchen until the middle of the day and neither of them mentioned the front door, which is pulled to the width of a hand at night, and neither of them mentioned the passage light, which has been burning since June.

At the end of it her sister got up and went round to the left-hand side of the table and lifted the cloth off the box with both hands, which is what she always does, and found what is under it.

Then she put the cloth back and squared it with the edge of the table on three sides the way she had found it, and put her hand flat on it once and took her hand away.

The box has not moved and it is not going to move and neither of them said a word about it.

At the front door the sister said she would say nothing to anybody about anything, and she said it twice, and went off down the road and turned off at the end and was gone.

Then she shut the front door and pulled it to the width of a hand and left it there.

She stood in the kitchen and did not turn the envelope over. It is face up, squared with the edge of the table, the pencil side up, six things on the back and not one of them crossed. She has not looked at the other side since the March before last, and she is not going to look at it now that her sister has been in this kitchen and asked her twice who wrote it, because looking would answer that, and she would rather go on not knowing than find out from a piece of paper lying in her own hand on her own table.
