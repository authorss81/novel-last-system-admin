# Chapter 0831 — The Mend Still In It

Tuesday the twenty-fifth of July 2023, in that office cabin for the whole of that Tuesday, and a window frame that is coming out whole is the whole of it.

**THERE IS A WINDOW FRAME IN THE OFFICE CABIN AT THE UNIT AND HE MEANS TO TAKE IT OUT OF THE WALL WHOLE WITHOUT BREAKING WHAT IS ROUND IT, AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN IS IN THAT YARD AT THE START OF THE DAY AND IS NOT THERE FOR THE FRAME.**

That cabin is timber on a low brick base with a felt roof, and the window is in the side wall looking along the frontage, a wooden frame in a brick opening with a sill below and a lintel above.

The frame carries nothing. That is the whole of why the job is possible at all, and it is worth saying before anything else about it, because a window frame in a brick opening is a thing people look at and imagine taking out and most of them cannot manage. A frame is timber in a hole, and the timber has nothing above it but air inside the reveal. That is why four screws, a knife and two hands can take this one out in an afternoon, and why it will take an afternoon whatever else goes wrong on the way. Above the head of the frame there is a lintel, and the lintel takes the weight of the wall, and the brick either side of the frame goes down to the sill and not to the frame. In the middle of the frame there is nothing but air and glass. Take the frame and the building does not know.

What is round it is the other thing. The outside face of the frame has been painted and rendered and painted again over however many years, and the architrave inside has been painted onto a bead of old paint, and both of those have to come off before the frame can come out or they come off in pieces and take the brick face with them.

In June he squared that frame where it stood, moved a tab three inches along the lintel into sound brick, and cut through the two sides and joined them with two hardwood pieces the width of the frame and four bolts. The leaf has shut without a knock since, with a quarter inch of daylight along the head left open on purpose.

He starts with the leaf out on a blanket on the bench, then the architrave off inside with a thin bar, then the screws out of the tabs.

The architrave comes off in one length down the inside of one side and down the other and across the head, and it is brittle with three coats of paint and it breaks where the head runs down into the side, and that is acceptable because the broken piece is on top and comes away in two. What will not do is putting the bar behind it and levering, because the bar goes into the bead of paint and the bead of paint is in fact a part of the frame and not of the architrave, and the frame has already been squared once this year.

The screws out of the tabs come out whole, four of them, and each has a washer under the head and the washers have gone into the timber a bit and he takes them off with a screwdriver and not with a pair of pliers, because pliers crush the head and a screw with a crushed head is a screw that has to come out with a grinder.

Here is what he has to look at in the middle of the morning with the frame loose in the opening.

The mend comes out with it. The two hardwood pieces and the four bolts are in the sides of the frame where he put them, flush, painted over once, part of the sides now and not plates on top of them. From the front the frame looks like any old frame with a thick side. From the back the bolts show as four round heads with washers under them, sunk square.

That is the stop, and it is not where he thought it would be.

Whether he opens it up again or leaves it. A mend in a frame that is coming out asks for a look at the wood under it, and one kept shut asks to stay shut. He runs a finger along the joint between hardwood and old softwood and feels no lip in it, and he puts a thumb on each bolt head and tries it for movement and there is none.

The joint is where he was afraid of a lip. If the hardwood had stood a hair proud after four months of wet and dry, the paint over the joint would have gone on it and shown as a ridge you could find with a fingernail from across the room, and the whole mend would have announced itself. It has not. He runs it again with a thumbnail from the inside and from the outside and there is the same nothing there in both directions, which is what a joint between two pieces of wood that move together in the weather is supposed to be.

He turns the frame over in the opening and looks at the back of it, and the four bolt heads are square and sunk and there is no rust anywhere around them, and rust around a bolt head in a wet frame is the only thing that would have told him.

He leaves it. It is now part of the frame rather than part of a repair, and a frame taken out whole wants to stay whole. To open it would be to make two frames out of one, and there is nothing in front of him to argue for opening sound wood.

The woman from two doors down is in that yard at the start of the day with a bucket for the tap by the gate, and does not stay past the first hour and is not there for the frame and does not look at it twice.

“Have you had the rain in your back room?” she tells him.

“Not since the felt went back,” he tells her. She goes out through the yard gate with the bucket and the yard is his again.

There is an hour of that yard with two people in it and no two hands on anything, and then an hour and most of a morning with one person in it and two hands on a frame that is about to come out of a wall it has stood in since before the cabin was at that unit.

---

The rest of the day is easing and lifting.

He runs the knife round the outside where paint has bridged the joint, so the frame comes free without tearing the brick face. He taps wedges in at the sill and at the head, takes the weight on his shoulder, and walks the frame out of the opening square, a little at a time, until it is clear.

The knife goes round the outside of the frame in four runs and it stops where the frame ends and the render carries on, and he can feel that with the back of the blade as it comes, so he is not going by the sight of it. Where the paint bridges, the blade goes in and the bridge comes off in a scale about the size of a thumbnail, and where there is nothing it slides. A bridged joint is a joint that has to be cut, and a cut joint leaves the brick face with a clean line round it instead of a torn one.

The wedges go in at the sill first, one each end, and then at the head. He does not take the frame out on the wedges; he takes the load off it with them and then lifts it off its own weight, because a frame that is being wound out on wedges has to be wound in four directions at once and this one is four feet by three.

Going in it goes square, and coming out it goes square, and the two hardest things about a frame of that size are that it does not twist on the way and that your hands do not come out from under it before it is out. He takes the weight on his shoulder the way he has taken the weight of a lot of things that were heavier than they looked, and he walks it back a foot at a time with his chin on the top rail.

The sill below and the head above are not touched in any of it. The opening stands empty with the brick showing square on three sides and the felt behind it, and the frame goes down on a stack of battens at the side of the yard on sacking, level, with nothing under it but air.

And what is in the cabin now is the opening. The quarter inch of daylight that has been along the head of that frame since June is a strip of outside across the top of a hole four feet by three, and it is the same width it has been for months and it is doing a different job altogether, and the brick reveal on the left of it is true and the one on the right is out of square by about the thickness of a finger because the brickwork either side of it is out of square and has been since the cabin went up.
