# Chapter 0579 — I'll Speak to Him Saturday

The Wednesday of the fifteenth of July 2020, from about half past eight in the morning until about six in the evening, at a shelter on a main road in this borough and in one street off it.

**HE SAID FIVE WORDS TO HER UNDER A SHELTER AND SHE TOOK THEM AS BEING ABOUT THE TRUCK THAT STANDS ACROSS A GATE ON SATURDAYS, AND HE MEANT HIS BROTHER, WHOM HE HAS NOT SEEN SINCE THE FEBRUARY AND WHO IS IN ON SATURDAYS ONLY, AND ON THE AFTERNOON OF THAT DAY SOMEONE TOLD HER WHAT HE HAD MEANT AND SHE SAID NOTHING AT ALL, AND AT SIX IN THE EVENING HE SAID TWO THINGS ABOUT A SHOP AND PUT THE TWO HALVES OF THAT DAY TOGETHER AND FOUND HE WAS STANDING RATHER WELL IN IT, WHICH WAS NOT THE SAME THING AS BEING RIGHT.**

The shelter is on the far side of a road with a hoarding behind it and a timetable in a plastic holder with the last two months curling out of the slots. It takes six people standing up. At half past eight on a Wednesday in July there were two of them in it.

He is a man in his mid sixties and she is a woman in her early thirties and they live twelve doors apart in that row, which is about the closest thing either of them has to the other.

The one that runs belongs to a man who comes in from over the water and has never once been asked to move it. It stands across the gate of number fifty-one on a Saturday and every other day of the week it stands four doors down, and has done for long enough that the whole row has stopped having opinions about it.

She had said, about a fortnight before, that somebody ought to do something about it, and he had said at the time that he would not get involved.

At about half past eight he said, "I'll speak to him Saturday."

She said, "Right."

She did not say anything else because there was nothing else to say about that sentence. It was a straight one. Somebody on that row has been going to be spoken to about that truck and about Saturdays, and she had said herself a fortnight ago that somebody ought to.

What she took it to mean was not wrong by the standards of that street. Nobody on that row knows that he has a brother. Two people have asked him about his family in the eight years she has been here and he has answered both of them about a woman who has died, and neither answer mentioned anything else.

He does have a brother. He has not seen him since the middle of the February, and he has not rung him since, and Saturday is the one day of the week the man has ever been at home in his life.

---

At about twenty to twelve that morning, in that street, the woman in her late fifties was standing at her own gate with the woman in her early thirties when the man in his mid sixties came out of his front door and crossed over to them.

He did not go to work. He said he had a bit of time and asked whether she was going down to the shops.

Then he said, "I've not seen my brother since the February."

He said it the way a person says something they have not said to anybody. He said it twice, once to each of them, in slightly different words, and both versions had a Saturday in them and neither had a truck in it.

The woman in her late fifties did not ask him anything about it. She said the weather was more than it was and went inside.

The woman in her early thirties said, "Oh," and then nothing, and then, "Right."

---

At about half past three the woman in her late fifties went up to number fifty-one and knocked.

She said, "That wasn't about the truck. That was his brother he meant. He hasn't seen him since the February."

The woman in her early thirties stood in her own open door and looked at the woman in her late fifties for about three seconds.

Then she said nothing at all. Not a word, and not a shake of the head, and not the face she would have made if she had been about to disagree. She stood there and then she said, "Oh," and shut the door.

Not unkindly. She shut it the way anybody shuts a door when somebody else is walking up the row behind them.

The woman in her late fifties went back down the row and did not knock again and has not mentioned it since.

At about six the man in his mid sixties came out of his front door for his tea and the woman in her early thirties was at her own gate with her back to the road putting a parcel in by the post, and he said, "Shop's shut, they said."

She said, "Is it not."

He said, "It is."

Then he said, "It's three days off Saturday. I've plenty of time."

She said, "Right," and went in.

That was the whole of it. He had been right about the Saturday and wrong about everything else, and a woman who had been wrong about both had stood in her own open door and said nothing at all, and a woman who had been wrong about the first thing knew nothing about the second one, and the man in his mid sixties put the two together and found that he was standing rather well in it.

**SHE WAS TOLD WHAT HE MEANT AT HER OWN DOOR ON THE AFTERNOON AND GAVE HIM NO SIGHT OF WHAT SHE HAD THOUGHT, AND HE TOOK NO SIGHT OF IT AS AGREEMENT, AND THE ONLY THING ANYBODY IN THAT ROW COULD HAVE PUT RIGHT WAS A SENTENCE SAID AT HALF PAST THREE.**
