# Chapter 0914 — Along The Earth Crease

Monday the third of June 2024, at the step of stone at the back of number nine until the light goes off the yard. The crease of earth at the foot of a step.

**SHE HAS A STEP OF STONE AT THE BACK OF NUMBER NINE WITH A CREASE OF EARTH ALONG ITS FOOT AND SHE MEANS TO NIP THUMB EDGE AND FOREFINGER SIDE INTO THAT CREASE AND TRAVEL ITS RUN AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING IS AT THE GARDEN GATE FROM THE LANE WITH A PURSE IN HER HAND.**

The yard was paved in a week and this step came down with the paving, and the paving about it has met a broom every week since while the step has not shifted a fraction.

It is a step of grey stone about three feet wide, with the top worn smooth where feet cross it and a crease of earth along its foot where the broom never reaches.

She seeks the bearing beneath the step, where treads avoid and showers pool longest.

Crease of earth along the foot of a step is where the ground under a heavy thing tells what the heavy thing is standing on.

So the thumb edge and forefinger side nip the earth crease and travel its run.

The pinch runs along a line of packed earth between the stone and the paving, cold and gritty under the pads, with the bristles of the yard broom lying broken in it.

The pinch travels about a foot along the foot of the step and comes up hard against a wedge of flint, driven under the stone a hand back from the rim, grey and sharp, with hammer-broken ends rough to the touch.

She walks the pinch along the wedge, and it spans near three inches each way before ending, with the ends biting the soil and locked where they sit.

What the wedge sits on is soil, and that soil goes down further than the fingers can reach, with the step above it spanning flat from wedge to wall without any dip between.

So the step does not sit on the ground along its foot at all. It sits on its back edge and on one flint wedge, with earth packed hard in the crease between, so that the packed earth takes up the rock of a heel before the stone has to.

The woman who does her shopping is at the garden gate off the lane with a purse in her hand, and she does not open it.

She stood at the gate with the purse shut in her fist and looked along the path to the step and then to the street and back again, and she shifted the purse to the other hand and kept it shut, and then she went out through the gate with the purse still in her fist and left the gate standing open behind her.

A step is where feet land coming out of a house, and a yard back is where the water off the roof lands on its way down, and one piece of stone has to answer for both.

The wedge sits snug in its pocket, the sort of flint that comes up out of field drains when a ditch is cleared, with earth in the crease slack above and dense below, and the underside of the step lying flat along its whole run is what let the wedge bed level with the paving on both sides of it.

Stone carries its load down to what is under it and lets the ground stay soft, and brick spreads it instead, so a step wants one hard bearing and a wedge under the front of it.

Set that way the step has neither dipped at the middle nor lifted at the ends, and it has done that through two winters of hard frost and one of them being the worst this yard has had.

Stepped on every day it has settled rather than moved, and the settling took out of the middle of the step rather than out from under the ends of it.

Flat to the sky the step sheds the morning rain off its face by noon, and each shedding carries a little of the earth in the crease out onto the paving.

An exposed face set level to the skies dries earliest after showers, while one tucked against a wall keeps its moisture along the base till nightfall.

The step crown dries ahead of every other stone in that yard, its top in the sun by eight, while the crease keeps its damp till the middle of the next dry day.

Dust settles on the crown of the step in a rainless spell and washes away in a downpour, and between those two the stone takes on a shade darker than the paving round it and gives it back again.

Hammer-broken flint shows fresh faces that bite the soil and lock, while sawn stone shows smooth faces that slip in wet weather. Fresh faces glitter where quartz points catch the noon light, and old buried faces lie dull and dark. The glitter fades after one winter underground as soil dulls each point to grey. Grey points still bite when rubbed, though they do not catch the eye at noon.

Wedges are broken and not cut for that reason, and this one has stayed where it was driven while smooth ones work out from under a step in a wet week.

What it is, is a packing flint, put there to bring the step up to the door when the paving was set.

---

A packing driven like that lasts for years, because stone on flint locks and stone on soil spreads its load across the earth beneath.

Soil under a wedge sinks a little each winter and the wedge follows it down, and each year the step sits lower than it did the winter before.

Rain gets under the flint where water stands in the crease and washes the sand out on the path side, where feet shake it as they cross.

That is why wedges on the trodden side of a step sink while those on the still side stay high, and why this step, which feet cross in the middle, has lost least at its wedge.

The middle of it stands only a hair high now, where it stood a finger high on the day the flint went in.

Steps dip toward the wash, because the water runs off the door and under the front edge and takes sand out with it.

The front edge has gone down half an inch and the back, sitting on stone, has barely moved.

She turns the pinch sideways and lays it along the bed to see how far the grit carries under the stone.

It carries true under the flint for about three inches and then softens into grit all round, with the grit damp where water stands under the middle of the step.

A bed of grit binds under tread the way road metal does, and every boot that crosses seats it a touch harder where the step bears most.

Road grit locks because each stone turns until its flat side finds another flat side, and then it stops and stays through the winters.

Grit under a step does the same on a small scale, which is why old steps sit harder than new ones, with each year pressing the bed a little flatter and truer.

The centre of that bed is dead still and the front rim stirs faintly after rain, when the water under it lifts a hair's breadth of grit and lets it down again.

The front edge of that step was brought up with sand, brushed under it with a thin stick and settled with rain, and then the step bedded onto it and tapped to seat.

Laid in wet, sand finds the voids under it and binds hard; brushed in dry, it sits where it was put and is gone before July.

The top of the step takes weight underfoot, with no cracks across it and the worn part smooth where feet have polished it.

Feet polish stone the way hands polish timber, because grit underfoot cuts a little every step until the top shines.

The polish stands where feet land most, which is the middle of the step and not its ends, so the middle of it shines while the foot stays rough.

The flint under the step lies true in the earth, with the top of it worn smooth where the stone rubs and the bottom rough where it bites the soil.

She lets go of the pinch where it was and knocks the earth off her fingers against the tread, then goes out to the path to look at the step with the light behind it.

The step stays where the paving crew set it, carried at the front on one flint wedge and at the back on its own edge, and the earth in the crease has packed itself firm around both of them, and the shine has gone off the middle tread where the feet cross it.
