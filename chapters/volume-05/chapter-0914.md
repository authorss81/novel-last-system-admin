# Chapter 0914 — Along The Earth Crease

Monday the third of June 2024, at the step of stone at the back of number nine until the light goes off the yard. The crease of earth at the foot of a step.

**SHE HAS A STEP OF STONE AT THE BACK OF NUMBER NINE WITH A CREASE OF EARTH ALONG ITS FOOT AND SHE MEANS TO NIP THUMB EDGE AND FOREFINGER SIDE INTO THAT CREASE AND TRAVEL ITS RUN AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING IS AT THE GARDEN GATE FROM THE LANE WITH A PURSE IN HER HAND.**

The step came with the back door when the yard was paved, and the paving about it has met the broom a hundred times while the step kept its seat.

It is a step of grey stone about three feet wide, with the top worn smooth where feet cross it and a crease of earth along its foot where the broom never reaches.

She seeks the bearing beneath the step, where treads avoid and showers pool longest.

An earth crease at a foot reveals beyond its crown, for the crown carries tread while the crease carries what tread presses.

So the thumb edge and forefinger side nip the earth crease and travel its run.

The stone feels cool on the outer fingers, the way yard stone stays cool till evening, and the crease drags where sand lines it.

Her fingers travel about a foot and stop on something firm. Not soil. A wedge of flint, driven hard under the step a hand back from the rim, grey and sharp, with hammer-broken ends rough to the touch.

She walks the pinch along it, and it spans near three inches each way before ending, with the ends biting the soil and locking where they sit.

What the wedge sits on is more soil, going down further than the fingers can reach, with the step above it spanning flat from wedge to wall without any dip between.

So the step does not sit on the ground along its foot at all. It sits on its back edge and on one flint wedge, with earth packed in the crease between, and the packed earth is what keeps it from rocking.

The shopping woman arrives at the garden gate off the lane, purse clasped shut in her fist.

She stood at the gate with the purse shut in her fist and looked along the path to the step and then to the street and back again, and she shifted the purse to the other hand and kept it shut, and then she went out through the gate with nothing in her hands but the purse and left it half open behind her.

A step on a wedge does a job beyond building work. A step is where feet land leaving a house, and a yard back is where it meets roof runoff year round.

The wedge sits flat within its pocket, the sort of flint turning up from field drains, earth in the crease slack above and dense beneath.

Grey stone lies flat on flint in a way that brick does not. Brick beds down and locks, and stone spans and bears, so it wants a flat wedge under it and the wedge wants soil under that.

That is why the step sits flat on its wedge at the front and flat on its back edge at the wall, with the flat stone to flat flint throwing the load down instead of spreading it.

Flat to flat like that the step sheds the morning rain off its face by noon, and each shedding leaves the crease a little deeper than before, because water runs where feet do not sweep.

An exposed face set flat to skies dries earliest after showers, while a nested one keeps moisture along its base till nightfall.

Hence the step crown dries earliest among yard stones, its flat cap sunlit, while the crease retains moisture till the following rainless spell.

The flat step cap carries its own warning. Dust settles on it after rainless spells and washes away in downpours, each wash leaving the cap a shade flatter than earlier, since fine dust departs and solid stone persists.

Hammer-broken flint shows fresh faces that bite the soil and lock, while sawn stone shows smooth faces that slip in wet weather. Fresh faces glitter where quartz points catch noon light, while old buried faces lie dull and dark. The glitter fades after one winter underground as soil dulls each point to grey. Grey points still bite when rubbed, though they do not catch the eye at noon.

That is why wedges are broken and not cut, and why this one has stayed where it was driven while smooth ones work out.

What it is, is a packing flint, put there to bring the step up to the door when the paving was set.

---

A packing driven like that lasts for years, because stone on flint locks flat and stone on soil spreads the load across the earth beneath.

Soil under a wedge sinks a little each winter and the wedge follows it, and each year the step sits a hair lower than before.

Rain gets under the flint where water sits and washes sand out on the path side where feet shake it, which is why wedges on the trodden side sink while those on the still side stay high, and why this step, which feet cross in the middle, has sunk least at its wedge.

That is why the middle stands only a hair high now, where it stood a finger high when the flint went in.

Steps dip toward the wash, because water runs off the door and under the front edge and takes sand with it.

That is why the front edge has gone down half an inch while the back on stone has barely moved.

She turns the pinch sideways to feel how the bed runs under the step.

It runs true under the flint for about three inches and then softens into grit all round, with the grit damp where water sits under the middle.

A bed of grit binds under tread the way road metal does, and every boot that crosses seats it a touch harder where the step bears most.

Road grit locks because each stone turns until its flat side finds another flat side, and then it stops and stays through winters.

Grit under a step does the same on a small scale, which is why old steps sit harder than new ones, with each year pressing the bed a little flatter and truer.

That is why the centre stays dead while the front rim stirs faintly after rain, as seep lifts it a hair.

The front edge of that step is brought up with sand, brushed under it with a thin stick and watered in, and then the step bedded onto it and tapped to seat.

Sand damped in sinks through the voids and binds, while sand swept in dry lies loose and leaves by July.

The top of the step takes weight underfoot, with no cracks across it and the worn part smooth where feet have polished it.

Feet polish stone the way hands polish timber, because grit underfoot cuts a little every step until the top shines.

Polish shows where feet land most, which is the middle of the step and not the ends, and why the middle shines while the foot stays rough.

The flint under the step sits flat in the earth, with the top of it worn smooth where the stone rubs and the bottom rough where it bites the soil.

She frees the pinch from the crease and dusts earth away upon the adjacent tread, studying the step from the path for its frontal testimony.

From above the step looks level and true, with the foot showing only as a dark line along the paving.

From the side the foot shows a hair of shadow under it, where the earth has gone back from the stone.

Level from above and a hair of shadow below at the foot, and that stone and that sliver of dark are one thing looked at twice, and the corner that will fret first for want of a bed is the front one floating on sand that washes out each rain.

Let the front be brushed firm onto the soil it has settled into, and let the wedge stand on its flint through another year, and the middle tread has lost its shine under feet.
