# Chapter 0684 — The Same Free Number On Two Cards

Friday the twenty-sixth of November 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from first light until dark with one woman in it, and the whole of the day is a box on the left of her own table and a door that does not latch and a date she was told about in October by a machine in her own passage.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS A PARCEL ON HER OWN TABLE AND A WOMAN WITH A BRIGHT VOICE UP HER OWN PATH ON A DATE THAT WAS GIVEN TO HER BY A MACHINE IN OCTOBER, IS TOLD BY THE SURVEYOR THAT THE WATER IS FOR THE FREEHOLDER AND NOT FOR YOU, IS HANDED THE SAME FREE NUMBER AGAIN AND PUTS THAT CARD IN THE APRON POCKET WITH THE TWO DROP BOLTS AND THE FIRST ONE, AND TELLS THE TRUTH ABOUT THE PARCEL OUT LOUD WHEN SHE IS ASKED IT DIRECTLY, AND HAS NOT OPENED IT.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is on her own all day. One woman speaks to her at the gate and in her kitchen, asks her seven questions and gets seven answers.

---

She has the box on the left of the table and the envelope on the right of it, and the front door is not on the latch and never has been since before Christmas, and the passage light has been on since June, and the brush is in the middle of the runner with the handle across the walking line.

She has had a run of weeks in which nothing has happened.

That is not a thing she would say to anybody. She would say the hotplate is warm, and the tap drips, and the bowl goes under it morning and night.

---

She empties the bowl under the bath tap first thing and puts the cloth back in it, and comes down, and steps over the brush, and puts the kettle on.

The bath ceiling mark has a hard edge and the middle of it is darker than the outside. The landing is over the front room. There is a pale patch on the ceiling of the front room just inside the door and it has got a defined edge now, where it used to be a cloud about the width of a tea tray.

The front room has a rush-seated chair against the wall by the window and a sideboard with nothing on it and she has not sat in there since the sixteenth of July.

The gas envelope is face up square with the edge of the table, pencil side up. Six things on the back. Nothing crossed off. There is no pencil in that house and there has not been since before Christmas.

---

In the middle of the morning the gate moves.

It is standing back against the wall of the coal shed and it moves about two inches, the way it does when somebody has put a hand on it, and then it stops.

She stands in the kitchen and does not go out.

Then she goes out, because it is her gate.

---

There is a woman at the path with a clipboard and a white van at the kerb with its side door open and a pole with a camera on it standing up in the footwell.

"Right," the woman says. "That's number twelve, isn't it."

"It is."

"I rang in October." She has got the bright voice off the machine and it is quite different in the open air, and quieter, and she is not wearing a coat over a uniform, she is in a fleece. "I left a message. It's the survey, it's about a first floor ceiling."

"I know it's you."

"You've heard it, then." She says it without any trouble at all. "That's fine. It's the water side, first floor, over the front room. There's a mark on that ceiling and it's in the report and I can't close a report on it without going back, because the report wants to say whether it's gone worse or not."

"I see."

"So I wanted to know — is it still there?"

She is standing on the path about six feet from the door and the door is behind her and the door does not latch.

"Yes," says the woman of seventy-eight.

"Right."

---

"But it's not for me," the woman says. "I want to say that first, because I know how it sounds. It's for the people that own the house. Nothing to do with you, no charge, no account, nothing comes on your bill. I just need to see it and put a line in the report so somebody with more than four legs than me can decide what happens about it."

"I understand."

"It's about twenty minutes. I've got a pole on it."

"Right."

The woman looks at the door, and at the step, and at the bin at the kerb with its lid up, and she does not go past the step.

Then she says: "Are you all right with me going up?"

---

She does not say no.

That is what happens and she has to sit with it afterwards, because the thing she expected to do in October was say no in a kitchen to a man with a clipboard, and she did say no to that, and she has had a printed card with a free number on it in the pocket of an apron ever since, and she did not ring it, and now there is a woman on her own path asking her a different question and the answer comes out before she has the sentence ready.

"Yes," she says. "If you want."

"You'll be here?"

"I'm here."

"That's all right. I don't need you to do anything. I need you to let me get to the ceiling and I'll be twenty minutes and I'll be off."

"Right."

---

The woman goes up.

She is let in and she steps over the brush on the way in, the way a person steps over a cable in a doorway, and she does not ask what it is for, and the woman of seventy-eight stands in her own passage and watches a stranger step over the thing she put down so that somebody would have to.

She has been in this house — the gas man, a lad on the step she did not let past the door, a man with a form — three times since the spring and not one of them asked her why anything was the way it was, and she has never once found out whether that was because they did not see it or because they saw it and did not ask, and there has never been anybody in that house to ask.

The woman goes through to the kitchen, where the kitchen is at the back and the front room is between the door and the kitchen, and the woman of seventy-eight watches her go through it.

---

Then she stops in the kitchen doorway.

There is a box on the left of her table and the envelope on the right of it and the cloth over the box and the cloth over the damp mark, and the woman with the bright voice is standing in the middle of that kitchen looking at the tape and the label and the cloth on it, exactly the way anybody would.

"What's that?"

"It's a box."

"What's in it?"

"I don't know."

The woman waits, and she is not pushing, and then she says: "Right," and looks away, and picks her camera pole up out of the bag on her hip.

"It came on the twelfth of October," the woman of seventy-eight says.

It is out. She had not decided to say it. It came out of her mouth in a kitchen on the same grounds as everything else in that house comes out of her mouth, which is that somebody asked her a direct question and she could not do anything else.

"And it's not been for anybody," she says. "It went out with the recycling once and they told me on Monday."

"Did you open it?"

"No."

"No," the woman says, and there is nothing in her voice about that at all, and she goes up the stairs.

---

She is up there a good while.

The woman of seventy-eight stays in the kitchen with the box and the envelope and does not go up after her, and does not stand at the bottom of the stairs, and does not go out to the coal shed.

She washes the cup she did not use. She wipes the drainer. She puts the cloth over the damp mark and it is still not quite dry and she puts the other cloth over that.

She takes the two drop bolts out of the apron pocket and puts them on the table, and then puts them back in the pocket, because the gate is open and the bolts are the reason it is open.

A little after midday the woman comes down and goes out to the van and shuts her side door and stands at the gate about four steps down from the house with her hand on the top of it, and she says, without turning round:

"You don't have to wait for me."

"Right."

"It's a free call, it doesn't have to be me."

"Right," the woman of seventy-eight says.

Then the woman turns round and comes back up the path four steps and puts a card into her hand, and it is the same card as the other one — a printed card with a number on it and a bit of a survey company's address — and she says the number is the same one she left in the message, and she says it is for the freeholder and not for her.

That is the first thing anybody has put in that woman's hand since the fifteenth of October, and she takes it, and she does not read it in front of the woman.

Then the woman gets back in the van and shuts the door and drives off, and after a bit she stands in her own gateway with the road empty at the end of it.

The gate is still back against the wall of the coal shed. The path is clear. The bin is at the kerb with its lid up because the hinge has gone soft and the lid has not shut since the summer, and a parcel went into that bin once and went out again with the recycling, and she has taken the lad's word for which parcel it was and has not checked it, and the strip of gummed paper on the label is the part of it she cannot follow, and she has never seen the inside of that bin and never will.

She goes in. She shuts the front door behind her, which is shut when she shuts it and does not latch, and never has.

She is standing in her own passage.

There is a brush in the middle of the runner about two feet inside the door with the handle across the walking line, and she steps over it, right foot then left, and goes through to her own kitchen, and the passage light is on the whole length of the runner down onto the wood.

---

She has been standing in her own passage with her hand on the brush handle and does not move it.

There are two free numbers in this house on two pieces of card and they are the same number and one of them came off the bottom of a form on the fifteenth of October with a man standing on her path and one of them came out of a hand on the twenty-sixth of November.

She goes and gets the first one off the shelf in the passage and puts it in the pocket of the apron hanging on the back of the front door, and then she goes and gets the second one off the side table where the woman left it and puts it in the same pocket, and the two drop bolts are in there too, and the pocket of that apron has four things in it and has had three since the summer.

There is a woman in a different house tonight who is going to write on a line in a report what she has seen on a ceiling with a pole, and it is going to go to a freeholder, and the freeholder has got her address and the number of the company that sent her and will do whatever it is that freeholders do in November.

And there is a man in this borough who would know in the time it takes to boil a kettle what that patch is, and he has never been up that road since the seventeenth of June and has a bit of card in his shirt pocket with TWELVE on it in his own writing, and there is a woman in a white van who has had a taped box behind the passenger bench of that van since the twenty-second of October and has read the number on the label twice and written it nowhere, and there is a lamp on a bracket by that gate that a man put up in the summer because somebody asked the council for it, and that somebody was her neighbour, and she was never asked.

None of them knows about any of the others. None of them has been told any of this.

There is a box on the left of her kitchen table with a forwarding strip on the label and a street on it she has never been to and a number at the end of it that is not twelve, and nobody in this borough has rung anybody about any part of it, and the coal shed at the end of that garden has had its door shut since the twenty-second of November and its padlock hanging open on the hasp since before Christmas.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS LET A STRANGER INTO A ROOM SHE HAS NOT SAT IN SINCE JULY AND HAS SAID YES WHEN SHE HAD PLANNED TO SAY NOTHING, HAS TOLD THE TRUTH ABOUT A BOX OUT LOUD IN HER OWN KITCHEN WHEN SHE WAS ASKED DIRECTLY, HAS NOT OPENED IT, HAS TWO CARDS WITH THE SAME FREE NUMBER IN ONE POCKET AND HAS RUNG NEITHER, HAS NOT MOVED THE BRUSH, AND FOUR OF SIX IS DONE AND NOTHING HAS BEEN CROSSED OFF.**
