# Chapter 0928 — Past The Jamb Corner

Thursday the twenty-fifth of July 2024, at the stone lintel over the yard doorway at number nine while the doorway keeps its shade. Under a stone that spans a door.

**SHE HAS A STONE LINTEL OVER THE YARD DOORWAY AT NUMBER NINE WITH A HOLLOW UNDER IT AND SHE MEANS TO SET THE HAND IN AT ONE END OF THAT HOLLOW AND TAKE IT PAST THE CORNER OF THE JAMB SO THAT THE CORNER COMES INTO THE MIDDLE OF THE PALM, TO FIND OUT HOW DEEP THE HOLLOW IS AND WHAT LIES AT THE FAR END OF IT.**

One stone in that yard wall has never been touched by a pointer, and it is the one that spans the doorway; about four feet long, set on edge across the opening, with a chamfer cut under it and a hollow running the whole length behind that chamfer. It is also the only stone on that wall with a worked edge left sharp on one side.

The yard wall and its doorway went up together in a week and the wall has been repointed twice since, and the hollow behind that chamfer is where the arch above the door has never quite got to.

A hollow behind a chamfer is a place a mason makes on purpose, and the depth of that hollow is the thing she came for, so the hand goes in at the low end and past the jamb corner. The stone is cool and feels slick along the chamfer's upper face and gritty under the arris of it. Past the corner the hollow opens out, and the depth at the middle of the stone comes to about three inches, with lime gone hard rolled into the back of it and the roll lying against the left-hand side.

The stone does not go beyond that. Two inches on from the roll there is stone again, dressed with a point and going down solid to the back, and it is the same stone to the last grain.

---

The chamfer under a lintel is a drip. It throws the water off the underside of the stone and out past the face of the wall, and it is the reason a stone over a doorway keeps its face clean where the stone below it never does.

The chamfer is cut three-eighths of an inch deep and its upper edge is the arris of the stone, and an arris that is left sharp drinks. It weathers back over about thirty years until it is a radius rather than a corner, and a radius lets the water sit where a corner threw it. That stone is about sixty years old and its arris under the lintel is still sharp, and it is sharp on one side and gone on the other, which is the whole of the difference between the two ends of it.

Where an arris is gone, the run has gone with it. Rain that reaches the stone above the door first crosses a face and then falls through the hole it has made in that face. The run across the lintel is four inches, from the low end to where the arris has failed, and a run four inches long that fails every time will put about a bucket of water a year into the head of the doorway.

The chamfer is not decoration and it was not put there for looks. It is cut with a point in about four strokes and it costs a mason half an hour, and it is the first thing a mason cuts on any stone set over an opening.

A stone over an opening gets everything the wall above it throws and nothing of the wall's own weight to help it throw it back. So the mason arranges for the water to leave before it arrives at the underside, and a chamfer is how it is told to leave. Where no chamfer is cut the arris serves instead, and an arris serves for about forty years, and after that it serves no better than a face and the stone below it takes the run.

---

The wall round that doorway is rubble stone bedded in lime, and the rubble is the same stone as the lintel, which is why the doorway reads as one piece from the front and is not one from underneath. Both of the jambs were built up of it stone by stone rather than rubble by rubble, and the difference shows from the side.

Bedded rubble carries its load on the ground through the pointing and through nothing else. A stone lintel carries its own weight on the jambs at either end and spans the whole of the opening between them.

A stone that spans a door is the one stone in the wall that is not in the wall. It gets none of the earth above it and none of the buttressing either side, and it works alone across four feet. The head of the opening was packed with the offcuts that came away when the flues were chased out, and the offcuts are smaller than the lintel and have gone further than the lintel ever will.

A hollow that runs the length of a lintel and stops at the middle is a masher's savings. The mason ran a point in behind the chamfer and took away whatever the point would reach and left the rest, and the rest is the three inches that the hand has found. The jambs either side of that opening are built up of the same rubble bedded tighter than the rest of the wall, because a jamb that works loose drops the lintel it is carrying about half an inch in a decade.

Neither jamb has worked. The one on the left is bedded with its stones cut rather than broken and its face out of plumb by about half an inch in four feet, which is what a rubble jamb built by eye always is.

The lintel rests on the flat left by the mason when it was set and has stayed on it. It has not tipped, which means the two ends have gone down together, which means the wall behind the lintel has gone down with it by the same amount and the same amount for both ends.

The roll of lime at the far end of that hollow is the driest thing on the whole yard wall. It has been behind a stone since the wall went up, and it is so far gone to powder that a thumbnail takes a crescent out of it and leaves the rest standing.

Powder that old runs. Given a hard frost it will break out of the back of the hollow in a day, drop about four inches, and stop, because below the hollow there is the top of the chamfer to land on. The chamfer will carry it down the face of the stone and out into the yard, which is where everything on a wall ends up in the end, and it will leave the hollow a third of an inch deeper than she has measured it.

A lintel that has lost its lime has lost the only thing in it that was loose. Everything else about it is stone doing what stone does, which is sit still for as long as the jambs will let it. The yard doorway stands with its own shade across it, and the ground under the lintel is the driest ground in the yard, which is why the weeds there are thin and never get to a foot.

Her sister does not come to number nine. The woman who does her shopping comes instead, to the front gate from inside the yard, the gate standing open behind her and her handbag gone inside it.

Handbag inside the gate, she stood on the flags and looked at the plinth, then up at the coping, then at the plinth again, and then she turned her head towards the lane and went on out through the gate with the gate left standing open.

The price of flour and the size of the shop's bags went into words there, Neither of those reached the stone, and the roll of lime lying behind its chamfer went unnamed the whole time. She had the gate open behind her again before she had finished saying both of them.

The powder waits behind the chamfer for a frost hard enough to move it, and the chamfer underneath it is slick where the arris has gone and takes what falls as readily as it took rain.

The lintel keeps its hollow with the lime gone hard rolled into the far end of it, and however level its two ends go on keeping themselves the stone at the middle of the span is three inches deeper than the stone at either jamb.
