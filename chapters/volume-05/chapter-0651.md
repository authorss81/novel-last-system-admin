# Chapter 0651 — Six Things On The Fridge

Saturday the third of July 2021, in this borough. She is in a kitchen at number nine from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon, and she has been in that kitchen every day since the Thursday of the twenty-seventh of May, and the whole of it is a back door that will not latch and six things written on the back of a pad with a magnet on it.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES WHO WAS GIVEN A STOOL BY A STRANGER AND NEVER ASKED HER ONE QUESTION SINCE THE MORNING SHE WAS GIVEN IT FIXES A BACK DOOR SHE HAS NO STANDING TO ASK ABOUT AND WRITES SIX THINGS ON A FRIDGE, AND THE FIRST THING SHE CROSSES OFF IS THE FIRST LINE THAT HAS BEEN CROSSED OFF ON ANY LIST IN THIS BOROUGH, AND SHE DOES NOT GO UP THE ROAD, AND THE REASON IS NOT POLITENESS.**

She is a woman in her late fifties. She is on her own. There is no man in that house and nobody else in the room.

---

Here is the state of that kitchen, and it is worth putting down because everybody on that road has got it wrong.

There is a table with a cloth on it. There are two chairs at that table and one of them has a leg that moves if you look at it and the other one has a back that has been out at the same joint since she inherited the house.

And there is a stool by that table, on the floor, on its feet, where she put it down on the Thursday of the twenty-seventh of May after a woman of seventy-eight had stood on her path for a minute with nothing in her hands.

---

And here is the part everybody has got wrong, including her, and it is the honest part.

She does not eat standing.

She ate standing because she could not sit down without doing something to a chair first, and she would not do that to her mother's chairs, and doing that to her mother's chairs is not a thing she has ever been able to explain to anybody, and she never had to explain it to anybody because there was nobody.

She had a stool from the twenty-seventh of May.

She has eaten sitting down at that table on every night she has been in before midnight since the twenty-seventh of May, which is three nights out of seven, and she has eaten standing on the other four, and the reason she has eaten standing on the other four is that she gets in at about ten past midnight and she eats before she sits down because she is not hungry until she has had her coat off.

That is not a thing anybody else on that road could have told you and it is the truth and it is boring.

---

The back door is the thing on the Saturday.

It is a door at the back of the house onto the yard, and it has been dropping on its hinges since the spring, and as of about a week ago it stopped latching altogether.

And here is why that is a real problem and not a good house: she goes out at about ten and she comes in at about ten past midnight or later, and there is nothing between that yard and her kitchen except a door that does not latch.

She has been in at about ten past midnight four nights out of seven with a back door swinging behind her in the wind and a latch that goes over and comes back up on its own.

---

So she does it herself.

And she does it properly, and the doing of it is about four hours of the best part of her week, and it is worth setting down because it is the only thing about that Saturday that anybody would call competent, and it is in a woman's own back door on a Saturday morning.

She has a screwdriver. She has a block of soft wood about two inches by one, which she has had in the toolbox since the house came, and she has a piece of cardboard.

She takes the butt off the hinge, which takes her about ten minutes and which everybody finds difficult and which is not difficult if you have done it once.

She packs the hinge leaves out with strips of the cardboard, folded twice, cut with a kitchen knife, on the hinge that has dropped, and she does not pack it tight, because a hinge packed tight is a hinge that binds and drops again inside a month, and she wants that leg back in a week and not in a month.

She hangs it and closes it and it does not drop.

And then she drives the block of wood under the door at the bottom, against the hinge side, and takes it out again, and closes it, and it does not drop.

And she opens it and shuts it four or five times with her whole weight on it, because a packed hinge that does not drop when you lean on it is not a fixed hinge.

---

And at about half past one she shuts it, and it latches, and she stands on the other side of it and pulls, and it does not open, and it has not done that since the spring.

And she goes and gets her tea.

---

And then she sits down at that table with her tea, and she is aware that she is sitting down on a stool, and she is aware that there are two chairs at that table and neither of them is a good chair.

And here is what happens in the next hour, and it is the whole of the afternoon.

She has not written anything down since she inherited this house.

She has not needed to. She has managed, for years, by walking round the house with her eyes and taking things as they come, which is what everybody does, and which is not a plan and is a perfectly good way to run a house.

And she sits at that table at about twenty to two on the Saturday afternoon of the third of July with a pencil and a pad off the shelf by the fridge and she writes six things down and puts it on the fridge under a magnet, because that is what a magnet is for.

---

And here is what she writes, and it goes on in the order she would actually do it in, which is the correct order, which is the only interesting thing about it.

The back door — hinge packed — DONE

The chair at that table, the leg

The chair at that table, the back

The bulb in the front room

The gutter at the back

A second door into the kitchen, so the mud stops coming in

And she writes DONE against the first one, rules a line through it, and underlines it twice, and that is the first line that has been crossed off on any list in this borough, and there are two lists in this borough now, and there is a woman three doors up who has never been told that the other one exists.

---

And she stands back from the fridge and looks at it, and here is what she sees and it is worth putting down because it is the first time since she inherited the house that anybody has looked at two chairs from outside.

She sees a woman in her late fifties writing six things on a pad and putting a magnet on it, in a kitchen with a stool in it that she has not asked a single question about, and two chairs at a table that are both wrong, and she is not the woman on the doorstep any more. She is a woman with a list.

And that is not a small thing and she knows it is not, and it went in the same hour as the pad, which is bad timing and she is a woman who has been on lates for about a decade and she knows what bad timing does.

---

And then she does the second thing on the list.

And she does not do it in the way anybody expects, and the difference matters.

She does not go and get her mother's chair out of the passage to work on at that table. She takes the stool out on the path, on its side, on a folded blanket, on a Saturday afternoon in July, and she puts the back of the chair that has been out at the joint flat down on it, and she works on it there, in the sun, at about half past two, on her own front step, where anybody going past can see her doing it.

And the reason she does it there is not the light and it is not the space.

It is that there is nobody in this house to see her fix it.

---

And here is what she does not do for about fifty minutes, which is the whole of it.

She does not go up the road.

---

Number twelve is three doors up. She has known the number since before she inherited this house and she has lived on this road for the best part of twenty years.

And she could go up there this afternoon. That is the plain fact and she looks at it for about two minutes and she works out that she cannot.

---

Here is what has happened on that road since the twenty-seventh of May, and she has learned all of it second-hand and has not asked a question about any of it.

A stranger gave her a stool on her own step on a Thursday morning and said nine words and went away up the road, and did not say her name and did not give one.

The woman at number ten, who is her sister, came to this house on the Sunday after and said, sitting where she is sitting now, in the middle of something else, without being asked:

*she's had a man in. She's had a light put in her passage.*

---

And the woman in her late fifties did not say anything to that.

And what went through her, and she has not got anybody to say it to and has not written it down, is this.

She has been given a stool for nothing.

Nobody has thanked her. She did not expect to be thanked and she would not have known what to do about it. She is a woman in her late fifties who has lived alone for a long time and she knows exactly what a free thing is.

But she did something on the Thursday of the twenty-seventh of May that nobody has ever done for her, and she knows what it was, and so does the woman who did it.

She did not ask a single question.

She let a stranger stand on her path with nothing in her hands and hand her a stool and say *it's for you if you want it, there's nobody else after it*, and she did not ask her where it came from, or who was going to ask, or what happened to the woman she got it from.

And she was being kind, and she was being careful, and she was being kind because she was being careful.

---

And the exchange rate on that road has changed and she can see it and it is the whole of what she has been thinking about since the Sunday.

Because a woman of seventy-eight has now had a light put in her passage.

Which means a man.

Which means the woman at number twelve has somebody who comes, and somebody who comes is a woman who was given something first.

And she was given it first.

---

And what she cannot do, sitting at that table at about half past two on the Saturday afternoon, is go up three doors and ask a woman of seventy-eight how she is getting on.

Not because it would be difficult.

Because she has nothing to bring and she has not been thanked and she has not asked one question about the stool since the Thursday she was given it and she does not know what her position is.

And there is not one single thing in this that anybody has done wrong.

---

And she is aware, sitting there, that the whole of it is her own.

She was kind, and the kind was complete, and the kind had a price on it that she did not name at the time, and the price is that she has now been in a relationship with a stranger in which she is owed something and cannot ask, and the something is not the stool.

---

So she does not go up the road.

And at about four o'clock she gets up and washes her cup and puts the pad on the fridge and looks at it through the kitchen door from the sink.

And six things and a magnet and a DONE.

And three doors up there is a woman of seventy-eight sitting in a front room who has not had a light put in that passage for anybody but herself.

---

And the last thing is this, and it takes about four seconds and it is the whole of it.

At about four o'clock she is at the sink and she looks out of the kitchen window at the back of the house, and across the back gardens there is her sister's kitchen light on at number ten, and her sister is in, on a Saturday afternoon, and it is a hundred yards from where she is standing.

And she does not go round.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES PUT A BACK DOOR RIGHT WITH A FOLDED PIECE OF CARD AND A BLOCK OF WOOD AND THEN WROTE SIX THINGS ON A FRIDGE IN THE RIGHT ORDER AND CROSSED OFF THE FIRST ONE TWICE, AND SHE COULD HAVE WALKED UP THAT ROAD AT HALF PAST TWO ON A SATURDAY AND DID NOT, AND THE REASON IS NOT POLITENESS AND NOT FEAR AND IS THAT SHE WAS GIVEN A STOOL FOR NOTHING BY A STRANGER AND NEVER ASKED HER ONE QUESTION SINCE THE MORNING SHE WAS GIVEN IT AND CANNOT NOW ASK FOR ANYTHING AT ALL.**

(End of chapter 0651)
