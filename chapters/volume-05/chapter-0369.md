# Chapter 0369 — No Ice

Friday the seventeenth of November 2017, from about half past nine in the morning until about half past six in the evening, a shop off a parade, and a yard about a mile off, in Tideglass.

**A SACK OF ICE HAS BEEN BROUGHT TO THE BACK OF THAT SHOP ON THE SAME MORNING EVERY WEEK SINCE THE SUMMER BY A MAN WHO HAS A BARROW AND PASSES THE PLACE THAT SELLS IT, AND ON FRIDAY IT DID NOT COME, AND THE MAN WHO WAS BRINGING IT FOUND THAT OUT AT TEN PAST SIX IN THE EVENING BY STANDING AT A COUNTER, AND A MAN WHO WORKS NIGHTS HAD ASKED FOR ONE AT HALF PAST FOUR AND WILL NOT ASK AGAIN, AND NEITHER OF THE TWO MEN HAS SAID ANYTHING TO ANYBODY.**

The shop has a counter at the front and a floor behind it and about nine feet further back, past the shelves, there is a white chest with a hinged lid and a cloth across it, and the chest is not for anything but ice.

She has had that chest since before the summer. She fills it herself when she can and it is not a thing anybody in that shop has ever ordered for her or told her to do.

The man in his forties buys a sack of ice about a mile off on the last working morning of most weeks, from a man who sells them out of a yard, for a price that has gone up twice since the summer, and he has a barrow and he goes up that road on his way and he has brought it to the back of that shop every Friday since about the middle of June.

There is no paper of any kind about it. He has never asked her to pay him anything for carrying it and she has never offered, and the money goes into the till at the price the ice is sold at and that is the whole of the transaction between them.

He has never been late with it once until the seventeenth of November.

---

His pickup was in a yard off the Cleeve road from about half past eight that morning and it did not come out until about half past ten, because a lorry came in at a quarter past eight that backs up to that yard door and there is nowhere in that yard to turn a lorry, and the lorry is loaded and the driver was on the phone about a load and went and had his lunch, and the man in his forties sat in his pickup with the engine off for about two hours and then moved the pickup to the side of that yard and let the lorry out, and by the middle of the morning he was moving again.

He could have gone up the road and got a sack. He did not, because that is not the way that job goes, and the sack is not bought by him and the ice is not his.

He went back out to the road at about three and finished a floor in a terrace at the top of the Cleeve road and came off at about half past five, and he was at the back of that shop off the parade at about ten past six with a bag of plugs in his hand.

The man who works nights was in that shop at about half past four that afternoon.

He finishes at about four in the morning and he sleeps until about two and he goes into that parade at about half past four most days, and he has been going in there at that hour for about six years, and he buys two sacks of ice to take home, and the woman who works in a shop puts them in a carrier by the till and he carries them out to his car in the back.

That Friday the chest was down and there was nothing in it, and it had been down since about half past ten that morning, because the last of it had gone the day before and there was about a stone left in it in the bottom.

He came in and went to the chest and lifted the lid and looked into it and put it back down, and she said from behind the counter that the man had not been, and he said, "No," and then he said, "Have you any?"

That is the one time he has asked her for one when there was not one, and he has not asked her for one since, and he will not, and he buys his ice elsewhere and has been buying it elsewhere since about the Tuesday before, and the man in the shop on the parade who sells it is half a mile further on and shuts at six.

He said nothing else about it. He stood at the counter for about four minutes talking about a bus timetable and then went out with a paper and a packet of tobacco and no ice, and he has not said a word to her about an ice since.

She had known by half past four that the ice was not coming, and by half past four she had a way round it, which is that the chest is shut and the counter does not need to be cold at that hour in that week anyway because there is not much on it that does, and she shut the lid and put a wet cloth on the outside of it and carried on.

He came in the back way at about ten past six with a bag of plugs. She was at the counter. The man who works nights was still at the other end of it, and he had been there since about half past five.

She did not say that the ice was not coming. She said what she said to everybody at that counter and asked him whether he wanted a bag, and he said yes, and she put the bag of plugs in a carrier and gave it to him.

The man who works nights did not say anything and did not move, and neither of them said anything to him either, and there were three people at that counter for about ten seconds with one of them waiting to say something and neither of the other two giving him anything to say it with.

He put his hand in his pocket. He has a hand on the counter about two foot from a woman who has known him a long while and who has a wet cloth on a chest at the back of her own shop because a man with a barrow was in a yard for two hours this morning, and he took his money out and put it on the counter and said that was all right and went out into the parade.

He has not been back up that road. He went on up the Cleeve road and finished at about half past eight and did not come down again until the Tuesday, and the Tuesday he came through with a sack of ice at about half past eight, because that is one thing he does at the end of a week, and it was not the end of a week, and he was on his way past.

---

She did not tell him. Not on the Friday, and not on the Saturday, and not since.

He has not asked, and he has not been in that shop to raise it, and there is nothing in that shop for him to have been let off about: she is not somebody he owes a sack of ice to and he is not somebody who owes her one, and the whole business has stayed inside that shop and inside that yard between them.

On the Saturday morning he came in the back way with two sacks on a barrow and put them both in the passage at the back of that shop and said the man in the yard had had a load come in on the Thursday and would not have wanted to be troubled with the rest of it.

She took one of them. The other one is in the passage at the back of that shop against a stack of milk crates, and it is still there, and it has been there since the eighteenth of November.

**HE CAME BACK UP THAT ROAD ON THE TUESDAY OF THE FOLLOWING WEEK AND SAID NOTHING ABOUT ANY OF IT, AND THE SACK OF ICE IS STILL IN THAT PASSAGE, AND ON THE FRIDAY OF THE THIRD WEEK HE CAME IN THE BACK WAY AT HALF PAST EIGHT WITH A SACK ON A BARROW AND A CANVAS BAG OVER HIS SHOULDER AND SHE TOOK THE SACK AND SAID NOTHING AND HE SAID NOTHING AND THE COUNT OF WHAT THAT SHOP HAS HAD FROM HIM IS STILL THREE SACKS AND A BAG.**
