# Chapter 0719 — The Number She Did Not Check

Thursday the twenty-first of April 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number nine from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a jug lid she is mending again and a telephone she answered without going to look at anything.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES PICKS A RINGING TELEPHONE UP IN HER OWN HALL WITH BOTH HANDS FULL, IS ASKED WHETHER THIS IS NUMBER TEN, SAYS THAT IT IS, AND HAS NOT LOOKED AT HER OWN FRONT DOOR SINCE.**

She is a woman in her late fifties. She is in her own house all day and at her own gate at the end of it.

---

She is at the sink with the jug in one hand and a cloth in the other when the telephone goes, and it is the reason she picks it up.

The tape she laid over the crack in the jug lid on a Sunday in March has come away at one corner where she washed the jug and dried it wrong, and she has been meaning to do that since then, and the lid is in her left hand and the cloth is in her right and there is no free hand anywhere on her.

The telephone is in the hall on the little table under the stairs where the back door card hangs on its hinge, and it goes four times while she is standing in the kitchen with her arms full.

So she puts the jug down, goes out with her hands wet, and picks it up in the hall with the cloth still in her fist.

"Hello?"

"Is this number ten?"

And what comes out of her is not thought about and is the truest thing in the room.

"That's this one."

---

The woman on the other end says thank you and she will wait then, and puts the line down.

She stands in her own hall with the cloth going cold in her fist.

She has said it. There is no way back into the sentence. She lives at number nine and she has just told a stranger on a telephone that she is at number ten, and she did it in her own hall, at the table under the stairs, with a cloth in her hand and a jug lid she has been meaning to do since Sunday in March.

The thing she could have done, and did not, is put her hand over the mouthpiece and go and look. That is all it would have taken. She has a hall table and a drawer in it and a door with a number on it and it is thirty foot from where she was standing.

She does not even think about that as something she failed to do. What she thinks about, standing there, is that there is no reason on earth a person would ring a house and ask a question she did not want to answer, and she has been on her own since before Christmas, and she answered.

---

She goes back to the kitchen and finishes the jug.

The lid has the crack from rim to knob and there is a piece of brown tape on it gone pale at the corners and lifting at one end. She tears a piece off her own roll with her teeth — a hand's width this time, not a half inch — and lays it over the old one so the two overlap, and presses it down round the knob with her thumb, and sets the lid back on the jug and puts her hand on the side of it.

It keeps the look of the crack. That is all it does now. It has taken two layers and it will take a third and the lid is not a jug lid any more, it is a jug lid with tape on it.

She fills the jug at the tap and it shuts true when she turns it, which she notes every single time and which has never once been a surprise to her and which she could not tell you about.

---

She eats at the kitchen table and then she stands in the front room doorway, which is where she has stood most mornings since before Christmas.

The ash stool is opposite the window, three oak and one ash, grain showing, joints tight, with the space under it empty since the fifth of November and dust squared round where the cloth went. She dusts it once a week. She does not move it and she does not put anything under it.

The sixty-watt bulb is in the ceiling above it and has been since the seventh of December, and the fire below the stool has not been lit since Easter, and the room is cold and will be cold.

The six pads on the fridge are the same six pads. The first one is crossed twice, which is the only thing in her house that gets crossed.

She does not go in and sit down. She has not sat in that room since before Christmas and she is not going to start this week because a telephone went off, and she was nearly in the doorway twice and turned round both times and would not have admitted why.

---

At the middle of the day a woman rings again.

She picks it up before the second ring, because it is now a number she has answered once and a telephone that goes twice in a morning is a telephone that has business with you.

"Is that number ten?"

"I did say," she says.

"Right, sorry. She's not answering, is she."

"She's not answering," she says.

"Is she in?"

And here is where she has her chance, and it is the cleanest one anybody has given her in this house since before Christmas.

The woman is waiting for somebody who does not exist. If she says *there's nobody there*, that is the end of it, and the woman will go away and find the right number, and this house will be a place where a woman answered the telephone and got the wrong thing.

What she says is, "She might be out. She might be at the back."

Which is a true sentence, because there is a back yard and a back door and the card on the hinge shuts true.

"So I'll wait a bit."

"You can wait at the gate if you like," she says. "Number ten's the one with the green bin out."

She has never seen a green bin at number ten. She has said it because she had to say something, and because saying something is easier than saying nothing, and she has never in her life been able to work out which of the two is the harder thing to do.

---

She rings it back herself in the middle of the afternoon.

She does this because she has decided that she will tell her that there is nobody there, and because a woman standing at a gate waiting for somebody who is not coming is a thing she would not want to be let into.

And she gets as far as the second ring before she puts it down again, and she leaves it ringing once more, and it goes, and then the telephone at number nine is quiet.

What she cannot get past is the other one.

That woman is going to be at a gate in this borough by the middle of the afternoon on a Thursday waiting for somebody at number ten, and if she is not let in she will go up the road and ask somebody else, and the road goes up past the end of it where there is a lamp on a gate, and there are houses up there she does not know and has never been to and will not go to.

So the woman will stand at a gate. And she will do it on her own. And that is the whole of what she has done.

---

She is at her own gate at dark with the lamp behind her.

The back door is behind her with the card on the hinge and the padlock hanging open in the hasp where it has hung since the first of September, and the key is on its hook inside, and the yard is cold and the wall is green at the joints.

There is nothing on the front path. There is nothing anywhere.

She stands with her hands in her apron and looks down her own front path and out at the road, and the road has nothing on it, and she has never once gone up that road and she is not going to start now.

She goes in and shuts the back door behind her and it shuts true.

---

The jug is where she put it down, on the sink, with the cloth beside it, and the corner she tore off on Sunday has dried and is standing up off the crack now, so the lid sits crooked on the rim.

She wipes the sink round the tap and hangs the cloth on the rail over the bowl and stands looking at the lid.

Four words in a hall with a cloth in her fist, and it has come from the top of that jug.

She could lay a third layer on it and it would shut true and she would have said something. She is not going to say something to a jug, and a third layer would be saying something.

---

The other thing is the gate.

A woman in this borough is going to be standing at a gate somewhere with a bag, in the middle of a weekday, waiting for somebody who does not live at it, because of a bin that has never been there.

And the only way to find out whether there is a green bin at number ten is thirty foot of her own front path and a door she has not looked at since before Christmas.

She has stood in that hall this morning with a cloth in her hand and said the address out loud without going thirty foot to check what number is on it. She is not going to go out and check it now, in the dark, in the cold, at the end of the day, when the answer is the same as the answer she has already given.

She puts her hand flat on the lid, the way she puts her hand on things, and leaves the tape as it is.
