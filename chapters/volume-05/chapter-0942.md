# Chapter 0942 — Into The Zinc Fold

Saturday the twenty-seventh of September 2024, at the foot of the chimney stack at number twelve with the top of the stack in the light. Into a turned fold.

**ZINC CLOTHES THE BOTTOM OF THE STACK AT NUMBER TWELVE AND THE UPPER EDGE OF IT IS TURNED UP THE BRICK AND DOUBLED BACK ON ITSELF, AND SHE MEANS TO PUT THE FINGERS CROOKED AT THE FIRST KNUCKLE INTO THAT DOUBLING WITH THE KNUCKLES LEFT OUT IN THE AIR, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR IT CARRIES THE HAND AND WHAT STANDS AT THE END OF IT.**

The stack was built in the same week as the house and the zinc was dressed round the foot of it in the summer after the first winter, and two men have pointed the stack since, on two separate occasions, and neither of them touched the zinc. It is on the side that nobody walks past, and nothing about it has ever been in anybody's way.

It is a lining of zinc about eighteen inches wide let into the mortar of the stack's base course and carried up the face of the pot, where the upper edge is turned a full inch and folded back flat on itself. She wants the fold, and the fold is the turned part with its two thicknesses lying one on the other.

---

A turned fold is made by hammering the edge of the metal over a bar and then folding it back on itself by hand, and the hammering is done cold because a zinc edge will not turn at all once it is hot. In they go bent at the first joint, which puts the knuckles out in the air where anybody can see them and the fingertips in along the back of the fold.

The zinc is cold and dull to the touch, no thicker than the back of a playing card, and the arris of the turn is blunt the whole way along it, and blunter where a hand has been in before. The gap under that fold is as deep as the length of two fingers and it runs the whole width of the stack, and the floor of it is the face of the zinc below it.

At the two ends of the fold the space stops where the zinc goes round the pot. In the middle of it the zinc stands the width of a coin away from the brick, and the line of that gap is where a skin of grey powder from the stack's own mortar has collected.

---

A zinc apron at the foot of a stack is the oldest cheap answer to a chimney and the one that is put up wherever a chimney has to meet render. The zinc goes in under the render or under the mortar, up the face of the brick, and then a lip is turned over so that the water coming off the stack runs out and away from the wall instead of down into the joint it came from.

A lip turned a full inch and folded back is a great deal more metal than a lip turned over a bar. It weighs more, it costs more, and it is the version that gets put up by a man who has never had to argue with anybody about the price of a length of zinc. What it gives for that inch is a gutter that will run for eighty years, because a gutter in sheet metal is not a joint at all but a channel, and a channel cannot come apart.

What it also does is keep whatever arrives. A turned lip keeps the water it has thrown and takes in every grain that came down with it, and grit that arrives in a gutter stays there and stays in the corner where the fold closes, and the corner of that fold is the last place in the whole arrangement that anything can be washed out of.

What that zinc was put there to do, it has never once stopped doing. Every year since it went up, all the rain that has come down the face of that stack has gone into the channel of that fold and out over the front of it onto the yard, and not one drop of it has gone into the mortar at the base of the stack.
The mortar there is two courses back from the edge of the brick and it is as sound as it was in the first winter, and a stack and its fold are the only two things on that side of that house a person can say that much about.

The brick behind the fold has been pointed twice, and both of those pointers had to come back to the zinc at this line because a mortar joint has to stop at a sheet metal edge and not go behind it. The second pointer left a good deal more lime along that fold, and under it is the lime of the first pointer, gone hard and pale.

The powder in the gap is neither of those. It is the face of the mortar above the zinc breaking down a grain at a time and staying exactly where it falls, because the zinc is the only thing under it and the zinc is flat and level and does not shed. Sixty years of that is about the depth of a thumb-nail.

Out of the wash-house door comes her sister, sleeves turned up past the elbows, and she crosses the yard towards the gate with the stack behind her the whole way. The whole of that was about the milkman and whether the hour has been put back again, and the foot of that stack stood three feet away from both of them throughout.

Sheet metal is a poor material for a fold and a good one for a channel, and the reason is the same fact. A sheet has no grain and no fibres and it will go on being folded for a very long time, and the only thing that will tell you how long is the crease itself. A crease in sheet zinc is not a sharp line; it is a radius of about a tenth of an inch across, and it is the blunter the more it is worked.

That is why the turn on this one has gone blunt all the way along it while the sheet itself is still flat and still sound. The fold was hammered on a bar in one afternoon and it has been hammered again by weather ever since, and a hammered edge in soft sheet metal loses its corner inside twenty years and takes about eighty to lose the whole of the radius.

Underneath the fold, on the zinc that it lies on, there is a line that is a shade darker than the rest of it. That is not damp and it is not rust; it is the shadow in the corner itself, which is the same length as the fold and the same width and will be the same shape in another eighty years.

The stack behind all of this is brick, and the brick is a soft red that came out of a kiln about four miles off and was laid in the summer the house went up. A stack of that brick on a wall of that height is the first thing on a house to need pointing and the last thing anybody wants to point, because the man who points it has to put a ladder up against the guttering to get at the bottom of it.

She puts the fingers in that fold once more along its whole width, and they go in the same distance at every point of it and come out the same, and there is nothing at the far end of that fold except more of the same zinc turned over.

The fold keeps its inch and its two thicknesses and its quarter of an inch of space and its thumb-nail of powder, and nothing about that zinc has shifted since it was dressed in a summer sixty-odd years ago, and a hand put into it to-day goes in as far as the fold carries it and comes out with grey on the first joints, save that the pot at either end of it stops the fold short of the full width of the stack and what lies beyond those two ends is not a thing a hand can get into at all.
