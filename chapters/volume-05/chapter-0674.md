# Chapter 0674 — Stepping Over

Friday the fifteenth of October 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from morning until midday, and a man comes up her path in the middle of it, and the whole of it is a brush on a floor and a box on a form and a gate standing open.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT STEPS OVER A BRUSH ON HER OWN PASSAGE FLOOR ON A FRIDAY MORNING TO REACH HER OWN KITCHEN, HAS A COUNCIL MAN AT HER OPEN GATE WITH A FORM THAT ASKS WHETHER THERE IS WATER STAINING, LOOKS AT A PALE PATCH ON A FRONT ROOM CEILING SHE WILL NOT SIT UNDER, LEAVES THE BOX UNTICKED, AND COMES BACK IN AND SHUTS THE FRONT DOOR.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is on her own all morning, and for a while in the middle of it she is not, and the not being on her own does not help her.

---

She wakes with the passage light still on and the brush still in the middle of the runner about two feet inside the front door, handle across the walking line so that you cannot step down the side of it without turning your foot.

She gets her dressing gown from the back of the front door and steps over it on her way to the kitchen, right foot then left, without looking down any longer, because looking down would make it a thing she was checking.

The kitchen is at the back of that house and the front room is between the front door and the kitchen, which is why a woman washing her hands goes through the room she has decided not to sit in.

She eats toast at the table under the window on the green chair with the cushion on it, and the table stands dead flat on a shim planed and sawn flush under the window-side foot, leg back in dry with a card on the joint, no glue.

The gas envelope is face up square with the edge, pencil side up, six things on the back in pencil, nothing crossed off. A parcel for her that is not hers is on the left of the table with a cold smell of ice coming off it, and the envelope has not moved and is not going to.

She does not pick it up. There is no pencil in that house and there has not been since before Christmas.

---

She wants to get to the kitchen and back without moving the brush, because the brush has been on the floor since the first of September unwashed with a curl of old egg-coloured gloss in the crimp, and moving it would make it a thing she had decided about again.

She washes the plate and the cup and puts them on the drainer, and she wipes the cloth flat on the table and pushes the green chair in.

She goes to the bathroom under the landing and empties the bowl with the folded cloth in it under the bath tap, morning and night, and puts it back.

The brown mark on the bathroom ceiling is still there, and the pale patch on the front room ceiling is still there just inside the door, small with a darker middle, and the two are one thing in her head now, the fifth thing and the second thing joined by water.

The back room window is done, a tape sheet with a blanket folded on a mattress. The hotplate on the left is done. The passage light is done.

The gate is done. The front room is not done. The bath tap is not done.

And there is now a printed card in an apron pocket with a free number on it for a survey of water staining, and a man in a white van who will say he saw nothing in a front room on the fourth of October because a woman at a kitchen window had her back to the glass, and neither of those is a line on the paper and neither of those is ever going to be.

---

She stands in the front room doorway with her hands on the frame and looks at the patch without going in, and the rush-seated chair is against the wall by the window and nobody sits on it, and the sideboard has nothing on it.

She has not sat in there since the sixteenth of July. The ceiling in that room is white round the patch and it will not be white by the winter.

She does not go in. Going in would make her a woman being asked about a thing in her own front room.

---

She puts her coat on over her apron and goes out of the front door and shuts it behind her, and the front door is not on the latch and it has not been on the latch since before Christmas.

She goes round the side on the grass past the coal shed on the walked-flat line, and the grass is wet and the air is cold in a way it was not in September.

The gate is standing back against the wall of the coal shed out of the way, and the path is clear, and the road has nobody on it, and there is a man on the path.

---

He has come up the path from the road and is standing in the open where the gate is, with a clipboard, and he has got as far as the gate and no further, because the gate is back against the wall and the path is clear and there is nothing at the gap to stop him, and he is the sort of man who does not need stopping.

"Morning."

She stops on the grass.

"Morning."

"You're the one at number twelve?"

"That's me."

"Right. Can I come up the path a moment? I've got a form and I'm in a hurry." He is already moving. "It's about the light."

"The light's there."

"Aye, it's there. That's not me, that's the contractor. We do the paperwork after." He looks at the lamp on its bracket and then back at her, and he has the ordinary face of a man who has done four hundred of these. "I need the number on here, and I need to know if it's yours or somebody else's, and I need to know if you want it on a bracket or moved round to the corner, because the corner lights the path up better and it's free to do at the same time."

"The bracket's fine."

"You're sure? Because the corner's the better one. Everybody takes the bracket because it's what's there."

"The bracket's where it was, so leave it."

"Bracket, then."

He writes on the clipboard and gets a pen out and offers it to her, and she takes it, and it is a ballpoint on a bit of plastic with a plastic cap on it, and she puts the cap off with her teeth because her hands have the paste on them from the breakfast, and she is aware of the paste on her hands.

She looks at the form and reads the four lines and gives him the number off the door and says that is the name as well, because it is the same word, and he writes it down in the box.

"Thank you."

"That's it, is it?"

"There's one more bit." He turns the clipboard round and puts a finger on the bottom half. "While I've got you — it's an easy one. Condition of the building. There's a box for damp, and a box for water staining, and a box for neither."

She looks at the three boxes.

"Any water staining in the house? Ceilings, walls, the lot."

"I have a bath tap."

"Right."

"It's been dripping since before Christmas. I empty a bowl under it morning and night."

"That's not what I mean."

"I know what you mean," she says.

And he is a decent man, and he looks at her face, and she can see him decide not to press it, and she watches him decide it, and it takes about a second and a half.

"No? You're all right with the ceilings?"

"I'm all right with the ceilings."

"Right." He taps the middle box with the pen. "So that's a no, then."

"That's a no."

"That's the no box ticked, not the yes box. Just so we're not going the wrong way in a year."

"That's the no box."

He writes a tick in the box for neither, and then he tears a slip off the bottom of the form and gives it to her, which is a printed card with a number on it and a bit of a survey company's address, and says it's if anything changes, ring that number and it's a local call and no charge, it's a free call, and she knows those three words, she knows them in a bright voice off an answerphone in the summer.

So he has said it. It is on a card in her coat pocket and it is a free call and it is twenty minutes and it is a woman with a van who will come and stand in a room where a rush chair stands against a wall.

"Thank you," she says, and means nothing by it, and puts the card in the apron pocket on top of the two drop bolts, and the card goes in and stays in.

"I'll leave you to it, then."

"Right."

"Sorry to bother you."

"You haven't bothered me."

He goes back down the path and along the road. She stands on the grass and watches him get to the end of it and turn, and the road has nobody on it again, and the gate is standing back against the wall of the coal shed out of the way, and the path is clear.

---

Both drop bolts are in the pocket of the apron she has on, and she feels them through the cloth and leaves them there, because leaving them there is what keeps the gate open.

She stands on the inside of her own gate with the path behind her and the road in front of her, and she does not go out through it and does not stand in the gap and does not look up and down the road for long.

Somewhere on that road there is a woman who clears houses with a drawer shut under a bench in a van, and a man who built the stool with nine pounds' worth of brush in an open drawer under a bench in another van, and the sister at number ten who knocked once and handed tape over at the doorway, and the woman three doors down with a stool against a wall in a front room.

None of them is coming today, and none of them has been told any of this, and there is a woman in a room on the other side of the borough who has put a tick in a box for a man and got a free number for it and would probably think she was being helpful, which she was.

---

She goes back in through the front door and shuts it behind her, because it is shut when she shuts it, and it does not latch and it never has.

She is standing in her own passage with the brush in the middle of the runner, and she steps over it on her way to her own kitchen, right foot then left, the way she has done since the first.

That is the whole of the morning. The first time she got past a thing by stepping over it she thought she was being clever, and it has turned into every time, and there is no morning coming that it will not do.

She sits at the table with the card in the apron in the passage and the parcel on the left of the table and the envelope square with the edge on the right of it, and the front room ceiling is white round the patch, and the front room ceiling is going to be brown round the patch by Christmas.

Four of six is done. There is no pencil in that house, and there is now a printed card in her apron pocket with a free number on it, and she will not ring it, and the not ringing it is going to cost her the ceiling in the room she will not sit in, and she has put herself in a position where the only person on that road who could have said a thing about the patch was standing on her path with a pen in his hand, and she looked at him, and he asked her, and she said no.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS PUT NOTHING AWAY AND HAS SHUT NOTHING THAT WAS OPEN EXCEPT HER OWN FRONT DOOR WHICH DOES NOT LATCH, AND HAS BEEN ASKED BY A MAN WITH A FORM WHETHER THERE IS WATER COMING THROUGH HER CEILING AND HAS SAID THAT THERE IS NOT, AND HAS LEFT A BRUSH WHERE EVERYBODY WALKS AND A GATE WHERE NOBODY GOES, AND FOUR OF SIX IS DONE AND NOTHING HAS BEEN CROSSED OFF.**
