# Chapter 0635 — The Chair Off The Back Step

Monday the nineteenth of April 2021, in this borough. She is in a garage off a road behind the parade from about half past seven in the morning and the last two hours of it are on her own back step in the last of the light, and the whole of it happens in one borough.

**A WOMAN WHO CLEARS HOUSES FOR A LIVING IS IN A GARAGE OFF A ROAD BEHIND THE PARADE AND IS GOING TO TAKE A FOLDING CHAIR WITH A SPLIT LEG OUT OF IT EVEN THOUGH THE LIST DOES NOT HAVE IT ON IT, AND SHE HAS ALREADY DECIDED WHICH KITCHEN IT IS GOING TO, AND NOBODY IN THIS CHAPTER ASKS HER WHY.**

She is a woman in her late forties. There is a man in his early twenties in the garage with her. He is on her books on a Thursday and a Friday, he is on a school holiday, and she has him cheap because he was outside her own gate at half past seven.

The garage belongs to a woman who is not in it. She is at her sister's in another town and she rings at about ten and again at about one.

The garage is about as long as a van and it is full in a way that is not tidy. It is full in the way of a house where two people have both died and the family have come in from a distance and put everything in one side of a double garage and shut the door.

---

The list is on the back of an invoice in the woman's handwriting, and the handwriting is not good.

One: fridge, upright, working.
Two: freezer, chest, working.
Three: mattress, double, on the roof.
Four: two chairs, wooden, dining.
Five: lamp, standard, no shade.
Six: rugs, three.
Seven: boxes, about six, mixed.

And she has got six of the seven things off. The seventh is not going in the van. The mattress is soaked through and she is not moving that down a path in a van with a plastic sheet over the back of it, so that is a telephone call, and the woman on the other end says take it to the tip, and the woman in her late forties says right.

That is the shape of the first four hours.

The young man is better with the upstairs than she is and she sends him up and he does not argue, which she notices, because the last one argued.

He is on the loft ladder with the mattress edge when she says, "Leave that. That's not going anywhere."

And he says, "It's fine."

And she says, "It's soaked. Look at the corner."

And he says, "It's a bit damp."

And she says, "That's soaked. Put it back down and we'll ring them."

He puts it back down and comes down the ladder and stands in the middle of the garage with his hands out.

And she says, "That's not on you. That's on the roof."

That is most of what is said between them until about one.

She finds the chair at about ten to twelve, behind the chest freezer, standing up against the wall on its edge with its back to the plaster.

It is a folding chair. Steel frame, green seat, the sort of thing that comes in four from a shop and goes back in four when nobody is looking. The hinge at the back leg has gone. Not rusted through. The pin has pulled out of the tube about a third of the way, the leg has splayed, and the whole thing leans back the width of a fist.

She gets it out and sets it on the floor and looks at it for a while, standing, with her arms folded, the way she stands at a kitchen door in this borough a great deal of the time.

The hinge pin is out. It is not broken. That is the thing she can tell in about two seconds by wiggling it, and a hinge pin that is out is ten minutes with a punch and a hammer and a bit of swearing, and the chair is worth exactly that much and not one minute more.

And it is not on the list.

She puts a hand on the freezer to shift it and finds the floor under it dry, which is a surprise, and stands there with a hand on a chest freezer thinking about a chair that is not on a piece of paper.

Number twelve, on a road that comes off the parade, third house on the left going up past the postbox. There is a woman there in her late seventies. She has known the woman there in a way that is not a friendship, which is that she has taken two clearances on that road and the woman at number twelve has let her stand in the passage both times and once brought her a cup of tea without being asked, and they have talked about a bin, and about nothing.

And the woman at number twelve eats standing up.

That is the whole of what she knows. She has known it the way you know a thing about a person that they have never told you, which is that a bowl goes from the hob to the sill and the person stands at the sill to eat out of it and it goes back in the sink and they wipe the front of the worktop with the cloth and that is the whole of the meal.

And she has asked herself why she noticed it, and the answer is that she cannot get a kitchen chair down a step and out of a house without one. She has carried two of them and a table and a chest of drawers out of a house on that road before Christmas, and on both of them she went back into a kitchen to get a roll of tape, and the woman at number twelve was not in the kitchen, and both times she heard the same noise behind her, and both times the noise was somebody eating.

That is how she knows. Not the woman. The noise.

---

She rings the woman on the other end at about one and asks her about the chair.

And the woman says, "Which chair."

And she says, "There's a folding one. Green. Behind the freezer."

And the woman says, "Oh, that thing. That's my mother's. Is it broken."

And she says, "The back leg's come off the hinge."

And the woman says, "Well, I'd rather it didn't go. I've been trying to ring you about it all morning. It came out of the house we cleared in March and I said to him I'd not have it back, and I have not told him I've put it in here."

And she says, "Can you take it off me."

And she says, "Yes."

And the woman says, "That's you then. Genuinely. Take it off me and I won't have to think about it again."

And the woman says, "You'll not tell him."

And she says, "He'll ask where it's gone."

And she says, "I'll say the skip."

That is the whole of the arrangement and it takes about ninety seconds, and it is the only theft in the whole day, and it is made lawful in ninety seconds by a woman who wants to be rid of a thing she is ashamed of having kept.

The woman in her late forties has never in her life taken anything that was not offered. That is not true and she knows it is not true, and it feels true because everything else she has taken she took off a skip or out of a house with the family standing in it.

The chair is different and the difference is that nobody is standing in the garage.

She takes it home in the back of the van and the young man does not ask, and she puts it in her own back kitchen stood up against the wall under the stairs where the hoover goes in the summer, and he is gone by about ten past four.

Then she comes back out with a spanner, a punch, a hammer, and a length of angle iron off a gate that came off its hinges and that she meant to take back and never did.

She does it on the back step because the light is there until about seven at this time of year, and there is no bench, and the step is cold by about half past five, and she would rather be cold than have it in the kitchen where she can see it.

The leg is on the wrong side of the hinge for a punch. She gets a flat screwdriver in the gap, a block of wood on the step, and knocks the pin up from underneath with the screwdriver handle. The pin comes about halfway and stops. She knocks it harder and the screwdriver skids off and puts a mark in the wood of the step, which she notices and is annoyed about for the rest of the evening.

Then the pin is out and it is not bent, and she puts it back with a washer off a box of tap washers under the sink, and she can see the movement is about a finger's width at the top, and she decides that is fine, and it is not fine, and she knows it is not fine by the time she has opened it and folded it twice.

So she does the other thing as well, which is a scrap of angle iron about a hand's breadth, two holes drilled with a brace she has not used in a bit and has to clean the burr off, and a bolt through both cheeks of the hinge and the nut on the back.

The brace bit slips once. The pilot hole goes in on a slant and she fills it and does it again.

The whole thing is about two hours, and she does it once, badly, and then at about twenty to seven she takes it to pieces again and files the leg where it has been rubbing and puts the pin back a third of the way through instead of all the way, which is the trick of it and which she knew before she started. Then the chair stands on the step in the last of the light with its four feet on the ground.

She sits on it to see.

It is a chair. It is not a good chair. It is the sort of chair a garden would have, and the green is the green of a thing bought without thinking, and there is a ring worn on the seat.

She sits on it for about a minute and a half. The ring is where somebody sat and had a cup of tea. The ring has gone through to the metal in one place and the metal has gone back a long way.

Here is the part of the day she tells nobody about, and she has been over it since, and it is the only part that has moved anything.

She could take it on the Monday. That was available to her at about ten past five with the chair mended and the light going off the side wall.

And she cannot take it without a conversation, and the conversation is going to be a sentence she has to say out loud, and she can feel the whole shape of the one she would have to have on the front step at number twelve.

It would go like this. I found this. I had it off a woman clearing her mother's house. I thought, you could sit down. And then the woman at number twelve would have to say something back, and there is only one thing anybody in this borough says back for a thing like that, and it is four syllables long and it means nothing at all, and she is not going to say it.

She has not said it on either of the two occasions she has had a cup of tea in that kitchen, and the woman in her late forties has never mentioned it, because mentioning it would have been a thing she said about a woman of seventy-odd, and a woman of seventy-odd does not want a stranger in her forties keeping a tally of her manners. But she has been keeping the tally, because that is what she is.

And here is the arithmetic of it, done on a back step at about half past five, and it took about four seconds.

In this borough, when somebody brings you a thing, the thing you say back is not a thing you say because you mean it. It is a thing you have to say, and it is said in a particular voice, and the voice is the signal that a debt has just been opened, and a debt in this borough is a standing invitation, and a standing invitation is somebody coming up your path over and over for as long as you can stand it.

And the woman at number twelve has not got a debt with anybody. That is not a compliment. It is the shape of how she lives. The last person who brought her something regular did her roof and moved to another town, and a roof is not a debt, a roof only comes once.

So she will take it round on a Sunday, when a woman in her late seventies lets a strange woman in without a reason, and a Sunday is not a day anybody keeps a list of.

And she is not going to say where it came from.

That is the decision, and it is the only one she makes on the Monday, and it is not the one anybody would call a decision. It is the reason a chair with a split leg is standing against a wall in a back kitchen in this borough, and a woman in her late seventies is at a kitchen window on a road off the parade eating out of a saucepan standing up, and the two of them are a mile apart and do not know it.

She took the chair in at about ten past eight with the lamp off, and stood it back against the wall under the stairs, and put her hand on it in the dark the way you do.

Then she washed the file and the punch and the spanner and put the tea towel out on the line because it was wet, and stood looking at the sink.

The angle iron is still out on the back step. She is aware, standing at the sink, that she has left it there, and that the only thing she wants out of the morning is that it is a Sunday and that the light is good.

---

**SHE SPENT MOST OF A DAY IN A GARAGE AND TOOK OUT A CHAIR THAT WAS NOT ON THE LIST, AND SHE MENDED IT ON HER OWN BACK STEP WITH A PUNCH AND A HAMMER AND A PIECE OF ANGLE IRON OFF A GATE SHE NEVER TOOK BACK, AND AT TEN PAST EIGHT IN THE DARK SHE STOOD IT AGAINST A WALL UNDER HER STAIRS TO GIVE IT ON A SUNDAY, AND THE REASON SHE WILL NOT GIVE IT ON ANY OTHER DAY IS THAT SHE CANNOT THINK OF ONE SENTENCE SHE IS WILLING TO SAY OUT LOUD AND BE OWED FOR.**
