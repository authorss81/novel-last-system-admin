# Chapter 0316 — Two Rooms

Saturday the twenty-second of April 2017, from about half past seven in the evening, in a hall on Carrow Lane in Tideglass, and later in that evening in the room at the back of a shop off a parade.

**A MAN IN HIS SIXTIES SAID SOMETHING ABOUT A LAMP OVER A DOOR IN A ROOM THAT HAS HEARD A THING SAID BEFORE, AND THE MAN NEXT TO HIM SAID IT AGAIN IN A DIFFERENT ROOM WITH NO IDEA HE WAS DOING IT, AND HALF AN HOUR LATER A MAN CAME IN AND SAID WHERE IT HAD COME FROM, AND TWO QUESTIONS WERE PUT ABOUT IT IN A ROOM AT THE BACK OF A SHOP AND NEITHER OF THEM GOT AN ANSWER.**

The hall on Carrow Lane is a long room with a folding table set the length of it, a bit of carpet worn through in front of the hearth, and an overhead fitting on a pull that everybody in that room pulls at some point in an evening without anybody saying anything about it.

There are seven in there. The man in his forties at the near end with his hands either side of a cup. The man in his sixties in the seat that has got the table leg coming through it. The man in his thirties, who had a bag at his feet and had come in about ten minutes before the gas came on because he had been in the weather. The woman who works in a shop, in from the parade about ten minutes before that. The woman of about fifty-five, in a coat, at the far end. A man with a walking stick who comes when it is warm. And a man who does the hall, who was putting a tablecloth over the corner of the table because there is something on that table he does not want seen, and he was not quick enough about it.

He did it when the door shut.

The man in his sixties had a cup he had not drunk from and he was looking at the strip fitting at the back of that room, which does not work and has not worked since before Christmas.

"There's a lamp over that door," he said. "The bank. Up at the corner."

"Aye."

"I was in the front room at about two in the afternoon on the twentieth and that lamp was on, and it was broad daylight, and I looked at it long enough to know it was on. And then about a quarter of an hour later I was in the front room again and a man was stood in the road under it, looking up at it, with a woman and a clipboard."

The man in his forties did not look up.

"He's doing what he likes with a lamp."

"He's doing a thing with it," said the man in his sixties. "He had his hands on his own head and then he had one of them up and he was measuring it with his thumb, the way you do. And then he put his hands down and went back in. And the light went off about ten minutes later and the lamp's been off every time I've looked since."

The man in his forties said that.

"I've been up there twice in three weeks and you're telling me about a man standing in the road."

"I'm not telling you anything," said the man in his sixties. "I'm saying a man stood in a road and looked at a lamp. That's all that's happened. There's nothing in it. I said it in here because I was in a room and that's what you do with a thing like that, and I can see straight away I ought to have had it in the other room and I didn't, and now it's in here."

The woman who works in a shop turned her cup about a half turn on the table.

"You've had that in two rooms now," she said, "and it's about a lamp in a bank in a town where there is no bank anybody in this room works at, and none of us has ever been inside, and none of us is going to."

"A bank is a bank," said the man in his forties, and drank his tea.

---

The room at the back of a shop off a parade is a room with a kettle, a fold-down table with a lip round it, one chair, a rack of delivery notes that is a shelf of paper with a batten across it, and a window out of which you cannot see anything but a yard.

The man who works nights was in there at about ten to eight with his coat still on, waiting on the kettle, because the man who cooks in that kitchen was in and out all afternoon and had gone out again.

The man in his thirties came in with his bag and put it down and asked the kettle was hot, and the man who works nights said it was going to be, and then said that the man in his sixties had been talking about a lamp.

"I heard him," said the man in his thirties. "I was in that room."

"Aye, well, he's been at it since seven."

"About a lamp over a door at the bank. Somebody's put a man in the road under it to look at it. And the man went back in."

"And that's the whole of it?"

"That's the whole of it."

The woman who works in a shop came in with the till sheet folded in four and put it on the lip of that table and started going down it with a pen, and the man in his thirties said the same thing over again to her, because she had not been there, and she stopped writing.

"How long has that lamp been on in the daytime," she said.

"About a quarter of an hour. Maybe less."

"You don't know."

"I was in a front room looking at a light and then I was in a front room not looking at a light."

"Whose lamp is it," said the woman who works in a shop.

"I don't know whose it is."

"Well, that's the lamp you were told about."

She went back down the sheet.

The man who works nights said, "Is that one of the four?"

"No," said the woman who works in a shop, without looking up. "It's not one of the four and we're not setting it beside any of the four, and I'm not saying it isn't. It's a lamp. If you put a lamp beside a thing none of us can see, then one day somebody's going to put a road sign beside it and we'll be doing all of that in here instead of drinking the tea."

The man who works nights said aye, well, and the man in his thirties stood in that room for about a minute doing nothing at all, and then he picked his bag up off the floor.

The man in his forties came in at about ten past nine.

He had a folded scrap in the pocket of his coat that he did not take out.

"You weren't in the hall on Carrow Lane tonight," he said to the man in his thirties, which was not a question.

"I was in the hall on Carrow Lane tonight," said the man in his thirties. "I've been in it since half seven."

"I know you have," said the man in his forties. "I was in it. What I mean is that there's four people going to know you said that tonight by tomorrow, and there's a fifth person going to know it by Monday, because the man in his sixties has been in a room in this town that I'm not going to describe to you, and he will have said a thing about a lamp in it, because that is what he does with things."

"Then it's in five places," said the man in his thirties.

"It's in five places and there is nobody going to any of them, and that is what I am telling you," said the man in his forties. "You have just been in that room and passed that on to a man and a woman in the back of a shop, and neither of them asked you for it, and one of them asked you two questions and could not get one answer out of you about whose lamp it is, and the two of you are now four people who know a thing about a light in a bank in a town none of you has been in. And on Monday there'll be five. And in a year, or whenever the six of you have got a book of your own, somebody's going to write it down as a thing that was found out about, and it will have been said in a room, and it was said in a room by a man who admitted he ought to have had it in the other room."

The woman who works in a shop said, "That's not a fair way to put it."

"It's the most fair way I can put it and I have been putting it this way since January," said the man in his forties, and went out.

**AND A LAMP OVER A DOOR IN A TOWN NONE OF THEM HAS BEEN IN IS NOW IN FIVE ROOMS, AND A MAN IN HIS THIRTIES CARRYING A BAG STOOD IN A ROOM AT THE BACK OF A SHOP AND HEARD THAT HE HAD BEEN IN ANOTHER ROOM AND HAD NOT KNOWN IT, AND HE WENT OUT INTO THE STREET AND THE STREET WAS THE SAME AS IT HAD BEEN AT HALF PAST SEVEN.**
