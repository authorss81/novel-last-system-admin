# Chapter 0727 — The Hall Line With Nothing Under It

Sunday the twenty-second of May 2022, in this borough. She is at the lock-up from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a pad with a ruled line and a bag that gets tied twice.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES SORTS RAGS AT THE LOCK-UP BENCH, RULES A NEW LINE IN HER INVOICE PAD FOR A CUPBOARD JOB AND LEAVES THE OLD HALL LINE EMPTY, SPEAKS TO THE MAN WITH KEYS ABOUT THE VAN, AND TIES THE RAG BAG SHUT BEFORE SHE LEAVES.**

She is a woman in her late forties at the lock-up on Sunday. A man with keys comes round to the bench near midday and she speaks to him out there.

---

She starts with the rags behind the bench, the heap she has worked from since October.

The blanket is in there, set aside and unopened since the twelfth of November. She lifts it out with both hands and sets it on the bench without unfolding it, and works through the rest, shaking out shirts and sheets and putting the good ones in one pile and the torn ones in another for the bin.

The blanket stays folded. She does not open it. A blanket that has sat unopened half a year can sit a Sunday longer, and she has no house on her books that wants one.

She puts it back under the rags where it was, flat and square, and dusts her hands on her apron.

---

At the middle of the morning she gets the invoice pad out of the van door pocket and sits on the bench with it on her knee.

The last page has a hall line ruled on it from the thirtieth of April, and there is nothing under it. That has never happened to her before in ten months of work, a job finished that she cannot put a mark on, and she looks at it a long while.

She does not fill it in. Filling it without the scene that fills it would be worse than leaving it empty, and she knows that the way she knows not to write a number she has only heard once.

She turns to a fresh page and rules a new line for the cupboard job off the ring road, two doors and a shelf, and writes the road and the date under it in her own hand. The hall line stays where it was, empty, on the page before.

She puts the pad back in the van pocket and shuts the pocket flap.

---

In the middle of the day the man with keys comes round the van to the bench with the drawer under it.

The drawer is shut. It has been shut since the middle of November with leads and screws inside it, and she does not open it, because the cupboard job wants nothing out of it.

"You want the van moved?" he says, with his keys in his hand.

"No," she says. "Leave it where it is. I am in and out of the back all day."

He looks at the piles of rags and the folded blanket on top and then away again, the way men look at washing that is not theirs.

"That bag wants tying," he says, nodding at the rag bag by her foot with its mouth open.

"I know," she says. "I will do it before I go. It stays open till then for the torn ones."

He nods and goes back to the doors, and she goes back to the sorting, and the bag stays open between them like a mouth that has not finished speaking.

---

She works the afternoon through, steady, without hurry.

Behind the bench the box sleeps under rags where it has lain since late October. She does not pull it out and she does not look behind it. The borrowed ladder she left in a porch on the third of December is not hers to think about, and she does not think about it.

At the end she gathers the torn pile into the rag bag and ties it shut with a double knot, and then she unties it and puts one more shirt in and ties it shut again, tighter this time.

She stands with the tied bag at her feet and the bench clear behind her, and the van doors open on a tidy back with the pad in the pocket and the blanket out of sight, and she looks at what she has done and it is enough for a Sunday.

She shuts the van doors one after the other and tries both, and they stay shut, and she walks away round the front with the keys in her hand and the bag tied behind her in the dark of the lock-up.
