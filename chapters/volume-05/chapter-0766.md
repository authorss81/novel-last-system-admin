# Chapter 0766 — Three Boards On The Path

Thursday the twentieth of October 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house and yard at number ten from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is three boards laid down a muddy path and every rain run along them into the one place they were put down to stop.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES MEANS TO DIG TWO YARDS OF MUD OUT OF THE PATH TO HER SHED AND PUT GRAVEL IN IT, HER SISTER LAYS THREE BOARDS DOWN IT INSTEAD, AND THE WATER OFF THE WHOLE YARD NOW COMES DOWN THE BOARDS AND OFF THE LOW END IN ONE PLACE.**

She is a woman in her early thirties at number ten on Thursday. Her sister comes through the gate in the morning and does the second half her own way.

---

The back door is shut true on its latch and the washing is in and folded along the table. Nothing has come out of the drawer under the worktop in a long while, and what is in there is a hammer, a screwdriver and a folded piece of paper with three lines written on it. The line across the yard is up and bare. The ply offcut is still standing where it has stood against the front of the shed, not nailed, and the felt above it is lifting at its top corner. At the end of the yard behind the shed the bed is as she left it, and the second row along the middle stands a paler colour than the rows either side.

The path from the back door to the shed door is firm for about five yards and mud for the last two of them. It has been mud since June, and the mud is two yards long because that is how long she has been going round it with the barrow, and the grass side has gone bare where her boots go.

Under the path there is hardcore she packed by hand the spring before last and it has never shifted. What has shifted is the top inch of grit, and that has gone into the mud and made the mud, and the mud has gone down into the hardcore where the path is lowest. At the top of the two yards there is a step down of two inches across the whole width. It was not there in the spring.

She has a spade, a barrow and a plank, and she means to lift the mud out, barrow it to the far end, tip in the sack of gravel from under the bench and rake it flat.

Her sister comes through the gate in the morning with her coat over her arm.

---

"You are going to dig that out."

"I am going to dig that out."

"For two yards."

"For two yards." She does not stop with the spade. "And it will be the same in a year, because it is the barrow that has done it and the barrow is going to keep going over it."

"Then you will dig it out every year for the rest of your life." Her sister stops at the top of the path. "That is not a path you have got wrong. That is a path you have not got, in those two yards."

"Then what am I meant to have."

"Boards." Her sister takes her coat off. "Three of them, down the two yards, straight, laid along the fall. Your boots on boards, the barrow wheels on boards, nothing at all going over that mud, and by the spring the mud has dried from the sides and gone to dust."

"That will not stop the mud coming back."

"Nothing stops mud coming back." Her sister is looking at the wall of the shed for something. "The mud comes back because you go over it wet. Stop going over it wet and you have two yards of hard ground with three boards on it, and when the boards rot through you put three more on and that is the end of it."

---

She does not want boards. She wants the gravel and has wanted it since the spring, and there is a sack of it standing at the back of the shed behind the bench since she brought it home in May, and she has been waiting all summer for a day when she is not too tired to put it down.

"You have had that sack since May."

"I have had it since May."

"What is it for." Her sister looks in through the shed door. "You had a bucket of grit off the front drive in the summer and spread it an inch deep on that path and it did nothing at all, and the sack has been standing in there since May because you keep meaning to do a day on it."

"It was the size of nothing on it."

"It was. And you have the size of a sack and a half standing in there." Her sister comes back to the top of the mud. "So which of those two things are you doing."

"I am digging it out."

"Then put the spade down and carry the boards first, and tell me they are wrong after a week of them."

She puts the spade down.

---

The boards are off the top of the fence at the far end of the garden, three of them, silver where they have been up there since before she came, with a nail hole in each.

Her sister lays them down the two yards, one above the other, each lapping the next by the thickness of a board so a wheel goes from one to the next and does not drop. The first goes at the top against the firm edge, the second below it, the third at the bottom with its far end standing out over the mud. The middle board is the one that sits in the lowest part, and she finds that on her own without looking for it.

They are good, and that is the trouble with them. The mud under them is untouched, the barrow goes up and down over three boards and off the end of the third one without touching a yard of ground, and the whole of it is an afternoon instead of two days.

"You will not get the barrow up that at the shed end."

"You will get it up. The end board is over the firm ground and not over the mud. It only looks as though it is not."

"Then where does the water go."

Her sister stops with her foot against the sack of gravel.

"Where does it go now."

"It goes down the path." She points with the spade at the two yards. "And the two yards spread it. That is the whole of what those two yards have done since June. Water comes off the side of the house and off the two apple trees and it comes down the path and goes into that mud and spreads over about three feet of it and soaks away. That is what a path is for."

"And you have put boards on it."

"I have put boards on it."

---

They do not see it until the rain comes in the afternoon, and there is rain coming in the afternoon. It comes in from the west and goes through the yard in half an hour, hard enough to hear on the shed roof, and they stand in the kitchen doorway and watch.

The mud does not come back. It stays where it is under three boards, as dry looking as it was before lunch. But the water comes off the roof along the channel where the eaves have always run it, comes down the five firm yards, and finds the top board.

The top board lies flat across the width of the path, so the water gets on it and goes along it, because that is what a board laid across a fall does, and it goes along all three of them and comes off the end, where the top one overhangs the bottom one by a hand's width and the bottom one overhangs the mud by four inches at the shed end.

It comes off that end in one stream into about eight inches of ground at the corner of the shed where the mud always gathers, and in ten minutes that corner has a hole in it a foot across with a stream going into it.

Her sister goes out with the spade and stands in it.

"Well."

"You have put a channel on it."

"I have put three boards on it." Her sister does not get out of the corner. "And a board across a fall is a channel, and it always was, and you cannot put a channel on a path and then be surprised where the water comes out at the end of it. It comes out where you have put the low end."

"And it is coming out in the one place in this yard where the ground was sound."

"It is coming out where the low end is. And that corner was always the low end. It was the low end when it was mud and it is the low end now. You have not moved the low end. You have moved where it arrives."

---

Neither of them lifts a board. Lift them and the mud is under them and wet, and the barrow goes back onto it and in a month it is worse than it was, because a board is not a fix. Leave them and the water has one place to go, and that place is digging itself out at the corner of the shed.

Her sister goes home at the end of it. She stands at the back door with her coat on and looks at the three boards in the dark, and at the mud either side of them that is not coming back, and at the corner of the shed where the water made a hole in ground that was sound in the spring.

Then she goes in for the torch and out to the shed, and stands in the doorway with the light on the floor behind the bench.

The sack of gravel is standing where she put it in May with the top folded over and about a shovelful gone out of it in the summer, and she stands and looks at it and does not open it and does not move it, and shuts the shed door and goes in.
