# Chapter 0986 — What She Wants In Ink

Undated in this draft.

**THE RANGE AT TWELVE IS OUT AND THE BACK DOOR STANDS OPEN ON A KITCHEN WITH A WINDOW OVER IT.**

The kitchen at number twelve is eight feet by ten and has been the same length since the house was built, and the range stands against the far wall under the window with its flue going into the breast of the chimney. It has been out since before the summer and cold iron is the cheapest thing in the room to touch, and the milk in the pan on the shelf beside it went to a solid white block weeks ago and was thrown out, and there is a new pan there now, also solid.

The wall behind the range was plastered over brick at a time when plaster came in a bag and went on thin, and it has been painted twice and is now crazed on the side the window lights and gone soft to the touch on the side it does not. Where the range stands, the plaster is out of true with the brick behind it by the width of a shilling at the bottom corner, because the range was set in against a wall that was not straight and nobody thought to pack it out. He has come about the wall, and he is standing with his back to the window looking at that corner when the back door goes and the sister comes in with her coat already half off one arm and the yard door left standing open behind her.

"Mother." She is in the room before she has finished the word. "Is she all right?"

"She's all right," he tells her. "Her chest is a bit shallow and there's nothing in the room I can do about that in a morning."

"That's what I asked. I asked whether she is all right and you have told me about her chest."

"Then she'll do."

He goes back to the corner. The old woman is in the chair by the window with her feet on the fender and a piece of paper on her knee that she has folded and unfolded and folded again, and she has been going through that fold and unfold through most of the conversation without either of them marking it. The sister has not so much as glanced at the sheet.

"She had this yesterday." The old woman puts the paper out towards him without getting out of the chair.

"Your eyes." His eyes go down to the bottom of the page while he puts the question. It is a printed sheet, one side, with a heading in capitals and four lines under the heading and a line at the bottom for a signature, and the line at the bottom has her name on it in a hand that wavers at the end of the long strokes and is very like the hand on the rent book and the income book and the pane of glass in the back door.

"Your eyes are what they have always been. I put my name to it."

"You read the four lines first."

"I read the heading." She lays the sheet flat on her knee again. "The resident is content to remain. I know what a heading is." The resident is a word from a printed form and there are two people in this kitchen and neither of them is a resident in the sense the form means it. The old woman has let a form decide what she is for about fifty years, and the form has never once been wrong in a way that came back to her.

He reads the four lines. He has heard a version of them at the lock-up twice this week and can hear at once that this is not that one, because this has no clause about a house standing empty and no clause about who answers for the water and no clause about anything at all except that somebody has to be content on paper.

"It means they want to be sure you're all right here," he tells her. "And it means nobody is expecting you up the river. Nobody is to be told anything more than that by that piece of paper."

"Then why did it come to me and not to her?" The sister has her coat off now and is standing in the middle of the kitchen with it over one arm. "There's a street of women my mother's age. Why this one."

"Because she lives alone, and because her daughter is on the crew." He folds the sheet along the fold it already has. "That is all of the reason for it. Nobody came here to ask whether your mother is well. Somebody came because they need somebody in the house while the daughter is away, and your mother is the one who puts her name to that."

The sister's face does something he does not like about a second before it arrives.

"I'm not asking anybody to sign anything. I have read it out and told her what it means and what it doesn't mean, and the pen is on the table and it belongs to her and not to me."

"She put her name there yesterday without asking anybody either."

"That is right, and it is the trouble with it, and it is not mine to put right." He puts the sheet down on the table in front of the old woman rather than give it back to her, and leaves it there with his hand on the edge of the table, because a paper handed straight back to a woman who cannot read it is a way of not having left it. "I would think a great deal less of him than I do of you if he could fix it."

"She's not going up the river whatever happens." He puts the pencil behind his ear, finds it there with the back of his own hand, takes it out again and lays it on the table by the sheet, and stands at the window looking at the yard and the shed roof and the long wall while she gets it out. The sister answers from the other end of the room. "She's seventy-eight and she's got the hip she has and she'd be standing in the rain in a queue inside a day. That's why I'm here. I've come to tell her I'm not going."

"You've come to tell her you're not going, and she has signed a sheet that does not care either way, and she will be in that chair on her own whether you go or not." He turns round. "That is not me being hard. That is what happens when everybody in a street goes somewhere and nobody asks the one person who is staying whether she has agreed to it."

The old woman had not moved through the whole of it. The paper is on her knee and her thumb is on the fold, and she has been looking at the two of them the way a woman looks at two people doing something on her behalf.

"Does the woman that keeps the book come round?" she asks him.

"I have never seen her come round to anything."

"Will you ask her to come?"

He does not go off after the answer. He puts his hand on the table beside the paper and looks at the ruled line along the bottom of it and sees, in the clear space under her name, how much room there is and how small the hand gets when the hand is not sure of itself.

"I can put down in ink anything you want on that sheet," he tells her. "Anything at all, in your own words. You dictate it and I set it down and you put your mark to it beside your name, and after that nobody can read this sheet and take it that you were never asked."

The sister makes a small sound and goes out to the yard with the door swinging behind her. She is back inside in a moment, standing against the range with her arms folded, and the three of them are quiet in there for a while.

The old woman thinks about it. The light comes off the yard wall and goes onto the roof of the shed, and the shadows of the two window bars walk up the floor towards her feet.

"Then set it down that I am not going up the river," she tells him, "and that I would like somebody to know I was asked."

He lays the pencil across the sheet and waits. That is not what he meant and she has taken it to mean something else entirely, and he can either let her have that or he can set her straight, and setting her straight is why he came here at all.

"That is not what I meant." His hand stays on the table where he left it. "I meant put down what you would like on it. Any of it."

"I know what you meant."

"Then what would you like on it."

The old woman folded the sheet again, and unfolded it, and set it square on her knee with both hands at the bottom corners, the way you set a table.

"That I have been in this room since the range went out," she said, "and I would like to go and look at a river."

The old woman keeps the sheet in her hand and does not give it back to him, and the ink along the line at the bottom of it is dry, and the wall behind the range is standing there with its bottom corner the width of a shilling out of true with everything behind it, and there is about half an inch of clear paper between her name and the edge, which is not enough for what she would like on it.
