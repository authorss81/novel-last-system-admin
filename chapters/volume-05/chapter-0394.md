# Chapter 0394 — An Hour Late

Saturday the third of March 2018, from about half past seven in the morning until about half past nine at night, a parade, a shop off it, and a hall on a meter, in Tideglass.

**NOTHING THAT HAPPENED IN THIS BOROUGH ON THE THIRD OF MARCH WAS DIFFERENT FROM WHAT HAPPENS, AND A GATE OFF A SIDE TURNING BEHIND A ROW OF SHOPS KEPT A MAN WAITING FOR FIFTY-FIVE MINUTES ON A MORNING WHEN NOBODY HAD ASKED HIM TO COME, AND TWO SMALL THINGS ON THAT PARADE ARE WRONG AND HAVE BEEN WRONG FOR SOME TIME AND ARE STILL WRONG AND HAVE BEEN PUT RIGHT BY NOBODY, AND A LINE UNDER THE LAST ENTRY IN A BOOK IN THIS BOROUGH IS BLANK AND IS AT A FIGURE OF DAYS THAT NOBODY HAS PUT ANYWHERE.**

The shutters went up along that parade in the order they go up in and the one at the top was left to the end of the morning, which is what it is always left to, and the man who puts shutters up did the whole length and did not stop anywhere and was gone by about half past nine.

The woman who works in a shop had that counter from ten until about nine and shut at half past three because it was a Saturday, and she had not been waiting on anything all week, and Friday's order was on the pad behind the till with the pen lying across it.

The man who works nights was up that parade at about seven that morning, where he has been going every morning since the beginning of December instead of lying in his own front room, and was in it again at about half past four with two sacks of ice in the boot of his car.

The man in his sixties was out on the low wall at the end of a turning for about an hour and ten minutes with a cup and went in before it got dark.

Nine people use that parade most days of an ordinary week. Every one of them went their own way on the third of March and every one of them went their whole way alone, and not one of the nine had a thing in his hands that was meant for another of the eight.

---

The man in his forties was an hour late at a gate off a side turning behind that row of shops.

He had been in that yard four times in about two years and there is nothing in it that is his and nothing in it that is anybody's, and on the Thursday of the week before he had stopped at the gate for about two minutes and had said that he would come by on the Saturday morning, which is not arranging anything with anybody.

He meant to come at ten past ten.

He had gone the other way first — down to the bottom of the town for something that took ten minutes and then twenty minutes more than that — and he came up that turning at about five past twelve, which is fifty-five minutes later than he had meant to come.

The man who keeps that place was out on a lorry. He had been out since about nine. The gate was shut and there was nobody in that yard and the man in his forties stood at it for about ten minutes.

At about ten past ten the man who keeps that place came back on the lorry and reversed it into the yard and got out and saw the man in his forties walking up the street with no bag.

Neither of them said anything about the hour.

The man who keeps that place went and opened the gate and put his hand on it and the other one went through and looked at about eight foot of pipework behind the building for about ten minutes, and they talked about a Monday, and the man in his forties came out again and carried on along the parade with the other two calls on that day still in front of him.

Nobody in this borough knows that either of those two men were ever an hour out of step with each other, and the man in his forties has not mentioned it and is not going to, because there is nothing in it that anybody was let down about and a man who was fifty-five minutes late for a thing nobody arranged has nothing to be late for.

Two things on that parade are wrong and have been wrong for some time and are still wrong.

The first is the shutter at the top of it. It has not come all the way down since the twelfth of February. It stops about a foot off the pavement and it goes over the catch and sits there, and the man who puts shutters up puts his hand under the bottom rail and lifts it twice every morning and puts it down twice, and it goes over the catch and sits there.

Nobody has told the woman who lets that shop. Nobody has told the man who owns it. There is a number on a piece of paper in a window a few doors along that a person could telephone, and nobody has telephoned it, and the man who puts shutters up is a man who does the whole length of that parade in a morning and does not do the other one for anybody, and the other one is not the only one on that parade that is not quite right.

The second is in the middle of it, about ninety foot from the top. A paving slab about eighteen inch by twelve has lifted about an inch and a half out of the bed it is in, and it sits across the full width of that footway with an edge on it like the lip of a step.

It was put down in 2011 and it has gone up on that frame in about three winters and every year somebody has said it is going to get worse. It is not going to get better. About nine people walk over it every day and none of them has said so to any of the others and the man who works nights puts his foot up on it in the dark and does not think about it because he is thinking about where he is going.

There is no form anywhere for reporting either of those two things and nobody has reported either of them to anybody, and the woman who works in a shop has walked over the lip of that slab about six hundred times this year and has not mentioned it at the counter to a single person.

The four answers are still going round and they still cannot be laid side by side, because the four of them are not about the same sort of thing.

Nobody at that counter in the week of the third of March has said which of the four they are holding. Nobody has gone back on the third one. Nobody has re-titled the fourth one as a fifth. Nothing new has come out of anybody, and the thing the four of them are answers to is still without one.

---

In a block by the water there is a book on a shelf with nineteen entries in it, and the space under the last of the nineteen has been empty for one thousand one hundred and ninety-three days.

Nobody counted it on the third of March and nobody has counted it this month, and nobody has been in that room that day or the day before it or the day after it, and if you stopped anybody in that street in the street and asked them what the heading on that last line is for, not one of them would be able to say, and the same was true a month ago.

In the room at the back of that shop on the parade there is a box on the table that has been on that table since the afternoon of the twenty-fourth of February.

The woman who works in a shop was in that room at about four on that Saturday and the man who works nights was in it at about half past four, and the box was on the table and neither of them said one word about it, and neither of them has said one word about it to anybody, and the man who put it there has not been in that room and did not come through that hatch on that afternoon and will not.

Down at the bottom end of that parade the hall on the meter had its door propped and the urn going, and the urn had been on since about half past ten and it was still on at about one, and a plate of cake sat on a table by the pay desk, and about twenty people went in and out over those hours.

The man who does that hall was behind that table the whole of that time and nobody in that hall put a question to him. He took a collection at about twelve o'clock in an old mug, and about thirty pence went into it, and the woman of about sixty who has come to that hall on a Saturday for about nine years put a pound note in and did not ask what it was for and nobody told her.

He wiped that table down at one o'clock and put the cloth in the bucket behind the hatch and put his hands in his pockets and stood outside that hall for a minute with the door open behind him, the way he does.

The man in his forties was not in that hall. The man in his thirties went past it at about half past twelve on his way to a job and did not go in, and would not.

**THE MAN IN HIS FORTIES STOOD AT THAT GATE FOR ABOUT TEN MINUTES ON THE MORNING OF THE THIRD OF MARCH AND WALKED UP THAT PARADE WITH NO BAG, AND HE HAS NOT MENTIONED THE HOUR TO ANYBODY SINCE AND IS NOT GOING TO, AND THE SHUTTER AT THE TOP OF THAT PARADE IS STILL SITTING A FOOT OFF THE PAVEMENT.**
