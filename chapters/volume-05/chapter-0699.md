# Chapter 0699 — The Drawer She Does Not Open

Thursday the twenty-seventh of January 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number ten from first light until dark and the whole of the day is a shut drawer and a shed roof seen from a kitchen window.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES DOES NOT OPEN A DRAWER WITH PLY AND TAPE AND A PENCIL AND A FOLDED PAPER IN IT, LOOKS AT A SHED ROOF FROM HER OWN KITCHEN WINDOW AND SEES THE FELT GONE AT THE FRONT EDGE, SPEAKS TO THE MAN FROM THE COAL YARD IN HER OWN YARD ABOUT THE DRAUGHT OFF THE TOP OF IT AND DOES NOT ASK HIM FOR HANDS, AND SAYS IN THE SPRING AND PUTS NO DATE ON IT.**

She is a woman in her early thirties. She is at home all day except for the man from the coal yard in her own yard at the middle of the day and her sister in the kitchen in the afternoon. She speaks to both of them.

---

She does not open the drawer on the twenty-seventh.

The drawer is under the worktop on top of the tape roll with about four inches of brown tape left on it. The hammer and the screwdriver are in the same drawer and have not been out since September. The bit of ply is in there on top of the tape. The pencil is in there on top of the ply, which is a stupid place for a pencil and which she will find again. The folded piece of paper with three lines on it that say what a felt edge would take and not what any of the three things is lies under the tape roll.

It is the fullest that drawer has been since she moved in. She looks at the front of it while the kettle boils and does not pull it. Opening it would mean seeing the paper with three lines on it that name what a thing would be and not what it is, and she has seen that paper enough.

The outside light over her own back door is off. It is switched on and off every Saturday morning, and she did not switch it on on Saturday the twenty-fifth of December and stood in her own dark yard looking for a light at the end of that road instead, and there was one.

---

She stands at the kitchen window with the cup in both hands and looks at the shed.

The shed roof has the felt gone at the front edge. From the window she can see the pale line where the felt lifts and the dark under it where the water gets in. The ply will not do it. She got up on the ladder before Christmas and found that, and the ply stays in the drawer on top of the tape, and the shed still has a draught off the top of it.

The tape roll came off a shelf in a house at the end of that road in May and neither she nor her sister has ever said where the roll came from to the other. Four inches is left on it. Four inches will not go round a shed edge. It will barely go round a parcel.

She drinks the tea and watches the felt lift a little in the wind and settle again. A repair with no date on it is not a repair. She said that to herself on the twenty-eighth of December with her sister in the kitchen, and she says it again now without her sister, to the window.

---

At the middle of the day there is a noise down the back.

It is the man from the coal yard with a sack on a barrow, come down the back the way he came in November and on the thirteenth of December, to look at the shed and say what he said then.

She goes out into the yard with her coat over her shoulders. The back door shuts true behind her the way it always does. The yard is paved and cold and the back mat is empty.

"Morning," he says.

"Morning."

He puts the barrow down and looks at the shed roof. He does not get up on anything. He looks from the yard the way a man looks who has seen a hundred shed roofs.

"That's still doing you in," he says.

"Aye."

"Felt's gone at the front edge."

"Aye. I got up on a ladder before Christmas and saw it. Ply will do nothing on it."

He nods at the shed as though the shed will do something the talking has not done.

"You want felt on that," he says.

"Aye."

"You want a roll of it. And the stuff that goes over the join. And a bit of timber under the new edge so there is a lip for the water to come off."

She looks at him. That is the three things on the back of the bit of paper in the drawer, near enough word for word, and he has said them without seeing the paper and without knowing the paper exists.

"I've got it wrote down," she says.

"Have you."

"Three lines. On the back of a bit of paper. In the drawer."

He laughs a little, which she does not mind.

---

"You could get some off the man at number eleven," he says. "He had it on his bench all summer, I saw it through the window."

"I know."

"You could ask him."

"I know."

She stands in her own yard with her coat over her shoulders and her hands in her pockets and works out what asking would mean. It would mean knocking at number eleven with the paper in her hand and saying she wants a bit of felt for a shed, which is a sentence with about nine words in it that she does not have yet. It would mean saying she has a bit of ply that will not do it and four inches of tape that will not go round it. It would mean saying she walked up a road once in her life with a step ladder on her shoulder and knocked once and has never gone back and has not told her sister that she went, though her sister knows she went.

She does not ask him for hands. He has a barrow and a sack and a back to spare, and a pair of hands on a ladder is the thing she has wanted since December, and she stands in the yard and does not ask.

"When will you do it," he says.

"In the spring," she says, and puts no date on it, because she has no date to put on it and because the spring is where the shed goes when it cannot be done now. "When it is dry enough to stand on without going through."

"Right," he says.

---

He goes with the barrow and she goes back in.

She stands at the kitchen window again with the empty cup and looks at the shed roof. The felt lifts and settles. The draught comes off the top of it and she can feel it through the glass though the glass is shut. The drawer under the worktop stays shut. The hammer and the screwdriver stay where they have been since September. The pencil stays on top of the ply. The paper stays under the tape roll.

She thinks of the house at the end of that road, which she walked up to once on the seventeenth of December with a step ladder on her shoulder, and knocked once, and has never gone back. The gate stands back against the coal shed wall since the first of September with two drop bolts in an apron pocket. The padlock hangs open in the hasp. The key is on a hook behind the back door. The folding step ladder she put in the front room stands folded against the wall under the window and has not been opened out and has not been moved.

The woman of seventy-eight eats at a kitchen table under a window on a green chair with a cushion and does not sit in the front room since the sixteenth of July. The table is dead flat. The box on the left of it is taped and under a cloth and has not moved. The envelope on the right of it is square with the edge. Four of six is still four of six.

She has told her sister two things about that house and neither of them was about the ladder. Her sister asked her on the twenty-eighth who the ladder was from and she said she does not know, which is true. That is the last time anybody asked anybody anything about the ladder and it is not the first, because a man asked at her sister's door on the seventh and the coal man asked on the thirteenth.

---

In the afternoon her sister comes round the back without knocking and lets herself in off the yard, and the door shuts true behind her the way it always does.

They do not go into the front room. There is a settee in it, a table with a lamp on it, and a wall, and neither of them has any business in there on a Thursday in January.

She puts the kettle on.

"You've been quiet," her sister says.

"I've been at the window."

"Did you see the coal man."

"I saw him. He said what he said in November."

"Did you ask him."

"No."

Her sister sits down and puts her feet under her and looks at her.

"Is that ply still in there," her sister says, nodding at the drawer.

"It's on top of the tape."

"Are you going to use it."

"No. It will do nothing on the front edge. I've been up on it. I've seen the front edge. If I put a board across the middle of that roof it stops one draught and lets two more in at the front and then I've spent the ply and it's still a shed."

"Then you'll want felt."

"I'll want felt."

"When."

---

And there it is, and it comes out of her sister without any weight on it at all, and it is the same question the coal man asked in the yard and the same question the man at the cottage asked on the telephone and the same question the lad with the note asked at a back door.

She looks at the wall.

"In the spring," she says.

"Right."

"Aye."

"Right."

She does not put any date on it. She does not open a calendar, because she has never in her life had a list on a wall and she is not starting one in January with four inches of tape and a bit of ply in a drawer.

And she knows exactly what that means, because she has watched her sister cross the first line of a pad off twice since March and never once properly, and she has a hammer and a screwdriver and a roll of tape in the same drawer that have not been out since September, and she has a shed with a draught off the top of it that a man from a coal yard has now told her about three times.

"In the spring," she says again, to nobody, and washes the cups.

---

She goes out into the yard after her sister has gone to see her off and stands there in the dark with the back door shut true behind her.

The outside light over that back door is off. It is a Thursday, so that is nothing.

What she does in that yard on a Thursday is not what she does in it on a Saturday morning, and she knows that, and she stands there anyway. On Saturday morning she puts her coat on over her nightdress and goes out and switches that light on and stands and looks at it and switches it off again. She has done it since the spring. She did not do it on the twenty-fifth of December.

And the reason she did not do it then is not that she forgot, and she has turned that over twice in the yard and she has not got a better reason than the one she already had, which is this: that she was stood in her own yard in the dark before there was any light looking for a light over a gate at the end of that road, and there was one, and it was on, and she looked at it for a while, and she did not go up the road to see what was making it.

There is a folding step ladder in a front room at the end of that road with tape round its second rail, and it will be there in the spring, folded, against the wall under the window, because nobody can paint round it and nobody can move it and nobody can ask anybody to take it away.

So she goes in and shuts a door that was already shut and the shed goes on doing her roof in.

---
