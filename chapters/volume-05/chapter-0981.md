# Chapter 0981 — Into The Empty Mortice

Undated in this draft.

**A FENCE POST AT THE SHED END HAS A SQUARE HOLE IN IT WITH LEAF LITTER PACKED IN AND NOTHING ELSE.**

The post stands at the corner of the yard at number twelve, on the line where the brick boundary wall gives way to a run of oak post and rail that carries on for about twenty feet. It is an oak post, squared on all four faces, driven into the ground with about a foot of it showing, and it has been in the ground long enough for the ground to come up against it.

At about eight inches below the top of the post there is a square hole in one face, an inch and a quarter by an inch and a quarter, cut clean through the wood to a depth of three inches. It is a mortice for the end of a rail, and it is empty, and has been empty for long enough for the inside of it to be the same colour on all four sides as the outside of the face it opens through. The leaf litter that has come down the boundary wall for years has packed into that hole and gone to a soft grey mat about two inches deep, and where a finger goes into it the mat takes the finger down with it and does not let it go, because there is nothing under it but the three inches of mortice and then the solid wood at the back of it. The face of the post round the hole has gone grey and is beginning to go soft along its arrises, and the arris at the top of the post has gone soft further, because rain gets at it from above and runs down the face in a line that goes straight through the middle of the hole.

That brick wall carries a coping of engineering brick, and its courses are out of true where something has got under the footing and did not go back again. The run of post and rail takes over at the shed corner, where the brickwork stops. The rail that was in the post is gone, and so is the section of it on the other side of the post, and there is a length of six feet of the run with nothing in it at all except the two posts at either end.

The ground along the line of the run is hard and bare for about two feet each side of it, because the grass there was killed by the shade of the wall and by the dogs and has not come back. Leaf litter blows along that bare strip and lodges against the foot of each post, and where the post stands proud of the ground by an inch or two the litter gathers on the top of the line of soil at its foot instead of running under it.

The oak of the post is damp at the foot for about four inches where the soil touches it, and wet to a foot where the leaf litter lies against it, and dry above that on a day when the wind is in the east. The rail socket in the post at the far end, where the rail still stands, is damp inside for the same reason and keeps its litter the same way.

The shed stands behind the post with its felt short of the brickwork at the eaves, and the three-inch gap along that eaves is what puts water on the wall behind the post and not on the post. The drips from it fall on the ground a foot behind the line of the fence and never touch the wood, except in a wind that comes round the corner of the shed.
---

The hand is laid flat on the top of the post and stays there, and the fingers are pushed down into the square hole in the face below it until the knuckles come to rest on the wood at the mouth of it.

That is what fixes the depth. The face of the post is four inches across and the hand is laid flat across the top of it, and the fingers go down the face and into the hole and keep going until the knuckles at the top of them arrive at the wood, which puts the depth of the hole at rather more than the length of the first joint and rather less than the second.

Below the knuckles the hole goes on for another inch and a half, and at the back of it the wood is not flat but dished, because whoever cut the mortice took more out of the corners than the middle and the grain has shrunk away from the ends of it since. The litter lies on the dish and does not reach into the back of the mortice at all.

What the hole is standing on is the solid heart of the post. The three inches of mortice are the whole of it, and behind that the wood is oak through and through, and the roots of the oak come out of the face and run across the back of the mortice as four lines that are slightly darker than the wood about them.

Nothing about it gives. The post does not turn in the ground, the face does not move a hair under the pressure of the hand, and the litter closes over the finger and stays closed where the finger was. All that comes of it is a mat of litter pressed into a shape that will spring back over a week. The rail that came out of that mortice was oak like the post and it went through the post rather than into it, and the far side of the post has a shallow open face on it where the tenon used to come out, cut much larger than the mortice and now full of its own litter and a spider's old web across the middle of it.

The cut of the mortice shows the marks of the saw in four places where the blade caught the end grain, and in three of those the blade had begun to go past the line and had been turned back into the wood again. Whoever cut it was not a practised hand and the only thing wrong with the work is that it was never finished at the back.

The post itself is sound. It has gone grey on the weather side and stayed brown on the other, and the sapwood about half an inch in from the face is darker than the middle and softer under a thumbnail, while the middle of the post is as hard as the day it was cut.

Somebody made a mark on the post above the hole at some time, three shallow strokes in the same place with the corner of something hard. The strokes have filled with dirt and there is moss nowhere near them, which is why they can still be found at all.

The sister is outside the yard gate at number twelve when the hand comes off the top of the post, coming from the lane side, with the gate shut and five bars between her and the yard.

She does not come through the gate and she does not touch it, and the post and the hole in it stand four feet inside the yard with the leaf litter packed in it and nobody looking at either.

Neither the fence nor what came out of the post is spoken of. She stands on the lane with her face to the bars, and after a while she remarks that the light has gone early today, and the reply is that it has gone early because the wind has come round to the west, and neither of the two takes that any further than it was taken.

Her hand goes towards the gate to push it as she is turning away, and is put into the pocket of her coat instead, and comes out of it again empty, and the gate stands shut in its hinges behind her.

The square hole in the post keeps its three inches and its dished back and its mat of grey litter, and the wood at the back of it will be standing open to the air for as long as the post is in the ground.
