# Chapter 0266 — An Arris

Saturday the twenty-fourth of September 2016, from about half past eight in the morning, the Methodist hall on Carrow Lane in Tideglass, and a parade of shops.

**THERE WERE TWO STONE SLABS ON THE STEP OF THAT HALL AND A CRACK ACROSS THE TOP ONE, AND TWO MEN CAME ABOUT IT ON A SATURDAY MORNING BECAUSE A THIRD MAN HAD SAID ONE SENTENCE IN A DOORWAY IN SEPTEMBER AND WAS NOT THERE AND WAS NOT ASKED TO BE.**

The man in his sixties had said, in a doorway on the parade, to nobody in particular and to two people who happened to be standing there, that the step at the Methodist hall on Carrow Lane was a trip and not a step.

The woman of about fifty-five was one of the two people standing there. She had been in this borough since June and she had a key to a room over a shop and a bag and nowhere to be until Monday, and she had a handbag with two pieces of A4 in it and a great deal else in it, and she had asked no questions and had said nothing to anybody about it since the second of September.

The man in his thirties who does building work and is in on the side of a house on a terrace off Corrance Lane heard the same sentence about four days later, standing at the counter of the hardware shop four doors down, and he went to Tomas Renn about it in that shop.

"I'm not talking about it in here," he said.

"Then don't," said Tomas Renn.

"What do you mean, then."

"I mean you've come into my shop and told me a man's said something about a step, and there'll be people in here Saturday, and I'd rather nobody heard a trade on my floor and thought it came off me." He put a bundle of keys in a tray and squared it. "Do it on a Saturday morning. I've got the ladder off the wall behind that door and it's no use to me and it was never any use to me."

"You'd rather nobody heard."

"A barrow can go out of a shop in front of anybody," said Tomas Renn. "A job has a name on it. That's the difference between them and it isn't a moral position, it's a barrow." He looked up then. "You want a hand with it."

"I've not asked you for a hand."

"You'd have asked me in about three weeks and I'd have said yes," said Tomas Renn, "and then it would have been me that did it, and it would have been in here."

---

The key to the Methodist hall is under the third stone from the left and everybody in that part of the town has known that for about thirty years, and the man in his thirties let himself in at about ten to nine and had the hall to himself and the fire door open and the cold coming off the boards off the floor.

The step is two slabs and it is the full width of the door with a hand's width of ironmongery on each side of it. The top one had a crack across it about four inches in from the front edge, and the crack went down through the face of it and across the face of it, and the mortar under it had gone soft and powdered where you put your thumb, and the front edge had gone round and crumbly along about two feet of it, and a yard of the wall above on both sides had a different colour on it, which is the damp coming and going over years and nobody ever repainting.

"See that," he said. "See the colour on the brick. That'll be twenty year. The ground's moved under the bottom of it and that crack's not the crack. The crack's a symptom."

The man in his sixties came in behind him and stood on the step and looked at the wall for a while without saying anything, the way he does.

"Take it all up," he said.

"Take it all up," said the man in his thirties. "Then what. The sub-base under it is sand and it's been sand since somebody laid a path across here. You'll have it up by half eleven and the hole in the ground by twelve and a bag of cement and a barrow-load of stone and a good few hundred of somebody's money, and you'll have the same step back in a fortnight, and the same crack in six years, and the whole of it will stand in a skip off the back lane for a fortnight before that."

"That's how a step is done."

"That's how a step is done if there's a man with a list."

"There's no man with a list."

"There's no man with a list in this, and that's the point," said the man in his thirties. "So it's either you and me and a Saturday morning and it holds for thirty years, or it's done properly and there's a number on it and a job number and it goes in a book in an office, and I'm not having a number on it. I've had numbers on things. I don't get paid till the number's paid."

The man in his sixties had his hands in his pockets and looked at the crack for a bit.

"You'll do it your way, then."

"I'll do it my way."

"Not because I'm asking you to. I'd like that said out loud in a room somebody hears."

"No," said the man in his thirties. "Nobody's asked me to do it, because nobody can ask me to do anything, and if somebody's sat there waiting on a favour off me they'll be sat there till next winter. I'll be here Saturday because I'm here Saturday, and I'll be here again Wednesday because the doors come off them on Wednesday."

The man in his sixty stood on that step for another few seconds and then said the thing about being asked to, out loud, in an empty hall, to a man with a bolster in his hand, and the man in his thirties let him say it, which was the whole of the accommodation anybody made that morning.

---

He cut the top slab back about two inches at the front with a breaker and a bolster and took the front course out whole, and the man in his sixties got the step ladder out of the cupboard by the fire door and set it up and held the other end of it while the slab came up on it, and neither of them wrote anything down at any point and neither of them would.

The old mortar came out in a dust that went everywhere, and the man in his thirties put his hand up flat and said "don't," and the man in his sixties took his hand away and stepped back off the step with a lot of dignity.

A woman was in the hall by about half past ten. The woman of about fifty-five, who had come in with her bag and stood at the back by the window, and she had not offered to help and was not asked, and the man in his thirties had looked at her twice and made his decision both times.

"You'll want that up before the doors," he said, meaning the slab.

"Can't," said the woman of about fifty-five. "There's a meeting here on Wednesday and the doors open on the hall and there's nowhere else to put it and I am not putting it in the corridor because there's a fire door in that corridor and a man will come and shout at me."

"Then it lives there," said the man in his thirties, "till Wednesday, and I'll come back Wednesday and put it up, and it'll be a thing I'm doing on a Wednesday and not a thing I did on a Saturday."

Nobody argued with that and nobody was asked to.

The new bed went down about one and it went on hard and came up true, and the top course went back on about twenty past twelve with the new joint cut off square with a bolster and a lump hammer, and the man in his thirties ran a line across the whole length of the step with a spirit level and hit it three times in the middle because that is what you do in the middle.

Then he stood looking at the front edge of it with his thumb along it.

"You see that," he said. "That there."

"What."

"Front corner. It's gone soft at the arris."

The man in his sixties said it back, twice, and worked at it.

"Arris," he said. "I've heard the word."

"You've not heard the word."

"I've heard the word," said the man in his sixties, and then stopped, because he could not put it to anything at all, and he stood there on a Saturday morning with his hands in his pockets going through the whole of his own trade in his head and not finding a corner anywhere in the country that it would fit into.

The woman of about fifty-five asked him from the window what an arris was.

"I've no idea," said the man in his sixties. "He said it."

The man in his thirties did not explain it and was not asked to.

**AND THE TAPE CAME OFF THE NEW COURSE AT ABOUT TWENTY PAST TWELVE, AND THE GREY DUST WENT DOWN THE STEP IN TWO PLACES WHERE TWO PAIRS OF FEET HAD TRAMPED IT AND NOWHERE ELSE, AND THE SLAB WENT UP AGAINST THE WALL BY THE DOOR UNDER A COAT THAT WAS NOT ANYBODY'S, AND THE CUPBOARD BY THE FIRE DOOR HAD ITS DOOR BACK ON IT AND THE LADDER WENT IN BEHIND IT AFTERWARDS.**
