# Chapter 0588 — The Thing They All Knew

The Saturday of the twenty-second of August 2020, from about half past three in the afternoon until about ten in the evening, in the back room of a hall off a road in this borough.

**THEY HAD ALL BEEN DOING IT SINCE BEFORE THE MIDDLE OF THE MONTH WITHOUT ONE WORD BETWEEN ANY OF THEM, AND A WOMAN SAID IT OUT LOUD IN THE MIDDLE OF A CONVERSATION, AND IT TURNED OUT THAT THE ONE MAN IN THAT ROOM WHO HAD NOT KNOWN WAS NOT THE ONE WHO SAID IT, AND WHAT FOLLOWS IS THE FOUR MINUTES AFTER.**

The back room of that hall is where a bowls side plays on Saturdays through the summer. Seven of the club was in it on the afternoon of the twenty-second of August, which is one more than usually turns up and one less than the eight that would make a side.

They are: a man in his early fifties who bowls third; a woman in her late thirties who bowls second; a woman in her mid forties who keeps the book; a woman in her early twenties who bowls fifth; a man in his mid sixties who bowls first; and a man in his late sixties who bowls fourth and owns the teas.

The man in his early fifties is a widower. His wife died at the end of June. He came back to the bowls in the second week of July and bowls badly and everybody has known that since the day he came back.

---

They had finished the game at about half past three and had the tea and the biscuits out, and the book was being squared off by the woman in her mid forties, and the man in his mid sixties was going round with the pot the way he does, and the woman in her early twenties was talking about a coach holiday.

Somewhere in the middle of that the woman in her late thirties said it. She had been in the side for two years and she had not led a single thing in it in her life, and she said it flatly and to the room and not to anybody in it:

"We've all been walking him home."

That is the sentence. She said it the way you point at the rain.

She went on, because she thought it needed the rest of it: "Every one of us. On the Tuesday it's the woman from the book. On the Thursday it's the man in his mid sixties. Sunday it's you,"" she said to the man in his late sixties. "And on the match days we all just happen to be going the same way."

Nobody said anything for about as long as it takes to put a lid on a pot.

---

The man in his early fifties put his cup down.

That is the four minutes.

The first half of the first minute was the room working out who had heard it, and every single person in it had heard it, and every single person in it knew exactly what it meant, and it was not news to anybody in that room except the man it was about.

The second half of that minute was the woman in her late thirties realising she had said it out loud in front of him.

The rest of it went like this, and it did not go like anybody had expected it would:

The man in his early fifties said, "No, that's not right," and everybody looked at him, and he said, "That's not right, that's not — you don't all just happen to go the same way. I know you don't."

That was true. That was the whole difficulty with it and it was why it had gone on for as long as it had without a word. If you drive a man home once because it is on your way, and then you do it again next week because it is on your way, and you keep finding yourself on his side of the town on a Tuesday evening for no reason a person could put a sentence to, after a while you start to think he is lucky, or he is careful, or he has somehow worked out how to arrange it, and you do not go and check.

The woman in her mid forties said, "You knew we were."

He said, "I did not."

"You knew somebody was coming."

"I knew somebody was on the road," the man in his early fifties said. "I did not know it was an arrangement. I thought I was a man people were kind to."

The man in his mid sixties said, "You were a man people were kind to."

"I was," the man in his early fifties said. "I just didn't know what for."

The man in his late sixties, who has run the teas at that club for nineteen years, did the thing that everybody in that room was grateful for, which was to make a joke, and the joke was: "It's the rain. It's always the rain."

It landed. The woman in her early twenties laughed. The woman in her mid forties began putting the biscuits back in the tin.

The man in his early fifties did not laugh, and it was not a hurt face, and after about the length of one cup of tea he said the thing that made the four minutes into a thing that nobody in that room has forgotten:

"Right," he said. "Well. That is that, then."

And then, because there were seven of them in that room and two of those seven are women who had been doing it as quietly as the rest:

"No, I mean it. I want to say it once and I want to say it properly, and then I never want to talk about it again, and that's the deal, so I can get on with the season."

The man in his early fifties said that to a room where nobody had wanted anything said, about an arrangement that had worked, and the arrangement went on working that afternoon and it has gone on working since, and not one of those seven people has mentioned it again, and the man still walks out at half past five and there is still generally somebody going his way.

**THEY HAD BEEN GOING ON DOING SOMETHING FOR SOME TIME WITHOUT SAYING IT AND A WOMAN SAID IT BECAUSE SHE COULD NOT STAND IT GOING ON, AND HE DID NOT KNOW, AND HE WAS NOT THE ONE WHO SAID IT, AND HE SAID ONE KIND SENTENCE INTO THAT ROOM AND HAS HELD TO IT EVER SINCE.**
