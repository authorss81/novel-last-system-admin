# Chapter 0919 — Into The Bench Joint

Saturday the twenty-second of June 2024, at the bench stretcher in the store at the unit while the side door stands wide on the lane. The joint gap at a bench leg.

**HE HAS A BENCH WITH A STRETCHER BAR JOINT OPEN AT THE LEG IN THE STORE AT THE UNIT AND HE MEANS TO DRIVE KNUCKLES EDGE-FIRST INTO THAT JOINT GAP WITH FINGERS BENT AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN IS AT THE WIDE DOOR AT THE END OF THE STORE FROM THE YARD WITH A COIL OF ROPE OVER HER ARM.**

The bench has stood in that store since the shelves went up, and the top above it has been planed once while the legs have kept their seats.

It is a bench of pine boards about six feet long, with the legs square and the stretcher bar set low between them, and the joint at the near leg standing open a finger where the timber shrank. He wants that joint gap, where the bar meets the leg and the air sits still.

The back of a joint is the one face that gets looked at last of all, and it is the face that says where the bench was made to stand. So the knuckles push edge-first inside the bench joint. The air in there feels cool on the skin, and the bar drags where dust coats it.

His fingers travel in past the knuckles and meet the peg inside, cool and close, with the paint on it shedding in scales and the shoulder sandy where the glue softened seasons past. He shifts the hand first left, then right along the peg, which yields nothing beneath the nails before turning damp a touch lower.

What makes it damp is sweat. Timber throws damp in spring when warm air meets wood that spent winter cold, and each morning beads it where the air cannot move.

So the bar does not touch the shoulder along its run. It stands a finger off the leg with air between, and that still air has kept the joint from rotting at the back.

He draws the hand out of the gap and wipes the dust off against the bench rim, and stands in the doorway looking at the bench with the yard behind him.

The woman from two doors down is at the wide door at the end of the store, come from the yard with a coil of rope over her arm, and she does not set it down.

"Is the side door open past dinner?"

"It stands open till the light goes."

"Their new one binds on the frame."

"It will find its own way in a year."

She shifted the coil higher on her arm and stood a while with her hands in her coat pockets while the draught came through the store, and then she took the coil on up the yard and left the wide door open behind her.

What his fingers find further along, about a foot from the side, is the second peg behind the bar. Not a second bar. A dowel of oak, fixed through the leg to keep it from spreading, about an inch round and set upright where the bar meets it.

He gets the bent wrist round it and feels the face of it with the backs of his fingers through dust and cool still air. It is a leg dowel, put there when the bench was made, to throw the leg off the damp floor and let air under it.

The bench stands a finger clear of the floor along its whole front because of that dowel line, and the finger of air under it smells of dust and cut ends that fell down there years back.

---

A bench that stands clear of the floor will take a winter with no soft feet. Air goes under it and keeps the feet dry, while one pushed hard to stone sweats at the feet and goes dark in two years.

With air under it, those feet have stayed pale and rough, while the top boards of the same bench have gone dark with oil and use.

Each leg stands on end grain against the floor with its width square to the boards, and pine set against stone that way passes damp up the end grain instead of keeping it in the side grain. The feet below stay rough where the oil covers them and soft in the grain where water gets in.

Timber stays sound under oil because oil throws rain while bare grain drinks it, and each winter the bare ones go a little deeper. Oil gone soft feels sandy under the nail and comes away in crumbs, while oil gone close rings a little when tapped with a knuckle.

Old oil flakes in scales where damp gets behind it and lifts it, while oil on dry pine stays close for ten years. Scales curl up at the rims where the damp pushes, and each scale leaves a pale patch that darkens again by next rain.

The vice jaw at the front of the bench runs true on its guides, with the grain across and the chop faced in beech, and the guide bars shine where hands oil them every use. A jaw that runs true meets its work face to face and grips along its whole depth, while one that runs skewed grips at one corner and bruises the timber. This jaw meets along its whole depth, and the beech face has taken the print of a hundred boards in shallow dents that show in slanting light. Below the jaw the floor stones lie dark with oil drips, each drip ringed where the dust settled round it, and the rings go deeper toward the vice where the work is done.

The pegs along the bench rim stand in a row of bored holes, oak dowels with the tops rounded by palms, and the third from the end sits a hair high where the hole wore wide. A peg high like that catches the sleeve in passing, and the polish on its crown shows it has been caught a hundred times. Roof water off the lane gutter ticks on the sill in showers and runs under the side door to the middle of the floor, where the stones dip and the water stands in a shallow pan till the draught takes it.

The boards of the bench top run true from end to end, with the grain running along them and the ends housed in the front vice. Top boards cup when one face stays damp and the other dries, because the damp face swells and the dry face shrinks, and the board curls toward the dry.

These tops have cupped a little toward the store, which lets more air in under and dries the legs faster each year. Cupped tops throw air down in the day and the legs throw it up at night, so a cupped bench airs its own feet every time the door opens.

Close tops throw nothing, and the feet stay shut and sweat, which is why close-topped benches want a dowel line and cupped ones stand with no help. The stretcher here lies rough to its pegs and is kept off the leg by them, while the tops, which cup a little, throw their share of air below on their own.

A face gone rough with use gathers dust even across it by evening, while one that stands in a draught gathers it in streaks. Dust lies on that stretcher even from end to end, with its rough pine shut to the wind, while the top boards carry it in streaks where hands pass.

Three bolts fix the vice down and all three have been turned once since it was made, and each one has gone slack a quarter turn in the damp and drawn back when the pine dried in June. A bolt turned once and then left goes slack every winter and every spring for as long as it sits in that bench, because pine swells across the grain in a wet month and shrinks across it in a dry one. The rust under those heads has bloomed out along the top of the jaw in a soft orange, and it stops dead at the first clear inch of pine, which is the line the damp got to and no further.

The bench keeps the mark the tools left on it, with its bar a finger off the leg and the air behind it cool as it stands, because the store has stood open on the lane since the heat came in.
