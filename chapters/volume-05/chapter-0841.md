# Chapter 0841 — The Twist In The Bench

Monday the fourth of September 2023, in the yard of the unit from first thing until the afternoon pales, and a twist in an oak bench is the whole of that Monday.

**THERE IS AN OAK BENCH IN THE YARD OF THE UNIT WITH A TWIST IN IT AND HE MEANS TO EASE IT WITH BATTENS AND NOT CUT, AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN CROSSES THE YARD WITH A BUCKET AND TAKES IT AWAY AGAIN.**

That bench is the oak one along the wall, thick legs and a thick top, standing out all summer under the overhang.

The top has taken a twist with the weather. The far end sits high on the near side by half a finger, enough to rock a toolbox and spill paint, and the legs on that end stand uneven, one lip off the flags.

He does not cut it. An oak bench wants its length kept, and a cut to cure a summer twist is short all winter when the twist goes back, and battens under will do for a season.

Easing is done with two softwood battens, wedged shims of offcut, and screws, with the bench left whole.

He starts by brushing the flags under the bench clean. Dust lies there in a soft mat, with shavings from another year under the far end, pale curls put there when the legs were shaped and never swept.

The shavings are not his and not this job. They have sat under that end since before the bench came out, caught against the wall, and he leaves them where they lie.

He sights along the top from one end. The twist shows as a thin line of light under the rule, there at the far end and gone in the middle, back again faint at the near.

Battens go under the low side, across the legs, to bring the bench up without touching the oak. He lays the first batten under the far legs and packs shims of offcut under it a sliver at a time, tapping them in with a mallet until the top runs true to the rule.

The woman from two doors down crosses the yard in the middle of the morning with a bucket, puts it down by the wall clear of the bench, and takes it away again full from the standpipe.

“Mind the battens, they are loose yet,” he tells her.

“I am clear of them,” she tells him, and goes out through the gates with the bucket, out of his way each time he steps back to sight.

He picks the mallet up again where he put it down and the shims go in another sliver at a time, and neither of them ever mentions the twist.

He thinks about oak while he drives screws, how it moves with seasons and comes back, slow as breathing across months. The twist of August will ease by winter without his battens, and his battens only keep the bench useful in between. That is enough. Usefulness in between is the whole of what yard furniture owes anyone.

The driver twists in his grip on the last screw, and the oak squeaks round the thread, and his wrist aches with the strain of it.

Screw down is the last of it. Two screws through each batten into the flags' joints would split the flags, so the battens are screwed to the legs and not to the ground, with the shims trapped between batten and flags by weight alone.

He drives the screws with a driver, slow, feeling each one bite into oak. Oak takes a screw hard and keeps it, and a screw into oak sings up through the driver into the hand.

Rule on again after each screw, because screws pull and a batten screwed down twists with the pull. After the fourth the top runs true end to end, and a toolbox set on the far end sits without rocking.

He brushes the dust off the top and off the battens, and sweeps the flags round the legs.

---

The driver goes back in the store, and the spare shims go in the pocket of the bench drawer for the winter, and the toolbox stays on the bench where it was.

He steps back to the gates when the afternoon pales and looks at what is there. A bench standing without rocking with battens under one end, screws bright in the oak, and beneath it the shavings from another year, pale against the dust.
