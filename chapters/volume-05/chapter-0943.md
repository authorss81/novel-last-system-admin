# Chapter 0943 — Between The Two Trestles

Tuesday the first of October 2024, at the two trestles against the office wall of the unit with the leaves going. Into a standing wedge.

**TWO TIMBER TRESTLES ARE LEANING AGAINST THE OFFICE WALL IN THAT YARD AND THEY MAKE A TAPERED OPENING WHERE THEY COME TOGETHER, AND HE MEANS TO PUT THE HAND INTO IT WITH THE FOUR FINGERS SPLAYED ACROSS THE FAR TIMBER AND THE THUMB KEPT ON THE NEAR ONE, TO FIND OUT HOW DEEP THAT OPENING GOES AND WHAT IS BEHIND IT.**

The trestles were put against that wall in the spring to dry some timber and they have stood against it since, leaning, and the wall has been repainted behind them at some point in there. The timber came off them in the spring and the pair have not shifted a finger since.

It is two timber trestles of the kind used for staging, about six feet tall and a foot and a half wide at the feet, leaning together against a rendered wall with their legs splayed and their heads touching. He means to get into the wedge between them, which is the opening they make where they lean.

---

Two things leaning on one another make a wedge and not a joint, and this wedge is the better part of a hand's width at the top. The hand goes in splayed: four fingers across the far trestle, thumb kept against the near one, and the angle comes off the difference between the two. The faces of both are blunt along their edges, and the top of the near one is blunter again where a boot has been set against it and lifted off.

The wedge is a hand's width at the top and closes to nothing within a foot of the ground, which is where the two legs come down towards their own feet. The four fingers go in as far as the top of the wedge and stop there, and what they come up against at the back of it is the render of the office wall, dry, standing about a hand's width clear of the far trestle at that height.

Two trestles leaning together make a triangle with a wall, and a triangle with a wall is the most awkward arrangement of building timber anybody ever puts up. It cannot be tied, because the legs are splayed and nothing about it offers anything to tie to, and nothing can be wedged in at the foot, because the feet are the whole of what is carrying them. It is only stable because the weight of the two leans against the wall.

They have been leaning there since April and the wall has taken the whole of that leaning for five months now without a single mark of any kind, and the reason it has is that the heads of the trestles are flat and the wall behind them is a rendered face that has been out of the weather and behind a roof overhang since the spring.

A mark would come if either of them moved. And either of them moving is what would happen if the bottom of the wedge were filled with something, because a wedge at the foot of a triangle does not stop it leaning; it stops it sliding, and it is sliding that these two have not done for five months because the flags where the feet stand are level with each other.

The faces of both trestles carry a mat of fine dust, and that dust is not from the yard. Yards throw up coarse stuff: sand, scale, bits of mortar. What is on those two faces is fine and grey and comes from the setting of cement, and it got there because for about a week in May a bag of it stood open on the flags about four feet from the wall.

The shavings on the near trestle came off the same job and off the same bag. There are about forty of them where the bag was set down and a line of them along the foot of the near trestle where the ground has been swept into it and out of it again by the wind coming off the yard.

Against the wall at the back of the wedge, where the four fingers reach, the render is sound and it is about two inches back from the far trestle at the height the hand goes in. At the top of the wedge, where the two heads meet, they are touching, and above the hand the two of them are together.

A trestle of that kind is made out of four legs, two rails and a pair of string pieces, and everything about it is cut on the assumption that it will be carried by two people at the two ends. A trestle carried by two people does not lean on anything at all while it is being moved. A trestle that has been standing for five months has forgotten how to be carried and would need four people to get it back on its legs.

The feet of both of these are splayed in the ordinary way for a trestle on soft ground, which means each foot is a length of timber lying on its narrow edge rather than its flat. Four feet lying on a narrow edge on a concrete yard is a poor footing, and it is the reason two trestles left leaning will sometimes come down in a wind and very often will not. The difference is whether the feet have found a hollow to sit in, and five months is enough for that.

What is on the faces of them has been there long enough to have gone grey, and what is on the ground at the foot of the near one has been ground into the dust of the yard by the broom, which is why the line of shavings along that foot is broken in three places and unbroken in the two.
The broom in that yard is an agency thing and it comes round on a Wednesday, and the line of shavings at the foot of the near trestle is dated to the last three Wednesdays of the month by nothing at all except the gaps in it.

The top of the near trestle has a fourth piece of timber nailed across it about a foot from one end, which is a thing nobody does to a trestle and everybody does to a trestle after the second time they have carried it.
Against the wall, at the height where the four fingers reach, there is a faint rectangle in the render that is lighter than the render round it, the size of the head of one trestle, standing about four inches off the wall from the top down to eighteen inches and then stopping dead. That rectangle is where a trestle stood once in the winter with a board across it, and it is the only sign on that wall that either of these two had ever been anywhere else.

The woman from two doors down comes through the pedestrian gate in the boundary wall from the pavement and stops a foot inside it to swing the leaf back against the wall, and then she stands there with her hand on the edge of it.

She stays like that for a while, neither in nor away, and the leaf of the gate is against the wall with her hand on its edge, and the two trestles are eight feet off on the other side of the yard and she does not look at them.

The hand is out of the wedge and down at his side before she is through the gate, and his pocket is empty and the ground by the feet of the trestles is as it was, and the wedge keeps the width it has got because two trestles are leaning against a wall at an angle that was set in April and are not going to take up a different angle on their own.

What is in that wedge is a hand's width of air, a couple of inches of render at the back of it, forty shavings on the near face and a mat of cement dust on both, and the two trestles will keep their angle and keep their width whatever else happens on that wall, were a handcart to come through that gate tomorrow and go by close enough to knock them.
