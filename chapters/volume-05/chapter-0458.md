# Chapter 0458 — The Door at the Bottom

The fourth of December 2018, and two evenings of it, a fortnight apart. One back passage and the doorway off it, in Tideglass.

**A WOMAN HELD A DOOR OPEN FOR A MAN ON THE FOURTH OF DECEMBER AND HE STOPPED HOLDING IT FOURTEEN DAYS LATER, AND EACH OF THEM WORKED OUT WHAT THE OTHER HAD DONE, AND NEITHER HAS RAISED IT, AND NEITHER IS GOING BACK TO THE OLD WAY.**

At the bottom of a passage off that street there is a door, and it is how about fourteen flats and a yard at the back get their rubbish out, and it is not a door anybody has ever locked, and it is not a door anybody has ever closed properly, and everybody in that building has a view about which of those two things it should be.

It has a catch on it. The catch has worked since before anybody in that building came to it and it is the only working part of the door. There is no key and there has not been a key on it for years. It is a self-closing door with a broken closer, so it stands at whatever angle the last person left it at.

---

He has a lease of his own on the second floor of that building and has been going in and out of that door most days of the week for six years.

What he has done for six years is not let it go. He comes down, he puts a hand on it, he takes it through, he does not let it swing back, and it shuts behind him with the noise it makes, which is a bang and then a rattle in the frame, and every other person in that building can tell from that sound which floor the man in his thirties is on.

He is not proud of that. He has never thought about it. It is a thing his hands do on a door and it has been in them since 2012.

The woman has the ground floor on the far side of that passage and has had it since about March.

What she has done since March is let it go. She comes out, she puts her hand on it to open it, and then she takes her hand off it, and it travels about two foot on its closer, and it either bangs or it does not depending on what the wind is doing, and she goes down the passage with the noise behind her and has not once gone back to shut it.

She does not do it on purpose. She asked herself about it once, in about April, and worked out that she does it because turning round to shut a door means a person in that passage has to stop what they are doing, and there are about fourteen people who have to come through that door every evening and she is the one who is nearest it and she would be the one who stopped them.

On the fourth of December she did not let it go.

That is the whole of what changed and it is the whole of what she could say about it if anybody asked her, which nobody is going to.

She came out at about ten past seven with two bags, she put her hand on the door, she opened it, and she kept her hand on it and held it while the man in his thirties came down the stairs behind her and went past her, and then she let it go and it swung and it banged, and she went down the passage and he went up it, and neither of them said one word.

He noticed at about half past seven that evening and did not go back to work on it until the eighteenth of December, which is a fortnight.

What he noticed was that the door had shut before he got to it. He had come down the stairs at his own pace, which is not fast, and he had put his hand out at about the height of the frame the way he does most evenings, and the door was already on the latch and the catch had gone over.

He stood in that passage for about three seconds. Then he went up.

He went back over it that night and again the next morning, and both times he worked out the same thing, and both times he told himself there was a reason for it that had nothing to do with her, and on the second night he caught himself doing the thing where a man goes looking for a reason in order not to have to think about a person, and he stopped, and he went to bed.

On the eighteenth of December he came down at about ten past seven and let it go.

He did it in the middle of the stair, deliberately, with his hand already off it before he reached the bottom, and it went about two foot and it banged, and he went up the passage with the noise behind him.

She was in that passage at about ten past seven that night and she heard it and she did not go back either.

She has thought about it since and what she thinks about is the two seconds of delay. There were two seconds between the time the door shut on its own and the time his hand would have been at the frame, which is two seconds of him not doing a thing he has done most evenings for six years, and she has worked out from those two seconds that he noticed.

If he had not noticed, the door would have been on the latch when he came down, as it was every evening, and she would have gone on letting it go for the rest of her life in that building and the whole of it would have stayed a thing about her and about nobody else.

And that is where it stops, and it is not going to start again.

Neither of them has said one word about the door. Nobody said one word about the door before the fourth of December either, in six years or in about a year, and the not-saying is not a thing either of them put there. They are in the same passage every evening of the week and they have exchanged, since the fourth of December, the word "evening" and about nine other words, none of which was about that door.

What neither of them will do is say it, and there is a good reason on each side and neither of the two reasons is the same as the other one.

She will not say it because saying it turns the door into an agreement, and an agreement in that building would be a thing that could be broken and would be noticed being broken, and right now the door is not an agreement. Right now it is two people doing the same thing at the same time and neither of them having said why, and there is no version of that anybody in that building could put a hand on.

He will not say it because he worked out on the eighteenth of December, standing at the bottom of that stair, that she had done it first, and he has not been able since then to work out whether that is a thing to be glad about or a thing to be ashamed of, and he is not going to find out in front of her.

---

The door is doing it on its own now. It is not a habit for either of them because there is no rule about it, and there is nothing in that passage anybody could take away from them, and if one of them started letting it go again tomorrow about one evening in seven nobody in that building would have the least idea that anything had changed.

That is what a fortnight does. It takes a thing that one of them chose once out of the two of them and makes it a thing that the door does.

**SHE WENT UP THE STAIRS AT TEN PAST SEVEN THAT NIGHT WITH BOTH BAGS AND THE DOOR BANGED BEHIND HER, AND HE WENT UP THE OTHER STAIR, AND NEITHER OF THEM SAID ANYTHING ABOUT IT THEN OR SINCE.**
