# Chapter 0280 — A Gate

Saturday the tenth of December 2016, from about half past ten in the morning, the low end of Peverell Street in Tideglass, and a lane at the end of it.

**THEY WALKED DOWN THE STREET AND PAST A GATE AND ONE OF THEM SAID THE ONLY SENTENCE THERE WAS, AND THE OTHER DID NOT WRITE IT DOWN, AND THAT WAS THE WHOLE OF THE MORNING.**

The bus from the college does not run in the vacation, and the vacation started on the Friday, and the young woman of about twenty had got off a bus on the Thursday night with a bag and a book she did not need to have brought and had walked up the parade and had not been in a room anywhere.

At about half past ten on the Saturday morning she was at the bottom of Peverell Street with her hands in her coat pockets, because it was dry and bright and the coldest morning of that winter so far, and there is a bus stop at the end of that street that goes out past the Marshmont turn and is the only one out of the borough that does anything before a quarter past five in the afternoon.

The man in his thirties was there too, and he was not there for her. He had a piece of batten off a skip at the end of that street, which is what is at the end of that street, and he was pulling it out of the skip on his own and getting it across the back of his van and he had his back to the stop.

"You've stopped your bus," she said.

"It's not due till quarter to twelve and it's never due at twelve."

"That's not what I meant."

---

The end of Peverell Street past number forty-one is a lane about twenty foot wide with no kerb on one side, and it goes up about two hundred yards and does not go anywhere anybody drives to, and the two of them went up it because that is what you do when you have got a bit of morning and nothing to be in.

Number forty-one is the last house before the lane, and it is a two-up two-down with a pump in the doorway that is not plumbed to anything and a stopcock that has been off since the summer. The wall beside the pump is bare where a length of something used to lie along it, and there is a clean strip of newer paint about a foot high with a grey line of dirt above it. There was a light on in the front room at half past ten on a Saturday morning with the curtains open. The letterbox on that door has had a leaflet in it since the summer and the leaflet has gone soft at the folds and is not going to come out of it.

They went past it at about the speed of two people walking, and neither of them stopped, and neither of them mentioned it, and nobody in that street has knocked on that door since the summer and nobody was going to.

There is a field gate at the top of that lane in a hedge, and there are about nine acres behind it, and the field has been in grass since the spring and there is a trough at the bottom of it that has been empty since the summer.

The gate is a five-bar gate on a flat concrete post, and it is shut, and there is a length of chain round the top hinge bar through a staple and a hasp on the near post, and the padlock is hanging open in the hasp with the shackle up and the key still in it, and it has been like that since about the summer.

There is a number stencilled on the top rail in white paint about two inches high. Three figures. The first of them has gone at the corner and the second has gone down the middle and what is left is a stroke of paint and a bit of the foot of a figure, and the whole of it is not a number any more, and there is nothing on the gate and nothing on the post that says what the number is a number of.

The man in his thirties stopped, and looked at it, and looked at the chain, and looked at the trough.

"That's been open since the summer, that."

"So it has."

"You'd not leave a padlock like that."

"No," said the man in his thirties. "I wouldn't. That's not me saying he's had somebody in, though. He might have had it open to get a van in and shut it after. There's a hundred things that is and I don't know one of them and neither do you, and I'm not stood here at half past ten on a Saturday working out a man's life off a padlock."

"I'm not asking you to work out anybody's life."

"I know you're not," he said. "I'm saying I'd not want to be the one who had a padlock like that on a field and had somebody make a thing of it on a Saturday."

The young woman of about twenty looked at the gate for a bit, and then she said, "It's a gate."

"Right," said the man in his thirties.

"That's all it is. It's a gate with a chain on it, and a number on it that isn't a number, and a trough, and a field."

"That's all it is," he said.

He had a pad of work notes in the front of the van and he had it out by then, because he has a job on the Monday at the top of that parade and he had come down the lane to have a look at a gully on the corner, and the pad was open on the wing mirror and there was a pencil stub in his hand.

He put the pencil stub between his teeth, and shut the pad, and put it back inside the door, and shut the door, and got the batten up on the roof of the van and went off up the lane.

The young woman of about twenty stayed where she was for about a minute with the gate in front of her, and then she went back down the lane to the bus stop, and the bus came at ten to twelve and she got on it, and at the end of that street there is a wall with a gate in it too, and that one is a different gate and it belongs to a different thing and it was shut.

**AND ON THE MONDAY THE MAN IN HIS THIRTIES DID THE GUTTER ON THAT CORNER AND WENT, AND THE BURN STACK THAT CAME OUT OF A LANE OFF MARSH LANE WENT UP THE PARADE ON A VAN BEHIND HIM, AND THE PAD STAYED IN THE VAN WITH A PENCIL STUB IN IT, AND ON THE MONDAY AFTER THAT IT SNOWED FOR AN HOUR AND THE PADLOCK WAS STILL HANGING OPEN IN THE HASP AT THE TOP OF THAT LANE, AND NOBODY WENT UP IT AGAIN.**
