# Chapter 1007 — Her Name At The End Of The Last Line

Undated in this draft.

**THE SCULLERY AT NUMBER TWELVE HAS ITS RANGE OUT AND A COLD SLAB.**

The scullery at number twelve is a room about seven feet by five with one window high on the wall that faces the back yards and no fire in it since before the summer. The range stands against the far wall on the left of the window and it is a big black thing on four legs with a hotplate on top of it and a flue going into the chimney breast, and it has been out since before the woman came back from the river in the autumn and has been standing in the cold ever since. The slab beside the range on the side the range stands against is wet the whole way down, and the wall behind the range carries a damp line about a foot high that is darker than the paint above it and has a soft edge to it where the limewash has come away in a curve. The edge of that slab is not square to the wall it stands against and has not been since the range was put in, so the wet always sits down in the corner and never runs off anywhere, which is the whole reason for the damp line and which nobody in that house has ever mentioned.

She is at the scrubbed table with her sleeves turned back and the front of her apron wet, and he came in through the yard door with the wall behind the range ready in his mouth, and she had the page out of her apron before he had his coat off. That is how it happened. He came up the frontage with a thing to ask for and it was ready, and she put a sheet of paper on a scrubbed table in a cold room in front of him and asked him for something first, and everything he came with went out of his head and stayed out.

"You never brought that page to the front room." She flattened it with the heel of her hand. "You told me you would bring it to the front room. I have not looked in the drawer and I have not opened the drawer and I do not intend to."

"I have not been in your front room since the coal shed."

"I did not want it in a drawer either." She turned the sheet round so that the bottom of it was nearest him. "Look at it. Six houses and a day against each, and a thing done against the day. And at the end of the last of them, in your own hand, your name. I have carried that about in this apron for four weeks and I have taken it out about nine times and I have never once read past your name."

"It is a good page."

"It is a page with a man's name at the end of it doing six jobs nobody asked him to do, and not one line anywhere on it has my mother's deciding in it, and there has not been one since before she went." She let the heel of her hand rest on the paper. "So take your name off it and put mine in the same place. My name, my hand, the same size. And then it is a page about this house and the woman in this house had it done, and when my mother comes back off the water she can look at it and see that somebody has been asking her what she wants for thirty-one years and has never once got an answer."

He looked at it for a while, and he did not look at it as long as a man who had come to ask for a wall would have. He had known what she was going to ask since the coal shed, when she had told him she would have to agree to whatever he wanted and would not afterwards be able to tell whether she had wanted it or had wanted him. He came up this frontage with the wall in his coat and a woman at number nine's words in his ears about a man who offers, and here was a woman asking him for the one thing he has wanted since he was twenty-three, offered across a scrubbed table by somebody who would have to agree.

"Your hand on it is not the same as your name on it. If your own hand is there it is a thing you did. If mine is there, it is a thing I did about you, and any woman on this frontage who comes in here and looks at it will tell her that a man came to the house and put her down, and she will be right, and so will the woman who put her down."

"That is what I am asking you for."

"You are asking me for a page in this house with your name at the end of it, and I am telling you what the page is. A page is worth what a person can be asked about afterwards. You would not be able to be asked about this one."

"Then I would be a name on a page who cannot be asked about." She did not raise her voice, and she has no reason to raise it, and she had the cloth out of her apron and was wringing it again without knowing she was doing it. "My mother had a line put down for her in her own hand in the spring and she did not ask for it and she has had it in a folded paper in her apron ever since, and she cannot tell you one thing about what she makes of it. I am not going to be that. I would rather be a name that cannot be asked about than a name that was asked for and did not get it."

He put both hands flat on the table either side of the sheet and thought about his own yard, where four feet of kerb was lying along a wall with nobody at the unit knowing anything about it, and about the wall behind that range, which is the only work on this frontage that a man with a page in front of him would have thought to ask him for, and about how he had come up here this morning to ask for it and was going to walk back down the frontage without having asked. A man who has just been given a thing in a room like this one does not stand at a scrubbed table and ask for a wall while she is still at the sink with her back turned to him.

"All right," he told her. "Give me the pencil and turn round."

She gave him the pencil out of the pot on the shelf and she turned round, and she put both hands in the sink and stood there with her back to the room while he struck his own name out with one line through the middle of it and put her own underneath in the same hand and at the same size, and it took him a great deal longer than it should have done, and the paper is thin and one of the lines went through.

That was the whole of the morning he came up here for, and it cost him the only thing he has ever had that anybody wanted, and it was done in a minute and a half by a man in a cold room with a pencil out of a pot. He is not sure it was honour. He has gone over it since and the only thing he can put on it is that she asked first and he had nothing else ready.

The page lies where she put it with her own name in the last line of it, and she will be able to tell her mother that a man came into this house and put no name anywhere on it, and she has the whole of that to put to whoever asks her, because her back was turned the whole of the minute and a half it took.
