# Chapter 0276 — A Job Number

Monday the twenty-first of November 2016, from about eight in the morning, a back yard at the end of a street off Marsh Lane in Tideglass, and an office on the far side of an estuary eight miles off.

**TWO MEN WHO BOTH WANTED THE SAME JOB GOT IT, AND A MAN EIGHT MILES OFF HAD ALREADY GOT A NUMBER FOR IT, AND THE ONLY THING ANYBODY IN THAT YARD LEARNED WAS ONE WORD, AND HE WAS NEVER TOLD WHY THE WORD IS THE WORD.**

The rain came in on the Sunday night and it was the rain that settled the argument.

The stack on the semi at the end of the lane had been leaning since the summer, and it had had a cap put on it in a hurry about then by a man on a ladder with a bucket of mortar, and the top course had gone over in the frost in the last week of October and had laid a course of brick down the side of the gutter, and a lump of it was sitting on the lead at the back.

By eight on the Monday morning it was down to the roof line and in a heap in the middle of the yard under a tarpaulin, and the flue was open at the top with nothing over it, and the rain was going straight down it.

The man in his thirties, who does building work and odd jobs out of a terrace off Corrance Lane, was barrowing the last of the brick out of the way. The woman who has lived in that house since the spring was stood in the doorway with her arms folded and a piece of paper in her hand with lean and a date on it.

"It is going straight down," she said.

"It is," said the man in his thirties. "It has been going straight down since about nine yesterday and there is water standing in the breast of the chimney at the bottom of it, and that is not my doing, and he told me on Friday it would be."

"So he was right."

"He was right about it," said the man in his thirties, "and I want that said, and I am not going to stand here and say I was right about it, because I was not."

---

They had both turned up at eight on the Saturday on purpose, and neither of them had told the other he was coming, and neither of them had wanted it more than the other, and there was a job on it in June and a price and a man who had come to look at the stack in June and said it was two days for one man.

They did not agree inside the first minute.

The man in his thirties said the stack came down to the roof line, the flue opening got a cowl on it, the hole got made good, and that was a day and a half and the cheapest way to make it safe.

The other man, who does roofing and guttering out of a van with a ladder rack, said the flue was lined and the lining was a bad one, and that you do not cap a bad lining, because the rain comes down it and stands in the top of the chimney breast and that is how the breast got soft in the first place.

"You'd know that if you'd been up it."

"I've not been up it," said the man in his thirties. "You have."

"Twice."

"When?"

"August and Friday," said the man with the ladder rack, and pulled the ladder off the van, and did not say it again.

That was the whole of the disagreement. It was about the job. It lasted the whole of the Saturday, and by half past two there was a flue open in a roof with nothing over it and rain coming down it, and the man in his thirties had watched about a minute of that go into the top of the breast and had not said one word, and then he said, "All right."

The woman with the piece of paper had a bill from a firm with an office over the water on her kitchen counter, and it had come on the Friday, and it was a printed sheet with a heading and a reference and a figure and a job number printed along the top, and the job number was five figures with a letter in the middle of it.

She had rung the office on the Saturday morning and a woman had been very pleasant and had said the booking was cancelled in August and that a credit would go back on the account.

"I haven't cancelled anything," the woman had said. "Nobody rang me."

"Then it will be looked at," the woman on the telephone had said, and had not asked her for the job number, because it was printed at the top of the sheet she was holding in her hand.

The man in his thirties was at that table at a quarter past nine on the Monday with a cup of tea he had not asked for, because she had asked him to look at the sheet and he had said he would.

"When did you first ring them."

"The second of June. Or near it."

"And they gave you a price, and you waited, and they never came, and you rang twice more and it rang out, and then you stopped ringing."

"I stopped because I felt daft about it."

"That is the whole of what happened here," said the man in his thirties. "There's nobody in that office who's done anything to you. He took the job in June and he was in a mess all summer and he took it off the book in August and told them so, and they are right, and he was entitled to take it off the book, and none of that has got anything to do with you and nothing is going to make it to."

The man on the estuary was on the telephone at half past ten and he was the same one who had come to look at the stack in June, and he was quick and he was not apologetic.

"Read me the number off the top of that."

The man in his thirties read it out.

"Right," said the man. "That's not a six. That's a two. You've a two on it."

"So?"

"So the six is a job at Sarn we had in the middle of September and the two is a flat refurbishment out past the bridge, and the two is finished and closed off with a final on it. That is what happens. When a fitter gets re-tasked the ticket is raised on whatever's open at the time and it is raised by whoever is nearest the pad, and the pad is a lad with a mobile phone and no interest in the number, and he does not get paid for writing it and he cannot do anything else while he is doing it. Your hours went on the two and his went on the two and the two was half a mile off, and it is the same site description and the same postcode on both, so nobody that saw it had any way of telling."

"You've billed me for a man who never came."

"I have not," said the man. "You'll have a credit note by Friday for the second of June and you will not be charged the call-out either, because the call-out was ours. But the ticket is the ticket and I am not going to unpick it by hand from here, because the day I do that it stops being a ticket and starts being a favour, and the office has not got room for favours."

"Fair enough."

"That is a recharge," said the man. "That'll show on the statement. Everything that went on the two instead of the six shows as a recharge and the year end sorts it."

"Right," said the man in his thirties.

"You don't know what that means, do you."

"I know what it sounds like."

"It sounds like nothing," said the man. "It's the word we put on it and I am not going to unpick it for you on a Monday morning, and neither is anybody else in that office, and the day one of us did it by hand it would stop being a ticket. Credit note Friday, bin the sheet."

He did not ask about it again, and nobody explained it to him, and he used the word twice in the fortnight that followed: once in a shop about a kettle that somebody else had paid for, and once, correctly, to a man who had come to look at a gutter and was quoting a price for a job that had already been done and invoiced by another firm in August.

**AND THE COWL WENT ON THE FLUE ON THE TUESDAY AFTERNOON AND THE BREAST WAS MADE GOOD IN THREE DAYS, AND THE WOMAN WITH THE PIECE OF PAPER PAID THE MAN IN HIS THIRTIES IN CASH ON THE SECOND AFTERNOON AND PAID THE MAN WITH THE LADDER RACK ON THE THIRD, AND THE CREDIT NOTE CAME ON THE FRIDAY WITH THE FIGURE OF THE SECOND OF JUNE AT THE TOP OF IT AND A LINE AT THE FOOT THAT SAID THE SAME THING TWICE, AND NEITHER MAN WOULD PUT ANYTHING IN WRITING, AND THE MAN IN HIS THIRTIES TOOK THE LAST BARROW OUT OF THAT YARD ON THE MONDAY AND WENT OFF DOWN THE LANE WITH IT.**
