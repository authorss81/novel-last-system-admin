# Chapter 0392 — Where It Came From

Friday the seventeenth of February 2018, from about half past eight in the morning until about half past nine at night, a street off the parade, in Tideglass.

**A MAN IN HIS SIXTIES PUT A TIN INTO THE HAND OF A MAN IN HIS FORTIES ON A STREET OFF THAT PARADE ON THE AFTERNOON OF THE SEVENTEENTH OF FEBRUARY AND SAID NOTHING AT ALL AS HE DID IT, AND THE MAN IN HIS FORTIES ASKED HIM ONE QUESTION ABOUT IT ON THE MONDAY AND GOT AN ANSWER THAT DID NOT ANSWER IT, AND HE CARRIED THAT TIN ABOUT WITH HIM FOR FOUR DAYS AND PUT IT BACK ON THE SAME LEDGE ON THE TUESDAY WITHOUT A WORD, AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS MENTIONED IT SINCE.**

The street runs back off the parade behind a row of shops and it is about a hundred yard long and it is not a through street. There is a low brick wall at the dead end of it about two foot high with a coping on it, and set into the brick of the first building on the left, about four foot off the ground, there is a ledge about fourteen inch by ten.

That ledge is a piece of dressed concrete about nine inch thick put into the brickwork to stop the older part of that building leaning over the newer part. It is not a shelf and it is not a step and nothing in the street has ever been put on it.

The man in his sixties goes and sits on that low wall at the end of the street when the weather is not doing anything much. He has done for years. He goes out at about half past one, more or less, and he sits there until about three, and if it is raining he goes and sits in the bus shelter at the parade end instead.

---

The tin came off the top of the wardrobe in the front bedroom of that house.

He does not know how long it has been up there. The top of that wardrobe is where things go in that house that nobody is going to throw out and nobody is going to look at, and there are about nine or ten things on it, and the tin has been one of them since about 2014, and nobody has moved any of them since before Christmas and nobody has ever counted them.

What it is: a tin about four inch across and about three and a half inch tall, galvanised, with a lid that fits down inside the rim, and a crease pressed into one side a third of the way down, and nothing written on it and nothing on the lid, and no rust in it at all, and nothing in it.

He has had it since about 2014 and it came from a house in which he did a day's work, and it came in with a load of things off a bench and it went into a bag and he took the bag home and it went up on the top of that wardrobe and it has been up there ever since.

He had it in his hand on the afternoon of the seventeenth.

That is the part he cannot account for and he has not tried to account for it. He went out to that wall at about half past one and he had the tin in the right hand because he had been in the front bedroom for his other coat and had picked the tin up off the top of the wardrobe instead of the cap he had gone in for, and by the time he was out on that wall with a cup in his other hand he had not worked out that he was holding a tin.

The man in his forties came down that street at about twenty past two.

He was going to a house at the parade end to look at a fire that had gone out, and he came down that street because it is shorter than the parade, and he came down it at the pace a man comes down a street when he has not got far to go.

He was about ten foot from the low wall when the man in his sixties said something.

---

Here is the whole of it.

The man in his forties said, "Evening."

The man in his sixties said, "There's a fella wants a hand at the Cleeve road tomorrow, if you're the one who was in that terrace last week."

And as he said it he put the tin into the other man's hand, and the other man took it because his other hand was in his pocket and there was a hand coming at him, and it went into his hand and the hand closed on it, and the man in his forties looked down at what he had been given.

There was no second sentence from either of them about the tin. Not then and not since.

The man in his forties said, "Right," and the man in his sixties said, "I'll let him know," or something of that order, and the man in his forties went on down towards the parade and the man in his sixties sat on the low wall for another half hour with the cup in his hand and no tin in it.

He had it in the front of the van on the shelf behind the seat, and it stayed there.

He put it there when he got back and it was on that shelf at about ten past three that afternoon and it was on that shelf that evening and it was on that shelf the next morning and the morning after.

He did not open it. There is nothing in it, and he did not put his hand in it, and it is not locked and has never been locked. It sat on a shelf in a van for four days and he did not know what it was for and he has not found out and he was not going to find out by opening a thing somebody had handed him in a street with no preamble at all.

He thought about it perhaps twenty times in those four days. He thought about the man in his sixties having it in his hand. He could not work out how a man comes to be holding a tin in a street on the Friday afternoon, and he could not work out why a man who wanted a man at the Cleeve road tomorrow hands over a tin as the way of saying it.

He put it on the seat beside him twice while he was doing a job and the first time it fell over and he picked it up and the second time he put it in the footwell. He could not think of a single thing to do with it.

He has not told anybody that he was given it. He has not told the man in his sixties, and he has not told the man in his thirties, and nobody else has seen it, and the one person in this borough he could have said it to in the ordinary way of things is the man who put it in his hand, and he did not say it to him.

He asked about it on the Monday.

He was in that shop at about a quarter to twelve and the man in his sixties was in there at about ten past twelve, and they are two men who have said good afternoon to each other in that shop a good few dozen times.

"Where did you get that tin?" said the man in his forties.

"It was up in the house," said the man in his sixties.

And the man in his forties did not ask what house and did not ask why and did not ask what the tin was for, and there is a second question available in that shop on the Monday morning which is why, and it is about eight seconds long, and the answer to it would have been a silence of about four seconds followed by nothing at all.

He did not ask it. He got his change and went out and pulled the edge of the shutter to behind him, which he does not do.

He has not worked out why he was handed it. He has not worked out why the man in his sixties said the thing about the Cleeve road and gave him a tin in the same breath, as though one had caused the other, and it had not, and the man in his sixties did not think it had either, because the man in his sixties had not noticed what was in his hand until the other man's hand was under it.

That is the actual reason and neither of them will ever know it.

He put it back on the Tuesday the twentieth.

He came down that street at about twenty past two on the afternoon of the Tuesday for the same reason he came down it on the Friday, which is that it is shorter than the parade, and there was nobody at the low wall and nobody in that street at all except a woman going in at one of the houses about halfway along.

He put the tin on the ledge.

He put it on the ledge the way it had been, which is that it went on flat and the dented side was towards the road and the lid went on the top, and he did not have to think about that, it just went the way it goes.

Then he went on down towards the parade, did the fire, and went home.

He said nothing to anybody. There was nobody to say it to.

The man in his sixties came back to that wall on the Wednesday the twenty-first and the tin was on that ledge and he took it and put it in his pocket and took it home and it went back up on the top of that wardrobe in about the place it had been, and it is up there now.

He did not think about where it had been for the four days it had been gone. He did not think about it then, and it has not turned up in his head since, and if anybody raised it with him on that parade a week after, he could not have answered it without being given the whole of the morning first, and what he would have said then is that he cannot remember a tin, and that would have been true.

**HE WENT DOWN THAT STREET ON THE THURSDAY AFTER AT TWENTY PAST TWO AND THE LEDGE WAS BARE AND HE WALKED ON UP TO THE PARADE, AND THE MAN IN HIS SIXTIES CAME BACK TO THAT WALL ON THE WEDNESDAY AND TOOK THE TIN OFF IT AND PUT IT IN HIS POCKET.**
