# Chapter 0350 — Two Doors

Monday the fourth of September 2017, from about ten in the morning until about half past four in the afternoon, a block by the water, a street off the Cleeve road, and a shop off a parade, in Tideglass.

**ON THE EIGHTEENTH OF AUGUST A SEWING MACHINE ON A WOODEN STAND WAS PUT DOWN ON THE STEP AT THE TOP OF AN OUTSIDE STAIR AND TURNED DOWN IN ONE WORD, AND ON THE SECOND OF SEPTEMBER A WOODEN CHAIR WAS CARRIED TO A DOOR A MILE OFF THAT STAIR AND LEFT AGAINST IT, AND NEITHER WOMAN WAS TOLD THAT THE OTHER THING HAD HAPPENED.**

The stair is an outside one at the back of a block by the water and it goes up the rear wall in one run, and the rail on it is fixed to the brick about a foot under the hand height, and at the top of it there is a square of roof over the landing so that the rain does not get on it, and four doors come off that landing and the fourth of them belongs to the woman of about fifty-five.

The man with a van is not the man in his thirties. He emptied a house on the Cleeve road in May and has taken two things out of it since, and there was a third thing in the back of that van on the eighteenth of August.

The sewing machine is a green one on a wooden stand about three foot long with a knee lever under it and a box in the stand about nine inch deep, and there is a foot in that box and a shuttle and a bodkin and a card of needles and a tin of pins with about nine pins in it, and the belt is on and the needle is threaded and the thread is on a reel in that box and it is not the machine of anybody in this borough.

It weighs about what a man wants two people for on a stair.

He got it up those nine treads in two carries on the Thursday of the eighteenth of August at about half past nine in the morning, and the first carry was the stand and the second was the head off it, and he set the stand down on the landing outside her door and carried the head up and put it on the stand and then knocked with the back of his hand.

She came to the door with her cardigan over her arm.

He did not tell her what it was. He stood aside so that she could see all of it, the stand and the head and the belt and the box and the foot in it, and he said the name of it, and he said where it had come from, which was a house he had emptied on the Cleeve road in May and had been keeping in his van since, and he said that if it was no use to her he would take it back down the way he brought it up and there would be nothing said about it either way.

"No," said the woman of about fifty-five.

He did not ask her why, and nothing at all came out of him, and the second between her no and his hands going back on to the head of that machine carried nothing out of either of them.

He took it down in two carries and left the street door on the latch at the bottom and shut the tailgate and drove off, and he went nowhere near that stair again after the eighteenth of August.

Going down nine treads with a sewing machine head in your arms is a different thing from going up them with it, and he went down it sideways, one tread at a time, with his back to the wall side, and he put the head on the boards at the bottom and then came back up for the stand, and he did that in about three minutes, and the noise he made on that stair is the only thing anybody in that block heard.

She is still in that room. She has not told anybody that a thing was put down on that step and she has not said one word about it to the woman in the room next door along, and nobody in that block knows that it happened, and there is nobody in that block anybody could tell.

The sewing machine is in a garage eight miles off with about nine other things that belonged to the same house, and a man there who keeps a list of what comes in has written one line against it, and not one person in this borough has ever laid eyes on it, and that line is the only record anywhere that the machine came out of a house on the Cleeve road in May and went up nine treads in August and came down again.

---

The chair is a wooden chair. It came out of the same house and it is the second of four and it has a spindle back and a seat that has gone the colour of tea in a ring and it is heavier than a chair looks.

He carried it up a street off the Cleeve road on the Saturday before this, at about ten in the morning, because he was working two doors further down that week and the van was full and the chair was stood in the middle of it where he could not get round it.

The house is number fourteen and it is a terrace and the front step is about a foot high and the door is a wooden one with a fanlight over it that has not been painted since March.

He put the chair down on that step against the door.

He did not knock. He did not leave a note. He did not put it under the mat or on the landing or out of the rain, which it was not, and he did not say one word to anybody, and the seat of that chair is a ring of tea and the front of the bottom rail has a white mark on it where somebody's boots stood.

Then he went on down that street with his bag to the house he was working at, which is number nine, and he was there until about half past four, and he has not been near number fourteen again.

The man in his forties was working at number nine on the Saturday and he is working at number nine on the Monday, and at about half past ten on the Monday morning the woman who lives at number fourteen came out of her front door to go to the shops and there was a wooden chair on her step against her door.

She looked at it for about a minute. She went back in and got her bag, came out again, pulled the door to behind her, and went off down the street with the chair, and the chair went with her, and it is in her front room now against the wall under the window where she has had a chair since before the summer, and the three that came with it are in a garage eight miles off in a load of other things belonging to other people.

Nobody has put the question to her. Nobody on that street has raised it. The man in his forties, who is working at number nine, did not know there was a chair on that step and has not been asked and would not have been interested, and the man in a fleece went past that house at about twelve and looked at the step and did not stop, and the two of them have never spoken.

The woman at number fourteen is about thirty-five and moved in at the end of February and her husband is on the boats, and there is a settee in her front room with a throw over the arm of it that is not big enough for the arm, and there has been a gap at the end of that settee for a chair since the first week of June. The chair went into that gap the same night and it does not match and it is a foot too wide and one of the spindles on the back of it is a replacement and a different colour. She has not mentioned it to a living soul and she has not shifted it since the first night.

There is nothing about that chair that a person could trace. It came out of a house on the Cleeve road in May, it spent eight weeks in a van, and it was on a step in a street for about ten hours and nobody saw it arrive and nobody saw it leave.

---

He was at the counter of that shop on the parade at about half past three on the Monday afternoon for about five minutes, and he bought a box of screws, and he told her about the sewing machine.

"I had a sewing machine up that outside stair on Thursday week," he said. "Big one, on a stand, with a foot in the box. Off the house I emptied in May."

"She turned it down."

"She turned it down in one word," said the man with a van. "I did not ask her anything and I did not say one word after it and I have not been up there since."

"That is about three times you have been up that stair since June."

"That is three times," said the man with a van, "and I have got two things I have brought out of that house and there is about nine foot of wall in a bedroom I could make if anybody wanted it, and I am not going up there again until somebody in that block asks me to, and nobody in that block has a telephone."

She said that was fair, and he paid for the screws, and he did not mention the chair, and he has never mentioned the chair, and there is nobody in that shop or on that parade who has any idea a chair went to number fourteen off a street where a man in his forties was working at number nine the whole time.

**A SEWING MACHINE WENT DOWN NINE TREADS IN TWO CARRIES ON THE EIGHTEENTH OF AUGUST AND A CHAIR WENT UP A STEP ON THE SECOND OF SEPTEMBER, AND THE MAN WHO CARRIED BOTH HAS SPOKEN TO A WOMAN AT A COUNTER ABOUT ONE OF THEM AND WILL NEVER KNOW THAT HE DROPPED THE OTHER TWO HUNDRED YARD FROM A DOOR HE WAS WORKING NEXT TO ALL MORNING.**
