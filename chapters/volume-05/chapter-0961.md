# Chapter 0961 — Behind The Standing Pipe

Saturday the seventh of December 2024, at the corner of the house at number nine with the light coming off the fields. Behind a standing pipe.

**THE PIPE HAS DROPPED AWAY FROM THE BRICK ACROSS THE BAY BETWEEN ONE BRACKET AND THE NEXT, THE UPPER FIXING HAVING WORKED OUT OF ITS MORTAR, AND THE GAP SO MADE IS CLOSED AT BOTH ENDS AND OPEN TO THE WEATHER, AND THE HAND GOES INTO IT AND IS PUSHED AWAY FROM THE WALL UNTIL THE PIPE COMES BACK ONTO IT, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE PIPE STANDS OFF AND WHAT IS BEHIND IT.**

The pipe is a cast iron rainwater pipe of the kind that was put on every house of that row when the house was new, and it has been painted over three times and is the colour of a plaster now rather than of a paint.

It runs down the corner of that house from the eaves gutter to a hopper near the ground, on a bracket at about every two feet, and each bracket is a cast piece let into the mortar joint of the brick with a wood plug behind it. The joint that has let go is the one about four feet above the ground, and it has not let go because the plug has rotted. It has let go because whoever set that bracket raked the mortar out of the joint to a depth of an inch, which is what anybody does to a joint a trowel will not go into, and a plug set an inch deep in a raked joint has nothing behind it at all.

So the bracket has come out far enough for the pipe to lean away from the brick over the two feet between that bracket and the next one down, and the pipe has taken its own weight with it and has leaned further, and the wedge between the pipe and the wall is about an inch and a half where the pipe stands highest and nothing at all where it lies against the bracket.

---

Into the top of that opening goes the hand, palm flat against the brickwork, and from there it is driven away from the wall.

Pushing away from a wall into a wedge is the way to find out how far a thing will come towards you before it stops, and this one comes towards the hand after about an inch and then stops, because a cast iron pipe of that diameter is stiff its own length and will not bend over two feet to put a hand away.

The pipe does not move at all. The wedge closes on the hand and the hand is squeezed in it, and the squeeze is not the arm's weight and not the pressure of pushing, and it is the first time in a long while that anything has pushed back. What the wedge closes on is the side of the pipe, which is stiff and cold and has a raised band round it where a socket used to be sealed with lead and a bit of oakum, and the band is about three inches above the hand and not at the hand.

Below the hand, the wedge runs on down the pipe and shuts at the bracket, and the bracket is where it shut, and there is nothing on the wall behind the pipe anywhere along that two feet except old brick and the ghost of a mortar joint that a man once raked out to a depth of an inch. The socket below the bracket is the joint that carries nothing, because a pipe is carried at its brackets and a socket between two of them is there to allow for the ground moving and not to take a weight. Above the hand the wedge opens out to the weather and the paint on the outside of the pipe at that place has crazed into cracks the size of a fingernail, because the weather has one side of it and the wall has the other.

A rainwater pipe of that size is put up in lengths of about six feet with a socket at the top of each, and every joint is wiped round with lead and oakum, and the lead on a joint that was done well is still grey and still soft enough to take the print of a thumb. There are three joints above the bracket that has worked and there is nothing wrong with any of them, and the pipe below that bracket lies tight against the brick for its whole length and stays tight, because the bracket is where the weight of a pipe is carried.

At the top of the run there is a hopper, a box about a foot across the front of it, with a grille set in the front, and that hopper is the sole thing on that corner reachable without a ladder or steps. Where the pipe passes the eaves gutter above it there is a cast lug in the brick for a ladder to go, and no ladder is against that wall and there has not been one since the gutter over it was last cleared.

Three coats of paint have gone on that pipe and each went on over the wet coat below it, which is why it is the colour of a plaster and not the colour of a paint, and why it is thicker at the foot of it than anywhere else. The brick behind the pipe has not moved. It is the brick of the corner and it was built about a hundred and forty years ago and it carries the whole weight of the two eaves above it and it has a crack over the bedroom window that was there before the house was let.

The paint on the inside of that wedge has not crazed at all, for the same reason the paint under the channel at the lock-up has not crazed, and the two of them are a hundred yards apart and the same observation. Where the bracket has come out, the hole it came out of is still in the mortar joint and it is a round hole and not an oval one, because a cast bracket screwed to a plug pulls straight out of lime and does not turn in the hole on its way.

There is a lime plug in that hole and it is a loose fit by hand and it has been a loose fit for four years, and the whole of what has happened on that corner is contained in the difference between that plug and the plug a foot below it. A raked joint is the commonest fault in any old wall of that kind and nobody knows it is there. It does not show on the face of the wall at all, because the face of a raked joint is the face of a raked joint and looks exactly like the face of a flush one, and the fault is a foot deep behind it.

Nothing has been made good at that bracket. The plug is still in the wall where it went, the mortar is still raked to a depth of an inch where it was raked, and the bracket is still carrying the pipe on one fixing at the top and one at the bottom. Below that bracket the next bracket along is sound, and its plug is the same vintage as the one that worked and has sat in that joint for a hundred and forty years without moving, which is what a plug does when it is set in a joint that was properly filled.

The woman who does her shopping is on the grass verge between the kerb and the front boundary wall, facing the house across the road, with both hands in her pockets. She stays on the verge a while looking up at the front of the house and once she shifts her weight from one foot to the other without coming any nearer, and nothing else happens on that side of the road. When she goes she goes along the verge towards the shop and does not come onto the front of the house, and the pipe on the corner of that house is where it has been all morning and where it will be this evening.

The hand that went into that wedge came back an inch narrower than it went in and there was a cold wet print of the brick in the creases of it, on finding that the pipe stood off that wall by an inch and a half and would not be pushed any further by anybody.
