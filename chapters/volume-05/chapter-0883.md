# Chapter 0883 — The Seam Gone Underneath

Saturday the tenth of February 2024, at the hessian sack in the far corner of that back yard at number ten, from the time the frost has come off the sacking until the middle of the afternoon. Inside the mouth of the sack, and how far down it is.

**THERE IS A HESSIAN SACK STANDING IN THE CORNER OF THAT BACK YARD WITH SOIL TURNED OVER INSIDE IT AND THE SOIL A FOOT DOWN FROM THE MOUTH, AND SHE MEANS TO PUT BOTH HANDS INTO THE MOUTH OF IT, ONE OVER THE OTHER, AND PUSH DOWN INSIDE AS FAR AS THEY WILL GO, AND HER SISTER IS AT THE YARD GATE WITH HER HAND ON THE LATCH.**

The sack came from somewhere in the summer and it has been in that corner since, and the soil in it has been turned over with a spade twice, which is the whole of what happens to a sack of soil in a corner: it gets turned over in the spring and it gets turned over in the autumn and it stands.

It is not tied. The mouth of it is folded over and pushed in, the way a sack of anything is closed when the thing in it is not going anywhere.

She has never put her hands in it. There is no reason to put your hands in a sack of soil. You know how full a sack is by how high it stands.

Except that you do not, because a sack of soil does not stand in the shape of what is in it the way a sack of something else does.

A sack of soil is a sack of loose material. Loose material finds its own angle of repose and it stands at that angle whether it is half full or three quarters full, and a hessian sack full of soil stands about the same height as a hessian sack a third full, and the difference is in the width of the mouth when you look at it and in how far the sides of it have come out.

So she puts both hands in.

One hand goes in flat, palm down, and it goes in a good way before it comes on anything.

The other hand goes in on top of it, and then the first one is turned palm up and the second turned palm down and the two of them go in together one over the other, and the arms go in after them to about the forearm.

Then they stop, at the same level, on soil.

The soil is about a foot down from the mouth of that sack.

The sack is standing about two foot six and a quarter full, which is to say it is a sack standing up on its own soil with a foot of empty hessian above it.

That foot of empty hessian is the part nobody would have guessed, and the soil below it gives under the hand in a way it did not give the day it was put in.

It is not slack. It is standing open. It is the shape of a sack that has had something taken out of the top of it, not poured in, because a sack that has been poured into stands with its mouth gathered up and its shoulders full, and this one is standing with its shoulders full and its mouth open and about a foot of hessian inside it doing nothing.

The soil inside has been turned over twice with a spade and both times the spade went in from the top, and a spade goes in through a foot of nothing before it comes on the pile.

And the two have become one thing.

This is the finding, and it takes about five minutes with the hands in there and it is not a surprise when it comes.

The inside of that hessian has gone furry. The fibre has come off the inside of the cloth and gone into the soil, and the soil has gone into the weave of the cloth, and the two of them are interlocked in the same way that a heap of stone locks against itself.

Which means the sack cannot be tipped out.

The thing that stops it is not the soil being heavy. It is that a full hessian sack of soil cannot be emptied by tipping it, because the soil comes out and the hessian comes out with it, and what is left in your hands is a hessian bag of soil and what is on the ground is a heap of hessian and soil, and neither one of them is a heap of soil you can put a spade into.

There is no way to get that soil off the cloth without taking it off it in handfuls, and that is a job with a barrow and a morning and a person who is not going to do it in February in a corner of their own yard.

Her hands go down the inside of the sack on both sides, because that is what a person does when they have their hands in a thing and want to know the shape of the bottom of it.

And on the underside of the sack, at the back corner, in the place where the sacking is doubled over because that is where a sack is folded when it is filled, the seam has gone.

Not a hole. A seam that has opened about a hand's width, along a foot, at the very bottom where the fold is thickest and where the soil inside has been sitting against it since the summer.

And under the sack there is soil on the flags.

A hand's depth of it, over about the width of a foot and a half, lying out on the flags inside that corner, under the sack, in the place the barrow does not go and the spade has not been since the summer.

It is dry underneath and loose on top, and it has been there long enough that there is a month of grit worked into it, which is how long soil gives before it will take a mark.

That is where it came from. Not out of the mouth, which is folded over and pushed in. Out of a seam at the bottom, over four months, onto flags that have been covered by the sack the whole time.

And what is under that soil is a flag that has been taken up and relaid.

She can feel it with the back of her fingers where the soil thins out at the edge. It is level with the flags around it, and it is the colour of the ones around it, and it has no joint at that end that a thumbnail will go into.

Those flags were up in the summer and back down in the summer and the soil in that sack was put down on top of one of them.

So the sack stands where it stands, over the one patch in that corner where the ground was made good in the summer, with a quarter of a barrow of soil lying out underneath it on a flag that is level, and no way of getting at the soil without lifting the sack off it.

The thread on the fold is still where it was drawn, three inches and stopped at a loom knot. The four others are still on the outside of the fold.

---

Her sister comes to the yard gate from inside about eleven with her hand on the latch and goes out through it.

She wanted to know whether the cold frame glass had come back from the shop and it had not, and it was ordered in November. She wanted to know where the roll of wire was kept and it was not in the shed and she had looked twice. She was told it was on the shelf in the back passage behind the boards and she went and looked and it was not there either, and she came back and stood with her hand on the gate for a moment and then went.

The far corner of that yard was against the wall and the gate is at the other end of it.

When anybody gets at the soil under that sack they will find it on a relaid flag, in the corner where the sack has been standing over it since the summer.
