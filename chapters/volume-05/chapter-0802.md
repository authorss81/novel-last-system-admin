# Chapter 0802 — The Heap Off The Shed Wall

Friday the twenty-fourth of March 2023, in this borough. She is out in her own yard at number twelve for most of that Friday with a barrow and a shovel, and twenty barrowloads of the heap that has banked against that shed wall since the summer is the whole of it.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS A HEAP OF CUT GRASS BANKED AGAINST HER SHED WALL SINCE THE SUMMER WITH A WET-TEA STRIPE UP THE BRICK ABOVE IT AND MEANS TO TAKE IT DOWN AND PUT IT OUT ON THE ROAD, HER SISTER TAKES IT DOWN AND SPREADS IT ABOUT A FOOT DEEP OVER GROUND AT THE FAR END OF THAT YARD THAT HAS HAD NOTHING ON IT SINCE BEFORE SHE CAME, AND THE STRIPE ON THE BRICK STOPS WHERE IT WAS AND DOES NOT GO FURTHER, BECAUSE THERE IS NOTHING BEHIND IT TO GO UP.**

Her sister comes in at the yard gate in the morning. That gate shuts now, and has shut since the seventh of March, and it has to be lifted a foot to get under it, and she has to lift it. They are in that yard from then until the light goes.

---

That heap has been against the shed wall since the summer.

It is grass. It is two summers of grass, cut short on the front and the back and banked up against the shed in the last week of June and again in July and never moved since. It is about six feet along the wall, four feet deep into the yard at the high end, and a foot and a half where it has slumped away at the low end, and it is against a shed standing on the low side of that yard with its back to nothing at all.

There is a stripe up the brick above it. It goes from the top of the heap to about a foot and a half above that, and it is the colour of tea. It has been there all winter and it has not moved up or down, and the brick above the stripe is as dry as a brick nine feet up.

She found the stripe in the autumn and thought it was coming off the shed roof, and had the top of that shed looked at from the garden twice over, and it was not the roof. It is the bottom of that heap lying against the bottom four courses of that shed. The brick above the top of the heap got wet in October and dried in the spring and has been wet again since.

Her plan is to take it down and put it out on the road.

---

"You are putting it out on the road."

"I am putting it out on the road. It has to go somewhere and the road is there."

"It is a hundred yards to that gate and it is the width of a barrow and you would be going up and down it twenty times." Her sister has a fork in her hand and she has not put it down. "And what are you putting on the road."

"Cut grass."

"Cut grass in March, on the kerb, in front of eleven houses." She looks along the wall. "Who is going to put it in with that grass in it."

---

So they take it down off the wall, and that is the first thing that has to be right.

You cannot pull a heap off a wall. It has been lying against that shed since the summer and the wetter it has got the more it has squeezed, and the bottom of it has gone into the ground and the top of it is a foot above the top of the lowest board of the shed.

Her sister cuts in at the bottom of it with the fork and gets a barrow under, and she works from the wall outwards, and she never tries to lift anything out of the middle. She pulls the outside of it off and lays it in the barrow and works inward, so that what comes off the wall is the top and not the bottom, and every time the barrow is full she heaps it above the rim so that nothing comes off the top of the pile while it is full.

It comes off the wall in about an hour and a half and it does not smell the way anybody expects it to smell. It smells like a hay barn and then like a drain, and it comes off the wall in black clods about as big as a fist at the bottom and in wet matting at the top, and one forkful of it in the middle has gone together into something with no structure in it at all and that is the forkful that goes deepest.

Twenty goes.

The old woman takes the barrow out.

She takes it across the yard on the line of the shed and through the gate and away to the far end, where there is ground that has not had anything on it since before she came and has not been dug since before the war, and she tips each load where she has decided it goes.

It is a hundred yards each way and the barrow has a flat on it that has been flat since she came, and she goes at a good deal of a walk and she does not hurry one of them, because the twenty loads are going to be laid and where they go matters more than how quickly she gets them there. There is a line of worn grass already in that yard from the back door to the shed and she goes along the edge of it and not down the middle, so that in the summer there will still be a path under that grass and not two.

---

And the order of the loads is not a thing anybody thought of on the day.

The first three barrowfuls are the black ones off the bottom, wetter than anything and sour at the edge of them, and they go down in the middle of the far ground and not on the edge, because that is the wet stuff and the wet stuff goes where the heap can keep its own water in one place instead of drying out in nine different places.

Then the paler stuff round the sides of it, and then the stuff off the very top, which has been drying since the summer and is about the colour of a dry hay and would blow away in an east wind if it were put on the edge of anything.

By the middle of the morning she has worked out that the fourth and the ninth are the two that were under the old heap's north shoulder and are wetter than the ones round them, and she puts those in the middle as well, and she does not say a word about it and neither does her sister.

---

Then the spreading, and that is the second thing that has to be right.

They spread it about a foot deep over that whole patch, and not in a heap. Grass left in a heap is grass in a heap, and it is what came off that shed wall in the first place. Spread a foot deep and loose, it has air through it and it dries and it goes back into the ground by the summer and there is nothing on that patch to see at all from the road.

There is about a ton and a half of it and it is nine parts dead grass to one part of the sand and dust that came off the paving of the yard in the summer, and there is seed in it — the first cut of the back never went to seed and the second one did, and some of that seed is in there now and it will come up in that patch in April and it will be a thin crop of grass about six inches high and there is nothing else in that corner of the yard to argue with it.

The old woman works it out with the back of the fork and her sister works it out with the heels of her boots, and where it is a foot deep it is a foot deep all the way, because a foot deep over the middle and nothing over the edge is a heap with a different name.

The last two barrowloads go on top and are left as they come, and the whole of that patch is a foot deep of grass over about ten square yards and it is the only thing in that yard that has been done properly since she came.

---

And the stripe.

They go and look at it about halfway through the afternoon and it is exactly where it was.

It has not gone up and it has not got any wider at the top. It is going to come down to where the top of that heap was and no further, because that is where the wall got wet from the outside, and above that line there is nothing behind that brick to bring anything up through it.

She has done this before, in a different house on a different road, and the wall there came back to its own colour in about six weeks and the paint never came back at all, and she put three coats on the top half of it and left the bottom half exactly as it was, which is the way to leave a wall that has been wet from the ground up and is not going to be wet from anywhere else now.

Her sister puts the flat of her hand on the brick above the stripe and it is dry and cold and has been dry and cold all winter, and she says so, and the old woman puts her own hand in the same place and it is the same.

---

They clear up late and her sister goes out of the gate with the barrow and the fork and the shovel, and the old woman goes down the yard to the gate and stands at it and watches her go up the lane and does not go out.

She knows exactly which barrowful went where. She can tell you that the first three were the black ones off the bottom and that the fourth and the ninth were wetter than the ones round them because they were under the north shoulder, and she can tell you about a foot deep over ten square yards and not a foot deep over four.

That took two people and a day and she has done every barrowful of it herself. She could not have got that fork into the bottom of that heap at the wall without somebody else standing at the barrow, and there is nobody in that yard who would stand at a barrow.
