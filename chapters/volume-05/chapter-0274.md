# Chapter 0274 — Ordinary

Tuesday the fifteenth of November 2016, from about ten in the morning, a building by the water in Tideglass, a parade of shops, the low end of Peverell Street, and a hall off the parade.

**THE REGISTER IS AT NINETEEN, THE LINE UNDER THE NINETEENTH IS BLANK, AND ON A PARADE ONE MAN ASKED ANOTHER MAN WHO HAD SAID THEY WOULD DO IT AND WAS TOLD YOU, AND NEITHER OF THEM MINDED, AND NOT ONE THING WAS ANSWERED ANYWHERE IN THIS BOROUGH IN THE LAST OF AN AUTUMN.**

Nell Ardent opened the register on the Monday because there was a line out of it that she wanted in her own hand for a job on a form for a man who wanted a boiler off a house in Corrance Lane, and she copied that line, squared the paper, and shut the book.

Then she opened it again, because she does that, and she went down the page with her finger, and the nineteenth is still the nineteenth, and it is still a yard light with a bulb in a maintenance column and the bulb is still out.

The line under it goes down the page and stops about two inches short of the bottom of a book that is about two inches deep, and she worked it out on the corner of the form with the end of her pen, because it is a habit and nobody has ever asked her to keep it, and it came to seven hundred and twenty days.

She shut the book and put it back in the cupboard it came out of with the other books, and the form went in her bag with the line on it in her own handwriting, and the register did not move, and there is no name in that margin, and there has not been one since about November two years ago.

A man came out of the door of that building at about half past ten with a piece of paper in his coat and a biro he was not using, and went up the parade at the speed of a man who goes up that parade every week of his life, and nobody in the street said anything to him and he said nothing to anybody, and he was gone by the bank.

---

The shutter on the shop went up at about half past eight and the window card with that shop's own telephone number on it and nothing else on it was in the window where it has always been, and the woman of about sixty-five was behind that counter, and nobody went in and told her anything.

A van came off the bridge at about ten with a good deal of pipe in the back of it, and two men took about three lengths of it out and stood it on end against the front of the shop next door along from the one with the trade counter, and one of them asked the other who had said they would do it, and the other said, "You," and the first one said, "Right," and neither of them said anything else about it, and that was the only thing anybody in that parade had agreed to do in a fortnight and it made no difference to either of them.

The man behind the counter on the parade was out on the step at about eleven to look at it and said to a man coming out of a bank, "That's not bad for a Tuesday," meaning the pipe, and went back in.

At the bottom of Peverell Street there is a pump in a doorway and it is not plumbed to anything and the stopcock is off, and the wall beside it is bare with a clean strip of newer paint on it and a grey line of dirt above it. A woman went up that street with a bucket and came back down it with a bucket and did not stop at the door, and a man came down it with a dog and did not stop at the door either, and the radio inside number forty-one was very faint and has been very faint since the summer.

---

The first floor of the building by the water is being emptied again. The works were back on the Monday with a date in the middle of November and a sheet on the notice board, and the corridor at the end of it was about twenty feet with a window at one end and three doors and a fire door, and on the Wednesday morning a woman carried a bundle of post out of it that was addressed to a man who had left that building in the spring, and a man carried an umbrella stand out of it that had nothing in it and put it on the landing for the bin men, and neither of them said the other's name and neither of them was asked to.

The woman of about fifty-five finished a room over a shop at the end of a parade in the last week of September and took a second one in the same building in the middle of October on a month, and she is in it, and it has a door that shuts because somebody put a hasp on it in the summer, and she has not said a word to anybody about that and would not if anybody asked her, and the shop that shuts early has not said a word either.

By seven in the evening it was dark and wet again, and down at the far end of that same parade the hall was on its meter, because it is free because nobody rents it, and the woman who has had the key to it for about nine years had put money into that meter on the Friday and the gas was on.

About nine of them were in that room and not one of them was on anything.

They did the price of glass, and a man in the third house in Corrance Lane whose roof had been off since April and which the man from the parade still called a hobby, and a letter that had gone to the wrong address in October and come back, and a woman who wanted to know whether anybody in that room had a boiler fitter they could stand behind, and nobody had.

At about a quarter to nine, at a table by the wall, a woman leaned over towards a woman beside her and said, sideways, not to the room:

"Have you heard the register one?"

"My aunt had that one off a fellow off a lorry in July," said the woman beside her, "and I had it off her, and I have had it off about four people since, and I never once asked her where she got it from."

"It's the borough's own book."

"It's the book off the water, is it."

"It's the oldest dated thing this place keeps," said the first woman, "and that's what they all say, and I have said it myself twice."

And the woman beside her, without being asked about anything, said: "It's the day now, though. That's the other one. The twenty-eighth of June. That's what they're all saying."

"Day."

"Day," she said, and gave it across the table the way you give somebody a thing that is better than the thing they had, and neither of them said where they had got it from, and nobody at that table joined in, and the man in his sixties was four feet away with a cup of tea and did not look up, and the room went straight back to the price of glass.

The man from the parade, who has put his hand up in that room twice since the summer, did not put it up tonight, and nobody noticed that either, and he sat there until ten with his cup in both hands.

**AND THE HALL WAS EMPTIED AND THE CHAIRS WERE STACKED BY A MAN WHO PUT FOUR ON TOP OF FOUR AND LEFT TWO ON THE FLOOR, AND THE METER TICKED ON AFTER EVERYBODY WENT, AND THE GAS WAS SHUT OFF AT THE METER BY THE DOOR AT TEN PAST TEN BY THE ONLY PERSON WHO HAD A KEY TO IT.**
