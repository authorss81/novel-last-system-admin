# Chapter 0905 — Behind The Middle Brick

Sunday the twenty-eighth of April 2024, at the loose bricks at the end of the bed at number ten while the morning rain still darkens the soil. The slit behind a middle brick.

**SHE HAS THREE LOOSE BRICKS AT THE END OF THE BED AT NUMBER TEN AND SHE MEANS TO SLIDE THE PALM DOWN BEHIND THE MIDDLE ONE WITH FINGERS TOGETHER AND THUMB ALONG ITS SIDE AND HER SISTER IS AT THE BACK STEP FROM THE YARD WITH A CLOTH IN HER HAND.**

The three bricks have sat at the end of that bed since the edging came down, and the bed beside them has been dug over twice while the bricks have stayed where they were set.

They are bricks of red clay about eight inches long, with the faces rough from the mould and the ends square, and they lie in a row with the middle one a finger forward of the other two.

She wants the slit behind that middle one, where the brick stands off the soil and the rain runs down.

A slit behind a brick teaches more than the face of it, because the face shows the weather and the back shows what the weather stands on.

So the palm goes down behind the middle brick with fingers together and thumb along its side.

The brick feels cool through the skin, the way clay stays cool after rain, and the back of it drags a little on the palm where sand sticks to it.

The palm slides down about two inches and stops on something firm. Not brick. Soil, risen up behind the brick in a bank, firm and damp, with small stones in it and a root cut clean across where the fork went through in March.

She eases the palm a little to one side, and then a little to the other, and the soil gives about half an inch under the edge of the hand and then turns firm again.

What stops it is grit, packed tight at the foot of the bank, grey and sharp, with sand in it that locks it where it lies.

So the middle brick at the end of the row does not stand on the bed at all. It stands wedged between the soil in front of it and the grit behind it, with damp earth packed in the slit between, and the earth is what keeps it upright.

Her sister comes to the back step from the yard with a cloth in her hand and does not lay it down.

She stood at the step with the cloth folded over her wrist and looked toward the bricks and then toward the bed and back again, and after a while she shook the cloth out and folded it again and kept it in her hand.

She was given word the cloth would be wanted at the shed for the dust on the shelf, and she tucked it under her arm and went on down the path with it and left the step empty.

---

A row of loose bricks at the end of a bed is put there for a use that has nothing to do with building. A brick row is where spare bricks go when they come out of a path, and the end of a bed is where they sit until they are wanted for an edge.

The bricks at the ends of it are dark and green on one face, the sort that sat through winter under leaves, and the middle one is red and drier, the sort that spent winter under cover by the shed.

Red brick goes flat in a row in a way that blue brick does not. Blue brick has a glazed face that throws water off, and red brick has an open face that drinks a little and then dries, so it beds down into soil and the soil round it locks.

That is why the middle brick sits flat on its bank while the end ones rock a little when trodden near. The foot of it has sunk about half an inch into the earth, and the earth has come up round the foot, and the brick now sits on a footing of its own making.

The slit behind it runs about four inches across at the top and nothing at the bottom, where the brick meets the bank on a bed of sand.

Sand gathers where a brick meets soil and on nothing else, because rain takes the fine earth down through the bank and leaves the coarse stuff where it catches.

The palm finds the ridge of that meeting if pushed, and it can be pushed, which is how the brick came to sit where it sits.

A brick wedged like that will take a summer. Heat dries the soil in the slit and shrinks it a hair, and rain swells it again, and each time the brick sits a hair lower against its bank.

In two summers the middle brick has gone down about a quarter of an inch, because the lime mark on its face that was level with the next brick sits now a quarter below it.

The lime mark comes from the day the path was whitened, when the brush touched the brick and left a spot of white on it.

That spot is the one measure on the three bricks, and it says the row sinks and does not spread. Whitewash powders off in rain within one winter, so the spot fading yet still seen says the mark sits sheltered by the lip of the brick above.

The soil in the slit stays firm and dark and full of small white roots that went soft with winter and will come again in June.

What lives behind a brick lives in the back of it. A spider has run the slit for a year, and the sand at the foot of the bank stays loose enough to take a fingertip.

The spider in that slit and the sand at the foot and the whitewash spot on the face leave that brick where it lies. A brick caught in a slit with soil packed round it moves only when the soil goes out of the slit, and the soil goes out only when water takes it.

Water off the shed roof runs down the path and along the foot of the bed in wet weather, and it has cut a run about half an inch deep past the first brick from the end.

Past the middle brick the run turns out to the path, because the middle brick stands a little proud of the row and throws the water off.

So the middle brick guards its own footing by standing out of line, and the bank behind it takes the wash instead.

The timber of the bed edge stays flat where the boards meet the soil, and the iron of the fork stays smooth where the handle meets the head, and the brick face sits flat where it faces the light out of the wet.

She draws the palm out from behind the brick and rubs the soil off the fingers on the side of the bed, and looks at the row from the path to see what it shows from above.

---

The row gives a straight line from above and a low bank from the side, and the second of those tells more, because a brick row beds on soil and not on sight.

From above the three bricks look level and true, with the middle one a finger forward and the faces flat to the sun.

Laid flat like that they throw the morning rain off in sheets, and each sheet leaves the faces a little cleaner than before.

A face that lies flat to the weather dries first after rain, while one that tilts in keeps the damp along its lower edge till midday.

That is why the middle brick dries first of the three, with its flat face to the south and its back to the bank, and why the end ones stay dark till dinner.

The flat of the brick top tells the same tale. Dust sits on it after dry days and washes off in rain, and each washing leaves the top a little flatter than before, because loose sand goes and hard clay stays.

She will leave the three where they lie through another summer, with the middle one wedged and the soil firm round it, and the row sinking a hair each year under its own weight while the light goes off the yard.
