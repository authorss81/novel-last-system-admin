# Chapter 0206 — What Is Left

Tuesday the seventeenth of November 2015, from about ten in the morning, the low end of Peverell Street in Tideglass, and then a vestibule on the ground floor.

**A MAN OF ABOUT SEVENTY-THREE HELD A GATE OPEN FOR NINE HOURS IN AUGUST, WAS PAID THE GOING RATE, WAS CALLED NINE TIMES SINCE THE TWENTY-SECOND OF AUGUST, HAS DECLINED NOTHING, AND ON TUESDAY THE SEVENTEENTH OF NOVEMBER HE WAS NOT CALLED AGAIN, AND HE SAID NOTHING ABOUT IT TO ANYBODY, AND SOMEBODY NOTICED.**

Jonas Mercer had spent six days not going round the city, on the grounds that a person who has heard a thing stop cannot tell the difference between a thing that has stopped and a thing he has decided has stopped, and that the only way to find out which it was is to walk the same streets and look at them. He had a piece of paper in his coat with the date on it. He did not take the paper out.

The low end of Peverell Street was as it was. There was about a quarter of an inch of silt on the pavement from the top of the yard to the fourth door back, because a gully at the bottom of a street that floods silts up in a month and nobody in this borough had come back with a bar, and the gully outside the third door was packed with it, and the man who has that door was out, and the woman on the second floor of that house came down about once a day and looked at it and went up again.

The river was down. The board at the top of the street was where it had always been, with the corner lifting, and there was one on the shutter of the shop at the bottom and one inside the hall off the parade, and the shape in the middle of every one of them was still a square with nothing in it, and nobody was standing in front of any of them writing anything down.

He walked the whole of it in about fifty minutes. The parade, the bottom of the water, the yard where the pump had been hired from in September, the two doorways that stayed dry in October, the fence whose bottom three boards had gone, and then back up. Nothing had been taken down. Nothing had been put up that had not been up before the first of October. There was no gap anywhere he could have put a finger into.

He was about a quarter past eleven at the corner by the parade when he saw the man of about seventy-three, who holds gates.

He is not a postman and he is not a doorman and he has never claimed to be anything. He came at seven in the morning on the twenty-second of August to hold a gate open on his own arm and he held it for nine hours and he was paid the going rate for it, in money, and it was the first time anybody had paid him for anything in this city, and he has been called nine times since and has not said no once, and holding a gate open with his arm is the whole of what he can do and the whole of what he is asked for.

Jonas Mercer looked at him for long enough to be noticed.

He had his coat over his arm and there was nothing in his hands. That was the difference and Jonas Mercer could not have put it in a sentence afterwards if he had tried for a week, and it was not that he looked unhappy or looked well or looked like a man who had been let off something. He looked like a man standing on a pavement at eleven in the morning on a cold Tuesday with his coat folded over his arm, which is what a man of about seventy-three looks like when there is nothing in his hands to hold it up with.

He saw Jonas looking and said good morning, and Jonas said good morning, and neither of them said anything else, and Jonas Mercer did not ask him, and he is not going to ask him, and there is nobody in this city he could hand the question to even if he had wanted to.

**AND THE ONLY LATTICE NOTICE IN THAT VESTIBULE THAT MORNING, printed and fixed to the inside of the glass at about the height of a person of average height, and it defines nothing, and nothing on it defines *holder*:**

> **A QUESTION ASKED AT THIS COUNTER IS A QUESTION AND NOTHING ELSE.**
> **SO IS THE ANSWER.**
> **NO ANSWER GIVEN HERE IS A RECORD OF ANYTHING.**

The vestibule is on the ground floor of a building with three doors off it and a shuttered shop to the left of it with a card in the window giving a telephone number, and the counter is the width of two people. A woman of about sixty-five has been behind it for about nine years. She has never been told anything.

Jonas Mercer had been in and out of that vestibule since May and had never once seen the inside of the place, because there is no inside of the place. The counter is the place.

The man of about twenty-eight came in at about half past eleven. He was in the room over a shop on the thirtieth of September when a woman from another city read a schedule out loud and he said one sentence about a rate, and he has been in this vestibule once before, in June, for about ten minutes, over the same counter.

He had a folded piece of paper with him and he put it on the ledge, which he was not supposed to do, and then took it back off and held it.

"I want to know if there's anywhere a person can put the low end of that street," he said.

The woman of about sixty-five looked at him.

"There's no form for that," she said.

"I know," he said.

That is where it stopped. He stood there for a while with the paper in his hand and did not put it down and did not open it, and the woman of about sixty-five went on doing what she was doing at the far end of the counter, which was nothing much, and after a bit he said the rest of it to the floor rather than to her.

"I've been trying since June to get the bottom of Peverell Street onto something. Anything. A bit of paper with that street on it that exists in a drawer somewhere in this city, so that I can put my finger on it and say it's that one. There's a drawing with the shape of the whole thing on it going up the walls. There's no name on it. There's no bottom of Peverell Street on it, and I know that, and I have not been in here since June because there was no point coming in and asking for a thing that isn't."

"There's no form for that," said the woman of about sixty-five, in exactly the same words, and not unkindly.

"I know you haven't," he said. "I wasn't asking you for a form. I was asking whether you'd got one. You've not got one and neither's anybody else and that's the answer and I wanted to have asked it in a room with somebody in it."

Nobody was going to be able to answer him about the other part, and the other part was that in the late summer nine people had stood in doorways in this borough and said yes, and one of them was the man he used to work with, and since the middle of September there had been no reason on any street in this city for anybody to be asked anything at all, and he had noticed it the way a man notices a bus that has stopped coming, and he had not told anybody, and he was not going to.

He put the paper back in his pocket.

"Right," he said. "Same time in a month, if you're here."

"I'm here on Tuesdays," said the woman of about sixty-five.

He went out. The door shut on its spring the way that door does and came back open about a foot and had to be pulled, and Jonas Mercer held it and let it go and watched him go up the parade.

Jonas Mercer had come in behind him and had stood at the other end of the vestibule the whole time, and he had not said one word, and nobody had asked him whether he wanted anything, because that is not a thing that is done at that counter.

**AND THERE IS NOBODY IN THIS BOROUGH WHO COULD HAVE REQUIRED ANYBODY TO DO ANYTHING ABOUT IT, AND THERE IS NOBODY IN THIS BOROUGH WHO WAS GOING TO BE ASKED, AND THE MEETING IN THE VESTIBULE TOOK ABOUT FOUR MINUTES AND IT WAS THE ONLY THING THAT HAPPENED ANYWHERE IN TIDEGLASS THAT DAY THAT WAS ABOUT THE LOW END OF PEVERELL STREET, AND NOTHING WAS MINUTED, AND NOTHING WAS PUT ON A SHEET, AND THE GULLY OUTSIDE THE THIRD DOOR IS STILL PACKED.**

He walked back up the parade at about ten to twelve with the silt under the boots and thought about the man of about seventy-three, and about a column in a book on a table, and about a man of about twenty-eight who had come four hundred yards to ask a question he had already known the answer to because the answer was going to be worse in a room than at a counter.

Then he went and had his lunch, which he has on Mondays and Fridays, and which on a Tuesday is at a different time because the canteen shuts its hatch at two.
