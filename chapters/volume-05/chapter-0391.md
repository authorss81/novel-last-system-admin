# Chapter 0391 — A Minute and a Half

Wednesday the fourteenth of February 2018, from about half past seven in the morning until about half past nine at night, a shop off a parade, in Tideglass.

**TWO MEN HAD WORDS FOR ABOUT A MINUTE AND A HALF AT A COUNTER IN THAT SHOP ON THE AFTERNOON OF THE FOURTEENTH OF FEBRUARY ABOUT A PATCH OF PLASTER THAT CAME OUT OF A CEILING IN A FLAT ABOVE IT, AND EACH OF THE TWO OF THEM PUT UP ONE THING THAT WAS TRUE, AND NEITHER PUT UP THE OTHER ONE'S THING, AND NEITHER GAVE ANYTHING AWAY, AND IT IS STILL OPEN UP THERE.**

The shop is on the parade and the woman who works in a shop is on that counter from about ten until about nine on six days.

Above that shop, on the first floor, there is a flat with two rooms and a landing, and it has been let and re-let about six times since this account of it began and it is not anybody's business.

The front room of that flat has a plaster ceiling and about eighteen inch of one corner of it is brown. That brown is about fourteen inch the other way and it sits directly over the hearth.

---

It came down on the Sunday before.

There was nobody in that room. It came down at about ten in the morning in one piece about the size of a tea tray, and it went across the top of a table and off the end of it onto the boards, and it broke a mug that was on the table and left a mark on the boards about a foot across.

The woman who has that flat was in the back room with the door shut and heard the whole of it and came out and looked at about two foot of ceiling with the lath showing at the edge of it, and she did not go up into the flat above, and she did not knock on anybody's door, and she has not told anybody about it.

The man in his forties had that ceiling in October.

He was in that flat for four days at the end of September and the beginning of October, on a different job entirely, and while he was in the front room he took the brown patch down and put the plaster back in that corner.

He has been doing thirty years and what he did is what a man does. He got the old plaster off in whole pieces, which means he did not have to make good and he could see what was behind it. He found that the laths were rotten for about two foot at the corner and the boards behind them were dark at the bottom edge and stained along the joist.

He cut back to sound stuff. He put new laths in on three bearers and he ran them in on the sound board, and he plastered the whole corner in one go on the third day with a finish coat, and it was dry by the time he left on the Friday, and it was a proper job.

The corner of that room is a cold corner. It is above the hearth but the chimney is not in that corner, and the outside wall in that corner is the one that faces the parade and has been wet on that side since the building was put up.

---

The man in his thirties was in that flat in the week before Christmas, on a job that was the length of a kitchen tap and a half day, and he was in the front room for about ten minutes of it because he had to get at the pipe.

He saw the patch. It was about the same size it is now.

He put his thumb in it, about four inch from the corner, and pushed, and the plaster moved about an eighth of an inch and came away from the lath in places, and it felt wrong to him the way plaster feels when it has been put on over something that is still moving.

And he said a thing out loud to the woman who has that flat, and what he said was that the corner of that ceiling was the soft thing in the flat and that it would come down one day, and that whatever it was it was not anything to do with the plaster and it was behind the plaster.

That was in December. She has not put it to anybody since. She has not told the man in his forties that a man was in that flat in December and said something about that corner, and she has not told the man in his thirties that a man was in that flat in October.

They argued about it on the Wednesday afternoon at about half past two.

The man in his forties was in that shop with a length of something and the man in his thirties was in there at the same time at the other end of the same counter with a box of plugs, and somebody said the first thing.

The woman who works in a shop did not start it. What happened is that the man in his forties said, in the ordinary way, that a ceiling had come down in the flat above that shop, and the man in his thirties put his box down.

It was a minute and a half. She rang two lots through in that time and one man came in and asked her something about a size and she told him and he went.

"What was behind it," said the man in his thirties.

"Rotten laths and damp boards," said the man in his forties. "I found the boards damp when I had the old one off. Every board in that corner had a dark line along the bottom of it. That is not a thing I did in October, that was there when I got to it."

"That is what I am saying. That is what I am saying to you. I told her in December the plaster was not the problem."

"You told her it was soft. You did not tell her what it was soft under. You put your thumb in it and went out."

"I told her the plaster was not the problem, which is what I am telling you now, and I have not been up there since December and neither have you, and neither of us has been up there since it came down, and it came down on Sunday and there is about two foot of lath open in that ceiling this morning and I have not seen it."

Neither of them gave the other the other thing.

The man in his forties had watched that corner dry for four months and had put his hand on it in November and it was solid. He knew that. He did not say it, because saying it would be saying that a job of his had held for four months, which is a thing a man does not put in front of another man in a shop.

The man in his thirties had seen the boards behind that plaster in December and had seen the dark line along them and had said nothing about it at the time, because he had a kitchen tap to do. He did not say it, because saying it now would be saying that a thing he had seen in December would have let a man in his forties know in October, and he does not know that it would and he has not worked it out and he is not going to work it out in a shop on a Wednesday afternoon.

So they had one true fact each and the two true facts are about two different things and neither of the two of them is the thing the other one needs.

"It might be a parapet," said the man in his thirties, at one point.

"It might be," said the man in his forties.

Neither of those sentences has anything behind it and neither of them is a concession and both of them were true, and the woman who works in a shop heard both of them and rang one more lot through.

It stopped because somebody else came in and stood at the counter, and because that is what happens.

The man in his forties paid for what he had come for. The man in his thirties put his box of plugs down and picked it up again and paid for those. They were about four foot apart along that counter and neither of them was in the other's way and they had both said everything they had come to say.

Neither of them said sorry. Neither of them said that he might be right. Neither of them said that both of them could be looking at two halves of it, and there is a version of that afternoon in which one of them says it, and it is not this one.

They were at that counter together again before the week was out.

The man in his forties was in that shop on the Friday of that week at about a quarter to twelve with a box of screws. The man in his thirties was in there at about ten past twelve the same morning with a roll of tape. They were about two foot apart with a basket between them and they were both at that counter for about six minutes.

Neither of them said one word about that ceiling. They did not nod. The woman who works in a shop rang both their lots through and told the one of them that the small washers were on the shelf under the one he was looking at and told the other one nothing at all, and neither of them put anything to the other about anything at all.

Both of them were in that shop again on the Tuesday after that, and neither of them raised it there either.

The patch has not been made good. There is about two foot of open lath in the corner of that ceiling and a strip of new plaster along the edge of it where the last piece broke off, and there is a plastic sheet on a length of wire hooked over the rail round that room to stop anything coming off the rest of it, and the plastic sheet has been there since the Sunday before and it is not fixed to anything.

Nobody has been up into that flat above that shop since the Sunday before except the man in his forties, who went up on the Monday morning to look at the edge of it, and the man in his thirties, who went up on the Wednesday morning after the argument, and the two of them were not in that room at the same time on either of those days and did not know the other had been.

The woman who works in a shop is not going to say anything about it. It is above her counter. She heard the whole of it and she rang two lots through in the middle of it and she has never once put to either of them whether either of them has been up there, and if it were put to her she would say that a ceiling is a ceiling and that she does not go upstairs.

**SHE RANG BOTH THEIR LOTS THROUGH ON THE TUESDAY AFTER AND TOLD ONE OF THEM WHERE THE SMALL WASHERS WERE, AND NEITHER OF THE TWO MEN LOOKED UP WHEN SHE SAID IT, AND SHE HAS PUT NEITHER OF THEM ANY QUESTION AT ALL SINCE.**
