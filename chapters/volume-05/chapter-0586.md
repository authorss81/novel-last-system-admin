# Chapter 0586 — Twelve Doors Down

The Saturday of the fifteenth of August 2020, from about twenty past one in the afternoon until about ten in the evening, at the end of a parade of shops in this borough and in the streets behind it.

**SHE SAID ONE SENTENCE WHERE THREE COULD HEAR IT, AND ONE OF THE THREE CARRIED IT OUT OF THAT SHOP AND DOWN TWELVE DOORS AND PUT IT DOWN IN FRONT OF A MAN WHO HAS NO IDEA WHAT IT IS AND WAS NOT TOLD, AND SHE NEVER LEARNED THAT IT WAS CARRIED AND WILL NOT LEARN.**

The parade is a row of about nine shops with a small yard at the back of them. At the back of it, past the shop that does keys and the shop that does the papers, there are two streets of houses that everybody who works on that parade knows about and no visitor ever finds.

She is a woman in her early forties. She has run the front of the greengrocer's on that parade for years and the man who runs the place with her is a man in his late thirties and has been there four.

On the Saturday afternoon there were four people in that shop. The woman in her early forties behind the till, the man in his late thirties out in the stockroom with the door up, a customer at the counter with a basket, and a third person — a woman in her mid thirties who had come in to ask after a job and had been put at the end of the counter to wait while the woman in her early forties finished a ring-up.

The man in his late thirties had the stockroom door up and the radio on in there, and he could hear the whole of the front of that shop perfectly, and so could the customer at the counter, and so could the woman in her mid thirties.

---

She was waiting on the card machine when she said it. The man in his late thirties had come out of the stockroom and was standing at the end of the counter, and the customer had gone over to the window for a bag.

She said it to the man in his late thirties and not to the customer and not to the woman in her mid thirties, and she said it the way a person says a thing that has been sitting on them:

"I couldn't sit in that house another year."

That is all she said. She said it in a normal voice, in a shop on a Saturday, with a queue behind her and a radio going in the back.

The man in his late thirties said, "Right," and looked at the window display, and said, "You've not told him."

"He knows I'm not happy," the woman in her early forties said. "He's known since the March."

That was the end of it. The man in his late thirties went back to the stockroom and put the door up again, and the customer came back with a bag of apples and paid, and the woman in her early forties carried her shopping out past the window display where the man in his late thirties was still standing.

---

The woman in her mid thirties heard both sentences and every word of the bit about the March, and she had no idea at all what it was about, and she did not go and ask, because a person does not go and ask in a shop where they are asking after a job.

What she did with it was this. She said her piece to the woman in the early forties, which had nothing to do with any of it, and when she came out of the shop at about half past two she went down the side of the parade rather than the front, which was the way she had come in.

Down the side of the parade there is a footpath with a wall on the shop side of it and the backs of the houses on the other, and at the far end of it the path turns into a street. She turned into it. There is nobody on that path at half past two on a Saturday in August except a man getting out of a van.

The van driver is in his mid fifties. He came out of the second street, not the first, and he came out of the gate of number forty-one, and the van is parked across the mouth of the street because the street is too narrow for it and has been since before he came.

"Excuse me," the woman in her mid thirties said, "do you know if number forty-one is on this side?"

"It's the one with the blue door," the man in his mid fifties said. "Can't miss it."

"That's it, then."

She went down the street. And this is the whole of what the sentence did: it came out of that shop with her, and it went down the side of the parade, and it went down a street she had never been down before, and when she got to the gate of number thirty-six she stopped.

She did not knock because she did not know the man. She stood at the gate of number thirty-six and she turned round and looked up and down the street, and the man in his mid fifties was still at the gate of number forty-one with his van door open, and she made her mind up and she went up to him.

She said, "I've come to apologise for something I've said in a shop, and I want you to have it right."

He said, "You've lost me."

She told him both sentences. She said them exactly as she had heard them and she gave them to him as the words, because she was not going to stand on a pavement outside number thirty-six and dress them up.

"Couldn't sit in that house another year," she said. "That's the one. The other one's the one about the March and the man not knowing she's not happy."

The man in his mid fifties shut his van door. He is a man who has been driving in this borough for about twenty-two years and he was not expecting a Saturday afternoon to be like this.

"That's somebody else's," he said. "You don't come all this way for that."

"I know that's somebody else's," the woman in her mid thirties said. "I've heard enough in a shop to know it's somebody else's. But she said it where I was stood, and I can't sit on it."

The man in his mid fifties looked at her for a bit.

"Can I ask you something," he said.

"Go on."

"You were stood at that counter for a job," he said. "That's a job, isn't it. You could have lost it."

"I've been out of work since the March," the woman in her mid thirties said, "and I'll be out of it again next week, and that's the reason I'm stood on this pavement handing somebody else's evening to a stranger."

The man in his mid fifties put his hands in his pockets and stood there on the pavement of a street he has driven into a thousand times and never once walked down.

Then he said, "Number thirty-six's a mate of mine. Ted. He's in most evenings."

And the woman in her mid thirties said, "He's got the blue door?"

"No," the man in his mid fifties said. "He hasn't got a blue door. He's got a green one. Number forty-one is the blue one. That's mine and my missus."

The woman in her mid thirties said, "Oh," and looked at the gate she was standing at, which was dark green, and had been dark green for as long as there had been a gate.

"Right," the man in his mid fifties said, and got back in the van.

She knocked on number thirty-six at about ten to three. The man who came to the door was in his late forties and had a tea towel over his shoulder and had clearly been in the middle of something.

"Can I ask you something daft," the woman in his mid thirties said.

The man in his late thirties said, "Go on."

"Somebody's said something in a shop on the parade and I heard it and I've walked all the way down here to give it to you, and I don't know if it's you or not. It was: couldn't sit in that house another year."

He looked at her, and the pause went on long enough for her to wish she had not come, and then he said:

"Which house?"

"That's it. That's all she said. She didn't say whose house."

"Right," the man in his late thirties said, and then, "There's about nine of us on that parade could have said that about a house."

"Do you want the rest of it?"

"No," the man in his late thirties said. "I don't, but that goes on you, not on me."

He shut the door, and it was not unkind, and the woman in her mid thirties stood on the step of number thirty-six and thought that there was a lesson in it and could not have said what it was.

**SHE PUT BOTH SENTENCES INTO A STRANGER'S EAR ON A PAVEMENT AND HE TOLD HER SHE HAD KNOCKED ON THE WRONG GREEN DOOR, AND HE WAS RIGHT, AND HE TOLD HER SO, AND SHE WENT AND KNOCKED ON IT ANYWAY, AND THE ONLY THING SHE HAS GOTTEN OUT OF IT IS THE NAME OF A MAN WHO PUT A TEA TOWEL OVER HIS SHOULDER.**

**AND THE WOMAN IN THE EARLY FORTIES IS AT HOME THIS EVENING IN A HOUSE SHE SAID SHE COULD NOT SIT IN ANOTHER YEAR IN, AND SHE HAS NO IDEA THAT ANY OF IT WALKED OUT OF HER SHOP.**
