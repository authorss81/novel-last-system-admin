# Chapter 0397 — Twenty Feet

Wednesday the fourteenth of March 2018, from about half past seven in the morning until about half past five in the afternoon, a street behind that parade, and a yard at the back of a shop, in Tideglass.

**TWO MEN WORKED ON OPPOSITE SIDES OF ONE BUILDING ON THE MORNING OF THE FOURTEENTH OF MARCH AND NEITHER OF THEM KNEW THE OTHER WAS ON IT, AND THE ONE WHO CAME BACK AT HALF PAST THREE FOUND WHAT THE OTHER ONE HAD DONE AND KNEW IN ABOUT TWO SECONDS WHOSE IT WAS AND SAID NOTHING TO ANYBODY AND HAS NEVER PUT IT TO HIM.**

The building is a shop that has been a shop for longer than anybody on that parade can remember. It is a narrow one, about sixteen foot, and it is set back off that parade about four foot, and there is a small forecourt in front of it with a rack of firelighters in it and a stack of baskets under the window.

The shop floor is about twenty foot deep. Behind it there is a counter along the left-hand side, and a partition at the back of that counter with a hatch through it, and behind that partition a store about ten foot by twelve and a door in the corner of that store.

Nobody has shut that door with a key since about 2011 and there is no key on the ring for it. It goes out into a yard about thirty foot wide and the full depth of the building, and there is a wall on the far side of the yard which is the boundary with the land behind the next building along.

The yard is where the sacks and the buckets live and where a man backs a van in when he has something heavy. That counter is hers from ten until about nine on six days, and the shop shuts at half past three on a Saturday.

---

The man in his thirties had that shopfront on the fourteenth of March from about half past eight in the morning.

The front door of that shop was a plywood sheet about four inches thick with a steel edge on it, a hasp and a staple, and it had been that since the week before Christmas. The woman who works in a shop had been after somebody about it for two months, and in the end she rang him at ten on the Monday and he went down and looked and said he would do it in March.

He had a new door and frame in the van on the Wednesday. It is a hardwood door with four raised squares in it, on a frame with a doorstep moulding on it, and he made it himself in the second week of March in the workshop he rents at the top of the town.

He took the old sheet off its hasp at about half past eight, which took ten minutes and made a noise that turned two heads at the front of that parade, and he set it against the forecourt wall on the sacking.

After that he was in and out of that doorway all morning. He took the frame out in three pieces and got the old lintel out from under a brick, and got the new frame in and wedged it and packed it, and he was on his knees on that forecourt with the door on four wedges for about an hour and the wind off the estuary coming straight up that parade at him.

He came in through his own new doorway for the tools about four times between nine and one, and each time she let him go past that counter without a word, and he went out the back way, through the store and out into that yard, because that is the shortest way to the van.

He was in that yard six times that morning.

In that same yard, from about eight until about half past one, was the man in his forties.

In the middle of February the woman who works in a shop raised the top of that wall with him, because two men at the front of that parade had told her the courses were going, and he said he would go and look at it.

He came on the eighth of March and looked, and said the top course wants taking off and doing again and the coping wants doing and he could do it in a morning.

There is no money mentioned in that. It is nine foot of wall and he said he would do it and he did it.

He started at about eight on the Wednesday. He had the top course off before nine, which is about a hundredweight of brick, and it went into two sacks in that yard, and he built the course back up new with a pail of mortar he mixed on a board on the ground, and he did the coping on the top of that as well, because the coping is a length of engineering brick laid on edge and bedded in the same mortar and the old one was in pieces.

He knocked the arris off every brick he cut. That is what he does and he has done it in front of the man in his thirties more than once, and he does it because a man who is dead told him in 1991 that you knock a sharp edge off a cut brick and the suction goes out of it and the mortar takes.

He had the whole of it finished and cleaned down and his mortar board washed off and his tools in the van at about half past one.

Nobody was watching him. Behind that counter, thirty foot off through a store, the woman who works in a shop sat and heard the lump hammer for about three hours, and she did not go out there once. The man in his thirties was in and out of that yard six times and was never in it at the same time as him.

They were about twenty foot apart at one point. At about twenty past ten the man in his thirties came out of the store with a bag of screws, and at the far end of that yard the man in his forties was on his knees with a lump hammer, and neither of them looked up, and about ninety seconds later the man in his thirties went back in with the bag and did not come out again until lunchtime.

---

The man in his thirties went back for his bar at about half past three.

He had left a long bar under the step of that new frame, because he had wanted it out of the way of the foot of it, and he came out of the shop at the counter, through the store, and into that yard to get it.

He was in there about a minute and a half.

He looked at the wall before he thought about the bar at all, because that is the order a man looks in when he walks into a yard.

Nine foot of the top of it is new. The brick is a shade off the brick under it, because the new stock came out of a different yard, and the mortar is a shade off the mortar under it for the same reason. The coping is new and it is laid true and it has been laid true by somebody who has laid copings before.

And along the top of that new course, the length of nine foot, every arris is chamfered.

Not broken. Not ragged. Chamfered, about a quarter of an inch, every one of them, in a line you can pick out from the gate.

He stood and looked at that from about twenty feet away without going any closer to it.

It took him about two seconds to place that wall.

Twice in two years he has stood in a room and watched the man in his forties lay brick, and twice more he has watched him do it from outside. He has stood in a doorway in a house in Bleam Street and watched him build a garden wall in a morning. He has watched him knock the arris off a cut with a bolster and a lump hammer every single time, and he has heard him give the reason once, to nobody in particular: take the sharp edge off a cut brick and the mortar takes, and it does.

Nobody else he has ever built alongside does that. Most men bury the arris. Some men leave it standing. Nobody else knocks it off on purpose, all the way along, and then draws a line with it.

He picked his bar up from under the step. He did not go any nearer to that wall and he did not touch it and he did not look at it a second time.

He went back through the store, put his lath on the counter and paid for it at the till, and she was across that counter with her back half turned and she did not look up.

Neither of the two men has ever put it to the other one.

As for the man in his forties, he knows the man in his thirties was on that front on the Wednesday, because everybody on that parade can see that shop has a new door on it and knows who made one. He has not been in that yard since the Wednesday, has not walked past that wall, and has not mentioned it to anybody.

Six times that morning he went into that yard and not once to the far end of it, which is where his own van stands and which is where that wall is. He did not know there was anybody in that yard with him at all.

Neither of the two men has ever put it to the other, and nobody has told the man in his forties that the other one came out of that store at about twenty past ten, and nobody has told him that a man was at the back of that building all morning.

**HE LEFT THE BAR UNDER THE STEP WHERE HE HAD PUT IT, HE WENT BACK THROUGH THE STORE THE WAY HE CAME, AND HE PUT A LATH DOWN ON THAT COUNTER AND PAID FOR IT WITHOUT SAYING ANYTHING, AND THE NINE FOOT OF BRICK WAS STILL STANDING AT THE BACK OF THAT SHOP.**
