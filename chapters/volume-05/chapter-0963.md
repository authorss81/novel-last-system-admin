# Chapter 0963 — Under The Rack Channel

Friday the thirteenth of December 2024, at the channel lying across the two sections with the bay doors open behind. Under a channel.

**THE CHANNEL HAS SETTLED ONTO THE TWO SECTIONS UNDER IT AND LEFT A CLOSED SPACE BETWEEN ITS OWN UNDERSIDE AND THE TOPS OF THOSE SECTIONS, WHICH IS SHUT ON ALL FOUR SIDES AND OPEN AT ONE END, AND THE HAND GOES IN UNDER IT AND SHUTS THE FINGERS ONTO IT AND PULLS DOWN, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE CHANNEL STANDS OFF THOSE SECTIONS AND WHAT IS UNDER IT.**

There are three such racks along that wall and the other two are full, and this is the one that things are put on when they have nowhere else to go, which is why there is a length of channel on it and nothing else loose.

The rack is in the open part of that yard and it is a rack of the ordinary kind, two uprights of angle iron with a shelf of sawn timber between them at about the height of a man's chest. A length of channel is a thing a yard ends up with and does not want, and it stays because throwing it away costs money and keeping it costs nothing except the space it takes up on a rack. The two sections are lying on that shelf side by side along their length, close enough together that their webs are an inch apart, and they came off a job and have been on the shelf since the summer.

Nobody put it there by thinking about it. It was put there by whoever had that length on a barrow and wanted it off the floor, and a hand went down and found the top of the rack, which is as much reason as it ever got. The channel is a short length of rolled channel about four feet long and lying across the two of them, and it cannot lie along a shelf and stay on it, and the weather cannot get at a thing that is up on a rack at the height of a man's chest. It has lain there since the summer and it has done what one unyielding thing does on top of two others, which is to take a set of its own where it crosses them and keep it.

So a space is left beneath the channel and above the two sections, a foot wide and four feet long and about half an inch high in the middle, shut at both long sides by the channel's own flanges coming down and shut at one end by the channel bearing on both.

---

The hand goes in at the open end of that space palm down, and the fingers shut onto the underside of the channel and pull down.

The channel is stiff and it does not want to come down, and pulling down on it is not the same as picking it up, because the hand has no purchase on it whatever and no leverage, and the only thing that has moved at all is half an inch of nothing on its own. A channel is rolled with a web in the middle and two flanges turned up at the sides, and laid on edge it is stiff along its length and hollow across the middle, which is the reverse of a section; so a length of one does the work of a plank and a length of the other does the work of a beam.

What comes back up is the underside of a rolled channel, which is not flat. It has a flat across the middle of its width and it turns at about forty-five degrees on each side of that, and the fingers close onto the flat and the sides of them lie on the two turns. That gap is bounded by two surfaces and by the two of them alone. Below that is the timber of the shelf, an inch clear of the tops of the sections, and that inch is the one open thing about this part of the rack.

That underside has not seen a winter in the sense that a roof does, and the whole difference between it and the tops of the two sections lies there, which have had every drop of rain for five months. A quarter of an inch of air is about as much as a knuckle, and the fingers closing on the underside of that channel found that quarter of an inch before they found anything else at all. The paint on the underside of the channel is hard and has crazed into small cracks about the size of a fingernail, and on the tops of the two sections where the channel has lain all that time the paint underneath it has not crazed at all, because it has had the weather kept off it.

The two sections are stiff lengthwise and hollow down the middle, and each one lies on that shelf with a piece of sawn timber between it and the timber of the shelf at the two ends, which is where a piece of sawn timber comes from in a yard, and which is as far as that packing has ever got. There is a third length of channel further along that rack, standing on edge in a rack of its own, and it has nothing laid on it and nothing laid over it and no part of it has been in a hand since the summer.

The bay behind that rack is a hundred yards from the bay doors and about as wide again, and there is a strip light along one wall of it and it is burning in the middle of the afternoon, and none of that light reaches under a channel lying across two sections on a rack at the open end of a yard. Nothing about the rack has been moved and nothing has been put under anything since the channel was laid across it. The two sections are about twelve feet long and they overhang the front of that shelf by four feet, which is why they carry themselves as well as they do and why nobody has ever put anything on their front ends.

The uprights of that rack are angle iron with the angle turned inwards, because a man building a rack for a yard puts the angle in where it will not collect water and leaves the flat out where a crate can be dragged against it. The paint on the tops of those two sections is crazed everywhere except in the two strips where the channel has lain, and the crazing has gone through the first coat to the grey primer and has not gone through the primer anywhere along their length.

Where the channel crosses, the set in it runs in the direction of the length of the two sections underneath, and at the two ends of it where it bears down, it is straight again, which is what a set is: a thing that has remembered the middle and forgotten the ends. The woman who books work is inside the yard with the weighbridge deck behind her, having stepped off it onto the flags, and her hands are down.

"There is a barrow at the ironmonger's for forty-one pounds."

"There was one at forty-one pounds in the spring."

"Then it has not moved since the spring either."

"It is the same barrow. They do not make them any more."

They get as far as whether anybody had been in that week wanting a barrow, and whether the yard would hire one out for a fortnight, and whether anything off that yard had ever gone out for that long. The fingers came back out from beneath that channel while she was still up on the weighbridge, and neither of them turns towards the rack. The channel is where it was laid and it is as near straight at its two ends as it ever was, and the shelf under it and the two sections on the shelf and the inch of air between the sections and the timber are as they were in the summer.

Nobody has lifted that channel and nobody is going to, and the two sections underneath it are not going anywhere, except that the top of each of them has been under something heavier than rain for five months.
