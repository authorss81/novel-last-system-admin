# Chapter 0875 — What Is Under The Bottom Lump

Monday the eighth of January 2024, at the heap of broken apron base in the yard at the lock-up from the time the frost has come off the sheet until the shutter of the near bay has gone down. What is underneath the bottom lump.

**THERE IS A HEAP OF BROKEN APRON BASE STANDING ON A SHEET IN THE YARD AT THE LOCK-UP AND SHE MEANS TO PUT ONE HAND UNDER THE BOTTOM LUMP OF IT WITHOUT LIFTING IT AND FIND OUT WHAT IT IS RESTING ON AND HOW FAR THE HAND GOES, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS AT THE MESS ROOM DOOR WITH A CUP IN HER HAND.**

The apron came up in the summer and has been in that corner ever since, on a sheet of polythene that came off a roll in the office and is a good deal larger than it needs to be.

The base was about the size of a door laid flat. It was broken in place, not taken up whole, because it was too heavy to come up whole and the only thing to do with it was to make it into lumps. It is in about thirty pieces and they are all shapes and none of them is a shape anybody would want. Two of them are as long as a shovel handle. Most of them are fist-sized and thick, and one of them is the width of the kerb at the bay mouth and about nine inches of it.

Mortar on the faces of every lump in that heap, and none on the sheet, and none of it coming off.

That is a fact and it is a good one, because it means nothing is being put down and nothing is being taken off. The mortar went hard on those faces where they stood and it has gone hard with them in the position they were broken in, and a lump of concrete with mortar on the face that was up when it broke will carry that mortar for ever, because there is nothing that will get behind it.

What she wants to know is what the bottom lump is standing on.

That is a narrow question and it took the whole morning to get to the bottom of it, in the sense that she had to get down to the bottom of the heap, on her knees on a sheet, with the shutter open and the office glass cold on the other side of the yard.

A heap on a sheet is a heap that is drying. Wet concrete dries from the top and from the sides and it does not dry at all underneath, and a sheet under a heap is there to keep the ground clean and not to keep the ground wet. So the wettest part of that heap is the part that is closest to the sheet, and it is going to stay the wettest part until somebody turns the heap over, which nobody is going to do in January.

The question underneath the question is whether it makes any difference. And it does, because concrete that dries at the top and stays wet at the bottom goes off in a different order from concrete that dries all over, and the order matters when the thing you want is the mortar coming off the faces and not coming off the sheet.

She has the whole of it to herself from the moment the shutter is up, because the heap is against the office wall and the office door stands open on the latch and goes in the other direction.

So she gets a hand under it.

The bottom lump is the one at the front of the heap on the sheet side, and it is one of the big ones, and it is about the size of a paving slab with a bite taken out of it.

The way in is to put the hand flat, palm upward, at the near side of it, and push it in under with the fingers leading. Not to lift. If you lift it you have lifted it, and a thing that has been lifted is a thing that has been moved and then put down and is not where it was.

The fingers go in, and they go in further than she expected, which is the first thing the hand has told her.

She has to bring the wrist in as well. The hand goes in flat, palm up, until the second knuckle comes up on the far side of the lump with nothing in between, and the lump does not give a fraction of an inch anywhere in that, and the lump is carried in the crook of the arm with the arm under it and has not moved a fraction.

There is a hand's width of air under that lump.

Not a gap in the ground. A space, and one that gives nothing to a hand laid flat on the sand at the side of it. The sheet has folded.

That is the whole of it, and it is not an accident, and it happened in the first ten minutes after the apron came up.

A sheet of polythene that is bigger than it needs to be gets laid down and then gets walked on, and where it gets walked on it folds, and a fold in a sheet is a ridge, and a ridge under a lump of concrete lifts it. So the bottom of that heap is standing on the folds of its own sheet and the air is between the lump and the flags, and the flag under the fold is dry and has been dry since the summer, and the lump is lying on air.

She works the hand along the front of the lump without lifting it and finds the fold. It runs the whole width of the lump and it is about an inch high at the highest part of it and it dies away at the two ends where the sheet goes back flat.

Then she puts the hand in on the other side of the same lump and finds a second fold, running the other way, and the two of them cross under it.

So the bottom lump is up on a cross of two folds in the sheet and on a hand's width of air, and the air has been there since the summer, and no water has ever been into it and none will be.

The other thing the hand finds is that the bottom lump is not one lump.

The hand going in under it comes on a line. Not a crack. A joint — a line across the underside where two pieces meet, and between them about a quarter of an inch of mortar, hard as the mortar on either face.

Two pieces were broken apart out of the base by the same bar on the same afternoon, and when they came apart a skin of mortar went with each of them, and the two skins met again under the lump because the lump was set down on top of the piece below it and the piece below it happened to be the piece that fitted.

Which means that lump is two lumps stacked. And it means the fold of the sheet is under both of them and the air is under both of them, and that when this heap is eventually taken down a piece at a time, that one will be the piece that will not come apart cleanly, because a quarter of an inch of old mortar across a joint is a quarter of an inch of old mortar across it, whatever anybody does to it afterwards.

---

The woman who books work comes out to the mess room door about eleven with a cup in her hand and stands there.

The office is short of a corner and somebody wanted the window shut because of the draught off the yard. She was told the yard door is propped with a lump of concrete and that is where the draught is coming from. She said it is not a lump of concrete, it is a piece of the apron that somebody has stood there since the summer, and she is right about that, and the yard door is propped with a piece of the apron that is one of about thirty and not from the heap. She went back in and the door shut, and the draught came and went for the rest of the morning.

---

There is no bar in that yard that will go into that gap.

That is the finding, and it is worth more than it looks, because the heap has to come down a piece at a time at some point and the obvious way to start a heap like that is to get a bar into the bottom of it and lift, and you cannot lift a lump on a hand's width of air. A bar wants an edge. There is no edge. You would have to go in over the top and get at the underside of the second piece down, which means standing a lump on its end on a sheet in the wet and breaking it there, and then sorting what came off it, and that is three jobs where the one you were picturing was one.

So the heap stands. Not because it is wanted where it is. Because the piece at the bottom of it is lying on a cross of two folds in a sheet with a hand's width of air under it, and nobody has a bar that will go in there, and it is January.

The lowest lump in that heap is standing on the cross of its own sheet with air under it, and no bar in that yard will go into a gap that size.
