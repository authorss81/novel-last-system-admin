# Chapter 0251 — Eight Names

Friday the eighth of July 2016, from about half past seven in the evening, the Methodist hall on Carrow Lane in Tideglass.

**A SHEET OF PAPER CAME OUT OF A DRAWER UNDER A TRADE COUNTER FOR THE SECOND TIME IN THIS BOROUGH, ON A FRIDAY NIGHT IN JULY, IN FRONT OF NINE PEOPLE, AND NOT ONE PERSON IN THAT ROOM COULD DO ONE SINGLE THING WITH IT, AND THAT WAS CORRECT.**

Hanna Wray brought it in her handbag, which is where a person who has put a thing in a drawer for five weeks and has not thought about it since keeps it, and she put it on the table in the middle face up and did not unfold it, because it was not folded.

"There's the thing," she said. "Second time it's been out. Last time somebody asked me to bring it and I said I'd want it back where it was by about five. It's half seven."

She put the key in her handbag before the last of them had sat down, and the woman in the front row said the gas was lovely, and the man in his sixties said that the boiler in the other hall would be free to anybody who wanted it and had been free since about February, and nobody asked her one single question about the paper, which she had expected, and which was the whole reason she had brought it.

The lock on the drawer is a lock and the key is in her handbag and it came in her handbag and it went back in her handbag. A man from the parade asked her, quite reasonably, whether the room could look at it over two Mondays instead, so that it did not have to keep coming out on a Friday.

"No," said Hanna Wray.

"I'm only asking if it might be better in one go."

"I know what you're asking," she said. "It's nine years I've had that drawer and I've never once given the key to anybody, and it's not about the paper and it's not about you, and if somebody wanted to look at it in March I'd have said no in March. Take it or leave it on the table for as long as you want and it goes back in at ten past five and the lock goes round twice, which is more than it takes."

It is A4. One line of printing across the top in about six inches of type, and under it a column of places, and no box anywhere on it, and no name, and no address, and no reference, and no date, and nothing at the bottom, and a blank back, and every one of those things was said out loud in that room in April and in June by people who were fed up of saying them, and nobody said any of them tonight.

Netherby. Tolforth. Halsey Cross. Ansterwick. Kirkmoss. Ellersby. Marleby. Ockbrook.

The woman who has been in that hall about two years read them out twice, slowly, from where she was sitting, and looked up after the second one.

"Same again," she said. "I did it on a bench in the wind in May and I did it in here in June and I've done it now, and it comes out the same three times, and I have not got any better at it, and I have never once been asked to be better at it by anybody."

The man in his sixties had a hand up before she had finished, and he did not put it up at the end of the list, he put it up at the second reading, and he said:

"How many of the nine of you had heard of any of them before this evening."

Nobody spoke for a bit.

Then the woman who had been reading put her own hand up, and looked round at it, and said, "Ockbrook. And it's on the way to my sister's and I couldn't point at it and I'd not be able to tell you which side of the road."

"That is one hand out of nine for one name out of eight, and the other seven have never been in anybody's mouth, and I want that written down in a room somewhere," said the man in his sixties, "which it will not be, and which is fine, because a thing you can't write down is a thing you can't be proved wrong about later, and that is a real property and it is the only one that piece of paper has got."

Then the man of about eighteen, who had not said anything for about half an hour, said:

"It's a list of places."

"It is."

"So it's a list of eight places, and it's got a line on top that says anybody may use any part of any system in this region." He put his hands flat on the table. "There's no ninth place on it. This borough's not on it. I've been looking at that for five weeks and I thought it was because we hadn't heard of it, and it isn't, it's because we're not on it. So if it was a list of places, the thing that isn't a place is the one that's on the list, and that's us." He looked at the woman who had been reading. "If you read the eight out, and then I read the names of eight people in this room, then the two lists are the same shape. And one of them is a piece of paper nobody can act on, and the other one is a room on a Friday night, and neither of them is a document and neither of them is anybody's job, and there is nowhere for either of them to go afterwards."

Nobody said that was wrong and nobody said it was right for about half a minute.

"That's the first thing anybody has said about that paper that isn't about the paper," said the young woman of about twenty.

"It's not going to change what the paper is," said the man in his sixties. "I want to be honest about that before we do it, because I've watched a room do this once. That sheet is A4 with a line and eight towns on it and a blank back and there is not one single thing anybody in this room can do with it, and that is not a failure of this room, that is the correct answer about that sheet, and every one of us would be better off if we stopped trying to get round it. If we read eight names out, it will still be A4 with a line and eight towns on it and a blank back, at ten past five tonight, and nobody will know in six months that we did it, and that is not the point of it either, and I am not going to pretend it is the point of it. It's just the only thing anybody can actually do, and doing it will not be any use, and I would still rather we did it than went home."

"That is the whole argument," said the young woman of about twenty, "and I have heard it about four times this year and it has never once sounded like a consolation prize before."

The eight places were read out again, a third time, without being asked for, and then eight people in that room said their own names out loud, one after another, in no order at all, in whatever order they got up in, and the man of about eighteen wrote nothing down, because there was nothing on the table to write on and he had not brought anything, and nobody else had brought anything either, and that had been true of every one of these rooms since the first of them.

It took about half a minute. One of the eight was a woman who works behind the counter in a shop on the parade and had never said her name in that hall. One of them said his own name twice because he said the first one quietly. At the end there was a pause of the sort that happens when people are not sure whether it is over.

"That's eight," said the young woman of about twenty, and nobody said another name, and the room sat there.

It was not asked of anybody and eight people said it, and nothing was written down, and there was no list afterwards in anybody's hand, and the eight of them did not agree on anything afterwards, and two of them had to catch the bus.

Hanna Wray put it back in the drawer under the trade counter at ten past five and turned the key twice, which is more than it takes.

**TWO OF THEM HAD TO CATCH THE BUS, AND THE WOMAN WHO HAD READ THE EIGHT PLACES OUT TWICE WALKED DOWN TO THE CORNER WITH THE WOMAN WHO HAD SAID HER OWN NAME QUIETLY THE FIRST TIME, AND THEY WALKED THE WHOLE WAY TO THE STOP WITHOUT SAYING ONE MORE WORD ABOUT IT, AND NEITHER OF THEM COULD HAVE TOLD YOU WHAT THE SHEET HAD BEEN FOR.**
