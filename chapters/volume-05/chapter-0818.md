# Chapter 0818 — Two Lines About Three Inches Apart

Tuesday the sixth of June 2023, along the front of her own path at number nine from the time the sun clears the roof of the house opposite until it has gone off the flags, and twenty feet of grass that has come over the edge of it is the whole of that Tuesday.

**ABOUT TWENTY FEET OF GRASS ALONG THE FRONT EDGE OF HER OWN PATH AT NUMBER NINE HAS COME OVER THE KERB AND OUT ONTO THE FLAGS AND SHE MEANS TO CUT IT BACK, AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING IS LET IN AT THE SIDE GATE IN THE MIDDLE OF THE MORNING, STANDS AT THE FRONT STEP FOR A MINUTE AND GOES OUT AGAIN BY THE SAME GATE.**

Number nine, at the front. The path is flags from the gate to the front step, about twenty-two feet, four feet wide, and there is a kerb along the front edge of it made of concrete sections about four inches by three, laid on their edge with the top of them a couple of inches above the flags.

Behind that kerb is a strip of grass about two feet wide and then the front garden, and between the kerb and the house wall is a bed about three feet wide where the tulips are and have been for nine years.

Twenty feet of the grass has gone over that kerb and is out on the flags.

It is not a mat of it. It is a run of blades lying flat across the stone, about three inches deep, dark green and coarse, coming out of the side of the grass strip and lying over the kerb and then going on another three inches onto the path the way grass goes over the edge of a path.

This is what grass does and it is worth saying why, because it is the whole of the job and the reason it is a nuisance.

Grass spreads sideways. Every tiller it puts out comes up at the surface and roots there and then turns and comes up again a foot away, and where it comes up it is looking for light, and the brightest place in a piece of grass four inches tall and two feet deep is the edge of anything.

A kerb is the brightest thing a lawn has got, because it is the only place in it where the sky comes down as far as the ground, and grass runs at that the way water runs at a low place. Put a straight edge in the middle of a lawn and the grass will be over it by the end of that season, and that season comes round twice a year at that house.

---

She has a half-moon edging iron, a hook, a hand fork, a bucket and a rake.

She starts at the gate end of that kerb because it is the end she can see the whole run from.

The first thing she does is put the hook in and pull the whole mat off the stone where it is thickest, because that is the fastest way to see what is underneath, and it comes away in about a foot's worth of it in one piece and the flag under it is grey and has a rim of mud round the edge of it about two inches wide.

That rim is the answer and it is the whole of it.

There is a rim of grit and dead grass and trodden mud all round the top of that kerb and about three inches out on the flags beyond it, and it is thin and it is even, and it is exactly the shape of the overgrowth above it. That is not dirt from anywhere. That is everything the blades have brought with them as they crossed, over three summers, and it has been swept and washed and trodden into a line.

And the line of that rim is the line where the grass got onto the flags.

She works back from that with the fork. She lifts the mat about two inches wide all the way along the kerb, all twenty feet of it, and she puts the arisings in the bucket, and she rakes the flags afterwards.

She steps over the gate going out to the bucket and steps over it coming back, four times in the first hour, because the gate is lying across the front path on its two wedges where it has been since the winter, and there is a spanner lying out on the flags near the step where a man dropped it, and she steps over the gate and steps past the spanner and neither of them is any part of this.

By the middle of the morning she has twenty feet of kerb with the mat off it and the flag edge showing and the dirt line there.

---

Which is the line, is the question, and there are two of them and they are about three inches apart.

The first is the kerb. The top of that concrete is the edge of the path. Cut at that and the grass is off the flags and the edge of the flags is bare stone and it is a clean edge and it looks finished and it will be a foot deep in grass again in about three weeks, because every tiller of that grass is now a foot back from where it has to be to get onto the path.

The second is the line of dirt, which is where the grass actually is.

She stands at the gate end of that kerb with the hook in her hand for a while before she starts on it.

She has been cutting this edge for eleven years and she has cut it at the kerb every one of those eleven years, and it has come back every one of those eleven years, and she has never once cut it at the dirt because cutting at the dirt means cutting into her own grass, and cutting into your own grass is not a thing anybody does.

But a kerb with the grass cut at the top of it is not an edge. It is a channel, about three inches wide, with grass on both sides of it and a strip of wet stone in the middle that stays wet and goes green and puts moss in it over the winter.

She goes down that run with the half-moon iron against a board.

The board is a length of batten about four inches wide, an inch thick, with no arris left on it, and she lays it flat on the flags with the edge of it on the dirt line, and she runs the iron down the side of the board with the arc of it against the wood.

That is the only way to get a cut edge that is straight, and it is straight because the board is straight, and it is level because she put a spirit level on the board when she laid it and took the high end up.

Where the iron goes off the end of the board she stops, and she moves the board along and she goes on, and she does not try to keep the iron on the same line by eye, because an eye cannot run a line at three inches off the ground for twenty feet and a board can.

The kerb is not straight either. It is four-foot sections laid by hand by somebody who was looking at the house and not at the front boundary, and it wanders about an inch in and out over twenty feet, and that is not a fault in anybody's work.

So the dirt line wanders with it, and she can see that, and she lays the board off the kerb and not off the straight line she first marked out with the chalk, because a fence and a kerb laid by a man looking at a house are both better than anything a chalk line says.

The woman who does her shopping comes at the middle of the morning.

She is let in at the side gate and she stands at the front step for about a minute with her bag on the flags, and she looks down that path at the flag irons and the barrow and the strip of grass that has been lifted and the strip that has not.

"That's come up since Easter," she tells her.

"It has," she says.

And the woman who does her shopping goes out by the side gate she came in by without going down the path at all, because there is no way down it with a barrow in the way and a gate across it.

She does not offer to help and she is not asked.

She gets to about six feet from the gate end of that run and that is where the Tuesday stops being about the kerb.

The kerb there has been knocked.

Not moved. Knocked, by a barrow wheel, some time in the spring, and the two sections either side of the place have shifted along their length by about two inches and there is a gap in between them where the sand of the joint has washed out and gone down into the bed under it, and about four inches of that gap is filled with fine soil that has come off the grass strip.

The grass has gone across that gap and about five inches onto the flags beyond it, and it is doing the same thing it does everywhere else on that run.

Except that it is not the same thing, because the ground under that gap has dropped an inch and the gap is a hole with soil in it and the grass has come up out of the hole rather than over the edge of the stone.

She puts the hook in and lifts it and there is no rim of dirt at all under it, because there is no stone under it, and the dirt that is there is the soil from the joint that has washed down into the gap.

She stands at that end of that run with the hook in her hand.

A line cut there would be a line cut on a hole. There is no edge to cut against on that four inches and the iron has nothing to run against and the dirt line stops at the edge of the stone on both sides and jumps the gap.

So she stops there.

She lifts the mat off the flags for about a foot past that gap, because that much of it is lying on stone and stone wants the mat off it, and then she puts the hook down and she leaves the four inches of grass and the soil in the gap exactly as they are, and they will go on doing what they have been doing.

The run from the gate to there is cut and the run from there to the step is not, and that is not a compromise she made to get to the end of the front path. It is the only place in twenty-two feet where there is nothing to cut.

After that she rakes the arisings off the flags.

That is the part nobody likes doing and it is the part that matters. Every blade you take off the stone and leave lying on it will be a mat again by the next wet week, and she gets down on one knee at the gate end and scrapes them into the bucket with the back of the hook and then goes along the run and does it again, all the way, including the foot past the gap.

Then the wind gets up off the front of that house about an hour before the light goes off the flags, and it takes the arisings she has in the bucket at the bottom of the path and it takes what she raked off the jointing and it takes about a foot's worth off the fresh cut edge behind her, and it puts a fine green film about a foot wide along the top of that kerb.

She stands at the front step and watches it do it.

A cut edge is not a finished edge until the grass is off it, and the grass is never all off it, and there is no way of doing this that ends it. The iron goes back in the bucket and the bucket goes in the shed and the barrow goes on the front garden where it will not stand where she put it and has to be moved about six inches.

In about a fortnight the grass will be over that kerb again and the three inches of dirt line will be there again, a little wider, and the flag edge underneath it will be clean and wet and going green at the corners.

That is the whole of that Tuesday and nobody has asked her for any part of it.
