# Chapter 0474 — The Four Foot of Pavement

Sunday the twenty-fourth of February 2019, from about twenty past ten in the morning until about half past one. A parade, the top end of it and a wall about chest high, in Tideglass.

**THAT SUNDAY WAS ORDINARY, AND THE ONLY THING THAT HAPPENED ON IT WAS A MAN GETTING A JOB DONE EARLY, AND UNDER THE JOB THERE WAS FOUR FOOT OF PAVING IN A DIFFERENT SHADE FROM ALL OF IT, AND IT CAME OUT INTO THE SUN WHILE A WOMAN WAS BUSY BEHIND A COUNTER, AND NOBODY LOOKED AT IT, AND THE OTHER STANDING THINGS IN THIS BOROUGH ARE EXACTLY WHERE THEY WERE AT THE END OF JANUARY.**

What that parade does in the last week of February is go on, and go on late, because it is a Sunday and because the shops on it that shut have never run to a timetable anybody else could learn.

Six of the premises on that parade are open every day of the week. Four more open Monday to Saturday. Three shut at half past four whatever day it is, and one shuts at two, and a further four were shut when it began to rain this morning, have been shut since the middle of January, and will open when the person who owns one of them decides to open it.

About nine people went up and down it between twenty past ten and half past one.

A man who puts shutters up came off the top end at half past ten and went back up the top end, which is the whole of what he does there. A woman with a dog came by at about a quarter to twelve and went back the same way at about one. The man who works nights went up at about ten to twelve and came down again at about ten past one. A woman with a bag went round a man standing in the middle of the parade with his hands in his pockets doing nothing whatever. The man in his sixties crossed from the far side at about ten to twelve with a cup in his hand and went up. A man in his forties went up that parade at about twenty past twelve and carried on out of it. A man in his thirties came down at about ten to twelve and went off the bottom.

The woman who shuts that shop at half past four stood on her own pavement from half past ten until about one and went inside once, for about four minutes, and came out.

That is everybody on that parade this morning. It is a Sunday.

---

Here is the small thing that went wrong and got put right in the ordinary way, because that is what this borough does.

Outside the shop that shuts at two there is about nine foot of pavement that nobody sweeps, because it is not anybody's, and about a fortnight of a wind off that river gets into it between one o'clock and the time that shop shuts.

The man who runs that shop comes out at about ten past twelve most mornings and pushes the whole of it into a bag with a scoop, and then he puts it back down again at about four, and it stays down until he comes out the next morning.

This morning he came out at about ten to twelve, which is an hour earlier than he came out on Friday, and he had a queue behind him and there was still water coming off the row, and he got it done in about six minutes and went back inside.

What was underneath had been there for years.

There is about four foot square of that pavement that is a different colour from the rest of it. It is a shade paler and it is smoother and it is the width of whatever stood on it, and it has been walked over four times a day by everybody who comes up that parade, and not one person in this borough has ever put one word about it to another person.

There is nothing to say about it. There is nothing to know about it. It is four foot of pavement that is a different colour.

At ten to twelve the awning over that frontage came down, the way it comes down every morning, and the whole of those nine foot went into shade with about four foot of a different colour in it.

At about ten past twelve the awning came up again, because the sun got round the end of the row, and the sun came onto that four foot at the same moment, and it was there for about two minutes with the sun full on it while the woman inside that shop was serving four people.

Nobody on that parade looked at it. Nobody has ever looked at it.

There is a wall about chest high at the top end of that parade with a door in it, and it is not shut all the way, because about four foot of it is off the bottom on the hinge side and it has to be lifted about two inches to come to.

Nothing locks it and nothing ever has.

There is no bench in it and there never has been, and there is about ten foot of ground behind it and then the back of a premises, and that is all it is.

Nobody went through it on that Sunday. Nobody has gone through it since before Christmas, and the reason nobody goes through it is not that it is locked, because it is not locked, and it is not that there is anything to bar anybody, because there is not.

It is simply that a door in a wall in a town where nobody has to be anywhere does not get opened by anybody on a Sunday morning at about twelve.

---

Off that water there is a building, and a passage off it goes to a room at the end of it, and there is a book standing open on a shelf in that room where somebody left it to hold its own place.

There are nineteen entries in that book. There have been nineteen since the March of 2016. Below the last one, on the same sheet, there is a gap the width of a pencil line going right across the page, and nothing whatever written in it, and on the twenty-fourth of February that gap had been sitting there for one thousand five hundred and fifty-one days.

Nobody in this borough is carrying that figure about. Nobody has counted it, nobody has ruled it, and nobody has gone down that passage since Tuesday.

Four answers to one question are still going about this borough and no fifth has joined them.

Not one of the four has been withdrawn, and no person in this borough has ever told another which of them is theirs. Between twenty past ten and half past one on that Sunday nobody set two of those four next to each other, and nobody asked any other person what any of them was for.

Underneath all four of them there is a question. Nobody on that parade put it to anybody this morning and nobody there is going to.

And the thing about that parade that still has no words for it is where it was at the end of January. What has come of that since is nothing. There is nobody left in this borough with a reason to go looking for it, which is not the same as it having gone anywhere.

A fortnight is a fortnight and a month is a month and fifty-two days is fifty-two days and none of them turned anything.

**AT ABOUT HALF PAST ONE ON THAT SUNDAY A MAN CAME ALONG THAT PARADE WITH A BROOM AND A SCOOP AND A BAG AND PUT WHAT HE HAD SWEPT INTO THE BAG AND CARRIED IT DOWN TO THE END, AND HE DID NOT LOOK DOWN, AND THAT FOUR FOOT OF PAVEMENT WAS IN THE SUN ON THAT FRONTAGE FOR ABOUT TWO MINUTES WITH NOBODY LOOKING AT IT, AND NOBODY LOOKED AT IT AFTERWARDS.**
