# Chapter 0689 — She Knocks Once

Friday the seventeenth of December 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from first light until dark and one woman comes up her path and knocks once, and the whole of the afternoon is a step ladder in a front room and two women in a kitchen who cannot get at the one question that would matter.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT OPENS HER OWN FRONT DOOR TO A WOMAN SHE HAS NEVER SPOKEN TO WHO HAS CARRIED A STEP LADDER UP THE ROAD ON HER SHOULDER, STANDS IN A KITCHEN WITH A TAPED BOX UNDER A CLOTH ON THE TABLE BESIDE HER AND TELLS THE WHOLE OF IT OUT LOUD WHEN SHE WAS NOT ASKED, CANNOT PAY HER BECAUSE SHE CANNOT ASK HER NAME, AND LETS HER PUT THE LADDER IN A FRONT ROOM SHE HAS NOT SAT IN SINCE THE SIXTEENTH OF JULY.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight. She is on her own all day. One woman speaks to her at her own door and comes in.

---

There is a noise out the side of the house that is not the shed door and is not the coal.

It is a thing being stood up on a hard surface, twice, with a bit of a bang at the bottom of it, and then nothing.

She is at the kitchen table with the cloth over the old damp mark and she does not go out for it. She sits with her hands in her lap and works out what it is from the sound, and it is a length of something being put down against a wall, and there is nothing in that garden anybody would put down against a wall except a thing out of the shed, and the shed has the coal in it and the shovel and the padlock hanging on the hasp.

Then the knock.

---

It is one knock and it is on the front door.

The front door is not on the latch and has not been since before Christmas, and there is no bell on it, and there has not been a bell on it in this borough in the whole time she has lived in this house.

She goes down the passage and steps over the brush, right foot then left, and puts her hand flat on the door and opens it.

There is a woman on her own path of about thirty with her coat on and her sleeves pushed up and one shoulder higher than the other.

"Right," says the woman. "I'm not going to talk you into anything."

"Was there somebody there."

"Was there somebody where."

"I heard something in the yard."

"That was me. I'm sorry. I put it down too hard." The woman shifts and looks past her into the passage. "That's all it was."

"What is it."

"It's a step ladder."

"I can see it's a step ladder."

"Right."

"It's mine."

"Is it."

"It's not, that's the trouble. I've had it since Sunday and it's not mine and I can't give it back because I don't know who it's off, and I've brought it up the road because there's nowhere to put it at mine and it's going to be rained on." The woman says the whole of it in one go and then stops, as though she has heard herself and wants to see what the house does with it.

She stands in her own doorway.

The woman has a face she does not know and she is about thirty and her coat is not a good coat and there is a mark on the top of her left hand where something has been carried on a shoulder, and the step ladder is standing against the side wall of her own house between the front door and the corner with a strip of dark tape round the second rail.

"Can I come in for a minute?" the woman says. "Not to — I've not come about anything. I can't stand in a gateway talking."

"You could come in," she says.

---

She steps back and the woman comes in over the step and does not look at the front room on her right, which is the room that is a room with a pale patch on its ceiling and a rush-seated chair against the wall by the window, and the woman looks at the runner instead, and at the brush in the middle of it with the handle across the walking line, and steps over it the way a person steps over a cable in a doorway.

The passage light is on the whole length of the runner and has been since June.

"That's a nice light," the woman says.

"It comes on with the dark."

"It's on now."

"It's on now because I can't find the switch that turns it off."

---

They go through to the kitchen, which is at the back, and the front room is between the door and the kitchen, and the woman goes through it and the woman of seventy-eight watches her go through it.

The kitchen is small and cold and there is a cloth on the drainer and a bowl under a tap in the next room and the green chair is at the table with a cushion on it. There is a gas envelope on the right of the table, face up, square with the edge of the table, pencil side up, six things on the back of it in pencil and nothing crossed off and no pencil in the house.

On the left of that table there is a taped box the size of two loaves with a cloth over it, and it is about the width of a bread bin and it has a label on the side of it that has gone soft at the corners, and the woman of seventy-eight watches the younger woman's eyes go onto it and stay there.

Then the younger woman looks away and looks at the window.

"That's a box," the woman says.

"It is a box."

"It's got a label on the side of it."

"It came on the twelfth of October," she says. "It was not for anybody. It went out with the recycling once and a lad came and put it back on the grass of my own gate and told me the parcels are not theirs. It came back off a different van with a second label gummed over the first one with a street on it I have never been to and a number at the end of it that is not twelve."

"You've not opened it?"

"No."

"A box in a house and nobody's opened it."

"That is exactly what it is."

And here is the thing she has been not doing since the twelfth of October, and she stands in her own kitchen and does it now and watches herself do it and cannot stop it.

She has said all of that out loud exactly once before. She said it to a woman with a bright voice and a camera pole on the twenty-sixth of November, in this kitchen, because that woman asked her a direct question and it was out before she had the sentence ready.

Nobody has asked her a question about it. That is the whole of what is different about this afternoon, and she has said it anyway, to a woman who was sent up a road with a step ladder on her shoulder to ask whether there was anywhere to put it.

She has not written a word on it since the twelfth of October and there is no pencil in the house.

The sentence is right there and she has just used it.

It is not that she does not know what it means. She has known since the twenty-second of November and she has worked it out in this kitchen more than once, on her own, out loud to nobody, and what it means is that a mislaid thing does not get a second address.

She looks at the cloth on the table.

"It's tinned fish," she says.

"Is it."

"I've not opened it. I couldn't tell you what's in it. I've not the faintest idea what's in it."

"That's not fish then."

"That's not necessarily fish."

And that is all either of them says about it, and the box is taped, and the cloth is on it, and the woman of seventy-eight sits down on the green chair with the cushion on it and does not go near it again for the rest of the afternoon.

The woman at the door stands in the middle of that kitchen with her hands hanging.

"It came off a porch," she says. "My sister took it out of a porch two doors from hers on Sunday because it was going to be rained on, and she couldn't ask anybody whose it was, and I couldn't ask her because she'd taken it out of a porch in a hurry and hadn't asked either. And it's not mine and it's not hers and it's not anybody's, and I've got a shed with a felt roof and she says I can use it, and then I got up on it and the front edge of the felt's gone so it's no use for the shed, so now it's got nowhere at all."

"And you brought it up here."

"I know."

"Why here?"

The woman looks at the floor of the kitchen for a second.

"My sister says there's a woman at the end of this road," she says. "She says she's never been."

There it is.

And that is the end of the reason, and it is the whole of the reason, and the woman of seventy-eight hears it and understands all of it in the time it takes, and what she understands is this: that the whole errand was built on a thing this woman was told by a sister who has never been either, and that she has walked a mile up a road with an aluminium ladder on her shoulder on the word of a person who has never seen this gate.

She cannot ask her anything useful.

She could ask her name. There is no reason on earth not to. She is seventy-eight years old and somebody is standing in her kitchen and you ask a person their name.

She opens her mouth to ask a woman standing in her own kitchen what her name is, and what happens is that she finds she has not got the sentence, and the reason she has not got the sentence is that she has not given her own name to anybody who has come to this door in about twenty years, and she does not know whether she would be entitled to one.

So she does not ask.

---

"You've not asked me anything," the woman says.

"No."

"You've not asked me whose it was, or where I got it, or what I want."

"No."

"That's all right. I wouldn't have known half of it."

She goes to the sink and gets a cup out of the drainer and dries it and pours the water off the hotplate into it and hands it over, and the woman takes it in both hands and does not drink it.

"It's not that I don't want to pay you," she says.

"I didn't say that."

"You've got a purse in that hall."

---

And that is the sentence she has been waiting for without knowing she was waiting for it, and it is the one that finishes her.

She goes out to the passage and gets the purse off the shelf and it takes longer than she means it to and it has a zip and there is nothing in it but notes and two or three pounds.

She can put a note in that woman's hand.

She cannot put a name on it.

A note with a number on it and no name on it is a thing that goes into an office somewhere and a thing somebody else is asked about, and it is the same shape as a tape on a box, and she has the purse in both hands in her own passage with a brush at her feet and she can see the whole of it at once for the first time in her life and she does not like it.

She goes back into the kitchen and gives her the cup back.

"No," she says. "I'd not know what to put on it."

"You could just give me the cup."

"I've given you the cup."

---

"Where am I meant to put it," the woman says. "That's all I actually need. I've got a man with a ladder is not the problem, I've got a van. I need a wall."

And the woman of seventy-eight looks at her.

"There is a room," she says.

---

She does not choose the front room. She stands in her own kitchen and works out that she cannot have it in the passage because of the brush, and that she cannot have it in her own kitchen because there is a table in there with two things on it and one of them is not hers, and that she cannot have it in the back room because the back room has a bed in it and a window with tape on the corner of it, and that there is a front room.

"There is a room at the front," she says. "It's not been used. You'd put it against that wall."

The woman at the door goes and looks at it.

She stands in the doorway of the front room the way everybody stands in that doorway, and she does not go in, and she looks at the pale patch on the ceiling just inside the door and at the rush-seated chair against the wall by the window and at the stool she took a stool's height on in the summer and at the sideboard with nothing on it.

"It's cold in here."

"There's a range in the kitchen."

"Right," says the woman, and steps in and puts both hands on the ladder and walks it in, and puts it down against that wall, and sets it so that it is standing on its folded feet with the tray end up and the tape round the second rail about the height of a hip.

She squares it.

---

She did not ask the woman to square it and the woman squares it anyway, the way people square a thing they have put down in a house that is not theirs, and the woman of seventy-eight watches her do it and something goes through her that she cannot put a name to and does not try to.

"Front room's the one with the light on the landing coming in under the door?" the woman says from the back.

"There's a light on the passage."

"Yeah. That's the one I mean. If anybody comes." She is already getting her coat. "That's all I wanted. I couldn't have it out on the step. It'll be wet through by the end of it and it's got a tray on the top for paint and you'd want it in a dry house for a fella with cans on."

"There is a fella with cans on?"

"No," she says. "There's no fella with cans on. I don't know why I said it."

---

She goes out down the passage and steps over the brush and goes through the front door, and the door is shut when she shuts it and does not latch, and it never has.

The woman of seventy-eight stands in her own passage with her hand flat on the door until the footsteps have gone down the path and out through the gate, and then she goes and looks in the front room.

The step ladder is standing against that wall under the window with its folded feet square to the edge of the floorboards and its tray end up, and it is the only thing in that room that is not a fixture, and there is a pale patch on the ceiling just inside the door with a defined edge, and a rush-seated chair against the wall by the window, and a sideboard with nothing on it.

She does not go in.

She stands in the doorway and looks at it and she can see the whole of it from there, which is the thing about that doorway, and the lamp on the passage is behind her and it is on, and the room is dark except for what comes round the door.

---

A thing has been put in her front room by a person she cannot name and cannot pay, and it is a step ladder, and it is standing square.

She has not sat in that room since the sixteenth of July. It has a mark on the ceiling and a rush chair in it and a sideboard and a stool and a step ladder, and the second thing on the back of the gas envelope is the front room and it is not a painting job and it is not on there because she will not sit in it.

There is now a piece of aluminium in it.

She is not going to be able to paint round a ladder, and she is not going to be able to move the ladder, and she is not going to be able to ask anybody to take it away, and those are three separate facts and they are all the same fact.

She goes to the kitchen and puts the cloth over the box on the left of the table and squares that, and the box is the width of a bread bin and the cloth is not quite dry.

---
