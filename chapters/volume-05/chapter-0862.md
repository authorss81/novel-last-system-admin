# Chapter 0862 — A Quarter Inch At The Hinge

Sunday the nineteenth of November 2023, at the near gate of the unit yard from the time the frost has gone to the first of the hand, and the quarter inch the hinge gives is the whole of that Sunday.

**THE TOP HINGE ON THE NEAR GATE OF THE UNIT YARD HAS PLAY IN IT AND HE MEANS TO LIFT THE TOP OF THE GATE A QUARTER INCH BY HAND AND LET IT BACK DOWN, AND THE LATCH STAYS HOOKED, AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN GOES OUT THROUGH THE UNIT GATE WITH A HANDCART.**

The near gate is a framed gate with two ledges and a brace across it and it weighs about as much as a full-grown man, and it is hung on the yard side of the two gate posts with a strap over the top hinge and a pintle into it, and a strap over the bottom one the same way.

There is play in the top hinge.

You can see it without touching anything. The gate is not square in its opening. At the hinge end the top corner of it stands about half an inch proud of the face of the post, and at the latch end it stands back about the same, so the whole top of the gate is out of true, and it has been out of true for long enough that the frame has begun to follow it.

What causes it is not the strap and it is not the post.

The post is sound. The staple that was going to go into the top of that post in the spring is not on it and is not going on today. The strap is where it always was, and the strap has drawn up into the grain of the stile the way a strap does, about a sixteenth, and that sixteenth is not the quarter of an inch being lost at the top corner.

The grain of a stile runs from the bottom rail to the top rail in one piece and a strap goes across it, and what a strap does to wood over a few years is squeeze the grain flat where it crosses it. You can find that afterwards with a thumbnail and it tells you nothing you did not already know.

The pin is worn. The top pintle is wrought iron, and it carries the whole weight of the gate, and it has turned in two holes in the strap and two holes in the knuckle of the post for long enough that the hole in the strap has opened out at the top. The pin is loose in it. The gate hangs from a hole that has got bigger than the pin, and everything the gate loses it loses out of the top.

---

What he is going to do about that is nothing yet, because he does not know yet which of two things it is.

If the strap is sound and only the pin is loose in it, the gate wants about a quarter inch taken up, and the job is a washer and a new pin on a day with somebody to keep a hand on it. If the strap has gone at the wood, the quarter inch is going to come back out of the top corner again in a month and the gate is going to be worse than it is, and that is a job for a new strap.

The difference cannot be seen from the front. It can be felt, and the way it is felt is by lifting.

You lift the top of a gate a quarter inch with one hand while the gate hangs on its own weight.

When you do it, the gate stops being a thing balanced on the bottom hinge and becomes a thing hanging from the top one, and it swings clear of the bottom pin by the amount you have lifted it, and it either comes up square in your hand or it does not.

---

He puts his hand flat on the top rail on the hinge side, about a foot in from the corner, and lifts.

It comes up. Not a sixteenth and not an eighth. It comes up about a quarter of an inch and stops, and it stops because the gate has come up against something, and what it has come up against is the strap, which is still gripping the top rail and is now gripping it with the weight of the gate coming off the bottom pin and onto the top one.

So the strap is sound. The strap is the soundest thing about this gate.

There is a noise when it comes up. Not a knock. A short gritty movement in the strap where it draws over the top rail, the iron on the wood going a fraction of a turn as the gate's whole weight transfers, and then nothing at all once the weight is over. That noise is the sound of a gate that is doing what it was built to do and a quarter inch short of where it ought to sit.

He lets it down. It comes down under its own weight the whole way and the latch end moves towards the post it should be against and does not reach it, and the gate stands where it stands with its top corner half an inch proud and its latch end half an inch back, and it is exactly as it was.

He does it again, and the second time is the same as the first, and that is the second thing he wanted to know: the quarter inch is the same quarter inch twice over. A gate that is settling is one that gives you a different gap every time. A gate that is loose is one that gives you the same gap every time you take the weight off the bottom of it, because there is nothing in it that is moving.

Then he lifts it once more and lets it down, and that is three, and he stops.

Three is where you stop, because the third lift is the one that tells you whether the pin has been walked into the strap and has started to take the wood with it, and there is nothing to be learned from a fourth that is not already written on the faces of both strap and post and can be read off them with an eye.

The woman from two doors down comes through the unit gate at midday with a handcart she has to push.

She gets it through the gap on her own. The gate is open that far and the handcart is about two feet across at the handle and he stands back out of the way of it and does not touch the gate while she is going through.

She gets it out onto the frontage, turns it round, and stops.

"That's a heavy box."

"It's the paving off the front step at the end of it. One at a time or the frame goes."

"Your handcart's been in that shed since the spring."

"Since Easter."

"The bus was late yesterday."

"It was."

"You'd think they'd get the timings printed somewhere."

"They print them in a place nobody goes."

"Then I'll not bother," she says, and pushes the handcart off along the frontage and out of sight round the corner.

He watches the wheels go over the flags and up onto the frontage and does not follow her. There are two sets of wheel marks going out along that frontage and none coming back yet, and the wind is getting up behind her, and none of that is anything to do with him.

He goes back to the gate after that and looks at the bottom of it.

The bottom hinge is a pintle and a strap the same as the top one, and the bottom of the gate clears the flags by about two inches at the hinge end, which is what a gate wants, and the gate is resting on nothing. All of its weight is on the bottom pintle and all of its play is in the top one.

He does not put a washer in it. He does not put a staple in the post. He does not take the pin out and he does not tighten anything, and the latch is still hooked where it was hooked this morning and will be hooked tomorrow.

What is on that gate now is a quarter inch of play at the top hinge, which was a quarter inch of play at the top hinge this morning, and one gate hanging on its pin about a hair lower than it was hanging on it when the day started, which is what a gate does when it is lifted three times and put down three times and is heavier at the top than it is at the bottom.
