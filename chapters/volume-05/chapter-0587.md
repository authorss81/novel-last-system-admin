# Chapter 0587 — The Second Wednesday

The Wednesday of the nineteenth of August 2020, from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon, at a first-floor flat in this borough and at a house a long way off where somebody else is letting her have the back room.

**A THING WAS SAID TO HIM ON A SUNDAY AND SHE WENT ON THE MONDAY, AND HE DID NOT PUT ANYTHING DOWN FOR THE BEST PART OF A WEEK, AND WHEN HE FINALLY DID IT WAS HER OWN SENTENCE BACK, WORD FOR WORD, AND NEITHER OF THEM CHANGED ONE WORD OF IT.**

The woman is in her late twenties and the man is in his early sixties. Six years married, and not one conversation in that time that either of them would afterwards have described as having gone well.

She left the Monday before this Wednesday. She is not a person who leaves, and the leaving was her own decision and not a row, and it was booked before it was announced. She is at her sister's until well into the next month, in the back room, and she has the kind of time there that she has not had since the two of them got the flat.

He is at the flat on his own. He is a man in his early sixties and he has been in that flat for twenty years and he has a habit of the place that has never once had to be shared with anybody.

---

On the Sunday before she went, they were in the front room and she said a thing. She said it while he was doing something to a cabinet he had taken the door off, and she said it to his back while he had the screwdriver out, and it was one sentence and it was about money and about what they were going to do about money.

Then on that Monday she said she was going to her sister's and he said, "All right," and she went, and he did the bank and the bins and the shop and the whole of that week on his own, and he did not write to her once.

He thought about writing several times. He has never been a person who writes, and she has never been a person who reads quickly, and between the Sunday and this Wednesday he drafted nothing and posted nothing and did not telephone, because a telephone is not a thing you do at a woman in her late twenties sitting in a back room a long way off with nothing on.

What he did instead, on and off, was the sentence.

On the second Wednesday she had been gone he got up at six and made the tea and did not post it, and then he got the paper off the sideboard and wrote it out.

He wrote it word for word, which was the part he had been turning over. He wrote:

*I want to sell the shop, and I want us to stop pretending we've not been saying it for a year.*

That is the sentence. She said it to his back on the Sunday with the screwdriver out. He had not put it anywhere. He had carried it round all week in his head the way you carry a jar of something to the top of a cupboard and then come away from it.

He wrote it out on the paper, and he read it back, and it was the same as what she had said, word for word, and he did not have a single go at it to soften one end of it or firm up the other, because it was hers and not his and it did not need him to do anything to it.

He put it in an envelope and put the envelope in his coat pocket, and then he stood in his own front room for about as long as it takes a kettle to come, and then he went down and posted it in the box at the end of the road, and the last collection was at half past four, so it went on that day.

It came on the Wednesday. Her sister brought it up with the shopping and said it had the postmark on it and the stamp on it and no name on it, because he had never once put a name on anything he sent her.

She opened it at about ten.

The woman in her late twenties read the one sentence on that paper four times. It was her own. It was the same words, in the same order, with the same comma in it where she had put the comma, and he had not added anything to it and had not taken anything out of it, and the sentence was still true at the back room in her sister's house exactly as it had been true in the front room on the Sunday with the screwdriver out.

She sat on the end of the bed with it for a bit.

What she could not work out, and did not try to work out that day, was why he had written it out. Not why now, which she could not have done either, but why at all, because she had said it once, to a man's back, over a cabinet door, and had not said it since, and there was no reason on that paper for it to be in her own handwriting back in front of her unless a person meant it to be a thing that had been said, and not a thing that had been said once and dropped.

She did not answer it for a while. That was not a plan; she was a long way off with nothing much on and she simply did not put a pen to anything that day, and the paper went under the postcard stand on the shelf in the back room, which is where her sister keeps the things that are not bills.

Here is the part of it that is true and neither of them will ever put together. The sentence was hers and it was on that paper word for word because he had not trusted himself to improve it. It was true on the Sunday and it was true on the Wednesday after, and everything between those two days was the two of them not doing anything, and not doing anything is not a thing a person writes about.

**HE SAID NOTHING FOR THE BEST PART OF A WEEK AND THEN HE SENT HER BACK HER OWN WORDS WITH HER OWN COMMA IN IT, AND SHE READ IT AND PUT IT UNDER A STAND OF POSTCARDS, AND IT IS STILL TRUE, AND NEITHER OF THEM WILL EVER KNOW THAT THE OTHER ONE WAS CAREFUL ABOUT IT.**
