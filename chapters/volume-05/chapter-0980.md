# Chapter 0980 — Behind The Plane Iron

Undated in this draft.

**A SMOOTHING PLANE ON ITS SIDE ON THE BENCH HAS ITS MOUTH OPEN AND THE IRON STILL IN IT.**

The plane lies on its side on the bench under the covered way at the unit, with its sole flat to the bench and its mouth facing out into the yard, and nobody has touched it since the shop it belongs to shut. It is a jack plane of the ordinary pattern, about fourteen inches long, with a cast body, a wooden knob at the front and a wooden tote at the back.

Lying on its side, the mouth of it stands open about three-quarters of an inch wide and about the same deep, and the iron is still in the mouth where it was left, tipped back at the angle for a shallow cut. Nothing about the tool has been disturbed: the lever cap is screwed down where it was, the wedge in front of the iron stands in its slot, and the depth wheel is where two full turns back from the end of its travel. The mouth is a slot in the sole of the plane and it goes the width of the sole and no more, and along its whole length the iron stands inside it and closes the slot from one end to the other except at the working edge, where about a sixteenth of an inch of daylight shows through to the other side.

Behind the iron there is a space about a quarter of an inch down and the same width as the mouth, and it is closed at the far end by the back of the iron and at the near end by the front of the iron and above by the cap iron and the lever cap. Nothing can be seen into it from any direction at all while the tool lies like that.

The bench under the covered way is of softwood and has been under a roof for thirty years, and the plane stands on it where the bench is driest, at the end nearest the yard mouth. The top of the bench carries the marks of a long line of work: paint in the grain, a ring where a cup stood, a cut across one corner where something sharp went past the edge, and a scatter of fine shavings that have been swept off and have come back.

The covered way is a run of corrugated sheet on timber bars at about the height of a tall man's head, and it throws a band of shadow across the bench and the flags under it from the middle of the morning to the middle of the afternoon. Outside that band the flags are pale and dry. Under it the ground keeps whatever it has taken in the night.

The flags of the yard stop at the gate end of the way, where they give way to a hardstanding of old concrete about ten feet deep and worn smooth in the middle by the wheels of a barrow. The junction is a straight line across the yard and about half the dust that blows about in the yard stops on it and goes no further. Along the store wall there are the sacks of sawdust that get emptied into the bay and are not always emptied, and a length of battens stood on end against the wall with its ends squared and its faces damp at the bottom where the wall runs wet in the spring. The cast body of the plane is damp at the foot of it in the shade, and the beech of the tote takes moisture out of the air and keeps it longer than the iron does, and the little boxwood wedge in front of the iron has been in a shut space with a wet thing and has not had long enough to dry.
---

One finger goes in through the mouth of the plane and up behind the iron, and is pressed forward along the bed, along the length of the sole, until the iron stops it.

The stop comes sooner than the length of the plane, at about four inches from the front of the mouth. What stops the finger is the back of the iron, which is set at a slight angle across the bed and does not run the whole way to the front of the mouth, and behind that the finger can feel the bed itself, which is cast iron and ribbed along the middle where the maker left it rough.

The space behind the iron is not the same depth all along. At the working edge it is about a quarter of an inch, and a third of the way back it closes to a sixteenth where the iron crosses over the top of the chip breaker, and between those two places it opens out again into a small chamber that runs into the back of the tool.

A shaving is in there. Not a cutting: an old one, about the thickness of a playing card and folded once, lying along the bed with its fold towards the front of the mouth and one end of it standing on edge against the iron. It has been in there long enough for the dust of the plane to be packed into both of its faces.

Nothing moves. The iron does not shift a hair when the finger presses on the back of it, and the lever cap does not lift, and the plane does not rock on the bench because the sole is flat and the bench is flat where it stands. The shaving does not stir, and the dust packed into it stays packed into it.

The body of the plane is a casting, and its surfaces are the surfaces of a mould that has not been a very good one. The knob and the tote are turned beech, and the tote has swollen across its grain where it has been wet and come back to shape again, so that the grain stands proud in ridges along the top of the handhold where the fingers go.

The lever cap is a piece of stamped steel with a slot in it, and the screw that keeps it down has its head filed across so that a wide blade will turn it. That screw has been turned a good deal. Its slot is polished bright on the parts that bear and rusted in the parts that do not, and it sits at an angle of about a quarter turn from square in the cap it runs through.

The mouth is a straight slot and its two long edges are sharp, as they are in every plane of that size, because a plane cuts because the edge of the iron is sharp and not because the mouth is. The gap behind the iron at the working edge is the only place in the whole of the tool where light comes through, and what comes through it is a line about the width of a hair and about a foot long. Where the sole of the plane has been set down on the bench it has left a clean rectangle in the dust, and the rectangle is about the size of the sole and quite exact, and it has not moved an inch since the tool was put down on it.

The woman from two doors down is standing on the concrete at the gate end of the covered way when the finger comes back out of the mouth of the plane, with the hardstanding behind her and the way in front, and both palms turned out where anybody passing could see them.

She does not come under the way and she does not step off the concrete to come and see anything. The band of shadow across the bench is behind her and the plane is on the bench, and neither of the two puts a word about it.

There is a question about the road up by the school, which is shut at one end for as long as the drains are done, and the reply to that is that it is shut to the through traffic and open to anything that lives at the top of it. There is a second question about what time the bins go out on the side of the road where the flags are, and the answer given is that it is earlier than it was and that the men come about an hour sooner and are gone before anybody is up.

Her hand opens on the concrete in front of her as if for something, and closes again on nothing, and she goes on standing at the end of the way with the yard open behind her and the shadow of the roof running away from her along the flags.

The mouth of the plane is left standing open on the bench with the iron in it and the shaving in it where they were, and the gap behind the iron is shut at every point along its length but the one that lets the light through.
