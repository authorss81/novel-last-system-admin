# Chapter 0920 — Behind The Loose Collar

Tuesday the twenty-fifth of June 2024, at the collared fence post at number twelve after the dew goes off the boards. Behind a loose timber collar.

**SHE HAS A FENCE POST WITH A LOOSE COLLAR OF TIMBER ROUND IT AT NUMBER TWELVE AND SHE MEANS TO SLIP THE BACK OF THE HAND BEHIND THAT COLLAR WITH FINGERS POINTING DOWN AND HER SISTER IS AT THE FRONT STEP FROM THE PATH WITH A SEED TIN IN HER PALM.**

The fence at number twelve went up in a week with posts of pine, and the third from the shed carries a collar where the rail meets it while the rest stand bare.

It is a collar of oak boards about four inches deep, with the grain running round and the joints pegged, and it hangs off its nail with cobweb in the fold where the draught moves. She wants the space behind that collar, where the post sweats and the spiders run.

A collar slit argues beyond its boards: weather on the boards, post exhalations in the slit. So the hand-back slips down behind the post collar. The timber feels cool across the knuckles, and the collar rims catch where splinters lift them.

The hand travels down a finger's length and meets a wad of moss rammed at the foot, green and damp, with small shells in it and a wire nail bent where the hammer missed it years past. Knuckles kneading the wad from flank to flank, she feels the moss sag a scant quarter inch and bunch anew on retreat.

Behind the fingers stands the post face. Sound pine set deep in the soil with its grain raised from rain and its knots close, standing where it was set. Finger-deep and no deeper, the slit bottoms in rammed moss. That moss is what packs the collar against the post every time the wind gets at the fence.

She frees the hand from behind the collar and cleans the green on the post flank, fence beside her as dew leaves the boards. Her sister comes to the front step from the path with a seed tin in her palm and does not turn it over.

Tin in palm, she took the step, eyes on fence, then beds, then fence. Presently she hefted the tin, capped it with her palm and kept it fast, before walking on down the path, tin in fist, the step bare behind her. A fencer's single morning set those collar posts, tarring feet and stamping earth round, dark-aged nails driving the collars last.

Dark-aged nails bite fences past the power of bright ones, their keen rims worn smooth seasons ago so they abrade rather than snap.

---

Keen rims worn away beneath the collar nail leave its head a whisper proud; front sag with rear lift beckons draughts aft. A collar hanging low like that puts its storm water down the lower rim, and that water scores a shallow groove in the dirt at the post foot.

Rill water at this post foot veers round the moss wad, the swollen tuft sipping the flow into itself. The wad drinks what the boards shed, and the ground past it stays dry, so the collar has cut itself off from the slope.

The pine of the post is rough under the palm where the weather ridged the grain out of it, and the collar boards are coarse with saw scoring. The nail head is coarse in a third way, pitted all over by rust. Dawn damp loosens off coarse timber in drops, each drop baring the face a little further.

The oiled length of that rail keeps its damp till evening, while the ridged cheek of the post has been dry since the sun cleared the fence. Sun-side first to dry, shade-side last: the collar post gives its south cheek to noon and keeps northern lichen while baring its crown.

Lichen goes wide where damp outstays the sun on the boards, and narrow where noon strikes through the gaps. Two summers of wet make a scab of lichen on those boards, figured so a thumbnail follows the lines of it and a finger scrapes nothing off.

Rub a crust in spring and it skins over from the outside in, the last of the middle taking longest, and nothing else gives the age away so plainly. The silver on those boards stays on long after the garden dust has gone pale, and June is the month that shows it.

Shower water off the rail centres on the collar back and drops together behind the boards. What comes down goes into the moss and the wad swells against the slit, while the post face throws the rest clear.

---

Behind the collar the post keeps its own wet, and the crown above the boards is dry by noon and dry again all winter. The post has carried that wet since before the rails got their last paint, and its footing has not moved a finger's width.

The rail has enlarged its housing by a coin's depth along three winters. One paint fleck has survived underneath it, and it stands lower now than the shoulder it was once level with. What survives of it sits under the shoulder and nowhere else, which is the shoulder that has kept it there through three winters of chipping.

No second mark anywhere on the fence, and the rail has come down plumb all the while. Slit moss lies green-wedged, marbled with wan filaments dormant in cold months and astir by July. Under the moss at that mouth there is a spider's web, and the dust on it powders loose under a fingertip that never quite clears it.

A strand strung across the shaded post betrays the tenant first, and the foot shows no strand since the rails got their oil. The rail ends are housed a finger deep in their shoulders, the nail heads bedded in the wet, and the garden stones bedded in the soil; none of the three has shifted.

A cap rail runs the fence top, weathered to silver, its upper arrises rounded where palms brush passing. The cap was pegged, not nailed, oak pegs that swell tight in rain and rattle faintly in drought. One peg mid-run clicks in wind, a tiny castanet the gardener knows by ear. Lichen powders scraped from the boards dye the fingertips faint green, a fugitive tint soap removes. The tint marks lichen years the way rings mark timber, each shade a wet summer survived. Peg click and lichen tint together map the fence seasons better than any diary, one sounding the wind and one staining the touch, and both outlast the paint on the rails, silvering deepest on the weather side and palest beneath the cap, twin witnesses no storm shifts.

Ivy ties along the fence lash the stems to the rails with tarred loops, loops the gardener renews each spring. The ties bite shallow grooves in the soft stems, grooves that swell shut by August and reopen when stems shrink in drought. Boards lapped on the rails overlap thumb-deep, upper over lower, shedding rain down the face course by course. Slaters lodge behind the laps in daytime crowds, grey commas that scatter at a tap and regroup within the hour. The regrouping leaves a faint frass of droppings on the rail tops, dark pepper the rain washes off.

The rail ends sit in housings cut a finger deep, with the shoulders tight and the tar run round them, and the lower housing of the middle rail has opened a hair where the water stands. A housing open like that takes grit in the wind and grinds a little deeper every summer, while the tight ones above stay close and dry through every rain.

Tar ridged by the brush keeps its weather along the foot of the post, and the cheeks of the housing are all chisel. The kerf the saw left in the post cap is rough under the thumb, and the wax on the nail head takes a polish in the middle of it, a pea-sized ease the rain rubs brighter every year. Nail ease shows where the hammer last fell, dim amid rough, sinking a hair with the collar.

She paces off to judge the post end to end, collar dangling and moss green beneath. Spills quit a storm-roughened crown each dawn, scouring pine bright; loose meal rinses out, tight grain abiding.

The collar hangs where the nail keeps it, the boards round the post with moss at the foot between, and before the paint is on the rails again the boards will have silvered another shade.
