# Chapter 0920 — Behind The Loose Collar

Tuesday the twenty-fifth of June 2024, at the collared fence post at number twelve after the dew goes off the boards. Behind a loose timber collar.

**SHE HAS A FENCE POST WITH A LOOSE COLLAR OF TIMBER ROUND IT AT NUMBER TWELVE AND SHE MEANS TO SLIP THE BACK OF THE HAND BEHIND THAT COLLAR WITH FINGERS POINTING DOWN AND HER SISTER IS AT THE FRONT STEP FROM THE PATH WITH A SEED TIN IN HER PALM.**

The fence at number twelve went up in a week with posts of pine, and the third from the shed carries a collar where the rail meets it while the rest stand bare.

It is a collar of oak boards about four inches deep, with the grain running round and the joints pegged, and it hangs off its nail with cobweb in the fold where the draught moves.

She wants the space behind that collar, where the post sweats and the spiders run.

A collar slit argues beyond its boards: weather on the boards, post exhalations in the slit.

So the hand-back slips down behind the post collar.

The timber feels cool across the knuckles, and the collar rims catch where splinters lift them.

The hand travels down a finger's length and meets a wad of moss rammed at the foot, green and damp, with small shells in it and a wire nail bent where the hammer missed it years past.

Knuckles kneading the wad from flank to flank, she feels the moss sag a scant quarter inch and bunch anew on retreat.

Behind the fingers stands the post face. Sound pine set deep in the soil with its grain raised from rain and its knots close, standing where it was set.

Finger-deep and no deeper, the slit bottoms in rammed moss. It goes a finger deep with moss rammed at the foot between, and the moss beds the collar against the post when the wind moves it.

She frees the hand from behind the collar and cleans the green on the post flank, fence beside her as dew leaves the boards.

Her sister comes to the front step from the path with a seed tin in her palm and does not turn it over.

Tin in palm, she took the step, eyes on fence, then beds, then fence. Presently she hefted the tin, capped it with her palm and kept it fast, before walking on down the path, tin in fist, the step bare behind her.

A fencer's single morning set those collar posts, tarring feet and stamping earth round, dark-aged nails driving the collars last.

Dark-aged nails bite fences past the power of bright ones, their keen rims worn smooth seasons ago so they abrade rather than snap.

---

Keen rims worn away beneath the collar nail leave its head a whisper proud; front sag with rear lift beckons draughts aft.

A collar sagged low like that sheds rain off its lower rim during storms, and the shed water scores a shallow groove in the dirt at the post foot.

Rill water at this post foot veers round the moss wad, the swollen tuft sipping the flow into itself.

So the proud wad guards its own seat by drinking the wash, and the earth beyond stays dry instead of letting it run.

The post pine runs coarse where weather ridged the grain, the collar boards run coarse where the saw scored them, and the nail head runs coarse where rust pocked it.

Dawn damp loosens off coarse timber in drops, each drop baring the face a little further.

A weather-ridged face dries soonest after rain, while an oil-dressed one carries moisture along its foot till evening.

Sun-side first to dry, shade-side last: the collar post gives its south cheek to noon and keeps northern lichen while baring its crown.

North-board lichen spreads wide where damp outstays the sun and narrow where noon strikes through.

Two summers of growth shellac a lichen scab there, figured so a thumbnail follows it but a finger scrapes nothing off.

A crust scraped in spring closes anew from its rims inward, the middle shutting last, a rim-led mending that marks old growth.

Lichen on the shaded boards stays silvered after the garden dust has paled, and June picks out the turn along the fence.

Shower water off the rail centres on the collar back and drops together behind the boards. The moss lap drinks the middle of the fall and the post face sheds the rest downward, and the wad swells and narrows the slit.

---

Past the collar the capped post sheds its own wet straight off the crown, so the top dries overhead while the slit stays damp behind through winter.

The post has carried that wet since before the rails got their last paint, and its footing has not moved a finger's width.

The rail has enlarged its housing by a coin's depth along three winters. The remaining fleck, flush against the post since the fence was painted, sits a fraction lower now.

Winter chips the paint through, so the fleck persisting under the housing proves the rail end its guardian.

The fence bears no twin mark; the lone one argues straight settlement.

Slit moss lies green-wedged, marbled with wan filaments dormant in cold months and astir by July.

A spider tenants the collar slit year round, and dust at the moss mouth sifts loose under a fingertip.

A strand strung across the shaded post betrays the tenant first, and the foot shows no strand since the rails got their oil.

Rail ends lodge home in housings, nail heads lodge home on boards out of the wet, and garden stones lodge home on soil.

A cap rail runs the fence top, weathered to silver, its upper arrises rounded where palms brush passing. The cap was pegged, not nailed, oak pegs that swell tight in rain and rattle faintly in drought. One peg mid-run clicks in wind, a tiny castanet the gardener knows by ear. Lichen powders scraped from the boards dye the fingertips faint green, a fugitive tint soap removes. The tint marks lichen years the way rings mark timber, each shade a wet summer survived. Peg click and lichen tint together map the fence seasons better than any diary, one sounding the wind and one staining the touch, and both outlast the paint on the rails, silvering deepest on the weather side and palest beneath the cap, twin witnesses no storm shifts.

Ivy ties along the fence lash the stems to the rails with tarred loops, loops the gardener renews each spring. The ties bite shallow grooves in the soft stems, grooves that swell shut by August and reopen when stems shrink in drought. Boards lapped on the rails overlap thumb-deep, upper over lower, shedding rain down the face course by course. Slaters lodge behind the laps in daytime crowds, grey commas that scatter at a tap and regroup within the hour. The regrouping leaves a faint frass of droppings on the rail tops, dark pepper the rain washes off.

The rail ends sit in housings cut a finger deep, with the shoulders tight and the tar run round them, and the lower housing of the middle rail has opened a hair where the water stands. A housing open like that takes grit in the wind and grinds a little deeper every summer, while the tight ones above stay close and dry through every rain.

The tarred foot of the post runs rough where brush ridges dried, and the housing cheeks run rough where the chisel chopped them, and the post cap runs rough where the saw left its kerf. Rough tar and rough cheeks meet behind the collar, and wax rubbed on the nail head smooths one grey dot within the rough, a pea-sized ease the rain polishes yearly. Nail ease shows where the hammer last fell, dim amid rough, sinking a hair with the collar.

She paces off to judge the post end to end, collar dangling and moss green beneath.

Spills quit a storm-roughened crown each dawn, scouring pine bright; loose meal rinses out, tight grain abiding.

The collar hangs where the nail keeps it, the boards round the post with moss at the foot between, and the shade will thin off the boards before the dew goes off the boards.
