# Chapter 0260 — A Lamp

Thursday the twenty-fifth of August 2016, from about half past ten in the morning, a kitchen at the back of a parade in Tideglass, and the first-floor corridor of a building by the water.

**HE WAS ASKED TO DO ONE THING BY SOMEBODY WHO COULD NOT ASK FOR IT OUT LOUD, HE DID IT, IT TOOK TWENTY MINUTES INSTEAD OF TWO, SOMEBODY PAID HIM TEN POUNDS FOR IT, AND HE DID NOT ASK THE THING HE HAS BEEN AFTER SINCE THE FOURTH OF JUNE.**

He is on the parade on a Thursday morning because a Thursday is not one of his four nights and is not the Friday, and he has been on the parade in the mornings since the summer began, and nobody has ever asked him to stop doing it and nobody has ever given him a reason to.

He was in the doorway of the shop next door to the one with the trade counter with a cardigan over his arm when the woman of about fifty-five came up the parade at about a quarter to eleven with her handbag over her wrist and no coat on.

"I want to ask you a favour and I want to be honest that I have no favour to give you back."

"That's all right."

"That's not the answer to a favour," she said. "That's the answer to a person talking. The favour is that you would have to leave where you are for about an hour and I would pay you for the hour, and I have never in my life bought an hour off anybody and I do not know the rate for it, so I am going to give you what is in my coat and I would rather you took it than made a thing of it."

"Go on then," he said.

She said: "The corridor at the end of the first floor. The light in it went about a fortnight ago and it has been like a cellar since and I have been working at the end of it. The man who does the works comes on the tenth of September and says it is not their light. I have got a stool and I have not got a ladder and I have not got anybody, and I would not have asked anybody except there is nobody to ask, which is why I am asking a lad who is on the parade."

---

The corridor is about twenty feet long and has a window at one end and three doors and a fire door, and the room at the end of it is hers.

The stool came out of the cupboard under the window and it is one of those kitchen steps with three treads, and the fitting in the corridor is at the near end, over the fire door, which is the furthest place in that corridor you can stand a stool and reach something, and the top tread of the stool would not get it.

"Give me the top step of the stairs," she said, and she had it round the door before he had asked for it, and she held the stool and stood looking up at the thing herself and said, "Oh, that's no good, that's no good at all."

"No."

"I'll get a man in."

"You can get a man in. It'll be Thursday week, and he'll want a couple of hours off his own job to come and look at it, and he won't come before he's had his dinner."

"A couple of hours," she said. "For a bulb."

"For a bulb, a ladder, a van, and telling you it was the fitting."

He got a bulb out of a box on the shelf in her room and a cloth, and she had a torch, and he put the stool at the foot of the top step and the top step of the stairs underneath that, which is not a thing anybody should do, and got the old one out with the cloth so he did not crush it, and put the new one in, and it did not light, because the box in her room had the old sort in it.

That is what took twenty minutes instead of two: the walk to the shop at the end of the parade and back at a run, and the man behind the till counting four out of a box behind the till and saying they'd want the money, and the four lamps in his hand for a week, and then the getting up onto the step again.

It went on at about twenty past twelve and the corridor was not a cellar from that point, and it stayed that way, because nobody in that building had ever once asked why it had gone off and nobody was going to start.

She stood at the bottom of the top step with the torch off for about ten minutes afterwards and looked down the length of the corridor, at the fire door and then at the window at the end of it, and then she went and got her coat.

"Ten pound is in the pocket of the coat on the chair," she said. "There's more in it than that and I'm not going to argue with you about it in a corridor."

He took it and put it in his back pocket.

"What I was going to say is that I've been thinking about asking you something since the summer," she said, "and I've decided not to, and I want to tell you I've decided not to, because the second I ask you it's an occasion and you'll have to stand there and think about your answer in front of me, and you asked it in a room once and a man told you to say it again slower, and I was in that room, and that is the reason I am not going to do it to you in a corridor."

"That was June," he said.

"It was June," she said.

He got the stool back under the window and the top step back where it goes and the cloth in his pocket, and went down the stairs and out of that building at about ten past one, and the door at the end of the corridor was shut and there was a light burning over the fire door all the way to the window.

---

He was back on the parade at about twenty to two and the woman he works for was doing the sink, and she looked at him over her shoulder.

"Where'd you go."

"Up the road. Done a light for a woman."

"Who for."

"The woman at the end of the corridor in the old place by the water."

"Did she pay you."

"Ten pound."

"Right," she said, and turned back to the sink, and he took his cardigan off the back of the door and put it on and started on the first of the plates, and there was a smell in that kitchen he could never afterwards name.

**AND THE CORRIDOR HAD A LIGHT IN IT, AND A LAD HAD TEN POUNDS IN HIS BACK POCKET AND A COLD BACK OFF A STEP-STAIR IN A STAIRWELL, AND THE BOX ON THE SHELF IN THAT ROOM HAS FOUR LAMPS IN IT AND ONE BULB USED OUT OF IT, AND THE MAN BEHIND THE TILL WILL TAKE THE MONEY FOR THE FOUR ON FRIDAY.**
