# Chapter 0267 — A Tin

Friday the thirtieth of September 2016, from about eleven in the morning, a kitchen on a street off Corrance Lane in Tideglass, and a shop that shuts early on a parade.

**THE NAME ON THE LID WAS NOT FOUND OUT BY ANYBODY, AND NOBODY WENT LOOKING, AND THE PAINT WENT BACK INTO THE TOP DRAWER OF A KITCHEN UNIT WITH A THUMB'S WORTH OF IT GONE AND THE LID STILL ON.**

She had come into that flat at the end of August and the kitchen unit was in it and would go with her or not as she liked, and the landlord had not been near it, and there was nothing in the place that anybody had put there on purpose.

She was looking in the top drawer of the kitchen unit for a tea strainer that turned out not to be in it, and the tin was at the back of that drawer under a stack of unusable menus from a takeaway that closed in the spring, and it was a tin of paint.

It was an old make and the label had gone the colour of a weak tea, and it was about a third full, and there was a strip of masking tape along one edge of the lid with a surname and an initial written on it in chinagraph pencil, pressed hard, by somebody who had written it to be read once and then folded away under the rim of the lid.

She put it on the table and looked at it for a while, and then turned it over and looked at the other side, and carried it round the kitchen and looked at it in the window, and put it back in the drawer under the menus and pushed the kitchen unit to and then stood in the kitchen doing nothing at all for about a quarter of an hour, which she has not done since the summer.

---

The shop that shuts early is at the far end of the parade and does about nine till one and then a lock-up, and the woman who runs it has been running it long enough that the stock list in the lock-up behind the counter has three years of other people's handwriting in the top half of it.

She carried the tin down there on the Thursday at about half past twelve because it was raining and because there was nowhere else in that part of the town to take a thing to.

"Come and have a look at that label," said the woman who runs the shop. "I know that label."

"It's not yours."

"It's not mine," she agreed, and turned it round, and then did not read the name on the tape out loud, and pushed it back across the counter. "That unit's been in that flat since the house was split in two, and whoever had it before me had it before him, and there's a man's name in the back of the book in the lock-up because he left a bag of leads in here in July and never came back for them, and he is not the name on your tin, and I have never once been told his name by anybody who ought to know it either, and that is the whole of my interest in the thing."

"Do you want it?"

"I can't use it. It isn't my colour and it isn't my tin and I'd have to paint something to get rid of it and I haven't the brush."

She asked what it was worth, and the woman who runs the shop said it was worth the lid and the hour it had cost her to come down there in the wet. Then she said that a man who does odd jobs in that part of the town had been in on the Thursday before that, and that he was the sort that would take a thing like that off you and not ask a single question about it, and that he was usually about until two and did not mind being asked to do nothing at all.

The man who does odd jobs took the tin off the counter in his own two hands and read the tape on the lid before he read anything else on it, and then put his thumb flat over the tape, which he did not do because he had thought about it.

"You want rid of it."

"I want it out of my drawer."

"Right," he said, and put it at the back of the van with a coil of rope and a bag of sand and a folding spade, and shut the door on it, and did not write anything down, and did not ask her name, which he had not asked when he came in either.

She did not see him again and there was no arrangement and there was no list and there was no question asked of her on the way out and she asked none at the counter.

---

It came back on the Friday.

It was on the step of that house at about half past ten in the morning, standing upright on the flags, with the lid on, and there was a thumb's worth of grey gone out of the middle of it and the surface of the paint inside had a dent in it the shape of a man's thumb.

The gatepost at the end of the yard behind that yard had been bare wood on the top half of it since the spring. On the Friday it had a hand's width of grey over it, brushed on badly and stopping about a foot short at one side, the way you do it when you have not got a brush and are stood there with the tin on your knee.

The man who does odd jobs was in the doorway of a van on a street off Corrance Lane at about ten to eleven, with a folding spade in the back and the rope over the wheel arch, and she told him she had asked nobody and he had not told her, which she said so that he would know she knew.

"I did a bit on that post," he said. "I hadn't got anything else to hand and I hadn't the brush either. I took the top off the tin to see if it had gone, and there was a bit of paper on the lid and I couldn't see what it said, so I put the back on."

"You read the name."

"I read a name," he said. "It was the man's name, because of the M at the front of it. I don't know the rest of it and I never had it in my hand for longer than a second and I'm not going to be the one who starts asking about it." He shut the van door. "I didn't know what it was. That's the whole of it. That's not a thing anybody's done to you. It's a tin somebody left in a drawer and I wanted a bit of grey out of it for a post that's been bare since April."

She asked him, standing in that doorway with the rain coming off a gutter beside her, whether anybody had ever come after a tin like that in this town in her time, and he said no, and she said that was what she had thought, and neither of them said anything else about it and he was gone by twenty past eleven.

The one other place a name of that kind turns up in this borough is a book at the back of a room at the end of a corridor on the first floor of a building by the water, and the man who does odd jobs said about that on his own, and unprompted, in the middle of talking about gateposts.

"There's a book in there," he said. "It's a list of names with dates and places and sums on them, eleven of them, and it's a big book, and somebody put it in there and nobody's had it out since the summer."

"What's in it."

"I've never seen it open," he said. "I know it's a list because a woman read the outside of it out loud to a man once, on the step of that building, and neither of them went in, and neither of them is from off this street now and one of them is from about thirty miles away."

**AND THE GREY ON THE GATEPOST OF THAT YARD IS STILL THERE AND WILL BE THERE AT CHRISTMAS, AND THE VAN WENT OUT OF THAT STREET AT ABOUT HALF PAST ELEVEN WITH THE ROPE AND THE FOLDING SPADE AND NOTHING ELSE IN IT, AND THE WOMAN WHO RUNS THE SHUT EARLY SHUT AT ONE AS SHE DOES.**
