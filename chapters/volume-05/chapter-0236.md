# Chapter 0236 — One at a Time

Tuesday the twenty-sixth of April 2016, from about eleven in the morning, the yard at Penhale and the office hut on the concrete, and the parade of shops in this borough.

**A COMPACT CANNOT BE AGREED WITH A YARD, AND IT CANNOT BE AGREED WITH A RULE, AND IT CANNOT BE AGREED WITH A BOOK, AND EVERY SENTENCE THAT EVERYBODY IN THAT YARD SAID ABOUT THAT ON THAT MORNING WAS CORRECT.**

Nell Ardent got to the gate at Penhale at about ten to eleven and Jonas Mercer got there about a quarter of an hour later, and neither of them was on the agenda and neither of them had written to anybody, and the man at the gate said so again, the way he said it in the February, and neither of them asked him to say it to anybody else.

He stood in the doorway of the office hut and did not come in.

Haldane Perry is eighty-nine and he keeps the book and his lanyard says PENHALE SHARED YARD and, in a different hand, since 1987, and he was on the bench outside in the sun with the ledger shut on his knee when they came up the concrete, and he did not get up, and nobody asked him to.

"You've come about a thing," he said, "but you'll have to tell me what it is, because I'm not anybody's office."

"We've come to find out what it would look like to agree something with you."

"Agreed with me."

"With the yard."

"The yard's a bit of concrete and a rule," said Haldane Perry. "There's four men in this county and a woman in the next one who could put a bit of paper in front of me and I'd have to say something about it, and there's not one of them, and it's not because anybody's kept them out. It's because there's no such thing to be a party to any of it. A rule can't be asked to agree to anything. A rule hasn't got a mouth." He put his thumb on the shut ledger. "I could sign that book all night and it wouldn't mean a body's said yes to anything, and it would mean a man of eighty-nine has written his name in a column."

Behind him, in the doorway of the hut, a man in his forties who keeps the book under Haldane Perry and who has been in that hut fourteen years said the other half of it without being asked.

"We can't say no," he said, to Nell Ardent, and he said it the way he says it, which is as a boast, and Nell Ardent heard him say it once, in the February, and it was true then as well and it was no use then either. "That's the point of the thing. Nothing in this yard can turn a crew away and that's why the region's got what it's got. Nine yards, none of them can refuse anybody, and about four of them have never sent a crew out and never took one and they put money in exactly the same, and you'll not find a bad word said about any of the four, including me, and I'm one of the four."

"You're saying the same thing twice," said Nell Ardent, "and the two things are not the same, and you know they're not, because you wanted that wall sheet down and Haldane wouldn't have it and you said he was wrong and you were told he was the boss."

"He is the boss," said the man in his forties. "That's not a joke, either. There's hundreds of these in the country, and the boss of each one is a man with a ledger, and if you want to get rid of a boss you have to stop needing a yard, and then you have no rule, and then you've got a booking system and a booking system has a manager and a manager can be asked to do something."

The woman of about eighty-six was at the far end of the bench with a piece of chalk, counting pipe. She said one thing while Nell Ardent was there and she said it to the yard and not to anybody.

"It was a field," she said, "and there's never been a pound off it, and there's never going to be, so whatever it is you're after, it's not coming out of there."

---

Nell Ardent asked the man in his forties one question on the concrete with the ledger open on his knee and the wind getting at the pages, and it was the first yes anybody gave anybody, and it took about nine seconds, and it was not the one that mattered.

"Would you let a line go in the book that isn't a claim."

He looked at the column. Then he said yes, and he said it cheerfully, and he said it was the easiest thing anybody had asked him in fourteen years, because there is a gap in that book about two inches deep and nobody has ever wanted to write anything in it that wasn't a job.

"That's worth nothing, though," he said. "That's worth exactly nothing, and you can have it. A column's a column. If you wrote a hundred of them down they'd still be words in a book that four of us turn the pages of."

"I know what it's worth," said Nell Ardent, and shut the book gently, which she does.

---

The parade in this borough at about two in the afternoon is a parade of shops and one of them has a trade counter at the back with a lamp over it that is on in the afternoon.

The woman of about forty-two who has had the key to the hall off the parade for about nine years, and who lets it out by a key out of a drawer and a notebook she keeps in the same drawer, and who put a pound in the gas in April because the gas is on a meter, was told the shape of the thing at about twenty past two and said yes in about four seconds.

"The hall," she said. "The key, the book with the weeks in it, the gas, the door, all of it, and you can have Tuesday nights and the Sunday before, and I won't take anything for the gas and I want that said out loud so nobody in that room thinks I have been paid."

"And what does that cost."

"It costs me the mothers' group on a Wednesday, because there is one Wednesday they can have and it isn't this one, and they've been having that room on a Wednesday for years and I'm the one who took the booking." She said it without any drama. "And it costs me that I have been a woman with a key doing people a favour for nine years and if I do it on purpose with a reason, then I'm a woman with a key running a thing, and there'll be a next one that wants a next reason. So you'll have it and you'll not have a word out of me about it, and that's the price and I've set it."

"It can't be agreed with a yard," said Nell Ardent. "It can't be agreed with a rule and it can't be agreed with a book, and you were all right about that an hour ago and I'd like it in words. It's people. It's one at a time, face to face, and each one of them has to give something that's actually theirs, because the minute one of them gives me something a body owns then that body can have it back."

Tomas Renn was behind the counter and had been for a while and had not said anything.

"No," he said.

"There's no question to answer yet."

"There's a question and you haven't asked it, and I'm answering it anyway." He put the cloth down. "A compact agreed with people one at a time is a list. That's all it is at the end. It's a column of lines that somebody could write down, and I know what happens to lines, because there is a bit of paper in a county we've not been to with lines on it and everybody in here knows whose names are on it, and I did those jobs in March and I wasn't asked and I wasn't counted and I went and I did them, and I'd do them again, and that's exactly the reason I'm saying no."

"That's not the reason," said Nell Ardent.

"It's the reason."

"The reason is that you think a person says yes to a thing and it means they're on it, and it doesn't, and you've known that since you were twenty."

"That is a different reason and it comes to the same place," said Tomas Renn, "and I'll not do it twice over in one afternoon."

Jonas Mercer was on the pavement outside with his hands in his pockets for about half an hour during that and did not go in, and was not asked to, and could hear the whole of it through a shutter that was up and went down three times in twenty years.

**AND A COMPACT WAS AGREED WITH A WOMAN WITH A KEY ABOUT A HALL AND A NOTEBOOK, AND A COLUMN IN A BOOK AT PENHALE WAS OPENED TO A LINE THAT IS NOT A CLAIM, AND A MAN WHO HAS DONE JOBS HE WAS NOT ASKED TO DO SAID NO AND WAS RIGHT, AND THE YARD IS A BIT OF CONCRETE AND A RULE AND HAS NO MOUTH AND CANNOT BE ASKED TO AGREE TO ANYTHING.**
