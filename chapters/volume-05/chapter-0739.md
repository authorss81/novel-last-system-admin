# Chapter 0739 — The Third Line

Tuesday the fifth of July 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house and her own yard at number ten from first light until dark, and the whole of it is three lines of paper read out loud because there is somebody in the yard to read them to.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES READS THE THREE LINES ON HER OWN PAPER OUT LOUD TO A BOY WHO HAS COME OVER THE FENCE FOR A BALL, GETS AS FAR AS THE THIRD LINE AND STOPS, TELLS HIM THAT A GATE WHICH WILL NOT SHUT IS EITHER THE GATE OR THE FRAME AND ONE OF THEM IS OUT, GIVES HIM THE BALL BACK OVER THE FENCE, AND PUTS THE PAPER FACE DOWN ON HER OWN TABLE.**

She is a woman in her early thirties in her own house and yard at number ten on Tuesday. A boy comes over the fence in the middle of the day for a ball and she speaks to him out there.

---

The washing is out by the middle of the morning, pegged across the draught with the peg at the shed end pushed a finger further than it wanted, where it has been since the end of May.

The shed felt is still gone off the front edge and lifting at the corner, and the ply offcut is against the wall where she leaned it, flat, not rubbish and not use.

The paper is in the middle of the kitchen table. Three lines on it in her own pencil, written one December night with the bulb on the landing left burning, in a hand she does not admire. It came out of the drawer at the end of April and it has been on that table since, and the drawer has the hammer and the screwdriver in it and no paper in it.

She reads it to herself about twice a day and it does not get any better and she has never read it out loud in her life.

---

The ball comes over the fence at about the middle of the day and lands in the yard square by the shed.

It comes fast, and it comes off the wind, and it hits the side of the shed and goes on past and ends up against the wall where the ply offcut is leaning.

A boy's head comes over the top of the fence about four seconds later.

"That is mine," he says.

"I will fetch it," she says.

He stays over the fence with his arms folded on the top of it and his chin on his arms and watches her go up the yard to get it, which is a long way for a ball and not the way she would have got it in the spring, before.

She brings it back and does not throw it. She sets it on the fence by him and lets it stand there, and a fence post on that fence is as wide as a man's foot and it is not going anywhere.

"That one has been over twice," she says. "The other end of it. I can see where it has scuffed the felt off the shed and the felt was already off at that corner before you came."

"It was going to go anyway," the boy says.

"It was going to go anyway," she says. "That is not the same as it not being your fault."

---

She goes out to the shed and crouches down at the corner and puts her fingers up under the felt where it has come away, and lifts it, and lets it go. It comes down and comes up in a slow cycle that has nothing to do with wind. The ply offcut stays where it is, flat against the wall, where it has been since the end of May and where she has not stood it up.

"Can you not nail that," the boy says.

"I have a hammer and I have a screwdriver and I have a roll of tape with nothing worth the name on it," she says, "and that is not enough felt to cover what is there, and if I put ply on the front edge of that roof I will have a roof with two things on it and one thing wrong, and I will know about the wrong one every time it rains."

"Two things is better than one thing," the boy says.

"Yes," she says. "And that is the whole of the argument, and I have been having it with myself since April and I have never had anybody agree with me before."

---

The ball has gone over the fence again while she was on the ply offcut, and this time it has not come back, and the boy has got his arm out over the top of the fence as far as it will go without leaving the path on the other side.

"Say the thing you have been saying," he says. "You have said three numbers in a row and you have not finished any of them."

She looks at him over the fence.

"Say what."

"You keep saying how many there are of things. Three of these. Two of them. And then you stop."

She stands in her own yard in the middle of the day and finds she has been counting things out loud all morning without doing it.

"You have made me do it," she says, and goes in, and comes out with the paper in her hand.

---

She stands against the wall by the back door and reads it out loud.

"A roll of something," she says. "That is the first one. A tin. That is the second one. The third one is a lip of timber under a new edge."

She stops.

"That is all of it," the boy says.

"That is all of it," she says. "It has been on that table since April and it is three lines long and it does not say what the roll is or what the tin is or how thick the timber is, and it does not say what any of them weigh, and it does not say what any of them cost, and I have not been into a merchant's shop since before Christmas, so I cannot make it any plainer than that and neither can anybody else."

"What would you ask for," the boy says. "If you went."

"I do not know what I would ask for," she says. "That is the whole trouble with the third one. The first two are hard. The third one I could say in a shop in about four words, and I have not been able to say it in four words since April and I have said it in about a hundred."

---

He shifts along the fence on his elbows.

"My dad fixed a gate at the back of ours," he says. "It has been on since before Easter and it will not shut. You push it and it goes and it stops about a foot off and you can put your hand through the gap."

"Is it the gate," she says.

"What?"

"The gate," she says. "Is the gate the thing that is wrong, or is the frame."

"How do I know."

"You do not know," she says. "And neither does your dad, probably. One of the two is out, and it is either the gate hanging crooked or the frame being out of square where it was built, and if it is the gate you can put it on new and the gate is no use, and if it is the frame then the gate is perfect and it has been perfect the whole time, and you cannot tell the two apart from the gate alone without taking it off its hinges."

"So take it off its hinges."

"You have to be able to put it back," she says. "That is the whole of what there is to say about it and I have been not saying it since April."

---

She picks the ball up off the fence at the near end and drops it over so that it lands on the path on the other side, and he comes down and goes to get it.

She goes in. The kitchen table has the dust of the ply offcut on one corner of it and a drawing of a ball on it in pencil where she put her elbow down.

She folds the paper in half once and then in half again, the way it has been folded since she wrote it, and puts it face down in the middle of the table, with the folded edge towards the chair she sits on, and takes her tea over to the sink where the light is better, and reads nothing at all for the rest of the day.
