# Chapter 0467 — Two Minutes of It at Half Past Five

Sunday the thirteenth of January 2019, from about half past five in the morning until about ten to six. One street and one length of footway, in Tideglass.

**A MAN WALKED DOWN HIS OWN FRONT PATH EVERY WEEKDAY BEFORE HALF PAST SIX MAKING A NOISE OUT OF IT, AND A WOMAN LIVING ABOVE A LOCKED GARAGE BEHIND HIS HOUSE PUT HER HEAD OUT OF HER DOOR AND ASKED HIM, ONCE, TO STOP, AND HE DID NOT, AND SHE HAS NEVER RAISED IT AGAIN, AND HE KNOWS EXACTLY WHY SHE CANNOT.**

He is the man in his forties who does building work and odd jobs and who is out of his door at about half past five most weekdays, and the reason he is out of his door at half past five is that his first job is about two miles off and the roads are not there yet.

There is a garage between his house and the next one and it belongs to a house behind, and above that garage there is a flat, and the woman who lives in it is out of that house by about a quarter to six.

He has never met her.

The thing he does is not a tune anybody has taught him and it is not the same tune every day.

It is about two minutes. It starts when his own door shuts behind him, which is a noise he makes, and it runs the whole length of that frontage, and it ends when he gets to the van, and he does not do it on the way back because he is carrying two things in the cold.

He has been doing it since 2010, which is the year he moved into that house and the year that woman moved into the flat above that garage, and neither of them has ever put the two facts together, because you cannot put a fact about a man in the street together with a fact about a woman behind a shut window unless somebody in that street is thinking about both at once, and nobody in that street is thinking about anything at half past five in the morning.

She has heard it every weekday morning for nine years. She has never once been outside when it happened. She has never once said to anybody in that building, including the man who owns it, that there is a man outside who makes that noise at half past five.

On the eighth of January, at about half past five, she opened her own door.

---

She was not being brave about it and she was not being clever about it either.

Her alarm had been off for two days because her shift had changed and she was back on the early one on the eighth, and she got up in the dark at the wrong time and her body went on doing what it had been trained to do and she was at that door in about six minutes, on the landing, in a coat over what she had slept in, with her bag in her hand, when the door at the bottom of those outside steps went.

She opened the door and she went down about four steps and she said it in the plainest words she had.

She said, "You make that noise at half past five every morning and I've got to be out at a quarter to six, and I'd be grateful if you'd leave off it."

That is what she said. There is no version of it that is longer.

And he stopped.

He stopped dead on the pavement. He had about nine inches of the end of that tune still in him and he did not finish it.

And he said, "Right. Sorry."

And she said, "That's all right."

And he got in the van.

Seven words apiece, and the whole of it took about ten seconds, and that is the only occasion in nine years on which either of them has heard the other one speak at all.

He did not do it on the Monday. He did not do it on the Tuesday. He did not do it for the rest of that week and he did not do it for the week after.

---

And then he started again on the Wednesday of the sixteenth.

He has not worked out a reason in a way he would be willing to write down. What he has got, if you pressed him, and you cannot press him, is this.

She has a removal booked for April. He has known that since about the middle of December, and he has known it from two things, and neither of them was a conversation. The first is a van outside that house on a Sunday in December with the sides open and nothing on the shelves. The second is that she has been in at about ten every night for a week, which is not what she does.

He worked that out and he told himself a thing with it.

The thing he told himself is that in April there will be nobody in that flat to ask.

And he has carried on since then, in the plain knowledge that the only person in that building who could have stopped it has stopped being able to.

He knows she has not asked again.

He knows it in a way that is more reliable than knowing, because of what she does now. She comes out of that door at about a quarter to six and she says good morning to him, and she says it in a voice that has nothing in it.

For nine years she said good morning to him in a voice that had not yet decided anything, because for nine years there was nothing to decide. For six weeks she has said it in the voice of a person who has asked a man to do a thing and has watched him do the thing and has watched him start again, and who is not going to bring it up, and who would be more than a bit off her head if he did.

He has not asked her either.

He could. He could stop at that gate one morning and say, "You going to be all right in there," and she would say, "Fine," and that would be the end of it. He has thought about that about four times. He has not done it, and the reason he has not done it is that he does not want to be forgiven, and he has not been able to work out whether that is a reason to say something or a reason to keep quiet, and he has been going round with it for six weeks.

On the Sunday of the thirteenth, in the four minutes she had the door open, she had looked at his van.

It is a van with a tailgate and it has had a plate of the same colour as itself on it since about 2014, and on the Monday morning after that he moved it about nine inch to the left so that it was not under her window, and he has moved it back twice since, and it is under her window now.

He has not thought about why he moved it. If anybody had asked him on the Monday he would have said it was the better place for reversing out and it is not, and he has known that since the Tuesday.

Nobody in that street knows any of this. The man who owns the flat has not been asked. The woman on the other side of that garage has a dog that she is not outside at half past five. There are four houses on that frontage and about two hundred people go past that garage every morning and not one of them has ever had a reason to think about a noise that stops and starts.

**AT ABOUT HALF PAST FIVE ON THE MORNING OF THE TWENTY-FOURTH OF JANUARY A MAN CAME OUT OF A DOOR AND WALKED SEVEN YARD TO A VAN AND MADE THAT NOISE ALL THE WAY AND GOT IN, AND ABOVE THE GARAGE BEHIND HIM A WOMAN WAS ALREADY AWAKE AND HAD BEEN FOR AN HOUR, AND SHE LET HIM GET IN.**
