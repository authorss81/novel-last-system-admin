# Chapter 0987 — Behind The Breast, In A Mirror

Undated in this draft.

**THE FIREBRICK BACK OF THE FIREPLACE AT NUMBER NINE HAS COME AWAY AT ONE CORNER.**

The front room at number nine has a chimney breast in it that takes up a foot and a half of the width and about a foot of the depth, and the room has not had a fire lit in it since before the woman came, which is a different thing from never. The breast is not even and never was, because the house was built by men who stood a stack on a wall and packed it out with rubble, and a chimney of that sort leaning half an inch over eight feet of height does exactly what it likes and keeps on doing it for a hundred years.

The firebrick back is the two courses of soft yellow brick that close the back of the opening against the flue. In this one they have come away at the bottom corner on the left, and not from any load at all, but because the flue has been cold for years and the brick at that corner has been wet every time the rain comes off the roof of the bay window and has never once dried through, and the mortar round it was mixed too wet and has gone out altogether. Where the two courses should meet there is now a black slot about the width of two fingers running round the corner and up into the second course.

The gap behind is not a big one. His little mirror on a lath goes round it and finds the second course square and sound and the back of the flue a hand's width beyond that, and the mortar on the first course crumbled to a putty that his thumbnail will go into without any trouble at all. Down in the bottom of the gap there are about a foot of old soot, hard as a floor tile and standing up in flakes where the cold has worked into it over the winters. He can see the top of the flakes and he can see the shadow their own edges throw.

"There's two inches at that corner," he tells her, turning the mirror a quarter at a time so that the light goes into the slot and comes back. "And it goes back a hand's width. You could put a whole hand in there up to the knuckles, if you were the sort to try it. I'm not, and neither are you."

"Nobody of my age is going to try that."

"You'd be the sort to know it." He takes the lath out of the slot and turns the mirror over in his hand. "That throat has been opening two winters. Another two and it wants the whole back out and a fitter's smoke alarm with it, and a fitter is three times what you just paid me in not-my-money and not-my-morning."

She studies that corner for a good while. Her hand goes up towards the mantel and stops a foot short of it and comes back down onto her knee, and there is nothing on her knee and nothing on the mantel and nothing at all in that room on show. The room is clean the way a room is clean when a person of her age has decided once what the standard is and then never lets it slip, and the two chairs are the same colour and the carpet has a worn lane between the door and the window and the clock has stopped and she has not had it going since she came in.

"She's been round," the woman tells him at last. "The woman that keeps the book."

"Who."

"The one who books work at the lock-up. She was at my door the day before you came and she'd a sheet with her, and she wanted to know if anybody here would go up the river, and I told her there was nobody here but me, and she put my name down anyway."

He puts the mirror down on the hearth and stands with his back to the cold grate and looks at her across the room, because there is a thing he has wanted to get out of himself since the yard outside number two and he can get it out now or not at all.

"I'd rather ask you than put you down," he tells her. "Do you want to go?"

The room goes very quiet, and he can hear the next door's clock through the wall, and he can hear the coat on his back moving as he stands there, and the two of them together last a great deal longer than the question did. She does not answer it at once, and she does not answer it at all, and what she does instead takes him by the front of the shirt and turns him round and shows him the back of his own question where he had not looked at it.

"You'd rather ask."

"I would rather ask."

"All right." She puts her hands together in her lap where they can be seen. "I'll tell you what I want, and then you'll tell me I'm wrong to want it. I want to be on it. Not to go up the river and stand in a queue in the rain, and not to be helped and be a woman who was helped, and not any of that. I want to be on it."

---

She goes out to the front room door for a pencil and a sheet of paper off the shelf in the passage, and comes back and sits down at the table under the window and lays the pencil on the sheet and then leaves her hand lying over it, and looks at him across the table for about as long as it takes him to work out that he has no right to stop her.

"There's a list," she tells him. "You go on it, you're helped. I'm not standing here telling you how it ought to be done, I'm telling you how it comes out, because I have had help once in my life and it came without one single person asking me a single thing, and I have spent a good while since trying to find a way to get to the front of that queue by myself instead of waiting around for somebody to think of me at the right moment. That's all this is. It isn't a favour to you or against you. It's me putting my name where it can be found."

He could stop her. He knows exactly how, because he has stopped three people doing three things this month and every time it worked, and here is the fourth and for once he is standing on the wrong side of it.

"I'm not on the book," he tells her. "Nobody has ever put me on it, and that's not an argument for anything. I know it's coming out of my mouth as one."

"Then don't argue with it. Let me put my name down."

He lets her do it. He stands at the table and stays there while a woman of his mother's age puts two words and a surname down on a sheet of paper with a pencil off her own passage shelf, and the pencil is blunt and she presses hard enough to score the paper underneath, and nothing is ruled under what she has put down and no space is left beside it for anything else, and she does not blot it and she does not blow on it and she does not put it in an envelope.

He goes back to the hearth afterwards and picks the lath up and stands the mirror back against the corner of the breast in the place it was in before he came, and the two inches and the hand's width are still behind it and will be there next week, and the thing he came to this room about is not going to be done this week either. The picture rail along the far wall is out of true with the ceiling line by a quarter of an inch at one end of the room and has been since the house was papered, and he sees that now because he is standing in a room where a woman has just done a thing for herself and he has nothing to add to it.
