# Chapter 0306 — Into the Kitchen

Wednesday the fifteenth of March 2017, from about half past seven in the evening, a hall on Carrow Lane in Tideglass.

**A MAN SAID A SENTENCE IN THAT ROOM THAT HE HAS SAID IN FOUR OTHER ROOMS, AND A WOMAN TOLD HIM TO STOP SAYING IT, AND SHE TOOK IT OUT INTO A KITCHEN AND SAID IT IN THERE AS WELL, AND SHE GOT A FIGURE WRONG IN THAT KITCHEN, AND NOBODY IN THAT HALL EVER HEARD THAT PART.**

The hall on Carrow Lane is a room with a folding table down the middle of it and eight or nine of them in it, and a grate full of old ash that nobody has made a fire in for a long time, and a urn on a table by the wall that makes a noise like a man clearing his throat every quarter of an hour or so, and the woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall has a key to it and does the gas and the padlock at both this one and the one off the parade.

The man in his forties had the scrap of paper out on the table in front of him, face down, and had it out again and turned it over and put it back, and the man in his sixties was watching him do it.

---

The man who repairs sewing machines had come in at about ten to eight with a toolbag in his hand and the strap over his shoulder, and he goes to that hall most weeks and has for a good few years, and he has never been in a room with a camera in it or a thing that is going to be written down, and that is why he says what he says.

"There's a woman in the second house of Marsh Lane," said the man who repairs sewing machines, "who's had a water bill come in that would put that landlord in a position, and he's told her it's a meter that's read wrong, and it isn't, because the meter was swapped in November and the old one's on a different supply and both of them bill her, and she's rung him about it four times and he's not rung her back."

"And what are you going to do about it," said the man in his forties.

"I'm not going to do anything about it," said the man who repairs sewing machines. "I'm not his meter reader. I'm telling you what I've been told by a woman at a door because a thing like that is not a thing a person can do anything about on their own, and if it were a thing a person could do anything about on their own, half the people in this room would be doing it."

The woman who works in a shop had her chair round and said, "You don't want to be saying that in here."

"I've said it in four rooms," said the man who repairs sewing machines. "It's a true thing about a water bill."

"It's a true thing about a water bill and you've said it in four rooms," said the woman who works in a shop, "and the man from the parade said a thing in this room in January and again in a shelter on Marsh Lane in the wind, and now you've brought a bill up, and the next one of you that'll come in here with a thing you've heard at a door is going to be a man with a bird that's had a window shut on it."

"They're not the same thing."

"They're the same thing in that nobody in this room can do anything about either of them," said the woman who works in a shop, "and that is the only part of it that matters, and you can go on all night."

The man in his forties put his hand flat on the scrap of paper.

"You leave it," he said, to the man who repairs sewing machines. "That's all. Not because it's not true, and not because it isn't a bad bill, and not because anybody in this room thinks you should be talking about the man from the parade. Because a thing that's been said out loud in a room goes round that room and then it goes out of that room into other rooms on the people in it, and the woman in the second house of Marsh Lane can't un-say it, and she never said it."

---

She was on the second cup at about half past eight and the young woman of about twenty was at the sink end of that kitchen doing the washing up in a cardigan over her work, and she told her.

"I'd not repeat that in there," said the woman who works in a shop, "not while he's sitting there."

"He said it'd been four times," said the young woman of about twenty.

"He did say that."

"Does a landlord ever ring you back," said the young woman of about twenty. "That's the bit. I can't see the shape of it. If you ring me and I don't ring you back, that's a landlord being a landlord. If you ring me twice and I don't ring you back, that's a landlord, and after the third time you're a person who's had a bill, and a person who's had a bill can be told to go to a different supply and put on an account, and then you've got two accounts and one of them will be for a hundred and ninety pounds and nobody can look at the meter because it's the wrong meter for that account."

"That's not happening."

"You don't know that," said the young woman of about twenty, and shut the tap off, and stood there, and then said, "I said a number, didn't I."

"You did."

"I did," said the young woman of about twenty. "That wasn't the number she gave me. She said it went over two hundred and she said it went over two hundred in the same quarter twice, and I heard myself say a hundred and ninety and I've not got anything behind that. You didn't tell me any of that, you told me a landlord hadn't rung a woman back."

"I didn't want it going round that room."

"That's not the same as it being right," said the young woman of about twenty. "That's just it not going round that room."

The woman who works in a shop stood at that end of that kitchen for a second.

"Twenty-five years," she said. "She's been in that house twenty-five years. She was saying it in the queue at the post office on the Friday, and I've been in this room every Friday for the best part of a year, and I've heard it twice before and I said right both times, and I did not come out here and ask you a figure. So that's mine, not yours. I've had that on my own account for a fortnight and I've not said a word to anybody about it."

"You don't have to tell me that," said the young woman of about twenty.

"You've said a number about a water bill in this kitchen and you've not got a woman behind it," said the woman who works in a shop, "and somebody in that room is going to hear it, because you are going to go back in there in a minute with that tray."

She went back in with the tray. Nobody in that room asked her where she had been and nobody in that room had heard a figure come out of that kitchen, and the man in his forties put the scrap of paper in his pocket and said that the tea had gone cold again and put the lid on the urn.

At the end of the table the man from the parade was on the far side from the door and the folding table has a brace across the two legs at the bottom, which is a length of a scaffold board about two foot long, and he has looked at that brace before.

"Where did that come from," said the man from the parade, to nobody in particular, about the brace.

"It came out of a skip on Cleeve Street," said the woman who has been in that hall about two years. "In the summer. Somebody brought it in and nobody wanted it and the man who does the hall had a bit of trouble with that leg."

"It's not bad."

"It's scaffold," said the woman who has been in that hall about two years. "It's the real thing. It's a lot better than what's meant to be under that table."

Nobody wrote it down and nobody took the brace off and it is under that table to this day and it holds.

**AND A FIGURE WENT OUT OF A HALL ON CARROW LANE INTO A KITCHEN NEXT TO IT AND CAME BACK AS A HUNDRED AND NINETY POUNDS INSTEAD OF WHATEVER THE WOMAN AT THE POST OFFICE ACTUALLY SAID, AND THE WOMAN WHO WORKS IN A SHOP WENT BACK IN WITH A TRAY, AND FOUR PEOPLE WERE STILL IN THAT ROOM.**
