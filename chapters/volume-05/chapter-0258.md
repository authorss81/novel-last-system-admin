# Chapter 0258 — A Sentence

Saturday the thirteenth of August 2016, from about half past nine in the morning, the first floor of a shop at the end of a parade in Tideglass.

**HE WAS ASKED FOR A SACK BARROW, AND HE DID NOT SAY NO, AND HE DID NOT SAY YES EITHER, AND HE PUT FOUR BOXES DOWN TWO FLIGHTS OF STAIRS HIMSELF, AND THEN HE SAID ONE SENTENCE ABOUT A MAN NOBODY IN THAT CORRIDOR HAD EVER MENTIONED.**

The room over the shop at the end of the parade had been empty since June and was being done up for letting, and the man doing the paper had started on the front wall at about eight, and when the letting agent came at half past eight and saw what was in the corridor he said it would have to be empty before he could carry on, and said it again at ten.

In the corridor outside there were four cardboard boxes that had been in that corridor since the first of August and had been in a room on the first floor of a building by the water before that, and on Saturday morning the woman of about fifty-five was going to move them into the empty room, which was where she was putting the two boxes she could not leave in a corridor that is being stripped between the tenth and the fourteenth of September.

"They'll have to be out," he agreed, at ten, from the top of the steps. "I'll not have them in the way and I'm not stood here all day for it."

"I know. I'm moving them now."

The first one she managed. The second one she got down about nine steps and had to stop and put her back against the wall.

The young woman of about twenty was there at ten with a bag of books she had come to fetch out of the empty room before somebody locked it, and she took the second box and got it to the bottom of the first flight, and they could not get the third one into the turn at the bottom of the stairs between them, and the woman of about fifty-five said, in the voice she uses, "That's all right. Leave it, I'll get someone."

"Leave it," said the young woman of about twenty. "It's got a chair in it."

---

Tomas Renn came along the parade at about a quarter past ten with a sack barrow, which lives at the hardware shop four doors down and which anybody in this town may walk out of a hardware shop with on a Saturday morning, because it is a barrow and it is not money.

The young woman of about twenty said, "Can we borrow that."

"Yes," he said.

She stopped.

"Say again?"

"Yes, you can have it," said Tomas Renn. "Push it back up the shop when you're done, that's all, and don't take it up the parade because the wheels are rubbish."

He did not put it down in the corridor. He carried it up the stairs himself, took the third box off the top of the pile without asking whose it was, put it on the barrow, went down, put it in the passage, and came back up for the fourth, and on the way down with the fourth he said, "There's a step in the light on the half landing, watch it," which is a thing you say to a person who is going back up, and then put the barrow in the passage and carried the box from there.

That is the whole of what he did in that corridor.

The young woman of about twenty shut the door of the empty room and stood with her back on it.

"You've said no three times," she said. "I have only been in this borough since June so I can't tell you about the other ones, but I have heard that you have, and everybody in the room I was in had a story about it. And that was a barrow."

"It's a barrow," he said.

"You could have said no to a barrow and nobody would have said anything."

"I could have," he said. "That's the difference between a barrow and the rest of them and it isn't a moral position, it's a barrow. You'll not get it back up the parade."

---

They got the barrow back to the hardware shop at about eleven, and he wheeled it in past the shop steward, which is him, and put it against the wall inside the door, and came out again because the parade was there and it was a Saturday in August.

The young woman of about twenty was on the parade with the woman of about fifty-five and about a third of the boxes, and they were standing by the kerb, and he stopped.

Then he said:

"There's a man sweeps this parade at about half five in the morning. He comes down the middle of it with the yellow light on and he does Peverell Street after, and he's not on anything, and I've been in that shop long enough to have watched him go past every morning of it. I couldn't tell you his name. I have never once seen anybody from this borough say one word to him — not a good morning, not a nod, nothing — and he does not expect any, and I have never asked him anything either and I'm not going to now. That's the whole of it. You can do what you like with it."

Nobody said anything for a bit. A lorry went up the parade.

"Somebody's got to know he's there," said the young woman of about twenty.

"He's there," said Tomas Renn.

"That's the problem."

"I know that's the problem," he said. "That's why I said it to you and not to anybody in a room. You'll write it down and it'll be a thing, and then somebody'll come and ask him does he want the hours changed, and he'll have had a conversation he didn't ask for, and he'll have to have an answer, and he hasn't got one and he doesn't need one."

"So it's just for us to know," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"It was for me to say," he said, and left it there.

Then he went up the parade to his own shop and put the barrow away again and did the rest of a Saturday, and the parade got on with it.

**AND THE TWO OF THEM STOOD BY THE KERB FOR ANOTHER FEW MINUTES WITH THREE BOXES AND A BAG OF BOOKS, AND THE RE-PAPERER CAME OUT OF THE ROOM AT HALF PAST ELEVEN AND SAID HE'D GOT THE LAST OF THE FRONT WALL, AND HE DID NOT LOOK AT THE CORRIDOR, AND THE SACK BARROW STAYED WHERE IT HAD BEEN PUT AGAINST THE WALL INSIDE THE HARDWARE SHOP DOOR WHERE IT HAS STAYED FOR YEARS.**
