# Chapter 0901 — Under The Lip

Saturday the thirteenth of April 2024, at the rack along the wall at the lock-up while the shutter is still half down over the bay. Under the lip of a length on a rack.

**SHE HAS A RACK ALONG THE WALL AT THE LOCK-UP WITH TWO LENGTHS OF TIMBER ON IT AND SHE MEANS TO PUSH THE BACK OF THE FINGERS UP UNDER THE LIP OF THE LOWER LENGTH WITH THE PALM DOWN AND THE WRIST KEPT LOW AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS AT THE OFFICE DOOR FROM THE YARD WITH A FOLDED CLOTH OVER HER ARM.**

That rack has leaned on that wall since the bay got its cover, and lengths have turned over while the frame has kept its ground.

It is a rack of two uprights with arms out front, about waist high, with the lower length sitting on the arms and the upper one stacked on packings above it.

She wants what the lower length sits on, where the timber meets the arm and the light does not reach.

The upper length sits a foot above it on packings as thick as thumbs, with air between the two that smells of pine and rain.

Up under a length the backs of the fingers tell most, because weather marks the tops while packings lift the bottoms clear.

So the back of the fingers goes up under the lip of the lower length, with the palm down and the wrist kept low.

The timber there is cool and firm, with dust thick on the arm and a line of grit where the length meets the wood.

The fingers go up about an inch and stop on something firm. Not timber. A packing, thin and hard, wedged between the length and the arm.

She works the backs along it, and it runs about three inches along the arm and then stops, with the ends cut square where the knife went through.

What it is, is a slip of old lath, put there to lift the length off the arm and let air under it.

The woman who books work arrives at the office door across the yard, folded cloth on her forearm, door closed at her back.

She stood there with the cloth going damp in the air and looked across to the rack and then to the shutter and back again, and she shook the cloth out and folded it again, and then she went back to the office steps with it and left the door shut.

A length on packings sits only on its packings and on nothing else, because the packings lift it clear and air goes all round it.

Pine sits firm on lath while oak crushes it, because pine spreads its weight and oak puts it all on one line.

Pine spreads it because pine is soft enough to bed down onto the packing over its whole width in a month, while oak stays hard on one arris and cuts through.

That is why oak on lath cuts a groove in a year and pine on lath only polishes it, and why the arms here show polish and no groove.

These two lengths are pine, pale and straight, with the grain running true from end to end.

Straight grain carries further than cross grain, because each fibre runs the whole length without a break, and a length with true grain will span two packings without dipping.

Lath packings go soft in a year where water sits on them, because lath is thin and drinks deep, and a soft packing lets the length down onto the arm.

That is why the lower length sits half an inch lower at the south end, where the packing has gone soft and the timber has sunk onto the arm.

Half an inch over six feet is enough to throw water but not enough to roll a tool off, which is why the length looks true from the front and shows its tilt only from the end.

A length that looks true keeps its place on the rack, while one that shows its tilt gets moved to the wall and cut up for packings itself.

She pushes the backs further under to feel how much the packing gives.

It gives nothing at the north end, where the lath is still firm, and a good half inch at the south, where it has gone soft with winter rain off the bay.

What makes the south soft is the drip off the shutter. Water runs down the shutter in rain and blows in under the half-down bay, and the south arm takes it.

---

A rack under a half-down shutter lives half in weather and half out, with the south end in the wet and the north in the dry.

That is why the north packing is firm and pale while the south one is dark and soft, and why the length tilts a finger between the two.

A tilted length throws water to its low end, and the low end drinks it, and each rain makes the tilt a little more.

Softwood drinks at the end ten times faster than at the face, because end grain stands open like straws while face grain lies shut.

That is why a cut end left through winter goes dark a hand back while the face beside it stays pale, and why oil on the end keeps a length twice as long as oil on the face.

Linseed on end grain soaks in deep and goes hard, while linseed on face grain sits on top and goes gummy by summer.

That is why the south end of the lower length has gone dark for about a hand back from the cut, while the rest stays pale.

The cut itself is firm, with the saw marks still showing through the stain, which says the dark is only stain and not rot.

Rot goes soft under the thumb and smells sweet when scraped, while stain stays hard and smells of nothing but timber.

She scrapes the end with a nail and the shavings come off pale and firm, with no soft in them.

Two new packings of hazel would take the tilt out of that, cut square and set under the length where the old ones sit, the southern one a little thicker so that the length comes level again by the summer.

A thicker south packing throws the tilt the other way for a month until the timber beds down, and then the length sits true through summer.

Packings cut square sit firm on the arm over their whole face, while ones cut on the slant rock and work loose when the length is dragged off.

Hazel keeps firm under pine where lath gives, because hazel is close-grained and throws water while lath drinks it.

The uprights are firm in the concrete, with no rock in them and no rust on the bolts through them.

Bolts through uprights go loose when the timber shrinks round them in summer and swell tight again in winter, and each year works them a hair looser.

That is why the nuts here have washers under them as wide as pennies, to spread the bite and keep the timber from crushing under the iron.

The arms are firm where they meet the uprights, with the joints tight and the iron through them gone dark but not thin.

Dark iron keeps its strength while bright thin iron is already half gone, because surface rust guards what is under while polished wear takes it.

She takes the hand out from under the length and brushes the dust off on the side of the rack, and judges the two lengths from the yard by what the stack presents.

Dust on a rack lies thick where the air is still and thin where the shutter moves it, which is why the north arm shows clean where the wind gets under the bay. Cobwebs hang from the upper length to the uprights and break whenever a board slides, then spin again by week end.

From the front the two lengths look level and true, stacked straight on the rack.

From the end the lower one shows its tilt, with the south end a finger below the north.

Two lengths lying level from the front and one of them a finger lower at the end, and the packing that will let go first is the southern, whose lath has gone soft under the drip off the shutter.
