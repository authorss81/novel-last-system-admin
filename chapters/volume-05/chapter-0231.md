# Chapter 0231 — A Schedule

Friday the first of April 2016, from about eleven in the morning, the room at the end of the first-floor corridor in a building by the water in Tideglass, and the corridor.

**A CLAIM FOR MONEY, MADE CORRECTLY IN ANOTHER CITY IN DECEMBER, AND A SCHEDULE, AND THE SECOND OF THE TWO IS THE FIRST THING THAT HAS EVER COME BACK ALONG A DOCUMENT TO THIS BOROUGH THAT NAMES A PERSON BY NAME, BY DATE AND BY PLACE, AND IT IS CORRECT, AND IT NAMES ELEVEN PEOPLE.**

The woman who has the room at the end of that corridor is about fifty-five and is not in on a Sunday and is in on a Friday, and on the first of April she was in at about a quarter to eleven with a heap of somebody else's post on the corner of the desk.

"Ask," she said, when he had got to the door.

"I want to see the one in the drawer."

"You've come a long way to look at a bit of paper."

"I have," said Jonas Mercer, "and I'd still like to see it."

She got it out of the drawer without any difficulty at all, which is the thing about a drawer nobody has ever opened. It is one sheet, folded twice, and it came in a post on Saturday the twenty-sixth of December, and it is a claim for money, made under the sixth clause of a permission signed at both ends, in a city called Alderwick, by a clerk, with a reference, a date, a sum and a signature at the bottom in blue.

"That's the one," she said.

"That's the one."

"It came in the tray under the window downstairs. It went up with the water office's post on the Monday and it sat on a shelf by the filing cabinets for a month because the water office hasn't answered it and isn't going to." She put it down on the desk and squared it, which everybody in this building does. "It's the only thing in nine months that's come back along a piece of paper. Everything else went out and didn't come back. I'd say I know what it is about, and I don't, and I haven't said that to anybody, and there's no reason anybody in this borough would want me to say it."

**AND ON THE DESK, ON TOP OF SOMEBODY ELSE'S POST, THERE WAS A SECOND PIECE OF PAPER THAT HAD COME UP WITH THE FRIDAY TRAY AND HAD NOT BEEN OPENED BY ANYBODY, AND IT WAS THE OTHER THING ENTIRELY, AND NEITHER OF THE TWO IS THE SAME SHAPE AS THE OTHER.**

"May I look at that one?"

"You may," she said. "I haven't opened it and I'm not going to. It came up on the Friday and it's got the yard on the top of it, and the yard is a place I have never heard of, and it's got a list down the side of it."

It came from Penhale, from the shared yard, on a single sheet with a rule across it and a column headed CLAIM, and it is ruled down the side and there are eleven lines of it. In the first column, a date: the fifth of May last year, and then the nineteenth of May, and then the second of June, and then a gap, and then the eleventh of August, and then the second of September, and then the fourth of October, and then the sixteenth of November, and then the twenty-ninth of January, and then the last two in March. In the second column, a place. In the third, in capitals, a name.

Ansell. Brackley. Colley. Danby. Ellery. Farnworth. Gilchrist. Hewitt. Inkster. Jarrett. Kelmar.

In the fourth column, a sum, and against every one of the eleven a sum the yard had already agreed, and the yard had got those right: one hundred and forty, one hundred and fifty-two, one hundred and sixty-three, one hundred and seventy-one, one hundred and sixty-eight, one hundred and seventy-four, one hundred and twelve, one hundred and eighty, one hundred and sixty-six, one hundred and ninety, one hundred and ninety-six.

"That is the same eleven as the ones in the carrier bag," said Jonas Mercer.

"Is it."

"Two of the names on it are two people I have spoken to in this borough in the last fortnight. Nine of the eleven are the ones a woman called Hanna Wray has been keeping in a carrier bag since the August before last, and she does not know that her eleven are on a list in a county she has not been to."

The woman of about fifty-five looked at the sheet for a while.

"Well," she said. "Then that's worse, isn't it."

"I don't know what it is," said Jonas Mercer.

"It's the one I'd have to think about," she said, "because the money one, I can put in a drawer. That one, I've got a woman who kept a carrier bag of them for a year and a half and thought she was being tidy." She put her hand flat on it. "And nobody's asked any of them. That's what I can't get past. It's a list of eleven people who did a good day's work and got paid and went home, and there's not one name on it with a question next to it, and there's not one name on it anybody in this borough has ever asked about, and that's not a crime. That's just a yard doing its job."

"It is a yard doing its job," said Jonas Mercer.

"Then what's the other half."

He did not answer that, because the other half was in a car on a parade and it was not hers and it was not for a room, and because the man who would have been able to hold it was four days a week in an office on the far side of the river.

The man of about thirty-one knows two of the names on that sheet. He has known them since the autumn. He has said so to nobody, including Jonas Mercer, who has not asked him, and who would not have been able to find out which two, and who was not going to ring him on a Friday to ask him a question in a tone.

---

"It's a copy," he said. "That's what it is, and I want to be exact about it, because it is not a demand and it is not a bill and it will be read as both. At that yard, when a crew finishes, they fill in a sheet and sign it, and the office keeps a top copy and a carbon, and the carbon is what comes down here, and the sums on it are the sums that were agreed before anybody involved was born. There is nothing on that sheet that anybody can be sued for and nothing on it anybody can be invoiced for, and there is nobody at the other end of it who is waiting for an answer. That is the thing about it, and I have not been able to put it anywhere."

Nell Ardent was in the corridor with her bag between her feet, looking at the window at the end of it, and she had four of the names written down in her own hand and had not written anything else, and she had not asked him for the other seven and he had not offered them.

"Six dates in the first half of the year and then nothing for three months and then five," she said. "That's not a man deciding. That's a yard filling a column in when a crew signs a bit of paper."

"Yes."

"And the fifth of May last year is the week the water went down."

"Yes."

**AND NELL ARDENT WENT DOWN THE CORRIDOR AT TWELVE WITH A HANDBAG AND A LIST OF ELEVEN NAMES IN IT, AND A DRAWER AT THE END OF THAT CORRIDOR STILL HAS A CLAIM IN IT THAT NOBODY IN THIS BOROUGH CAN ANSWER, AND NEITHER OF THE TWO IS THE SAME SHAPE AS THE OTHER, AND A TRAY UNDER A WINDOW DOWNSTAIRS WAS A TRAY UNDER A WINDOW ALL ALONG AND IS ONE NOW.**
