# Chapter 0476 — The Far Side of That Footway

Monday the fourth of March 2019, and the four mornings on either side of it, from about ten past six until about quarter past seven. Two dogs and about three hundred yard of footway, in Tideglass.

**THAT STREET HAS HAD TWO MEN AND TWO DOGS ON IT AT TEN PAST SIX EVERY MORNING FOR FIVE WEEKS, AND ON THE TWENTY-EIGHTH OF JANUARY THE MAN WHO CAME IN MOST RECENTLY WALKED A HUNDRED YARD THE WRONG WAY OUT OF HIS OWN FRONT DOOR WITH HIS DOG ON THE OFFSIDE, AND THE MAN WHO HAS WALKED THAT WAY FOR TWELVE YEARS WATCHED THE WHOLE OF IT FROM HIS OWN GATE, AND ON THE FOURTH OF MARCH HE CAME OUT AND STOOD ON HIS OWN KERB FOR A MINUTE AND A HALF WHILE THE OTHER MAN'S DOG DID WHAT A DOG DOES, AND NEITHER OF THEM SAID A WORD.**

The dog is called Bess and Bess is twelve, and twelve is old for one.

He has had her since she was a pup that a man on a building site in about 2016 gave him at the end of a job, and he has walked her at the same hour every morning since, and the hour has not moved and neither has the way.

It goes like this. Out of his own front door at ten past six. Down to the corner where that row meets the parade. Along about three hundred yard of parade with the houses on his left, so that the traffic is on her side and she is between him and the traffic and he is on the building side. Past nine front gardens, past the end house, past the bus stop, and then back again, about six in the morning, twice a day, every day, and on Sundays at nine because it is clear earlier and in the dark when it is not.

That is the whole of it. It is the same three hundred yard, at the same hour, on the same side, for twelve years.

The other man came into the row in the second week of January. He is not young and he is not what anybody would call loud, and he has a dog, and he takes the dog out at the same hour, which is the hour everybody in that row takes a dog out, and there are four other dogs in that row that go out at about the same hour because everybody sets an alarm for the same time.

He does it on the other side.

Which means that for about three hundred yard, twice a day, every morning, the two of them are coming towards each other in the middle of that road with about a car between them and nothing else.

---

He noticed it on the second of January and put it down and picked it up about nine times in three weeks.

What he put it down as was the usual thing. Two men with two dogs in one street of about thirty houses, and the dogs are going to end up at each other one morning, and everybody who has ever lived anywhere knows that, and it is not a matter of character.

He was still putting it down on the twenty-eighth of January when the thing that made it impossible to put down happened.

The other man came out of his own door at ten past six on the morning of the twenty-eighth, and he did not go to the near side.

His own front door is nine doors along. The near side of that road goes past his door. The dog went out onto the footway, the way it has gone out every morning since the second of January and every morning since, and the man turned the dog round and walked it — past his own door, past the next one, past the next two — for about a hundred yard, and crossed, and came the other way, facing the traffic with the dog on the offside, so that the dog is between him and the traffic and he is on the traffic side.

Bess did not see it. She was asleep. He was not, and he saw the whole of that hundred yard, and he stood at his own gate and watched a man walk his dog a hundred yard the wrong way on purpose.

He has never said one word to the man about it and he never will, and what is holding him back has nothing to do with the dog.

The reason is this. If he says something — anything, one word, *morning* with a bit in it, or stopping and standing in the road with the dog and looking at him the way men do — then he becomes, in that row, a man who says things. He has been in that row since 2013 and he has been the sort of man who does not say things for the whole of that time, and it is not a boast, it is the arrangement he has got, and it works, and everybody in the row knows which sort he is and lets him be it.

If he opens his mouth about a hundred yard of road he does not get to be that again.

---

There is a woman at number 4 and he has spoken to her about the dog about four times in twelve years, always about the waste bags, which is the only subject two people who own a dog and do not otherwise have anything to say to each other can talk about.

In the second week of December she stopped him at the corner with her hand on Bess's collar.

She said, "You'll be having that man. He's asked me twice where I keep mine."

He said, "Has he."

She said, "Twice. Once on a Sunday and once in the middle of the week, and he wanted to know whether it was the blue bag or the black."

He said, "Right," and went on.

And that was the whole of that conversation, and he has not mentioned it since, and it has sat on him since December, and it is the piece that finished the argument.

Because a man who has just moved into a row does not ask a woman at number 4 twice about the colour of a waste bag unless he has decided he is going to be out on that road every morning and has decided to be ready for it. And a man who has decided to be ready for it has decided it because of something, and there is one thing on that road in the morning.

Bess stopped on the morning of the fourth of March.

She has never stopped there. She has stopped about nine times in twelve years and every one of them has been at a front garden where somebody has had something out that smells, and the other man was about thirty yard behind her at the time, coming the other way along the far side.

He saw Bess go down.

And what the other man did was stop.

Not slow down. Stop. About thirty yard short, on the offside kerb, with his dog standing beside him, and he waited there for as long as it took, which was about a minute and a half, and he did not come up the middle of the road to say anything, and he did not call his dog, and he did not go past.

Then Bess got up and went on, and he went on, and they passed each other about nine foot apart, and neither man said anything, and the whole of it took about four minutes including the minute and a half.

He came in at about quarter past seven and put the dog on the floor and stood in his own kitchen and thought about it for longer than he has thought about most things.

And what he has arrived at, standing there, is that the man is not being careful. A man being careful keeps to the far side. A man being careful crosses the road, or comes back after he has passed, or does not come out at all. This man came the whole hundred yard the other way and then stood on a kerb for a minute and a half and let the thing be over, and that is not caution, and it is not a man who is worried about a dog.

And he cannot get any further than that, and he is not going to get any further, because getting further would mean walking across that road, and at ten past six with about nine people on that road, a man who has been the one who says nothing for twelve years does not stop being that in the middle of a morning in March.

**ON THE FIFTH OF MARCH BESS WENT DOWN AGAIN AT THE SAME PLACE AND THE OTHER MAN STOPPED ON THE SAME KERB FOR ABOUT THE SAME TIME, AND THE MAN WHO WAS WATCHING FROM HIS OWN KITCHEN SAW ALL OF IT AND DID NOT GO DOWN.**
