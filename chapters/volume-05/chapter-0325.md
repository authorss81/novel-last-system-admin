# Chapter 0325 — Across a Gate

Sunday the twenty-eighth of May 2017, from about ten in the morning, and again on Thursday the twenty-ninth of June 2017, from about half past four in the afternoon, off a parade in Tideglass.

**A WHITE VAN WAS ACROSS A GATE IN A YARD BEHIND A PARADE ON A SUNDAY MORNING AND WAS ACROSS A DIFFERENT GATE ON THAT PARADE A MONTH LATER ON A THURSDAY AFTERNOON, AND NEITHER TIME DID ANYBODY ASK ANYBODY ANYTHING AND NEITHER TIME DID ANYBODY FIND OUT WHOSE IT WAS.**

That parade is nine shops and a shuttered unit at the end of it, and there is a service passage behind the eight of them that are let, running the length of them off the alley, and every one of those eight has a gate on that passage, and the passage is the width of a van and a half.

On the Sunday morning the man in his thirties was in the yard behind the sixth of them, taking an old washing machine out of a lean-to that was coming down, and it was gone and up on the tailgate by about a quarter past ten, and the van was nose in against the back of the lean-to with its tail about four foot inside the gate.

He came round it with his bag, got into the cab, put his hand on the starter, and could not get the nose any further forward because the lean-to post was in the way. He reversed, came out crooked the first time and put his nearside wing against a downpipe on the way, and did it again more slowly and got the back of the van square with the gate and stopped.

There was a white van across the outside of that gate.

It was nose on to the pavement and half its width over that pavement and about two foot of its offside over the line of a wall. It was mid-sized, white, with nothing written on the side of it, and there was nobody in it and nobody near it, its driver's door shut and its sliding door shut, and it had been standing there long enough for the pavement under the near side of it to be dry in a ring and wet in the middle.

He got out, walked up to it, and put his hand flat on the bonnet. It was cold.

That is a warm morning and it had been in that sun long enough for the metal to have been in it, and the ring says it has been there since before nine.

---

He waited.

You cannot get a van out of a yard with a foot of road on each side of a gate, and you cannot get it through a foot of a wall, and there is no second gate on that passage, because a passage behind a parade has one way in and it is for the vans of the people who work there.

The parade was shut. It is shut on a Sunday and it had been shut nine hours and would be until about eight the next morning, and the man who puts shutters up does not come in on a Sunday because there is nothing to put up.

He went up the passage and looked at it from the other side. A yard gate further along was open. He stood in it and looked back down the passage, and the white van was exactly where it had been, and about a quarter of it was over the pavement, and a person can get past that on the alley side by turning sideways, which is not a thing a person with a sack barrow does.

He was there about an hour and a half. At about a quarter to twelve he went and put his thumb on the little catch under the handle of the driver's door, which he has never in his life found out what is behind, and nothing came of it.

There is a thing you can do with a van like that, and he has done it once, in a car park in this town, in the dark, with a man helping him. It is not a thing you do to a stranger's van on a Sunday outside a parade with people at an upstairs window.

So he went back into the yard, moved his own van out — a small one, and it came out of that yard in a quarter of an hour, forward at a crawl with a wheel on a bucket, and up the passage and out into the alley — and did the rest of that day's work on foot.

He had hinges on a gate at the other end of the parade at about half past one, and it wanted a screwdriver and a wedge and about three quarters of an hour, and he did it standing in a gateway with his bag on the kerb while a woman came out and went in four times with her shopping. And he had a thing at the top of Marsh Lane at about three, which was a look at a washer that did not need replacing, and he walked to it, and it is about a mile and a quarter.

He got his van back into that yard at about half past four.

The gate was clear and the white van was not there, and the ring on the pavement was the same ring of dry inside a wet edge it had been at ten o'clock, and there was no mark on the wall of the sixth shop, and nothing on that van had been driven into anything on the first attempt but the wall itself had not shifted.

He got in and sat in the cab a minute before he started it.

He had lost about five hours of a day he is paid by the job for. He is not paid by the hour, there is no timesheet, nobody in that parade knows there was a van in that passage at all, and there is nobody in this borough he could ask whose it was.

---

On the Thursday afternoon, at about half past four, the woman who works in a shop went out of the front of that shop into the alley with a sack for the bin store, which is thirty yard along that alley and round a corner, and there was a white van with nothing written on the side of it across the back gate of that shop.

It was in the yard and not on the pavement, and it had gone in nose first and turned round in there, which told her it had not come to be left.

Her gate is eight foot wide and the van had four foot of that in it.

She stood in the alley and looked at it. It did not move, and there was nobody in the kitchen at the back of that shop, because the man who cooks in there is not in until six.

What comes into that shop on a Thursday at about half past four is bread, milk and a bit of card for the shelves, and it comes in on a trolley a man wheels from the alley. He had come at about ten past four and gone at ten past, and it had all been done and the shelf card was in.

The next one was at half past five, and the man with the trolley could not get his front wheels through a gate with four foot of van in it. He stood in the alley about two minutes and then backed the trolley out to the corner and phoned.

She did not hear what she said. She was at her own counter with the sack in it and the door open and he was talking in the corner of the shop.

He came in and pushed the trolley to the middle of the floor and stood there.

"I can't get down your yard."

"I can see that."

"I can put it on the pavement."

"No," said the woman who works in a shop. "You can't put it on the pavement, because that pavement goes round the corner and there's about nine inches of it, and if you put six boxes down there nobody can walk past them and there is nowhere else for them to go."

"I can come back at six."

"You can come back at six," said the woman who works in a shop, "and I'd rather you did than carried them round and put them on that alley."

The man with the trolley stood in the middle of that shop a while with his cap in his hands and then said the thing about leaving it, and she said it was not a thing to be phoned about, and he said it was not the first time, and she said she had not been here the first time.

He went out, got a sheet off his own trolley, put the six boxes on it, and carried them in on it, one at a time, in two goes. She asked him which were heavy and he said the ones with milk in them, and he put them on the floor by the cold room, and she put the two heavy ones on the cold room counter and the rest in the bay, and he went.

It took about a quarter of an hour and none of it was about the van.

She went out into that yard twenty minutes later, when he had gone, and put her hand on the side of it the way anybody puts a hand on the side of a thing, and it was warm, and the back of it was facing the gate and the front of it was facing the lean-to, and the key was not in it.

She shut the gate, which goes on a latch, with a bolt on the inside that is on a drop, and she went back in and shut the shop, and she has not been in that yard since except to take her own bin out, and the bin is on the pavement.

The man in a fleece came in at about five for a paper and stood at the counter and said, as he does, that there is always one.

"I have had a van across that gate," said the woman who works in a shop.

"Course you have."

"I have not, in my life, had a van across that gate before."

"You'd not be the first," said the man in a fleece. "There's a fella comes down that alley one day a week with a tipper and puts it over two gates at once and never knows he's done it. Somebody had a bike chained to my front rail last winter and it was gone by the time I got back from the tip."

"I am not going to knock on anybody's door about it," said the woman who works in a shop.

"You'd not find the house anyway."

"No," she said. "And I am not going to stand in that alley looking at every plate that comes up it. That is not a job. And the first one I stopped, I would have to stop the next one, and in a month I am the woman at the end of that passage that stops vans, and there is no coming back off that."

The man in a fleece put his paper on the counter and looked at her.

"You've had a bad day."

"I've had a man with six boxes on a sheet and I've had a van in my yard," said the woman who works in a shop, "and that's the day, and neither of those is a thing I can ring anybody about."

And that was the whole of that Thursday, and the man in a fleece took his paper and went.

She has not said to the man in his thirties that there was a van in that yard. She has not said it to the woman who runs the shop at the end of that parade, because that is not the shop with the yard. She did not ask the man with the trolley whether he had seen whose it was when he came in at ten past four, and he was gone.

And the man in his thirties, if anybody had told him, would not have asked her any more than she asked the man with the trolley, and neither of them would have said a word about it if they had, because you do not have to be a person who is on nothing in this town to know that saying a thing out loud in a passage behind nine shops is how a thing stops being a thing that happened to a person and starts being a thing that people say.

**A MAN SPENT FIVE HOURS OF A SUNDAY CARRYING BAGS AROUND A PARADE BECAUSE A WHITE VAN WAS IN HIS GATE, AND A WOMAN SPENT A QUARTER OF AN HOUR CARRYING SIX BOXES ON A SHEET OUT OF HER OWN YARD BECAUSE A WHITE VAN WAS IN HER GATE, AND NEITHER OF THEM ASKED ONE PERSON A QUESTION.**
