# Chapter 0938 — Into The Board Step

Tuesday the tenth of September 2024, at the back boarding of number ten with the wash on the line. Into a step between boards.

**THE BACK OF THAT HOUSE IS BOARDED AND THE BOARDS LIE ONE OVER ANOTHER, AND SHE MEANS TO PUT THE HAND INTO THE SHADOW LINE WHERE ONE CROSSES THE ONE BELOW IT, THE FINGERS SHUT AND CROOKED AT THE FIRST KNUCKLE, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THAT LINE RUNS AND WHAT STANDS AT THE FAR END OF IT.**

The boarding went up in eight or nine days at the end of April, in the year the roof was done, and it has been repainted twice since while the boards have kept their steps. The back of that house faces the only thing in the row with nothing built close to it, which is why it was boarded at all.

It is boarding of softwood about five inches wide and three-quarters of an inch thick, sawn on all four sides and set horizontally with the lower edge of each board covering the upper edge of the one below by about an inch. The step is the shadow line between one board and the one under it, and that line is the one part of that boarding nobody has ever had a hand in.

---

A step like that one is made by the thickness of the board and by nothing else. Into that shadow it goes, the fingers together and bent at the first joint, so that the fingertips run along the back of the step while the knuckles stay out in the air where a person can see them.
The upper board's lower edge is blunt along its whole length, and it has been blunt since the day it was sawn, and the arris of a sawn softwood board is the blunter thing than the arris of a dressed stone.

The step runs the whole length of the back wall without a break. Where the boards cross the corner of the house the step gets deeper, because a board that turns a corner has to be a little longer on the inside than on the outside, and the extra length shows as about half an inch of extra overlap.

At the far end, where the last board stops short of the corner, the step closes over the head of the board below it, and standing up in the shadow at that head is a bead of old white paint about the thickness of a boot lace.

---

Weatherboarding is a trade of its own and the step is the whole of it. A board that lies over the one below sheds water downwards and a board that butts against a board sheds water sideways, and sideways is where a wall behind it cannot breathe.
Overlap puts every drop on the outside of the board below and lets it fall clear of the wall, which is the reason an overlapped board is still doing its job after sixty years and a board that has been screwed tight with a gap behind it is doing none at all.

The step is set by the nail, and the nail in a boarded wall goes about half an inch above the lower edge of the board and about two inches in from the head of it, so that the head of the nail is covered by the board above and cannot come out through the face. Nails put higher than that show their heads in the weather, and a head that shows in the weather goes orange in two wet seasons.

Boards are sawn to a pattern rather than to a width, and the pattern is what makes the step the same on every house done in the same week. The head of one board is cut at a very slight taper, so that the board below it draws up to it as it dries and the step does not open along the head.
A board cut square at the head goes four points out of true in its first wet winter and the step at the head of it opens into a line you can put a finger into, and that comes out of the sawing and not out of the wall.

The whole of the boarding on that back wall was sawn from one tree, which is why the colour of it is even and why the two boards at the corner where the wall turns are a shade paler than the rest.
The sapwood went in at the corners because the man worked round the house in one direction and ran out of the good side of the log at the fourth corner, and the pale boards ended up on the two corners, where the weather is least and where anything left over has to be put.

Each board on that back wall is fixed with three nails and has been fixed again once at the head, because the head of the boarding had come loose and a man came round and put four more nails into it rather than four fewer. Those four are the only thing about the boarding that has been done in thirty years and they are the only nails on it that show at all.

That bead of paint is not paint which went on after the boards went up. It is paint which came off a board above it, because the two boards touch along that head and the paint between them was pressed into a ridge as the boards were pushed against one another. That ridge has been squashed flat by thirty years of the boards moving a fraction each summer, and it now stands out of the head of the board like a welt on a coat.

What is on the lower board's edge all the way along is a line of grit, and the grit is not from the yard. It comes off the top of the boards above it, which take the weather and dry out and shed, and the shed of five or six boards all at once is a fall of fine stuff that goes down every step on that wall and stops wherever a step is deep enough to keep it.

The steps at the two ends of the back wall are deeper than the steps in the middle, because the middle of a boarded wall is carried on three studs and the ends on one, and a board nailed at one end only lifts at that end whenever the wall behind it moves. So the line of grit is heaviest at the corners and thinnest over the middle, and it is why the corner steps are the ones a hand can go furthest into.

Her sister crosses the back lawn towards the washing line with the sheets off the line in her two arms, and she does not turn in at the wall.

"She has brought them in before this."

"She has brought them in wetter than this."

"Then I will leave them until the wind is off it."

"I will leave them where they are."

She goes on towards the side passage with the sheets across her arms and does not look along the wall, and the boarding is eight feet behind her the whole time she is crossing that lawn.

What went into words was the wash and the wind and nothing else at all, and the hand was out of the step and down at her side before her sister came round the corner of the shed, and the beads and the nail and the shaft of that step did not enter into any of it.

A line of grit in a step is not dirt that somebody left. It is the falling of everything the boards above have shed since they went up, and it is one board's shed going down onto the next, and where the step is deep the grit stays and where the step is shallow it goes on to the one below. Over sixty years that line builds up to the depth of a boot's welt and then washes out again at the corners in a hard winter and builds up once more.

She puts the hand flat on the boarding above the step to feel the step from outside, and the two are the same width there and the step is only there from the inside.

The step keeps its depth along the whole of that back wall, keeps its line of grit and keeps the welt of paint standing up at the head of the final board, and it will go on being exactly what it is and nothing will ever come out of it that was not put there when the boarding went up, given that the boards are fixed where they were fixed and the wall behind them moves about as much as a wall of that kind ever moves.
