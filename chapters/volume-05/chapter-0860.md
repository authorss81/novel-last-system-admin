# Chapter 0860 — The Thumb Across The Top Face

Monday the thirteenth of November 2023, at the heap by the gate at number twelve from the time the shadow has come off it until the light has gone. One stone, and a thumb across the top of it.

**SHE MEANS TO TAKE ONE STONE OFF THE TOP OF THE EDGING HEAP AT NUMBER TWELVE, RUB ALONG ITS TOP FACE WITH HER THUMB AND READ IT FOR THE TOOLING, AND PUT IT BACK WHERE IT CAME OFF WITHOUT PICKING THE GRIT OUT OF ITS HOLLOWS, AND HER SISTER GOES OUT OF THE BACK GATE AND BACK IN AGAIN.**

Six feet of edging came off that bed in the spring and went into the heap against the wall, and there have been eleven good stones in it and five bad ones ever since, and the loop of blue string is still over the top of the heap where it was put back in the summer.

You cannot tell them apart by looking. That was settled on Saturday and there is no argument left on it. Eleven stones have a dressed arris along the top and five have none, and all sixteen are lying the wrong way up in a heap with their bed faces showing, and a bed face is the dullest thing a stone has.

What she wants to know this week is not which stones are in the heap. She has known roughly what is in that heap since the day it was barrowed in and she will look at all sixteen properly on a day with a trowel that works.

What she wants to know is what one of them is.

---

The one she takes off the top is a long one, about the length of her forearm and an inch and a half across, and it is on the top of the heap at the wall end and it comes off with the fingers because nothing is packed in against it.

She carries it the way she would carry a plate, a hand at each end, and turns it over once, and puts the top face towards her at about the height of her waist.

The face is grey and it has a skin on it, six months of weather, and there is grit in the two hollows along the middle of it where the rain has sat and where the grit off the flags has got into the dip.

She rubs it with the pad of her thumb.

Not across the marks. Along them, first, to find out which way they run, and then across them, which is the way you feel for them.

The thumb finds them in about three inches of travel.

They are about a quarter of an inch apart, running in one direction across the face, from the arris down towards the near edge, and they are shallow and they are perfectly even, and the pad of her thumb going across them at right angles goes bump bump bump bump and knows every one of them.

That is a dressed face. Those are claw chisel marks and a dressed arris is cut from the same piece with the same tool in the same sitting, so the marks and the arris are at the same angle to each other, and a stone with an arris and marks that do not agree with it is a stone somebody has dressed an edge onto after it had been used.

The grain under them runs the same way. That is what decides which way a stone goes down and it is why the marks and the grain and the arris are all read together and not one at a time. The grain in a stone runs along the length of the bed it came out of, and a tool follows the grain and cannot cut across it without jumping, so the marks are a second opinion about the long way round.

She puts her thumb back on it and goes over the whole face, and there are the marks from one end to the other, and there is not one interruption in them.

She goes back into the heap and finds one of the other two.

That one is half the length and about twice the thickness, and the face of it has no marks on it anywhere. What it has instead is one long swell running the length of it, about the depth of a thumb, with the grit settled in the low side of the swell and the high side of it scoured pale.

That is a split face, and a thumb can tell it in a second, because a swell is a swell and the pad goes over it in one movement and there is nothing to count. A split stone comes out of the ground the way a potato comes out of a bank. It breaks along the grain and the break goes round a bed of flint and the flint is still half in it. There is no arris on that stone to dress and there never was one, and it is one of the five.

She puts it back where it was and puts her thumb on the third.

Two dressed and one not, out of three, and there are five not among the sixteen, which is about what a thumb has to be worth before anybody gives it an hour.

The eye is no good at this and it is not the eye's fault. An eye looks at a stone for a second and takes the general light off it and the general shape, and after the first stone and the second stone the eye is looking at a heap of stones and not at faces. The thumb has no opinion about the heap. The thumb is put on the thing it is pointed at and the rest of the yard does not exist.

Her sister goes out of the back gate in the middle of the day with her shopping bag and comes in again by the same gate in under ten minutes, and the gate bangs, and that is the whole of that.

She does not pick the grit out of the hollows.

She stands with the stone in one hand and puts the other thumb into the first hollow and tries it, and the grit is fine and it is dry and it is in there to the depth of a fingernail and it goes nowhere.

A stone has hollows in it because it was quarried out of a bed and has been about the world ever since, and the hollows are not going to be filled in and they are not going to be ground out either. There is no tool in that shed that would take the grit out of a hollow without going into the stone, and the only things that would go into the stone are a nail and the end of a cold chisel and a great deal of patience, and the arris along the top of that stone would be the first thing to go.

And the grit wants to be there anyway.

When this heap is laid in the spring, every stone in it goes down on sand and every stone in it has grit in the hollows, and that grit goes into the bed with the sand and it is the grit that stops the stone shifting its first winter. A hollow scoured out and dried is a hollow that will take water. A hollow with a skin of fine dry grit in it is a hollow that will not.

So she does not clean it, and she does not leave it either, and she puts the stone back on the top of the heap in the same line along the grain it was lying in, which is how a heap gets put back together by hand, and she puts the other two that had been resting against it back against it, because a stone taken off a heap and put back on a heap in a different place is not a stone put back.

She goes round the heap after that with the same thumb, standing at the wall, and finds dressed faces on about half of what she puts her hand on, and not on the rest, and that is no more than she expected and it is enough for a Monday.

The lines are all one way on that heap, or nearly. There are two or three with the marks running the wrong way for their length, and those are the ones that will have to go in at the ends where nobody looks at them.

Her thumb comes away grey off the top of that stone and the lines are still under the grey, and they will be under it tomorrow and under the white of it in February, and the only thing that is going to take them off that face is about four inches of the front bed turned over in the spring, which is a different job in a different month.
