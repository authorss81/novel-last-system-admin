# Chapter 0867 — The Step In The Kerb

Wednesday the sixth of December 2023, at the kerb by the bay at the lock-up from first light until the office glass has gone dark. How wide that kerb is, and how wide it stops being.

**THE KERB ALONG THE MOUTH OF THE BAY AT THE LOCK-UP IS WIDER IN THE MIDDLE THAN IT IS AT EITHER END AND SHE MEANS TO SET BOTH HANDS AT THE TWO ENDS OF IT AND WALK THEM ALONG UNTIL THEY MEET, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK COMES OUT OF THE OFFICE DOOR AND STANDS ON THE OFFICE STEP.**

The kerb runs along the front of the bay for about nine feet and it is not one piece. It is three.

The two at the ends are the ordinary ones, a strip of concrete about eleven inches wide with a square arris on the top edge, and they came from the same place as the rest of the kerb along the frontage, which is all one make and one width and has been for twenty years. Eleven inches is the width of a kerb because eleven inches is the width of a footpath and a kerb is the edge between a footpath and a road, and every kerb in this country is as wide as the thing it carries.

The one in the middle is not that.

It is a foot four. And it stands about a quarter of an inch higher than the two it sits between, so that a wheel run across the middle of the bay mouth climbs and drops over three inches of its length, and a barrow crossing it in the dark knows about it.

You cannot get your hands round a kerb from the yard side, because the back of it is against the wall, so she goes down off the flags onto the frontage side of it and puts her hands round it from underneath instead, thumb on the front face and fingers on the back, which is the only way a hand gets round a thing that is set against something.

Then she walks them along.

That is the whole of the technique and it is not a thing anybody teaches. A kerb is not a smooth run. It is a strip of concrete with a square arris on the top of it, and the arris is the part that takes your hands as you pass. She goes along it with her hands low, over the front face and under the back, thumbs on the top edge and fingers on the underside, and about four feet along the thumbs come up onto something flat.

The flat is the top of the middle piece, and it is flat because it has been walked on and wheeled on and swept against for years, and a quarter of an inch of arris on a kerb is gone in about five years.

So the first half of the walk is square arris under the thumbs. The second half is round arris under the thumbs, and her hands meet in the middle of it a quarter of an inch higher than they started, and her palms know that before her eyes do.

She does it again from the other end, because a walk in one direction can be wrong by a hand's width and she wants to know where the step is, not whether there is one.

It is in the same place. The step runs diagonally across the width of the kerb, from the low back corner to the high front corner, because that piece was set with a toe of a yard wide and came out of true when it was levered into its bed, and everything that has crossed it since has crossed it downhill.

She gets up off the frontage and stands on the flags looking down at it.

Nine feet of kerb, eleven inches, eleven inches, a foot four in the middle.

The bay mouth is about three feet six wide where the opening is, and the barrow goes over the middle of it, and the middle of it is the piece that is a foot four. Which means the piece in the middle of that kerb is the piece that has had every wheel in this yard over it for the length of the bay, and it is the piece that was the wrong width when it went in, and it is the piece that was chosen because the two ends were already down and the short one would not close the gap.

That is the whole story of it. There was no decision. Somebody was short one length of kerb on a wet afternoon and there was a foot four piece lying on a pallet behind the office and it fitted, and it was set, and it has fitted ever since.

And here is the thing she did not expect, which is that the step in it has a direction.

The quarter of an inch is not on the kerb evenly. It is highest where the piece is narrowest and it dies away towards both ends, so the middle of that run is not a step at all but a shallow hump about three inches long in the middle of nine feet. A wheel goes up over it and comes down off it, and it goes over it every single time, and it has been going over it for the whole of the life of that bay. There is no wear on the concrete of the kerb anywhere else on that frontage, because nothing else on that frontage is ever crossed by anything, and there is a yard's width of wear along nine feet of kerb in the one place it can be.

That accounts for the line in the bay floor.

There is a dish in the concrete of the bay about five inches wide running from the kerb into the bay for about four feet, and it goes down and then comes back up, and it is the same line as the swept heap at the foot of the rack leg, because the rack leg is stood at the end of it and everything that sweeps that bay sweeps along that line and pushes the grit out of the low place and leaves it in a crescent at the edge.

She does not touch any of that. The kerb is a thing about widths. The floor is a thing about grit and a broom and the place where water goes, and neither of them has anything to do with the other except that they are both in the same bay and one of them is the reason for the other.

---

The office door has stood open on the latch since the summer because the glass on that side has been out since the spring, and it stands open on this cold morning with the latch not down, and she does not go through it.

The woman who books work comes out of it about eleven and stands on the office step.

She was asked whether the window glass had gone up that morning and the answer was that it had not. Her view was that it does not want shutting in this, and that view was agreed with. She put it that the office has been cold since that glass came out, and it has. The fan on the wall, she put it, does nothing whatever, and it does nothing whatever, and she went back inside, and the door was open on the latch again a minute later.

She went back inside without looking at the frontage, and the frontage is where the kerb is, and neither of them was in any doubt about that.

Nobody has ever lifted it either, which is the other half of the answer and the reason it is still there. A foot four piece of concrete with three inches of its length bedded and the whole weight of a barrow over it comes up in about twenty minutes with a bar and a bit of swearing, and then it has to go back, and there is nowhere to put it but along the frontage, where it will not sit square either.

So it stays in the mouth of the bay, being a foot four in a run of eleven, and everybody in this place goes round it and nobody has ever gone to the trouble of finding out whether it is even the right profile for a kerb, which it may or may not be and which is not a question anybody can settle with two hands.

---

Eleven inches at one end and eleven inches at the other and a foot four standing in the middle of it, and a quarter of an inch of arris gone off the top of that middle piece in the five years since it went in, and nothing that has ever been set against that kerb has been square to it, and nothing standing in this yard ever will be.
