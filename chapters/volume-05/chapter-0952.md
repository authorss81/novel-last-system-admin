# Chapter 0952 — At The Lifted Corner

Thursday the seventh of November 2024, at the corner of the stanchion plate at the unit with the rain coming in off the roof. At a lifted corner.

**A STANCHION IN THE YARD OF THE UNIT STANDS IN A PLATE WELDED TO ITS FOOT, AND THAT PLATE IS SET IN A BED OF OLD MORTAR WITH ONE CORNER STANDING A QUARTER OF AN INCH OFF THAT BED, AND THE PALM GOES DOWN ON THE TOP FACE OF THE PLATE WITH THE FINGERS HANGING OFF THE FAR EDGE INTO THE CORNER UNDERNEATH AND THE WRIST PUSHES, TO FIND OUT HOW DEEP THAT CORNER IS AND WHAT IS UNDER THE PLATE.**

The stanchion is four feet of steel tube with a rail on it at about three, and the rail on it does nothing whatever except stop a barrow, which is the only job it has ever had and the only job it is likely to have. It stands between the store door and the office door to stop a barrow going the wrong way across that corner of flags, and it was set up in the spring of the year before last on a plate of quarter-inch steel.

The plate is a square of about seven inches with a fillet weld all round where the tube meets it, and it has been painted once since it went up, which is why the colour of it is not the colour it came out of a shop in. The bed of mortar under it was laid by the same man on the same morning and has gone hard and gone pale in the ordinary way.

The corner in question is the far one, at the edge of the plate where the flags slope away towards the channel. The palm goes down on the top face of the plate, the fingers hang off its far edge into the corner underneath, and the wrist pushes so that the fingers are driven by the wrist alone and not by the arm.

The corner is a quarter of an inch deep at the point of it and shuts to nothing about three inches along each of the two edges, which is the shape of something that has been lifted at a point and has not been put down square again.

Inside the corner the bed of mortar has cracked and gone out of it over about two square inches, and what is under the corner is not mortar but flag, the top of the flag gone pale where the mortar that was on it has left. A bed like that is laid dry and not mixed wet. It is tipped under the plate, spread out with a trowel, the plate set on it, and the bed struck off level with the top of the plate all round, so that the plate sits in a solid bed of lime and sand with nothing hollow under any part of it.

A plate bedded on mortar is bedded so that the plate cannot get at the flag. Steel and a flag have nothing in common, and a steel plate resting straight on a stone flag will find the high places first and stand on those and leave air under the rest of itself, which is where the water goes.

Mortar under a plate is the answer to that, and the whole point of it is that it is packed and not poured, so that it fills the underside of the plate exactly instead of lying on the high places and leaving hollows under the rest.

Where the corner of that plate stands off, the mortar did its work and then lost it. Old lime laid thin enough at the edge of a bed goes crumbly in the way that old lime always does, and a quarter of an inch of movement anywhere under a plate will find that edge first, because an edge is the one part of a bed that nothing on two sides of it keeps in.

Something lifted that corner once and let it down again without putting it back, and the plate has not been straight since. Nothing about the plate is twisted and nothing about it is bent, either of them. The fillet weld at the foot of the tube is continuous and the tube above it is plumb to a nail's width across four feet, because a corner of a plate coming up a quarter of an inch is a small thing beside the thickness of the plate.

There is a second stanchion four feet away at the other side of the same corner of flags and it is on a plate in the same way, and that one has not been lifted at any corner because the flags under it are level and the flag under the first one is the lowest of the four.

The paint on that plate is green and old and brittle over the whole of it, and it has gone back off the top of the corner lip to the bare steel, which is bright there and rusted in the angle behind.

The channel along that wall is the low point of the whole yard, and every bit of rain that falls on the store roof finishes its run there, so that corner of flags is the wettest piece of ground in the yard and has been since the store was built.

The bed of mortar round the other three corners is intact and has not moved, and along the lifted corner it is brittle in the way lime is brittle, being a lime with nothing in it at all, while the rest of it is hard enough to take a heel without marking, which is what a lime bed does when it has been twenty months under a plate.

The flag the plate stands on is one of the yard flags and it has not moved, and it is the flag at the channel edge, which is a flag of a different make from the rest and about an inch and a half thick, and the ones on either side of it have not moved either. The whole of that corner of the yard is level apart from a quarter of an inch of plate at one corner of it.

---

She arrives out of the next yard through the gate in the dividing wall, carrying a coat over one arm, and the gate swings against the wall behind her and stays where it lands.

She gets three steps onto this side and stops there, back to the opening and facing along the wall. The two yards are one yard with a wall and a gate in the middle of it, and the flags run straight on across the gap, so there is a single stretch of light in it and she is standing in the middle of that.

The fingers came out of that corner before she was through the gate, and the corner stayed as it was for as long as she stood there, which was long enough for the coat to come off her arm and go back on again.

When she goes she goes back out into the next yard, and the gate stays where the swing left it, and the flags on this side are as dry as the flags on that side.

The rain comes off the store roof along the channel and across the flags towards the corner, and it goes past the plate without stopping at it. The water that has been going past that corner for twenty months has not moved it a sixteenth of an inch.

The plate keeps its seven inches and its quarter-inch thickness and its fillet weld, and the mortar that came out of the lifted corner is a dust the colour of chalk lying in the crack beside it. The stanchion stands on that plate plumb and the rail on it is the rail it was put up with.

That corner of that plate is a quarter of an inch proud at one point and level along the rest of three edges, and the stanchion above it is plumb to a nail's width across four feet, and the bed of old lime round the other three corners is hard enough to take a heel without marking, and nothing about that corner is touched again, with a view to no purpose beyond the weather getting at it in the ordinary way.
