# Chapter 0299 — The First One

Tuesday the fourteenth of February 2017, from about ten past eight in the morning, the top of Marsh Lane in Tideglass, and a bus shelter.

**HE ASKED IT AGAIN IN THE OPEN AIR AT HALF PAST EIGHT IN THE MORNING AT THE TOP OF THAT LANE AND HE WAS TOLD THE SAME THING HE WAS TOLD IN THE SUMMER, AND A WOMAN SAID OUT LOUD ON A PAVEMENT THAT SHE HAD HAD THAT ONE OFF HIM BEFORE, AND NEITHER FACT CHANGED ANYTHING.**

The lorry that goes to the shop that sells radios has not been down that lane since about half past six and the man who runs that shop had come up on foot at about ten past eight to see whether it had come down the Cleeve road instead and gone past, which it has done twice this month.

There was ice on that bit of hard at the top of the lane and about two degrees and a wind coming up the low end of it off the water, and the sun was up over the roofs of the bank and doing nothing, and the bus shelter at the top of the lane had about six people in and out of it inside four minutes.

The man in a fleece going to the works was at the far end of the shelter with his hood down and his hands in his pockets and had been there since about ten to eight.

The woman who works in a shop came up that lane at about a quarter past eight with her coat buttoned to the neck and went to the shelter and stood at the open side of it, and the man from the parade was about thirty foot from the shelter on the open bit of hard with his hands in his pockets, and he said it.

He said it at nobody, out loud, the way he has said it four times now.

"Somebody in this town is going to be able to say which one of the things we did once is the first one that gets proved," said the man from the parade. "And it isn't going to be anybody from a council because there's no council. And it isn't going to be the men who did them, because the men who did them have gone. So it's going to be a piece of paper. Somebody's going to find a piece of paper one day and it will be the right one, and I want to be in the room when it happens and I don't care what it's about."

Nobody answered him for about ten seconds, and a bus came up the lane and went past the shelter and turned at the bottom.

"It's the register," said the man in a fleece.

The man from the parade turned round.

"It's the book," said the man in a fleece. "The one they keep by the water, with the entries in it. Because it goes back years and it's got everything one under the other with the dates on, and anybody that says it's the day it's never a day, it's a book. It's a book with a date at the top of every page. That's what gets proved. Everything else is a thing somebody did. That's a thing somebody wrote down in the sequence it happened."

"That's the one," said the man from the parade. "That's exactly the one. Who said that first."

"You did," said the man in a fleece. "You said that in the summer, down the shop, about the time the heat went off. It was the first one anybody said and it's the only one of the lot of them that's got a date on it in ink."

---

The woman who works in a shop was standing at the open side of that shelter and had heard all of it.

"That's the one off an aunt," she said.

The man in a fleece looked over.

"That's what it came off," said the woman who works in a shop. "It came off your aunt, and your aunt got it off a man that was on a lorry in July, and you have said that on that parade and in that shop and in a room off a parade about nine times since the summer, and it is the same answer every time and I have never once seen anybody answer it."

"I've not said it in a room off a parade," said the man in a fleece.

"You said it in a room off a parade," said the woman who works in a shop, "because I was in it, and so was the man from your shop, and so were two other people, and you were on the wall saying it was a book with a date on it."

The man in a fleece put his hands further into his pockets and said, "Well. I've said it outside more than you've said it in, and it's the same answer, and it isn't going to change because I've said it twice in two places."

Nobody argued with him about that and nobody said he was right either.

At the open end of the hard, about two minutes down the lane from where any of that was happening, the woman with a clipboard was standing with a board on a folding easel and a rubber band round the top of it, and she was not doing anybody's business, and the man in a fleece looked down the lane at her and then walked down to her across the ice on the hard, because his bus was not due for a few minutes and he had been going to ask her something anyway.

"Bus or people," he said, when he got there.

"People," said the woman with a clipboard. "Two-way, in and out, at the door. The course runs to the end of April and then it stops, and I am doing it because I have never in my life counted a bus stop and it seemed a silly thing to be asked for."

"Do you want a coffee or something for standing in it."

"I've got a flask," said the woman with a clipboard, and did not take her eyes off the lane, and he went back up to the shelter.

---

There were about nine of them in that hall at about half past eight that night, and the meter was on, and not one of them was in there for a matter.

The woman who works in a shop had her coat still on and was standing at the end of the table with it buttoned, and she put it on the table between them and said, "It went round the top of Marsh Lane at half eight this morning."

The man from the parade said, "What did."

"Which one you're on," said the woman who works in a shop. "The book. The register by the water. It went round that shelter in the wind, out loud, at about ten past eight in the morning, and the man going to the works in the fleece said it, and I stood there and told him he'd said it nine times since the summer, and he said it was the same answer and it wasn't going to change because he'd said it twice in two places."

Nobody said anything about it.

"The sheet on the table is a sheet," said the woman who works in a shop. "That has not changed either."

The man in his forties looked down at the boards of that table for a while and then at the window.

The man from the parade was at the end of the table nearest the fire with a cup in his hands and did not say a word about any of the four of them, and he had come early, and he stayed longer than he usually does, and he did not say the thing out loud again that evening, and two of the people at that table noticed that he had not and neither of them mentioned it.

**AND THE WIND GOT UP OFF THE WATER AT ABOUT NINE AND THE HARD AT THE TOP OF THAT LANE WENT FROM ICED TO WET, AND BY SATURDAY MORNING THE FOLDING EASEL HAD BEEN TAKEN DOWN AND LEANED AGAINST THE SIDE OF THE BANK AND GONE ROUND THE BACK, AND THE WOMAN WITH A CLIPBOARD WAS STILL ON THAT HARD.**
