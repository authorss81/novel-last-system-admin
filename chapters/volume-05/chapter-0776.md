# Chapter 0776 — The Sheet Of Ply On The Bay Floor

Friday the second of December 2022, in this borough. She is at her own lock-up and yard from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a dip in the bay floor filled over with a sheet of ply, and the bottom of her roller door now has something softer under it than concrete.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES HAS THE BOTTOM OF HER ROLLER DOOR CUTTING A DIP INTO THE BAY FLOOR AND MEANS TO KNOCK IT OUT AND SCREED IT LEVEL, THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK BRIDGES IT WITH TWO BATTENS AND A SHEET OF PLY SCREWED DOWN, AND THE PLY TAKES THE CUT THAT WAS CUTTING THE CONCRETE AND WILL BE CUT THROUGH IN A WINTER.**

She is a woman in her late forties at her own lock-up on Friday. The woman who books work comes into the bay in the morning and does the second half her own way.

---

The roller door is up to its bracket and the grit along the bottom of its rail is where it was in the autumn. Along the wall outside, the four pallets are standing on edge where she put them in October. The trolley is stood against the far wall of the bay with the load off it. The drawer with the leads and the screws is shut in the bench under the window, and the box behind the bench has not been opened. In the corner of the floor at the leg of the rack is a heap of grit swept into a crescent and trodden flat.

The dip is where the door lands. The bottom bar of the shutter is a steel section about the depth of a forearm with a rubber strip along its underside, and it comes down the two guides and stands on the floor at the front of the bay. It has been coming down a little further since the summer, because the curtain is old and the slats have worn at their joints and the whole of it has stretched about the thickness of a finger. Every time the door goes down it lands a little further in than it did before, and it has cut itself a hollow in the concrete along a line about the width of the bar, and the hollow is deeper than it was a month ago.

The hollow is not even. It is as deep as the thickness of a coin at the far end from her and as deep as two fingers at the near end, and the bar does not sit down flat in it. It sits on the near strip of sound concrete with its other edge over the hollow and its face canted, and every time it comes down it rocks once across the hollow before it stops.

She has a lump hammer and a bolster and a bag of ready mix and a float, and she means to knock the hollow out to the sound concrete and fill it back up level, because a door that lands on a level floor lands flat on all of it and will not cut.

The woman who books work comes in over the roller door with a batten under her arm.

---

"You are not going to screed that."

"I am going to screed that."

"Not before you have had a look at how that bar lands." She stands at the edge of it with the batten still under her arm. "Which end is deeper."

"The near end."

"How much deeper."

"Enough." She does not stop with the bolster. "It has been cutting since the summer and it has cut itself a comfortable seat and it is not going to cut any deeper once I have put the floor back under it."

"You will put the floor back under it and it will go on doing exactly what it is doing now." The woman drops the batten and crouches at the edge of the hollow and lays her hand flat on the sound concrete beside it, then on the low part. "Feel the gap between your two hands. Now put your hand on the sound strip on the other side. There is floor between those two places that is lower than either of them, and that is not the bar digging. That is the bar coming down on one guide harder than the other with a face that is not flat, so it has been riding on the two edges of your own hollow and cutting the bottom out of the middle of it."

"Then it will stop when the floor is level."

"It will stop when the floor is level in the one place and not in the other." She stands up and looks along the line of it. "You will put a screed in there and it will be a foot wide and an inch thick at the edges, because a screed runs out thin onto whatever it is laid against, and that is not a floor. That is a slab with no edge. And a bottom bar of that door is going to come down on it and rock on it, and a screed will rock. It will not cut through, because it is stone. It will chip at the edges first, and every chip you knock out leaves a shoulder, and every shoulder is somewhere the bar can catch.""

"Then what is going under it."

"Nothing."

---

They do it with the ply off the top of the rack, where it has been leaning behind the boards for two years and has gone the colour of weak tea on the face of it.

She does not take the hollow out. That is the first thing and it takes them a while to agree on.

"Take it out."

"No." The woman is back at the rack with the batten under her arm. "Leave the hollow. It is under a ply and it never will be. Taking it out is a morning with a breaker and a lump of it on the floor, and then you are laying a screed with a soft edge in the same place, which is the thing we are avoiding."

"Then what is the job."

"The job is the floor the bar lands on." She cuts the batten into two lengths with a hand saw and does not measure them. "Two of those, screwed down one at each side of the hollow, and the ply across the top of them over the hollow. The bar comes down on the ply. The ply spans the hollow on two battens that are on sound concrete. There is no concrete left in the path of that bar anywhere."

"And the ply is not as hard as the floor."

"No."

---

They fit the battens first. They go down either side of the hollow, a hand's width back from the sound concrete, on the flat and on the sound, screwed through the bay slab with four screws each and the heads down level with the top of it.

Then they bring the ply in from the rack and lay it over.

It comes off a pallet load and it is about four feet square with a corner off it, and it is the only sheet of any size in that building. They stand it on edge against the wall of the bay while they mark it out, and it does not move.

"It will not lie flat over that."

"It will lie flat over that." She marks it with a chalk line run off the end of a batten, snaps it, and they lay the pieces one at a time over the battens and over the hollow. "It is not going to lie flat on the hollow and it is not meant to. It is lying on the two battens and there is a hand's width of air under it where the hollow is, and if you put a straightedge across the top of the battens it is level from one edge of the door to the other and it does not know there is a hollow under it at all."

They screw it down through the ply into the battens, six screws, and each one goes through about three-quarters of an inch of sheet into a batten an inch and a half square.

Then they take the roller door down.

It comes down about four inches and stops. It is not the bar on the concrete any more. It is the bar on the ply, and the ply does not chip and does not roll, and the bar sits on it flat along the whole of its length for the first time since she has had the place.

"That is better than it was."

"It was never as bad as that for long, because the concrete fills itself back in around it and then you are back to a hollow with gravel in it and the bar finds the gravel." She is packing the saw away. "And when that ply goes, which it will, you take four screws out and put a new sheet down and it is half an hour."

---

The woman goes at about the middle of the day, and the bay is quiet with the door down and the light coming in along the top of it in a band.

She gets the bar up by hand and puts it back on its bracket and the roller door up to where it always is, and then she gets down on the concrete with a torch and puts the light along the ply.

There is already a groove in it.

It is not a cut-through. It is about the depth of the head of a nail and it runs the whole width of the sheet at the line where the bottom bar came down, and it is the width of the bar, and in the middle of it the fibres are standing up out of the face of the ply in a fine pale ridge where the bar has rolled on it and taken the skin off the top of the grain.

She puts the blade of a chisel into it and lifts a shaving about as long as her hand and as thin as a sheet of paper, and the ply under the groove is still one colour all the way through, and there is nothing under the ply but the hollow she did not take out.

She lays the shaving on the ply and puts the torch down on the concrete beside it and stays on her knees looking at the groove.
