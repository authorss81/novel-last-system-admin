# Chapter 0957 — Between The Crossing Leaves

Monday the twenty-fifth of November 2024, at the two gate leaves standing open across the path with the light off the house behind them. Between crossing leaves.

**THE TWO LEAVES STAND OPEN AND CROSS ONE ANOTHER IN FRONT OF THE PIERS, AND THE SPACE BETWEEN THEM IS A WEDGE THAT IS SHUT AT THE FAR END WHERE THEY CROSS AND WIDE OPEN AT THE NEAR END BY THE HINGES, AND THE HAND GOES INTO THE WEDGE AND OPENS THERE, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR APART THE TWO LEAVES ARE AND WHAT IS ON THE FAR SIDE OF THE NEAR ONE.**

The gates have been on that drive since the house was built and they are the ordinary iron kind, made of flats with a spear at the head of each stile, and each leaf is four feet across and about three and a half feet high and weighs more than it looks like it does.

They are hung on pintles set into the piers, and they were hung an inch too wide for the gap between the piers, which is the ordinary fault with a pair of gates that were made by a man in a shed rather than to the opening they were going into.

A pair hung an inch too wide will close and shut and latch perfectly well, and the fault only shows itself with them open, because each leaf swings back towards its own pier and the free end of each one goes past that pier and past the free end of the other one. So they stand open and crossed, as they have stood every day since they were hung, with the far end of one leaf in front of the far end of the other and the two stiles of the two leaves about two inches apart where they cross.

The wedge that makes is the only air on the whole of that drive that a hand can go into, because from the drive side it is shut by the crossing of the two leaves and from the other side it is shut by them again, and the only way in is under the bottom rail at the hinge end where the two leaves have come away from one another.

---

The hand goes into that gap at the hinge end palm down and flat, and then it opens.

Opening it inside there pushes the near leaf one way and the far leaf the other, and the two leaves are both stiff enough to argue with a hand for about a second, and then they give about an inch each and the wedge is three inches wider at the crossing than it was. The fingers spread and the space grows against them. A spread hand takes the widest distance between the thumb and the little finger and nothing else, and what it is spread against here is not a wall and not a floor but two pieces of wrought iron each pushing away from the other.

With the hand shut it goes in the same way and stops the same place. It is the difference between the two that tells anything at all, and the difference is about three inches. Past the near leaf there is the lawn and then the side wall of the house; past the far leaf there is the gate pier and the front kerb of number eight beyond it. Between the two of them is nothing at all but air and the two bottom rails.

Those rails are the reason the gap is a wedge and not a hole. Each leaf has a bottom rail about four inches deep set a foot above the ground on the drive side, and where the leaves cross those two rails cross as well, and below them the only thing in the way is the grass going along under the gates for six feet.

A hinge that has been on a pintle for a hundred years has a knuckle worn into a crescent, and the crescent takes up what the leaf loses at the top of it, so a gate leaf does not sag in the middle. It settles at its free end and its free end is the end that has to clear the pier, and those two leaves clear the piers by an inch because the opening they were made for was an inch wider than the opening they are in.

A leaf of that kind keeps all of its stiffness in its two stiles and gives nothing anywhere in between them. Push on the middle of a rail between them and they move together. Push on the near stile of one leaf and the whole leaf goes over on its pintle, which is exactly what is happening to the near leaf here. The pintles at the hinge ends are still in their housings and the collars at the foot of them are still on, and the straps at the head of the two leaves are where they were riveted, and none of that has been touched since the day the gates were hung. A pintle is a stiff piece of iron and it is the one fitting in a gate that never needs anything done to it.

A drive like that takes two cars at once and has done since the house was built, and the gate piers are set back far enough behind the crossing that nobody has ever hit them standing there, which is luck and not arrangement. Both pintles have been re-hung once, and the second hanging is the one a man can see, because the housings have been chiselled out and made good again and the new lead is a different colour from the old.

Where the two leaves cross, the flats of one are standing a little off the flats of the other, because iron swells in the sun and the leaf that faces the house takes more of it than the leaf that faces the road. Neither leaf stays where it has been shoved, because a gate pushed off its line and let go comes back along its own arc and not along the line it was pushed on, which is why the two leaves are still two inches apart at the crossing an hour after a hand has been in between them.

The piers are stone, about a foot square at the head, and they were built before the house and are the older of the two things by sixty years, and the pintles are set into the inner faces of them at a height a person could lift a gate to and no more. A pair of gates on a drive is the one thing on a front of a house that everybody moves and nobody fixes. It gets a push to get past with a barrow, and a lift by a child, and a haul by somebody carrying something they did not want to set down, and every one of those comes off again and none of them is anything the hinge was not built for.

The ground under the crossing of the two leaves is grass, and it has never been worn, because the leaves stand a foot off it and a foot is too high to be walked over. Her sister comes through the gate in the fence between the side path and the back yard, with the gate standing open against its post behind her, and she stops on the path and looks at the two leaves crossing.

"There is a man been at the porch at number eight."

"There is a man been at that porch since the summer."

"Then somebody has taken that man on for something at last."

"That man goes and stands there. Standing is not the same as being taken on."

They get as far as the hall, and whether the heating had been in at the hall on Friday, and whether the woman who opens the hall would have turned it on for a cold morning. The fingers were out of the wedge and down before she reached the fence gate, and neither of them glances down at the ground between those two leaves on the way back. The wedge is shut at the crossing and open at the hinge end again the moment the hand is out of it, and the two leaves come back to two inches apart where they cross and to an inch past the piers at their free ends.

Two iron leaves hung an inch too wide will cross at that drive every day of their lives and neither of them will ever be the one to move first, so that the wedge between them is exactly what it was an hour ago and will be exactly that tomorrow.
