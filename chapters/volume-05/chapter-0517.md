# Chapter 0517 — If It Comes to It

The Thursday of the fifth of September 2019, between about half past four in the afternoon and about six. A corner in this borough, in Tideglass.

**IN 1997 TWO YOUNG WOMEN AGREED SOMETHING IN A LINE OUTSIDE AN OFFICE IN A TOWN AND SAID NOTHING FURTHER ABOUT IT THEN OR IN THE TWENTY-TWO YEARS AFTER. ON THE FIFTEENTH OF AUGUST A THIRD PERSON TOLD ONE OF THEM THAT SHE HAD READ THAT AGREEMENT CORRECTLY ALL ALONG, AND IT IS EXACTLY THREE WEEKS NOW, AND SHE HAS NOT DECIDED ANYTHING.**

They still do it on a Thursday.

That is not an arrangement either, in the sense that neither of them has ever said it out loud. It is a thing that has happened every Thursday for twenty-two years because both of them happened to be free on a Thursday, and then it was ten years, and then it was twenty-two years, and at some point in the middle of that neither of them could remember a Thursday when it had not happened.

They stand at the corner by the postbox that has had a notice glued to it for about two years and that nobody has taken down, and they stand on the same side, and they have never once said why that side.

Here is what happened in 1997.

It was a wet Tuesday in the September of 1997 and they were both in a line outside an office that dealt with something neither of them has ever been able to name, and they got talking about nothing, the way people do in a line, and at some point the woman who is now the older of the two said, "If it comes to it, it'll be me."

And the other one said, "Right."

That was the whole of it. There was no second half to it and there was nothing after it. The older woman has told herself, over twenty-two years, that she meant: if one of us ever has to go and do the thing nobody wants to do, I am the one who will go.

The other woman has told herself, over twenty-two years, that she meant: if one of us is ever put in a position where she has to ask somebody for something, then neither of us ever will, and this is how we do that.

Both of them have been right about their own half of it for twenty-two years. Neither of them has ever once said to the other which half she had in mind.

What has come of it is this.

In 1999 the younger one had her hip done and the older one took her to the hospital and sat in the corridor for the best part of the morning and drove her home and did the shopping that week and did not mention any of it.

In 2004 the older one's sister died and the younger one went the day before and did not go to the funeral and has never given a reason and was never asked for one.

In 2011 the older one was unwell from November right through and the younger one went in on a Sunday and on a Wednesday and on a Thursday and did not mention any of it.

Twenty-two years, and there has not been a single occasion on which either of them has asked the other for anything. Not a cup of sugar, not a lift, not a phone call in the middle of the night, not a shift of an hour. There are two women in this borough who have never asked each other for a thing.

And it is not because they are unfriendly. They have been to each other's houses for birthdays. They have both looked after the other in a way that is not nothing.

And neither of them has ever once put the other in a position where an asking was possible, and both of them have managed that, and that is the part that is unusual.

Here is what the older woman has been told, and by whom, and on what day.

A man who cuts their grass has cut the older woman's grass since 2014 and the younger woman's since 2016, and he is a man of about fifty and he talks, and the older woman has always been careful with him.

On the fifteenth of August he was in her kitchen getting a drink of water because it was hot, and he said, in the ordinary way of a man making conversation, that he had heard the younger woman had had a bad run of luck since the summer.

And the older woman said, "Had she."

And he said, "Three times. I had it off her."

And then he said the thing, and he said it without any idea at all that he was saying a thing.

He said, "And she was telling people she does everything for you and nobody ever does anything for her."

And the older woman said, "Did she."

And the man said, "She said it to a woman two doors down and to the woman who keeps the post," and he laughed, because he thought it was a joke about somebody else, "and I said, my gran did that, and he did that, and so on."

And the older woman said, "Right."

And he said, "You all right?"

And she said, "I am. It's just the heat."

And he went and cut her grass for another three quarters of an hour and she stood in her kitchen and did not do anything at all for a while.

It is the fifteenth of August to the fifth of September and that is three weeks and it has been three weeks every day.

Here is the whole of what she has done with it, and she has done it in her own head and nowhere else.

She has worked out that she was right.

Not about the hospital. She has no way of knowing anything about the hospital and she is not going to find out and she made herself a rule about that on the sixteenth of August and she has kept it. She is not going to ask, and she is not going to have the grass man asked either, and if the younger woman ever mentions it she is going to act as though it is news.

What she has worked out is that she was right in 1997. She said if it comes to it, it'll be me, and it has come to it more times than either of them has ever counted, and it has been hers every time, and not one of those times has ever been spoken of afterwards by anybody, and she has never once wanted it to be, and none of that is the point.

The point is that the younger woman has been doing all of that for twenty-two years believing it was a fair share.

And the older woman has worked out why she is not going to tell.

It is not pride. She went over that in the second week and pride does not survive contact with a man cutting grass.

It is that the man who cut the grass said where the younger woman said it. She said it to a woman two doors down and to the woman who keeps the post. Two people. Both of whom will say it to somebody else inside a month, because that is what people do with a thing like that, and it is not a cruel thing, it is a complaint.

And the older woman has understood, standing in her kitchen on the fifteenth of August and again on the nineteenth and last Tuesday, that if she goes to the younger woman and says it, then the younger woman is going to hear her own voice coming back at her from somebody else.

And a woman who has gone twenty-two years without asking anybody for anything does not need another person to tell her what that costs.

And there is a second thing, and this is the one she has not told anybody, not even herself in words.

The younger woman did not say that the older woman does nothing.

The younger woman said she does everything.

If the older woman goes over there on a Thursday and puts it right — if she says, that is not what I meant, I meant if it comes to it — then she is telling the younger woman that she has been enjoying something for twenty-two years that was not there. And the older woman has not enjoyed it. And saying that would be the one time in twenty-two years that she has asked the younger woman to do something for her, and she is not going to do it standing on a pavement.

They met at the corner at about half past four on the Thursday, as they do.

The younger woman was there first. She is always first. She had got there at ten past and she had stood on the same side of the corner and looked at the notices on the postbox.

She was carrying a plastic bag and the older woman took one look at the shape of it and knew before she got there that the younger woman's hip had been bad again since the summer and that the younger woman was going to say nothing about that for a month.

The older woman said, "How's your mother."

The younger woman said, "She's at my sister's. She's fine. She's been fine since June."

They stood there for a while.

Then the younger woman said, "The grass man told me you'd been in a lot in August."

The older woman looked at her.

And the younger woman said, "You've had a busy summer."

And the older woman said, "It's been quiet."

And the younger woman said, "Good."

And neither of them said one word about any of it, and they stood there until about ten past five, and then the older woman said she had to get back and the younger woman said she was going that way as well, and they walked a couple of hundred yards in the same direction and then the younger woman went off at a turning and the older woman carried on.

The older woman got home at about ten past five and stood in her own front and did not take her coat off for a minute.

Because the younger woman had known.

The younger woman had said it in the middle of that as though it were the weather, and it had taken the older woman until the corner of her own street to understand that the grass man had been to the younger woman too, and that the younger woman had asked him the same question she had asked him, in the same week, and had been given the same answer, and had heard the same two people named, and had said nothing about any of it either.

There are two women in this borough who have been told the same thing about each other by the same man and neither of them is going to say so.

**THE OLDER OF THE TWO WALKED HOME ON THE EVENING OF THE FIFTH OF SEPTEMBER AND STOOD INSIDE HER OWN FRONT WITH HER COAT STILL ON, BECAUSE THE WOMAN SHE HAS NEVER ASKED A THING OF HAD ALSO BEEN TOLD SOMETHING BY THE SAME MAN, AND NEITHER OF THEM IS GOING TO MENTION IT.**
