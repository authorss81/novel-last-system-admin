# Chapter 0804 — A New Bottom Sewn On

Monday the third of April 2023, in this borough. From the middle of the afternoon until the light is going she is in her own shed at number ten with the sack lying on its side, and the hole worn through the bottom of it is the whole of that day.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES HAS A SACK LYING ON ITS SIDE IN HER SHED WITH A HOLE WORN THROUGH THE BOTTOM OF IT ABOUT THE SIZE OF A COIN AND HALF A BUCKET OF TOPSOIL BANKED INSIDE IT AND MEANS TO TIP THE TOPSOIL OUT, HER SISTER SAYS THE SACK IS ON ITS SIDE BECAUSE THE BOTTOM HAS GONE AND THE TOPSOIL IS GOING THROUGH THE HOLE AS IT STANDS, SO THE SACK IS TURNED OUT AND THE OLD BOTTOM IS CUT AWAY AND A NEW ONE IS SEWN ON WITH A NEEDLE AND A HANK OF TWINE, AND THE TOPSOIL GOES BACK IN.**

Number ten, on the Monday. The sack has been lying against that shed wall since February. Her sister arrives by the yard at the end of the afternoon, when the light has come off the flags, and the two of them go into the shed together.

---

It lies against the shed wall on its side, and it is the only object in that yard she cannot get upright.

It is an old builder's sack standing about as tall as her hip, and two patches about as wide as a coin have worn right through the base of it, in two places, in all the months it has been lying on the concrete of that shed. Banked up inside it against the wall is the rest of the topsoil she lifted off her own front path last spring, dark and fine and the best bulb compost in that borough, and it is coming out through both patches before she has turned it over.

She means to tip it out.

She has got a board to put across the shed floor so she does not spoil it, and she has the bucket, and she means to roll the sack over, put the bucket under the corner where the hole is, and tip it, and then go and get a new sack from the ironmonger's and use that.

---

"You are tipping it out."

"I am tipping it out. Then I will put the soil in the bucket and buy a sack."

"You are tipping it out and the soil is going to go out through the hole at the same time as you tip it, and it will be on your floor and not in your bucket." Her sister has put her bag down inside the door and has not taken her coat off. "And then you will buy a sack, which will be a sack with no hole in it that you could have had in November, and this one will go in the bin with two coins' worth of hessian in it."

"It has got a hole in the bottom."

"It has got a hole in the bottom because you filled it with half a bucket of earth and stood it on its side." She puts her hand flat on the outside of the sack. "It cannot stand on its own and it will not stand on its own until there is something under it at the bottom. That is not a fault in the sack. That is a fault in the bottom of the sack and the bottom of that sack is a piece of hessian four inches deep with stitching all the way round it, and it has worn through on a shed floor."

---

So they do it the other way round, and it is about two hours of the last of the light.

She cuts the stitching of the old bottom out and takes the sack out through the side door — not the front, which is four inches of step and would have put the frame under the full weight of it — and she stands it in the yard on a board with the mouth to the sky.

The topsoil comes out on its own. She gets the sack up onto its end and lifts it about a foot and shakes it, and the earth drops out through the two patches in the bottom into a heap on the board, and what is left in the sack is the stuff in the corners and the lining, and she works that out with a stick afterwards and it all goes on the board and none of it is lost. It is a little over half a bucketful when it is out. She does not lose a spoonful.

Then they cut the sack down the front with a knife, all the way, and spread it out flat on the shed floor, and the old bottom is a ring of hessian lying flat with the stitching all round it about an inch from the edge, and the two patches are in the middle of that ring where the sack has been standing on the floor since May.

The two of them stand there looking at it before anybody says anything, because that ring of hessian is the whole of the fault and it is four inches deep all the way round and there is nothing else in it at all.

Spread out flat it is bigger than anybody expects. A sack that has been three feet high on its end with half a bucket of earth in the bottom of it opens out to a shape about as wide as that shed door, and the two of them have to walk backwards across it to get it flat on the boards, and it is hessian that has been folded into thirds in a sack for three years and it does not want to lie flat at all.

The outside of that base is grey with grit off that floor. Inside, where nothing ever reaches, it has kept the colour of a new tea chest, and the two patches have spread about the size of a coin and no more, which is not very much to have ruined a whole bottom over.

---

Her sister cuts the whole old bottom away, stitching and all, with the knife going in under it and not into the body of the sack, and that is the only cut that is made in it that afternoon.

That leaves the body of the sack with a clean raw edge all the way round, about five inches deep, and a hem on the inside where the stitching used to be, and the hessian is good for about another year of standing on a stone floor with nothing in it.

Then she cuts a new bottom out of the corner of a new sack she has in her bag — which is why she came with a bag and not with a shopping — a little bigger on every side than the piece it replaces. It goes on the inside of the body of the sack and it laps up the inside of it four inches, and there are three layers in the join and the third one is the body of the sack itself, and that is what stops a hessian base being two pieces of cloth with a hole between them.

She trims it round to the shape of the body of the sack and presses it flat all the way round so that the join she is about to sew has nothing in it but three layers of hessian and a bit of air.

---

Then she sews it, and that is the part that takes two people and it is not the weight.

A needle is a darning needle and it has an eye in it and there is no other way of making a hole in hessian than pushing a blunt thing through it, and hessian will not let you push a needle through two layers of itself without something on the other side to take the point.

She takes a hank of twine out of the bag and waxes the end of it with a stub of candle off the shelf and threads the needle and pulls about two feet of it through, and then she sews the two layers together from the inside.

The stitch is a lapped stitch the whole way round, every half inch, through all three layers and back up through all three, and she pulls each stitch up tight before she puts the next one in, because a sack sewed slack is a sack that hangs open along that join.

And she cannot do it alone, because somebody has to keep that sack flat.

She gets down on the floor of the shed with her knees on the boards and her two hands on that join, one either side of where the needle is going in, with her thumbs spread on the hessian pushing the two layers together and her fingers hooked round the edge of it so that it cannot creep and bunch up while the other person is putting a needle through it, and she has to move round the whole of the sack as the join goes round, and she has to press it just as hard at the end of it as she did at the beginning.

The point of the needle comes out on the inside and goes in again on the outside about half an inch along and the twine comes up through both layers at once and a thumb takes it and pulls. That is the whole of the stitch and there is nothing else to know about it.

It takes about two stitches to the inch and there are a bit over five feet of that join all the way round, so there are rather more than a hundred stitches in it, and every one of them is pulled tight before the next one goes in.

Her sister sews it in about fifty minutes and does not rush any of it.

---

Then she turns it the right way up and it stands.

It stands on its own bottom on the shed floor for the first time since the May before last. It rocks a little on one corner where the ground is uneven and she puts a finger under it and finds the new bottom is tight and hard and there is no daylight anywhere under it, and she can put the whole weight of a bucket of earth into it and nothing goes anywhere.

Then the topsoil goes back in.

It goes in in three goes and she puts it in with a bucket and she levels it off with the back of her hand, and the sack stands up on its own bottom with three quarters of a bucket of topsoil in it, and she lifts it by the rim with one hand and walks it to the beds and puts it down, and it does not go over.

The hank of twine is on the bench with about a foot and a half of it left on it and the needle is beside it stuck in a piece of cork, because that is what she has always done with a needle.

The offcut of hessian that came off the bottom of that sack is folded and pushed to the back of the same shelf, because it is the right size to be the bottom of the next one that comes through that door, and there has been one a month at a time for four years and there will be one in August.

---

Her sister goes out of the back door of that yard with her bag and her coat and the knife, and the woman of the house stands in the shed doorway with the sack behind her and watches her go out through the yard and does not go out of it.

She kept that join flat all the way round a sack with her thumbs and could not have got a needle through it by herself, and there is not going to be anybody standing in that shed with a bag in August or in October or in any month after this one.

The sack will stand on its own bottom. The one after it will not.
