# Chapter 0399 — Raked Out

Thursday the twenty-second of March 2018, from about eight in the morning until about four in the afternoon, a yard behind a row of houses, in Tideglass.

**A MAN IN HIS FORTIES REPOINTED NINE FOOT OF A GARDEN WALL ON A SATURDAY IN FEBRUARY FOR NOTHING AND WAS TOLD BY NOBODY, AND ON THE TUESDAY OF THE TWENTIETH OF MARCH A MAN IN HIS THIRTIES RAKED ALL OF IT BACK OUT AND PUT THE RUBBLE BACK AND SAID NOTHING, AND THE FIRST MAN WENT AND LOOKED AT THAT WALL ON THE FIFTH OF APRIL AND SAID NOTHING EITHER.**

Off the Cleeve road there is a terrace of nine houses with nothing behind any of them but a footway. At the bottom of it, past the last house, a gate in a brick pier gives onto a strip of ground the length of a small car and the width of a doorway, and on the far side of that strip there is a wall with a gate in the middle of it.

The wall runs about twenty-two foot and it is about five foot high and it is the boundary between that strip and the land behind the next building along. It is a wall of nine-inch brick with a coping of engineering brick on edge, and it is about a hundred and ten years old.

The land on the far side of it belongs to the man in his sixties. He has had it since about 1994 and he is having a garage built on it in June, and the wall is coming down in June, and he has not told anybody in this borough that except one man in a shop off the parade on the eighth of March.

The face of it that looks into that yard is the north face, and the north face of a wall in this borough is the one that gets the weather.

---

The man in his forties was doing the guttering and the eaves on the front of that last house on the Saturday of the sixteenth of February, and he was on a pair of steps at the back of it from about nine until about three.

He was on those steps because the parapet at the back of that house comes out over that yard by about two foot, and he could stand on the top of them and work on that parapet from above, and there is no other way to get to it from the ground.

He had the old lead out and the new lead going in along about nine foot of it, and he had his scaffolding plank over the steps and a bucket up there with him and a brazier he lit at half past nine and let go out at about one.

He looked down at that wall from about seven foot up for about four hours.

The face of it was frost-blasted. There was a patch about the size of his two hands where the face had gone back into the rubble, and the face had come off it in flakes the size of a shilling all the way along about nine foot of it, and about four foot of that was bad enough that the coping over it had begun to lift at the north-west corner and had a bit of moss in the joint.

He did not want the water off that gutter going down that face.

That is the whole of the reason and it is a good one. He has been in this trade for thirty years and a north face that goes like that ends up with the foot of it loose inside ten years, and two people live in that last house and the girl of them is at college four days a week.

So he came down off those steps at about half past two, put his bucket in that yard, and repointed it.

It took him about three and a half hours.

He raked the loose face off with a bolster about three inch wide, back to sound stuff, over about nine foot, and he kept the old mortar in a sack, because old mortar is the right thing to put back and not the mortar out of a bag. He wetted the face above the joint properly, which you do not do in March and which he did. He laid it in three lifts and keyed the top one in, and he finished at about ten past six when the light had gone, and he stood in the dark for a minute and looked at it by the light from the window of that house.

He made about nine pounds of mortar. He had a fire going in a bucket for about two hours of it.

He told nobody. Nobody was there. Neither of the other two men was in that strip of ground, and that last house was shut behind him, and the man who lives in it came in at about six and could not see that wall from the kitchen.

The man in his sixties saw it on the Monday of the nineteenth of March, from the gate, in about a minute.

He was on his way out and he stopped and looked at the top of that wall and stood there and said nothing to anybody, because there was nobody there.

He has never mentioned it to anybody. He left it out when he spoke to the man in his thirties about that wall a fortnight later, and the subject has not come up with him since, and the answer he would give it now is that he does not know who has been at that wall.

On the Thursday of the eighth of March he said a thing to the man in his thirties, in about half a minute at the shop off the parade, and what he said was that he was having a garage built on that land in June and that the brick wall on the far side of it was coming down, and that he did not want anybody spending anything on it.

On the Tuesday of the twentieth of March at about ten in the morning he carried a length of downpipe and a bag of fittings into that yard, and the wall was not why he was there.

He had a length of downpipe to take off a building at the bottom of that row and a bag of fittings, and he went up the side of the last house into that yard and put the bag down against the wall by the gate.

That wall had been pointed.

About nine foot of the top of it, in a mortar a shade off the mortar under it, and the coping laid true and the arris on the coping knocked off the whole way along, and the face of it new and even and about a quarter of an inch proud of the brick under it in places.

He looked at it standing up. He did not put a hand on it and he did not think about it. He has never in his life seen a man repoint a wall in this borough and he was not going to start, and what that wall needs is done.

He came back on the Tuesday after, at about half past ten, and he came back on the Wednesday, at about the same time, and on the Wednesday he took a bolster and a lump hammer and a chisel out of the gate and raked the whole of that new pointing out.

---

That took him until about half past four on the Wednesday and it was filthy work. The mortar had been down six weeks and some of it had gone hard, and he got it off in lumps about the size of a fist and knocked what would not come off with the point of the bolster and the lump hammer.

He put it in a sack and that went to the tip on the Saturday.

And he put the rubble back.

The old mortar was not on the ground. It was gone, because the man in his forties had taken it away in a sack when he did that wall, because old rubble cannot go back into a wall and he knew that.

So the man in his thirties shovelled the clean stuff out of the face with the point of the bolster, back about an inch and a half, and damped it and packed it back in loose, and packed it in loose because he was not pointing it and did not intend to, and it stands about half an inch proud of the face it was in, which is what loose rubble in a joint does.

He finished at about half past four. He swept it up and put the sweepings in the sack with the rest. He put the tools in the van, put the gate back on its hinge, and went.

One thing in that wall is not the way he found it.

The point of the bolster went through the face of the brick at the top of the raked length, at the left-hand end, about a foot in from the gate pier, and it took a piece off that brick about the size of a thumbnail, and there is a raw pale place in it now where the face was.

He did not put that back and it cannot be put back.

The rest of that face is loose rubble standing a bit proud and about four shades off, and the coping over it is bedded in nothing and one end of it rocks if you put a hand on it, and all of that is what a wall that is coming down in June looks like in March.

Nobody has said anything about any of that.

The man in his forties went into that yard on the Thursday of the fifth of April about half past two.

He was at the last house in that row about a door that had dropped and went out the back to put a length in the van.

He stood at that wall for about half a minute.

He saw the loose rubble standing proud of a face that was not flush, and the coping that rocks, and the raw pale place in the brick at the top of the raked length, which is about a foot in from the gate pier and which is not a thing that weather does.

He worked out inside about four seconds that it had been raked out, and that it had been raked out on purpose, and by somebody who knew it had been pointed, and that it had been done inside a fortnight and not by a child.

He also worked out that it was the man in his thirties. There is about one other man in this borough who will take a wall apart that belongs to somebody else, and the two of them have not raised it, and it is not going to be raised.

He put the length in the van and shut the gate and went.

**HE STOOD AT THAT WALL FOR ABOUT HALF A MINUTE AND HE HAS SAID NOTHING TO ANYBODY, AND THE MAN WHO RAKED IT OUT HAS NOT ASKED HIM WHY IT WAS DONE AND IS NOT GOING TO.**
