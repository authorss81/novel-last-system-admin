# Chapter 0636 — The Man Who Came For The Fridge

Saturday the twenty-fourth of April 2021, in this borough. She is up at about ten past six and she is in her own kitchen until the light goes off the back wall and then she is on her own front step, and the whole of it is one house at number twelve on a road that comes off the parade.

**A MENDED FOLDING CHAIR IS PUT DOWN IN HER KITCHEN BY A WOMAN SHE HAS SPOKEN TO TWICE, AND SHE ACCEPTS IT WITHOUT ASKING ANYTHING, BECAUSE THE ONLY KITCHEN CHAIR SHE HAS GOT HAS HAD A SPLIT LEG SINCE BEFORE CHRISTMAS AND SHE HAS BEEN EATING STANDING UP SINCE THEN, AND WHEN A WOMAN AT NUMBER TEN SEES THE NEW ONE ON HER STEP IN THE AFTERNOON SHE GIVES IT AN ORIGIN, AND THE ORIGIN IS A MAN WHO CAME ROUND IN MARCH ABOUT THE FRIDGE, AND NO MAN CAME AND NO MAN WOULD HAVE ANY REASON TO.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is not on her books for anything. She has a gas fire, a table with a cloth on it, four chairs, a fridge in the run that came with the house, and a window over the sink that she does not look out of except when she is washing up.

---

The chair has been leaning against the dresser since the Friday before Christmas and it has been leaning there because the back leg has come off its hinge and the whole thing wants to go over backwards.

She has had it like that since before Christmas. That is the length of it. Not a length anybody would put a date to.

She eats standing.

That is not a decision she made. It is what happens when you have a kitchen and a chair that will not sit you down safely and a table with a cloth on it that you have had since the house came and you would not use the cloth for anything. She has a stool in the passage, a wooden one with a back, that she used to sit on to do her boots on. She moved the stool into the kitchen in February and used it a few times and then stopped, and she cannot tell you why, and the reason is that sitting down to eat alone in a house for somebody her age is a thing you do while somebody is in the room, and there is nobody, and so she eats at the sill with the plate on the sill and the tea on the sill and her back to the room, and it takes the same length of time it always took.

And there is a thing about the sill. It is the one place in a kitchen where a person stands with their back to the door and cannot hear the door. She likes it. She has liked it since before Christmas. That is not a thing she would tell anybody either.

---

The knock is at about ten and it is a knock she knows, which is two knocks and a pause and then one more, and she knows it the way you know a step.

She does not open it straight away. She stands in the passage for the length of it and then goes and undoes the latch, and the woman is on the step with a green folding chair held under one arm.

And she says, "I'll not let you in."

And the woman says, "I know."

And she says, "Then what is it."

And the woman says, "Put it by the dresser and don't ask me about it."

That is the whole of it.


Here is what she has got afterwards, and she wrote it out later and then tore it up, and the writing out is the part she remembers.

A woman in her late forties, who has taken two clearances in this road and who stands in a passage in rubber gloves. No gloves on that day. She was in a plain cardigan and she had been in a garage, and the woman at number twelve could tell, and she could tell because of the hands, which were not dirty but were dry, and the nails, which had a lot of nothing under them and nothing at the sides of them, and that is a thing you only get from handling cardboard and paper and nothing else.

And the chair.

And she did not say where the chair came from.

Not once, and not in a way. Not a shrug, not a smile, not the sort of pause a person leaves when they have decided not to mention something. She set it down against the dresser and she said *put it by the dresser and don't ask me about it* and she went straight back down the path, and she did not look back to see if the woman was watching her carry it in, and she did not knock again, and she was gone in about a minute and a half.

And the woman at number twelve stood in her own kitchen and thought, and this is the first thought and it is the one she has not been able to get away from since: *she has told me not to ask.*

Not *don't ask her*. Not *you can ask me*. Not *I don't mind if you ask.* She has said *don't ask me about it.* Which is an instruction, and it came from a woman who has never given her an instruction in her life, and it was given at the door with a chair under an arm.

And she did not ask.

That is the whole of what happened at the door and it took about a minute and a half and there is not one thing in it that a person could have done differently except ask, and she did not ask, and she wants that written down, and she has wanted it written down since the Saturday and it is the only thing in the whole of that Saturday she would call a decision.

---

The chair is green. It is a steel frame with a seat and a back of the same green, and the seat has a ring on it, and the ring has gone through to the metal in one place.

And somebody has been at the back leg.

That is the thing she looks at longest. There is a piece of angle iron about a hand's breadth long on the back of the hinge, drilled twice, with a bolt through it and a nut on the outside and a washer under the head, and there is a mark on the step of nothing, because the wood of the step is marked where somebody knocked a screwdriver off it, and the mark is new and it is a pale gouge in a dark brown, and it means somebody was sat on their own back step in the last of the light in a bad mood about a screwdriver.

Nobody does a thing like that to a chair they are going to leave at a tip.

She knows that. She does not know how she knows it, and she has kept a house since before the ring was worn, and what she knows is that a person does not put iron into a chair for a skip.

And she is not going to ask.


She moves it about a quarter past ten and gets it up to the table, and the table is the wrong height, and she knows that before she has sat in it, and she tries it anyway.

Too high. She sits in it and her feet do not quite reach and her back is not against the back of the thing and after about a minute she can feel the ring through the seat.

She gets up. She goes and gets a cushion off the settee, the good one, the one that came out of the same house as the chair and that she keeps for somebody who does not come.

She puts the cushion on the seat and sits down again and that is better and that is the arrangement, and it has taken her four minutes, and she sits in it and looks at the window over the sink and the window is doing what a window does at half past ten on a Saturday at the end of April.

Then she gets up to wash the plate.

And that is the first thing. She has eaten every meal since before Christmas standing at that sill and she has got herself into a chair and eaten nothing and got up to wash the plate, because her hands go to the sink at the moment they have always gone to the sink, and the sink is where they go.

She stands at the sink for a bit with the plate in her hand.

And then she thinks: I have got up twice now, and both times it was to do something I did not need doing.

And then she thinks: and both times I stood at this window.


She puts the chair out on the step in the afternoon because it is the first warm day and there is a damp patch in the corner of the kitchen floor that she cannot get at with a cloth, and the chair is in the way of it, and she would rather have the chair in the way of the damp than the damp in the way of the chair.

She puts it on the step facing out, the way you put a chair out, and she leaves it there about twenty minutes, and the woman at number ten walks past with a bag of shopping and stops and says, "That's a nice bit of kit."

And she says, "It is."

And the woman says, "New?"

And she says, "No."

And the woman says, "Where'd you get it then."

And here it is.

And she says, "There was a man come round. I don't know what he came for. He asked me if I wanted my fridge out, in March, and I said get rid of it."

And the woman at number ten says, "No."

And she says, "He was in a van."

And the woman at number ten says, "Well. I've not seen a van."

And she says, "You'd have been at work."

That is all of it, and the woman at number ten has gone on up the path by then, and it took about a minute.

And the woman at number twelve stands on her own step with a mended green chair in front of her and finds that her heart is going and that she has about four minutes of having to stand still before she can go inside.


Here is the thing about what she said, and she has been over it a good many times and she has not got a better version of it.

A man came round. In March. About the fridge. In a van.

Every part of that is a lie and the whole of it is not, and that is why she said it.

A man did come round. It was in March, or near enough. He was not asking about the fridge, he wanted the road, he wanted the way down to the parade, and she came out and told him and he went. He was in a van. It is the only thing in this house that has ever come on wheels, and the man in the van, whoever he was, did not come for the fridge, and there has never been a man at this door about a fridge, and the fridge is built into the run and it came with the house and it is the loudest thing in the kitchen and nobody in this road has ever had it out.

And she said it anyway.

And she says now, standing on the step with the light going, that she cannot work out the moment it stopped being a thing she was saying and became a thing she was doing. Because there is a difference. There is a woman at number ten who will remember saying a man came for a fridge, and that woman is going to be on this road until one of them goes, and the thing the woman at number ten remembers is now a thing that happened, and the woman at number ten is not a liar for it. The woman at number ten did not lie. The woman at number twelve did.

And that is the price of it, and she paid it in about a minute, standing on a step, with a heart going, and it is the cheapest thing she has ever bought.

She bought a green chair and she gave away a fact about a house that does not exist, and she gave it away so that a woman in her late forties can go back down a path without anybody asking her where the chair came from.

And she does not know whether that is a kindness. She has been turning that over since the afternoon and she has got as far as this: if she had asked, the woman would have had to answer, and there is no answer that does not cost the woman something. Either the chair came off a skip, which is a thing to be ashamed of, or it came out of a house where somebody died, which is a thing to be sorry for, and the woman at number twelve is seventy-eight and she has one of each of them and no room for a stranger's.

And there is a third answer, which is that the woman did it because the woman eats standing up, and that answer is the true one, and it is not available, because it can only be said by the woman at number twelve, and if the woman at number twelve says it then she has admitted to a stranger that she eats standing up, and nobody in this borough is going to say that to her face at seventy-eight.

So the man came for the fridge.

And the chair is on her step and the light has gone and she has to decide whether to bring it in or leave it out, and leaving it out means anybody who walks past tonight sees a mended green chair on number twelve's step, and mended is a word with a story in it, and mended is the one thing about this chair she cannot account for.

---

**SHE PUT THE CHAIR OUT ON HER STEP FOR TWENTY MINUTES BECAUSE OF THE DAMP AND A WOMAN AT NUMBER TEN STOPPED AND ASKED WHERE SHE HAD GOT IT, AND SHE SAID A MAN CAME ROUND IN MARCH ABOUT THE FRIDGE, AND EVERY PART OF IT IS A LIE AND THE WHOLE OF IT IS NOT, BECAUSE A MAN DID COME ROUND IN MARCH, BUT NOT FOR THE FRIDGE, AND NOW A WOMAN WHO IS NOT A LIAR HAS A THING IN HER HEAD THAT SHE WILL CARRY, AND THE PRICE OF A GREEN CHAIR WAS NINETY SECONDS ON A STEP WITH HER HEART GOING.**
