# Chapter 0968 — Through The Wire Coil

Undated in this draft.

**A COIL OF WIRE ON THE GROUND AT NUMBER TEN KEEPS A ROUND HOLLOW THROUGH ITS MIDDLE.**

The sideway at number ten threads house wall and dwarf hedge, paved with flags dipped in rotation and risen in past winters.

A coil of fencing wire lies on the ground beside the path where it was left after the hedge was tied back in spring. The coil is about a foot across and wound some twenty turns, each turn bright where it rubbed the next and dull elsewhere with rust.

The hollow through the middle of the coil is round and about the width of a wrist, and it runs straight through from one side to the other. Grass has grown up round the lower turns and keeps the coil where it lies, and a dry leaf has lodged against one side of the hollow without blocking it.

The wire itself is of drawn steel about an eighth thick, with a galvanised face gone grey in most places and rusted through at two spots where the coating failed. The cut end of the wire stands out from the coil by about six inches and turns back on itself, sharp at its tip where the cutters left it.

Drawn steel wire snaps cold across the palm, bright and immediate. Below the coil the flagstone breathes cold upward where damp sand touches its belly. Oxidised freckles sip less warmth, rasping the cuticle. Verging grass borrows zero warmth, bowing mute.

Hand-wound round a post then slipped, the loops vary in calibre and the bore skews slightly off-axis. The inner turns are tighter than the outer, and the hollow narrows toward one end by about half an inch across its length.

Dew settles on the upper turns overnight and runs down into the hollow, leaving the lower turns damp till midday while the top dries within an hour. Roof water does not reach here, because the eaves throw clear of the path, and only rain falls on the coil directly.

The flags flanking the coil were bedded in river sand, since sluiced from beneath one lip to open a shallow runnel. The run is dry and full of grit that came off the flags above, and a fingertip slipped within meets flag belly sleek from casting. The overhead flag bridges its cavity firm, chiming softly to a knock.

Box hedging walls the far verge, annual-clipped knee-high, thick with tiny foliage. Spring cord restrained the hedge, strangling trio of stems where tensioned. The stems there have swollen round the twine and grown over it in part. Boxwood of that age grows slowly and cuts hard, and a stem no thicker than a finger will turn a saw. The clipped tops show pale wood at each cut, and each cut has dried to grey through summer while the leaves around stayed dark.

Twine of that kind draws no heat and gives under the nail, while the box stems are hard and draw little. The leaf litter beneath the hedge is dry and deep, and a beetle moves through it without rustling.

The path itself falls gently toward the lane, so water runs along it rather than across. At the lane end the last flag has sunk by half an inch with rainwater pooling post-storm in a thin lamina reflecting clouds overhead till wind ruffles it. A shallow gutter has fretted the sand by the nether flags where boots herded grit. The grit there is sharp underfoot and pale against the soil, and each grain shows a fresh face where it broke from the flag above. The channel is no deeper than a fingernail and runs for about two yards before it spreads onto the soil.


Box hedges answer clipping with dense twiggy growth, each cut provoking two shoots where one grew before. The hedge here shows three seasons of doubling in its interwoven twigs, brown without and green within. The interior twigs never see sun yet leaf anyway, pale and soft, drawing on stores from the sunlit crown. A wren nests deep in that thatch each spring, its dome of leaves invisible till it sings.

The soil beneath the hedge runs dark with leaf mould to the depth of a hand, then pales to yellow clay. Worms work that boundary constantly, casting pale below and dark above. One casting near the coil contains a fragment of eggshell, white against brown, dropped by a thrush that uses the coil as an anvil. The shell cracks further with every rain. Thrushes revisit favoured anvils for seasons, one stone hollowed by generations of shells.
---

One finger goes straight into the hollow of the coil from the open end, with the thumb braced on the outer turn and the finger kept clear of the cut end.

Entry stays gradual, for the bore wanders off-axis and the finger crooks to trail it. It deciphers the interior haptically, loop by loop, snagging never.

Initial news is the distance through. The finger reaches to the second joint at the near end and to the third at the far, because the hollow narrows and the finger wedges where the inner turns tighten. The far opening shows as a round of daylight no wider than a coin when sighted from the near end.

Later news is what touches the wire at the far mouth. Through the greater stretch only air resides, wire gliding smooth either flank. Near the far end there is a plug of grass stems and leaf mould drawn into the hollow by wind, soft and damp, through which the finger passes with a little pressure.

The plug gives under the tip and stays parted after, and the finger comes back with a green stain and a smell of leaf mould. Wire ringing the plug bides, coil ballasted by self-mass and grass stitching. The cut end stands clear throughout and does not touch the finger at any point.

The fingertip quits the bore dusty-flanked, tip dewy, otherwise spotless. The hollow endures, its grass plug divided solely down the finger line.

---

The coil carcass abides on turf, lower loops sewn by grass and massed wire. A boot nudge wobbles it a whisker, soil print reclaiming it at once.

The garden-side sibling waits beyond the dwarf box at number ten, knee-high green dividing them, palms unladen both.

The fingertip backs out of the wire round and settles on the flagstone as the sister peers across the hedge top. The hedge between them is dense with rain from the night, and the path beyond runs clear to the lane with the sun on the upper flags. Wire and pathside litter go unmentioned between them. The sister looks once at the coil and once at the house, and then turns back to the garden without coming through the gap.

The cut end of a wire tells how it was cut when read closely. The end here shows two flat faces where the cutters met and a small burr on one side that catches the skin. The burr is sharp enough to prick but not to cut, and it stands away from the hollow so nothing passing through need touch it. The bright metal at the cut has dulled to grey in the months since spring, while the faces beside it have stayed bright where they rub each other in wind.

Wire of this gauge work-hardens where it is bent, and the outer turn is harder at the bend where it leaves the coil than along its straight. A fingertip run along that bend feels the wire resist and then ease, as the hard spot gives way to the softer straight. The rusted spots are elsewhere, where the coating failed at a rub, and each is no larger than a grain of rice with bright wire showing at its centre.

Grass grows through iron without hurry and without harm to either. The stems that pass through the lower turns have thickened round the wire and keep it from lifting, while the wire has cut a shallow groove in one stem where the wind moved it through summer. The groove is pale inside and dry, and the stem above it grows on as before.

The coil keeps its hollow round and true through rain and sun, because the wire was wound to last and not to please the eye.
