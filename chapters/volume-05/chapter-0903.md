# Chapter 0903 — Behind The Middle Board

Saturday the twentieth of April 2024, at the stack of boards against the wall at the unit with the yard gate open behind the work. Behind the middle board of a stack.

**HE HAS A STACK OF ELEVEN BOARDS AGAINST THE WALL AT THE UNIT AND HE MEANS TO RUN THE LITTLE-FINGER SIDE OF THE HAND DOWN THE GAP BEHIND THE MIDDLE BOARD WITH THE THUMB KEPT CLEAR OFF THE FACE AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN IS AT THE SIDE GATE FROM THE PAVEMENT WITH A BAG ON THE GROUND BESIDE HER.**

That stack has stood against that wall since autumn, and the boards in it have been taken out and put back while the stack has stayed where it was set.

It is a stack of eleven boards of pine, about ten feet long, with stickers between every third one to let air through.

Ten feet wants three bearings to lie true, which is why the batten sits in the middle with the ground at one end and the wall foot at the other.

He wants what is behind the middle board, where the wall sweats and the air does not move.

Sweat on brick beads in the morning where the night's cold meets the day's warmth, and runs down in streaks by noon.

Behind a stack the streaks never dry, which is why the middle stays dark while the ends pale.

Behind a stack the side of the hand reads best, because fronts face the yard while backs face brick that sweats unseen.

So the little-finger side of the hand runs down the gap behind the middle board, with the thumb kept clear of the surface to leave it free.

The wall behind is brick, cool and rough, with old paint flaking off it in scales and grit in the joints where the pointing has gone soft.

The hand goes down about three feet and stops on something firm. Not brick. A batten, running across behind the stack about waist high, with the boards resting on it and not on the wall.

He turns the side of the hand along it and feels how far it runs. About two feet, with the ends cut square where the saw went through.

Two feet carries a ten-foot stack without bending, because the load spreads out along the batten to the brick behind.

A shorter batten lets the stack belly in the middle, while a longer one throws the ends out past the stack and catches sleeves.

What it is, is a packing batten, put there to throw the stack off the wall and let air up behind it.

The woman from two doors down reaches the side gate off the pavement, her bag set on the ground unlifted.

"Has the stack got my shelf in it?"

"It has your shelf third from the front."

"Will it go on the bike?"

"It will go if it is tied at both ends."

She nudged the bag with her foot and left it where it was, and told him the side gate dragged on the path since the rain and would want lifting, and that she was not lifting it in spring. He told her a gate that drags leaves a arc in the grit and the arc shows where it binds, so the bind was at the heel post and not along the whole swing. She told him that came to the same as asking for new. He told her it did not. She kept that, and carried her bag back up the pavement.

---

A stack on a batten stands only on the batten and on nothing else, because the batten lifts it clear and air goes all round the backs.

Pine backs sweat on brick where air cannot move, because brick throws damp in spring when warm air meets a wall that has been cold all winter.

That is why the middle board is dark at the back for a hand either side of the batten, where air moves least, while the ends stay pale where wind gets in.

A dark back pales in a week once the board is taken out and given air all round, which says the dark is only weather and not decay.

Decay yields under the thumbnail and smells sweet when pared, while weather stain stays firm and smells of timber alone.

He pares the back with a nail and the curls rise pale and firm, with no give in them.

Stickers between boards keep them true the way packings do, because air goes between and each board dries even on both faces.

Without stickers the middle boards cup toward the damp wall, with the backs swelling and the faces shrinking, and the stack leans out at the top.

Cupping toward damp is how pine tells where the wet sits, because the damp side swells across the grain while the dry side stays put.

That swell over ten feet is enough to throw the top a finger out, which is what the end view shows.

Stickers stop it by letting air to both faces, so neither swells more than the other and the boards stay flat.

That is why this stack leans a finger out at the top, where the stickers stop three boards down and the top three ride on each other.

Three boards without air between will always lean to the damp, while three with air stay true through winter.

What the stack wants is two more stickers up top to bring the lean back, with the top board weighted down with an offcut to press it flat.

An offcut as long as the arm and as thick as the wrist presses even along the whole top without denting it.

Short weight dents where it sits and leaves the ends to lift, which is worse than no weight at all.

Weight presses a stack flat the way sun warps it, and each does its work a little every day until the boards take the set.

Sun warps by drying the top face while the bottom stays damp, so the top shrinks and cups up at the edges by dinner.

Weight undoes it by night, pressing the cup flat while dew damps both faces even.

The brick of the wall is firm where the paint covers it and soft in the joints where water gets in.

Brick stays firm under paint because paint throws rain while bare joints drink it, and each winter the bare ones go a little deeper.

Pointing gone soft feels sandy under the nail and comes away in crumbs, while firm pointing rings a little when tapped.

Old paint flakes in scales where damp gets behind it and lifts it, while paint on dry brick stays flat for ten years.

Scales curl up at the edges where the damp pushes, and each scale leaves a pale patch that darkens again by next rain.

The batten is firm under the hand, with the grain tight and the ends square, and hazel dust in the cracks where it was cut.

A batten cut square bears even on the brick over its whole back, while one cut on the slant bears on one edge and rocks when the stack is pulled.

That rock works the nails loose in a year, which is why square-cut battens outlast all others behind stacks.

Hazel dust smells sharp when rubbed, the way pine dust smells sweet, and each tells what timber stood under the saw.

Sharp dust cuts the nose while sweet dust coats it, which is how the nose knows one from the other in a shut store. The shut air keeps both scents till the door swings, when yard wind sweeps them thin in one gust. Sawdust on the floor drifts toward that draught and settles in a pale fan inside the doorsill.

He takes the hand out from behind the stack and sights down the boards from the yard for what the faces proclaim.

From the front the eleven boards look straight and true, with the stickers showing even between them.

From the end the top three show their lean, with the top one out a finger past the one below.

Either look tells straight, and touch sorts the cleanest face, yet which length would she lift for shelving to lie level past August?
