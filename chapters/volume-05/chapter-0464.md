# Chapter 0464 — A Quarter to Twelve

Thursday the third of January 2019, from about a quarter to twelve until about ten past two. One parade and the top end of it, in Tideglass.

**NINE PEOPLE WENT UP AND DOWN THAT PARADE ON THE THIRD OF JANUARY, A LENGTH OF IRON CAME OFF A WALL AT HALF PAST TWELVE AND WAS PUT BACK BY THREE MEN AND TOLD TO NOBODY, AND THE ONE THING A PERSON IN THAT STREET COULD NOT NAME IS EXACTLY WHERE IT WAS.**

What that parade does in the first week of January is go on, and it does not make any accommodation for the fact that it is the first week of January.

It goes on being a street with shops on both sides of it and a bus stop near the bottom and a car park halfway up where a woman parks and a man in his forties parks, and on a Thursday morning most of the people who open are open because they came in on that morning and not because the street asked them to, and about a third of what is on that street is shut and has been shut since Christmas and will open again when the person who owns it opens it.

---

Nine people were on that parade between about a quarter to twelve and about two, and there was not one reason among them for being there that was about any of the other eight.

The man who puts shutters up came down from the top end at about ten to twelve and went back the way he had come. A woman with a dog went past at about twenty past twelve and went on. The man in his forties had a brown paper bag under his arm and was on his way up the road with it. A man in his thirties went down at about twenty to one. The man in his sixties stood outside a premises that opens on a Thursday with a cup in his hand. The man who works nights went the other way at about ten past one. A girl of about sixteen went up to the top end at about twelve and came back at about two with nothing. Two more came through and were not stopping.

That is the whole of the people on that parade. It is a Thursday.

At half past twelve a length of iron came off a wall at the top end of that parade and about eight people heard it and about two of them turned round.

It was about two foot of painted iron, knee-high, bolted through the front of a premises there to hold off a step about four inch high that anybody coming in while looking at the shop opposite walks straight off.

It had been on that wall since before the shop in front of it was a shop. It came off at half past twelve on the third of January, landed on the footway flat, hit nobody, and made a noise about the size of a bucket going over. A man thirty yard away with a dog said, "Oh." A woman with a bag went round it.

Three of them had it back up in about ten minutes.

The man in his forties was nearest. The man in his sixties came over from the far side with his bag of tools, which he had brought in a car that belongs nowhere near that parade. A third man, who had been in that street for his own reasons and had not intended to stop, stopped.

What had gone was the angle the iron was fixed through, and it had gone because the wall behind it is rendered, about a hundred year old, and the plate behind the fixing has been turning out of that render in one place for about two years, slowly, the way a thing does when nobody looks at it, and one of the two holes is in solid brick and the other one is in nothing at all.

They used a different length of angle out of that bag and two screws a couple of inches longer, and the iron is back on that wall about three inch to the left of where it was. The step is protected. It does not look wrong from anywhere except the angle you would be looking from if you had been standing there before the third of January and knew.

What came off the wall went into an old carrier and the carrier went into one of the bins at the far end of that street.

Nobody telephoned anybody. The woman whose premises it is came in at about two, put her hand flat on it the way anybody puts a hand on a thing that has been on a wall longer than they have, and went in.

The only words said about it at that top end on that afternoon were one word from the woman with the dog to the man in his forties, and the man in his forties does not know she said anything.

There is a building by that water. At the end of a passage off it is a room nobody has opened this week. A shelf runs along one wall of that room and a book is open face down on it, holding its own place, where somebody left it.

It has nineteen entries in it. It has had nineteen since March. Under the last entry, on the paper, there is a space ruled the width of a pencil line and nothing in it, and on the third of January that space had stood empty for one thousand four hundred and ninety-nine days.

Nobody on earth is carrying that number about. That room was shut on Tuesday, was shut again on Wednesday, and will be shut on Saturday, and nobody went down that passage, and nobody ruled anything, and the paper is exactly as empty as it was in March.

Four answers are still going round this borough and there is still no fifth.

Nobody has withdrawn one of them. Nobody has ever said which of them they hold. On the third of January, on that parade, between a quarter to twelve and two in the afternoon, no person put one of those four answers next to any other one, and no person asked anybody else what any of them was an answer to.

There is a question underneath all four of them, and nobody in that stretch of that Thursday put it to anybody, and nobody on that parade is going to.

There is one other place on that stretch with a door in it. It is a small building at the top end, about four foot square, with a door, a hasp, a padlock and a danger sign, and about a foot square of grit inside under the roof and two cables in a conduit going up the wall.

There is no bench in it and nothing to sit on, and nobody has ever slept in it, and nobody went near it that afternoon. The padlock is where it has always been. The sun came onto that door at about a quarter to twelve and the metal gave the heat back all afternoon.

---

And here is what is the same as it was.

Go into that street at two o'clock on a Thursday afternoon and stop the first person you meet and ask them what in this borough has changed in a generation.

They will not agree. Every one of them will give you a different answer, and every answer they give you will be a true thing, and not one of them will give you the answer that is in front of them.

That answer has not moved by so much as a single day since the middle of August. Nobody on that parade has put it to anybody. Nobody is going to.

The stretch from the twentieth of November up to that Thursday was a stretch of the same kind as every stretch before it, and it did not turn anything, and there was nothing in it that a person standing on that parade at two o'clock would have been able to point at.

**AT ABOUT TEN PAST TWO ON THAT THURSDAY THE WOMAN WITH THE DOG CAME BACK DOWN THAT PARADE AND WENT PAST THE WALL WHERE THE IRON NOW IS THREE INCH FROM WHERE IT WAS, AND SHE DID NOT LOOK AT IT, AND SHE WENT DOWN TO THE END.**
