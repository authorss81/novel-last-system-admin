# Chapter 0989 — Two Men, Six Weeks

Undated in this draft.

**THE BOOK ON THE DESK IN THE OFFICE AT THE LOCK-UP IS OPEN AT THIS WEEK.**

The office at the lock-up is one room with a desk, two chairs, a telephone that is not used much, and a window that looks south over the yard and the three bay mouths. The book lies open on the desk at a page for this week with a ruled column down the side of it and a man's job against each ruled line, and the woman who books work has a pen in her hand and is not marking anything on the page.

"You've come to ask me something and you're standing in the doorway doing it backwards," she tells him. "Come in and shut it. You'll let the yard in."

The sash in that window is out of true in its frame, standing half an inch back at the lower corner on the left, because it was hung by a man who packed that corner with a piece of card and the card is not there any more. It has stood that way since before the book moved onto this desk. The draft off it comes in along the desk and moves the corner of the page every time a lorry goes past on the frontage, and she has never had it looked at, and neither has anybody else.

"I want a job on that page." He stays where he is in the doorway while he asks for it.

"You want a job on that page and your name against it, which is a different want, and I know which one you mean."

"Yes."

"Sit down. I'm not having this across the office." She drops the pen into the tray. "There's two men wanted, six weeks, up the river at the next town over. The clause is on the wall behind you in a frame because somebody before me used to take it off and lose it. Go and read it if you haven't."

---

"I know what the clause says, then."

"Then you know there's no trick in it, and I want that understood before anybody starts. The crews go where the need is and the need is up the river, and this town's short of hands and their town is short of hands, and the paper sets down how many go and for how long and what it costs the town they leave, and every part of it is correct." She laid a finger on the page without pressing it. "There's two lines on here this week with nothing against them and two men on the ground who could fill them. That's all it is. There's no arrangement underneath it."

"And the men who go — do they keep their place here?"

"They keep their rate and they keep their name on this page, and the page closes at the end of the season whether they've been here or not, and it does that to everybody whether they go or not. That's how it works. You know that as well as I do."

"It's not mine." She leaves it lying there on the desk where he can reach it. "That is what you came in here to be told and you haven't asked for it yet. I enter work.

I don't put men on. Every man on this page is on it because somebody who was on it brought him, or because a fitter put him there when he was twenty-four and had no pull at all. If I start choosing, this book is a different book inside a week and every man on it will know it by the Friday." He looked at the two empty lines, and she let him look at them, and neither of them put a word in for a while.

She gets as far as that and then stops him. "Then ask. No — don't. Listen to me first."

"I've asked."

"You've stood there and been about to." She turned her chair round so that her back was to the window and her face was to the door. "I am not going to be the person who asks you to go up the river. If I ask you in an office with the door shut, then for the rest of your life you will not know whether you offered or whether I took you. And I will not know either. That is my whole reason, and it is not modesty."

"You could put it to the yard. To everybody at once."

"And get one hand up and nine men deciding in the tea break that the one who put his hand up is the one who thought about going." She spread both hands on the open page, one either side of the two empty lines. "Last spring I asked three men in this room, one at a time, because we were that few. Two of them went.

Both of them came back before the year turned. And there was nothing on this page for them until well into the winter, because the work that came in went to men already on it, and neither of those two has been on this page since. One of them has gone to work on the far side of the city and he is not a fool and he is not ungrateful. He simply was not here when the work came in, and there was nobody to speak for him, because asking a man and entering a man are two different operations and only one of them is on this page."

The yard went past. Two men crossed it by the rack and neither of them looked in at the window.

"So no," she told him. "I will not be the one who asks you."

"Who is it, then. The two lines." He stood up out of the chair and put his hands in his coat pockets, because there was nothing at all to do with them, and he stood by the window with his back to her for a second and then turned round again.

She turned the page round to him and put her finger on the first name, and he had known for a minute and a half what was going to be on the end of that finger and had been standing there the whole while waiting to see whether she would bring him to it.

"That one I know. She's at number nine, alone, and she has been on it since she was twenty-two, and nobody has ever asked her a thing in her life."

"Yes."

"And the other."

"She is the one I want you to go and ask."

"Go and ask him what?"

"Not him." She shut the book, which she does not do, and put her palm flat on the cover of it. "Go and ask her. Go to number nine and ask the woman who books work that at number nine what it is for. She will tell you. She has told me, and she told me the same thing she will tell you, and if you had been in this office ten minutes ago I'd have told you both of them myself instead of making you walk down there for it."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No." She props her chin on her knuckles. "That's the thing about you, and none of it is criticism. You've never been asked a question you couldn't answer, and you have spent ten years being the man somebody brought along, and you'd give a great deal today to be the man somebody asks. And the two things are not the same, and it has gone wrong twice for two men I put in this office myself."

"You tore it out." He took his hand off the door frame and looked at the shut book under her palm and at the two empty lines on the sheet he had been given a copy of, because she had torn it out of the book and pushed it across the desk without any particular expression and he had not asked for it.

"I tear out about six a week. It's the week that matters. It comes off at five and then it's a piece of paper and I'm not interested in it." She wheeled her chair back square to the desk. "Go down the frontage and ask her, and don't come back up here and tell me what she told you, because if you come up here with it you'll want something for it."

The window draft moved the corner of nothing, because there was nothing on the desk, and the sash was still half an inch back in its frame at the lower corner where the card used to be, and the yard was going about its business the way it did whether anybody came through the office or not.
