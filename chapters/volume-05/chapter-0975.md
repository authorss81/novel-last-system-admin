# Chapter 0975 — Where The Scraper Stands Off

Undated in this draft.

**A BOOT SCRAPER IN THE FLAGS OF THE FRONT PATH HAS LIFTED ITS BACK EDGE OFF THE STONE ALONG ITS WHOLE LENGTH.**

The scraper stands in the third flag of the front path at number nine, in a square of cast iron about eighteen inches long with a slot across the middle for a blade to stand in and an ear at either end for the setter to grip. The blade is gone. What is left is the slot, and the slot has taken the same set as the path, so that the scraper leans a little downhill toward the grass the way the path does.

Casting iron this old was done in a wooden pattern, and the pattern maker's letter is raised on the underside of the square, a sixteenth of an inch high and full of rust. Across the face of the iron runs the line where the two halves of the mould met, and that line has never been dressed off, because nobody wanted a scraper that looked at anything.

The iron is damp along the slot through every night and dries back by the middle of the morning. The cast stone of the flag it stands in takes the damp into itself and gives none of it back, and the pale ring round the two ears is the drying of it.

The bed the scraper was set in was a barrowful of sand laid on the soil and levelled by hand, and the fall of the path takes the rain to the same corner twenty or thirty years running. That bed has washed out from beneath the back edge and gone down into the soil in a narrow cone, which is why the back edge stands off the stone along the whole length of the scraper while the front edge is still bedded in what is left of it.

The slot is a little under a quarter of an inch across where the casting is sound, and a little more where the iron has thinned at the bottom of the blade bed. Along the middle of the scraper there is a place where the edge of the bed has come almost up to the top of the iron, and along that place the slot is a hair wide and does not admit a fingertip lying down.

The path itself is of cast slabs bedded the same way, and about half the joints along it stand open now for the same reason. Slabs that were laid level in September are a little down by Christmas, and a slab laid down will rock under a boot corner because the ground under one end has gone.

Beyond the scraper the path runs four slabs to the porch, and the porch is brick with a wooden door in it that has not been repainted since the sash went in. The iron gate at the pavement end stands open against the pier on its hook and has stood open for years, because a gate that shuts needs a latch, and the staple for the latch was never put in.

The wall along the left of the path is a low boundary wall with a coping of two courses of brick on it, laid dry with nothing in the joints but grit and the grass that has since grown along the top of them. On the wall's outer side is the footway, and beyond that the road, and on the far side of the road the church spire stands above the roofline of the terraces.

Between the path and the wall there is a strip of grass about four feet wide that gets no sun until the middle of the morning, so it grows in patches and dries in patches. A dog has worn a bare place at the near end of it, and the ground there has been trodden down to the finer roots and keeps the damp longer than the rest.

The bay under the house eaves collects the roof water of the porch and takes it away again along a channel cut in the flags, and the channel passes the scraper on the grass side and is the reason the bed under the scraper went when it did. Rain that comes off the porch roof in a storm will stand in the scraper's slot for an hour or two before it finds the low place at the downhill ear and goes out through it.
---

One finger goes in behind the scraper at the near ear and stops against the flags. It is pushed in far enough to take the skin off the knuckle of the finger behind it, and then it is drawn straight back against the stone, gently, until something stops it.

What stops it is the bed. At the near end the sand comes up under the iron within about half an inch of the back edge, and the finger finds it as a gritty floor and cannot pass under at all. Pushed harder it moves perhaps a sixteenth before the sand packs, and it comes back with grit under the nail and a taste of iron on the skin.

At the far ear the finger goes in the same way and comes back with nothing to stop it until the tips are out past the second joint. The bed there is gone entirely, and what is under the iron is the compacted soil of the path's foundation, close and dark and full of small stones, and the fingertip can be walked along it to find the line where the sand begins again.

So the scraper stands off the stone most at the far end and least at the near end, and a man's hand is the whole of it from one to the other. Along the whole run in between the two, the lift is not even: it grows by degrees from nothing at the near ear to the most at the far one, which is the shape a bed of sand takes when it goes from the high corner downward.

The iron itself does not stir. It is cast in one piece and bedded at the front edge and has not shifted in any way that a finger can find, and the ears are still down on the stone at the front and the slot between them is still square where the blade used to stand.

The slot does what it has always done, and the flags around it go on being laid in the same sand and losing the same bed at the same corner. The scraper will lift a little further at the far end with every winter that goes by, and the line of pale stone round the near ear will widen by about a hand's breadth each year without anyone doing anything to widen it. The hand comes out of the slot with grit under two nails, and the scraper is left standing off the stone at the far ear exactly as far as it was, and the sand in the bed is packed where the finger found it and loose where the finger never reached.

A shovel blade is propped against the house wall by the porch at an angle that has leaned a little further each winter, and the rivet at its neck stands out a little further each winter as well. The handle is ash and has gone grey along its length while the blade end of it has gone brown, and where a thumb has been laid on the top of it for years there is a patch of wood a shade lighter than the rest.

The bottom step of the porch has a hollow worn into the middle of it where three generations have put their feet on the same place, and the hollow is about an inch deep and keeps a film of water after rain. The rims of the step stand square where the chisel left them, and the front edge carries a shallow groove where the water runs off so that it does not go back under the door.

---

In the four-foot strip between the path and the wall, the woman who does her shopping has arrived by the time the hand leaves the slot, having come the last of the way along the outside of the wall and in at the gap at the near end, and her hands are empty.

She does not move out of the four feet between the grass strip and the path, but she looks along the line of the path from the scraper to the porch step and back again. The scraper and the thing in hand are not spoken of between them, and no mention of either reaches the other.

Talk runs instead on whether the post still comes twice a day, and on whether it did come twice in the summer. The reply is that it came once in the last week of the hot weather and twice since the weather turned, and neither of them offers to name which of them believes which.

Her hand goes out at some point palm up for balance on the top of the wall and comes back down with nothing on it, and she goes on standing in the four feet of grass between the path and the wall, and the scraper stands where it stands.

The slot behind the scraper keeps the width it has had since the bed under it went, the iron standing off the flags along its whole length.
