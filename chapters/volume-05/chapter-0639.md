# Chapter 0639 — The Queue On The Parade

Saturday the eighth of May 2021, in this borough. She is in a queue on the parade from about twenty to ten in the morning to about a quarter past ten and she is home on her own front step by about half past ten, and he is in the same queue about two yards behind her from about ten past ten to about a quarter past ten, and neither of them looks at the other.

**A WOMAN IN A QUEUE ON THE PARADE SAYS, TO A FRIEND, THAT A MAN HAS COME ROUND ABOUT A FRIDGE, FOR REASONS THAT HAVE NOTHING TO DO WITH ANYBODY IN THIS CHAPTER, AND THE WOMAN WHO INVENTED THAT SENTENCE ON A SATURDAY IN APRIL IS STANDING TWO YARDS IN FRONT OF THE WOMAN WHO SAID IT, AND A MAN WHO BUILT A STOOL FOR HER IS STANDING BEHIND BOTH OF THEM, AND HE DOES THE SUMS IN ABOUT TWO SECONDS AND THE SUMS ARE WRONG AND HE WILL NEVER KNOW THEY ARE WRONG.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. He is a man in his early forties. There is a woman in her late twenties at the front of the queue with a friend, and neither of them has ever been in this borough's number twelve or in a white van.

---

The shop shuts at one on a Saturday and shuts at half past six on the other days, and the woman at number twelve has a slip of paper in her coat pocket with a thing on it that a man at the front of a shop in the parade wrote down for her on Thursday, and it is a Saturday and this is the day she can come, because on a Saturday it is empty, and on a Saturday she can stand at the front of a shop for a quarter of an hour and go home again and nobody has to know.

That is the whole of the reason she is out. She is out because a queue on a Saturday morning in this borough is the only kind of going out there is that costs nothing and asks nothing of anybody.

She is there at about twenty to ten. It is a warm morning. She has her coat on because it is a warm morning and because she has not got the other one out and because the woman at number ten has told her more than once that the woman in the terrace opposite walks down to the parade in a shirt, and she is not going to be the woman in the terrace opposite.

Here is the queue, and there are nine or ten people in it, and it moves about a foot every time and then stops.

There is a woman in her late twenties at the front with a friend of about the same age, and they have a big bag of shopping between their feet on the pavement and they have been talking without stopping since before the woman at number twelve got there, and the woman at number twelve can hear all of it and has heard none of it, which is a thing she is good at.

Behind her there is a man with a dog that does not go in the shop.

Behind him there is a man in his early forties with a hand in a jacket pocket.

And the man with the hand in the jacket pocket is the man who built a stool at a bench off an industrial estate on the far side of the parade, and he is in that queue because he has a prescription of his own that he has meant to take in since before the bank holiday, and he does not know that the woman in front of him is the woman the stool went to, and she does not know he is there, and that is the whole of the arrangement between them and it is the arrangement that has not changed since April.

---

The woman in her late twenties says it at about ten past ten.

She says it to her friend, at volume, in the flat voice of a woman discussing somebody else's house, and the shop is quiet, and a queue is the quietest thing there is.

And she says, "So he had a man round about the fridge."

And her friend says, "The old one?"

And she says, "The old one. He said it's not worth moving, it's a brick, so he said leave it and get it plumbed out."

And her friend says, "Plumbed out."

And she says, "That's what I said. He said it's a job for a man who does that. Which is fair."

And her friend says, "It's a mess, my auntie's."

And she says, "It's a brick, that's what it is. It came with the house."

She does not move.

That is the whole of what she does and it is the hardest thing she has done in this borough in a considerable time. She does not move, she does not turn round, she does not put her hand up to her mouth, and she does not go out, and she stands in a queue on the parade with a slip of paper in her coat pocket and her heart going like a bird in a box and she listens to a woman in her late twenties talk about a fridge in a house she has never been in.

And the two sentences are not the same sentence.

That is the thing she has got before the queue has moved twice, and she has got it by about ten past ten, and it is the only thing that gets her to the front.

The woman's fridge is an old one and it is a brick and it is in a house she has never been in and the man is coming to plumb it out and the woman at number twelve will never know any of it. Her fridge is in a run and it came with the house and it is the loudest thing in her kitchen and no man has ever been near it.

There is no connection. There is no woman at number ten in it. There is no leak. There is nothing.

And there is also no version of a Saturday morning in this borough in which a woman does not stand in a queue and hear the word fridge, and the woman at number twelve put a man in a kitchen in April because a woman on a step asked her a question, and that is the price of the sentence and she is paying it now, on a Saturday, in a shop, because fridges are a thing that gets said.

The queue moves. It gets to about four people in front of her and then stops again and the woman at the front turns round to say something else to her friend and has to step sideways out of the line, so there is a gap of about two yards between the woman in the late seventies and the woman in the late twenties, and the man in the early forties is behind the woman in the late twenties and is now about a yard and a half from the back of the coat of the woman in the late seventies.

He hears it.

Not because it is loud. Because a queue is a place where a man stands with nothing to do for a quarter of an hour, and a man who does kitchens for a living listens to two women talking about a kitchen, and that is what happens.

And what he hears is: a man came round, about a fridge.

---

Here is what he does with it, and it takes about two seconds, and it is the only two seconds of the day that are his.

He does not know that the woman in front of him is number twelve. He knows a woman in her late seventies in a kitchen off a road that comes off the parade, and a green folding chair that was in the middle of that kitchen floor on a Monday when he was on a tower in the road, and a stool that was on a bench on Wednesday morning, and a van he can hear from a kitchen window.

And what he has just heard, in a shop, is a woman of about twenty-five saying a man came round about a fridge.

And the two of them go into the same place in his head and they come out joined up, and what comes out is a woman of seventy-odd who has told a lie about a man and a fridge, and the lie is a small one, and the smallness of it is what makes it join up, because a woman who tells a big lie is a woman with something to hide and a woman who tells a small lie about a man and a fridge is a woman who is either ashamed of something or has nobody of her own to give the credit to.

And he has got that in about two seconds, standing in a queue, with his hand in his pocket.

And it is wrong. Every part of it is wrong. The woman at number twelve invented the man because a woman in her late forties would not say where a chair came from, and she has been carrying it since, and she has never once repeated it to anybody except the woman at number ten, and she did not repeat it to the woman at number ten to lie to her, she repeated it because the woman at number ten was going to carry it unless somebody gave her a version that cost the woman at number twelve nothing.

But he does not know any of that. He has got a woman who tells lies about men and fridges, and he has got no way of finding out that she does not, and there is nobody he can ask, because the only person who could put it right is the woman herself, and the woman is two yards in front of him with her back to him in a shop on a Saturday morning.

He has about four minutes in the queue after that and he uses all four of them doing the thing he does.

He is not a man who thinks badly about people. That is not self-praise; it is the other way round, because a man who does kitchens for a living is a man who is professionally suspicious, and being professionally suspicious is not the same as thinking badly about a woman, and he has never had cause to move from one to the other.

And he moves from one to the other in a shop queue in about four minutes and does not notice he has done it.

What he decides in the four minutes is this.

He is not going to ask her.

Not because asking would be intrusive. He has got a legitimate question and he is allowed it, and the reason it is legitimate is that he put a thing in her kitchen on a Wednesday evening and did not tell her where it came from, and so she has a stool with no story, and a stool with no story in a house where there is already a chair with no story is a thing that is going to come out.

He could go up that path and say: there is a green chair in your kitchen and it did not come off a skip, and somebody has told you it came off a man, and I do not think that man ever came.

That is a whole speech. He has built the speech in the queue. It has got four sentences in it and the fourth one is about a fridge.It is not modesty. He does not want the four seconds. He does not want the debt, and the debt is the last thing he wants, and that is all true and none of it is why.

It is because if he says it, then she has to say something, and whatever she says will be a lie, because the lie is already in the room, and the lie is the thing the chair is standing on.

And if she lies to him about it, then he will have made a woman in her late seventies lie to him in his own kitchen, and he will have done it to be kind.

She gets to the front of the queue at about a quarter past ten and she puts the slip of paper on the front of the shop and a woman reads it and goes away, and she stands there, and she waits, and she has been out of the house for fifty minutes.

And when she goes out, the man in the early forties is putting his own slip of paper on the front of the shop.

She does not see him. She has not once turned round in that queue and she is not going to start now, and she goes out onto the parade and turns left towards the road that comes off it, and she gets home at about twenty past ten and puts the thing in a drawer and stands at her own window for a while.

And the man comes out of the shop with a bag and stands on the parade for a minute and a half and does not get in the van, and then he gets in it.

And the green chair is against the dresser and the stool is on its side by the sink and the two of them have not been in the same room since the Wednesday, and the woman in the late seventies has been swapping them over about four times a day since then and has not enjoyed it once.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE TWENTIES TALKED ABOUT A MAN PLUMBING OUT A FRIDGE IN A HOUSE NONE OF THEM HAD EVER BEEN IN AND A WOMAN IN HER LATE SEVENTIES STOOD TWO YARDS IN FRONT OF HER AND DID NOT MOVE, AND A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES STOOD A YARD AND A HALF BEHIND THAT AND IN ABOUT TWO SECONDS JOINED THE SENTENCE ONTO A GREEN CHAIR HE SAW THROUGH A WINDOW, AND HE BUILT A FOUR-SENTENCE SPEECH IN THE QUEUE AND DID NOT USE IT BECAUSE THE ONLY THING SHE COULD HAVE SAID BACK WAS THE LIE, AND HE WILL NOT KNOW THAT SHE WAS NOT LYING.**

(End of chapter 0639)
