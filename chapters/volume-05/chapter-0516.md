# Chapter 0516 — The Bays

The Sunday of the first of September 2019, from ten in the morning until about one, in the thirty-one bays behind the shops in this borough, in Tideglass.

**FOR THREE YEARS HE HAS CROSSED THOSE BAYS ON A SATURDAY MORNING WITH ANOTHER MAN'S GROCERY AND PUT IT DOWN AT HIS OWN MOTOR AND SAID NOTHING, AND IN AUGUST HE STOPPED, AND ON THE WEDNESDAY AFTERWARDS THE MAN BEHIND HIM WAS TOLD ABOUT IT BY A THIRD PERSON AND SAID NOTHING TO ANYBODY EITHER. NEITHER OF THEM HAS PUT THE QUESTION AND BOTH OF THEM HAVE MOVED THE HOUR.**

There are thirty-one bays behind those shops and on a weekday they are full and on a Saturday morning they are not. A man in his sixties who has a stall two rows up comes in at half past seven with a flat-back and a hand-cart and goes out again at eight, and after that the place is where people park who have come to the shops on a Saturday for a bag of this and that and are not in any hurry about it.

There is no attendant. There has never been an attendant. The man who owns the row owns the paint.

---

The man who stopped carrying the bags is called nothing by anybody. He is a man of about fifty-eight and he is a partner in a firm that does heating, and he has had a motor in the second bay on a Saturday morning for longer than anybody has been keeping notes.

The other one is a man of about sixty-one and he has a small silver one in bay nine and he goes to the same shops on a Saturday because that is what he has always done.

For three years, on a Saturday, the first man would put his bag down at about ten and go and take the other man's two bags off the top of the little silver one and carry them, and the other man would walk beside him at the speed of a man who does not need to walk, and neither of them said anything, and it took about ninety seconds.

There was never any arrangement about it. There was no talk and there was nothing said on either side about doing it, and if you had stopped either of them in the street in the middle of it and asked why, neither of them could have told you.

In June the first man saw it on a Sunday.

He had been going into the town for a different reason and had come back the long way and had gone into those bays for no reason at all, and the man in bay nine was there with the small silver one, and there was a younger man with him, and the younger man took the two bags out of the back of the small silver one and carried them to the shop, and came back and stood beside the older man, and they went.

The first man stood at the far end of the row among the trolleys and he watched this for the length of it.

And what he took from it was not that another man was helping. What he took from it was that it was a different pair of hands doing a different thing on a different day, and that the older man had not asked, and had not looked at himself being helped, and had not said the thing that men say when they have been helped, which is that it was not necessary.

And the first man went home and thought about that for a fortnight.

He stopped on the first Saturday in August.

There is no other way to describe what he did. He got there at ten as he had for three years, and he did not get out, and he sat in the second bay until the other man came in and took his two bags out of the small silver one and carried them himself, and he watched him do it, and then he drove away.

He drove away first. He got out to the top of the row and went home by a way that is longer than the one he came by.

He has not been into those bays on a Saturday since. That is four Saturdays, the tenth, the seventeenth, the twenty-fourth and the thirty-first of August, and on every one of them he was somewhere else, and on every one of them he has known all morning what time it was.

The other man found out on the Wednesday.

He was told by a man who sells in the row and had seen the first man go out of the second bay on the Saturday without getting out of it, and the man who sells in the row said it as a joke, and said, "He never got out of it. Did you see that."

And the other man said, "No."

And the man who sells in the row said, "Years I've watched him do that. Never once did he not get out of it."

And the other man said, "Right."

And the man who sells in the row went off to serve somebody, and the other man stood there in the middle of the row for a while with two bags in his hand and then put them in the small silver one and drove home and said nothing to anybody at home either.

That was the seventh of August. It is now the first of September and it is twenty-five days.

Here is what the other man has done with it, and he has not said this to anybody and is not going to.

He has worked out that the first man has not come into that row on a Saturday since the seventh of August. He has not driven past. He has not been seen. He has not asked anybody. He has not sent anything.

And then, because he is not a fool, he went over the mornings in his own head and worked out the thing the first man worked out in June.

He has worked out that somebody in June put a younger man in that row on a Sunday and that the first man saw it, and that the first man was there because he came back a different way, and that a man who comes back a different way and stands at the end of a row watching somebody for the length of two bags has not come back a different way by accident.

And having worked all that out on the tenth of August, the other man has spent the best part of a month trying to work out what to do about it, and there is nothing to do.

There is nothing to say. Saying why would be to say that he had noticed. Noticing is the whole of it. Three years of a man getting out of a bay and carrying two bags, and the noticing is what it was, and it cannot be taken back by being spoken about.

And on the Sunday of the first of September he was in those bays at ten in the morning because he had come back from a funeral, and the man from the second bay was there with the small silver one and a younger man.

The first man got out of the second bay and started towards them. He had not decided to. He had been sitting in the second bay for four minutes with the engine off and then he got out.

He was about ten foot away when the younger man said, "Shall I get them?"

And the other man said, "No. It's all right."

And the younger man said, "It's no bother."

And the other man said, "I said it's all right."

And the younger man said, "Right," and put his hands in his pockets.

The man from the second bay stood there and he did not say one word. He did not say good afternoon and he did not say anything at all, and after a moment he turned round and went back to the second bay and got into the small silver one and shut it.

And he sat there with his hands on the wheel for a while.

He has not taken it back up and he is not going to.

Not because he is proud of it, and not because of the man in bay nine, who is not looking at him and did not look at him. He has worked it out over the last three days and the reason is much smaller than that.

If he went back into that row on a Saturday and got out of that bay and picked up two bags, then the woman who sells in the row would say something to somebody inside a week, and the man in bay nine would know he had come back, and that man would then have to decide what to make of a man who stopped and then started again.

And no reading of that leaves the three of them honest. He would be doing it so that people could see it had not bothered him. The other man would have to decide for himself whether it had.

And a man who has been carrying two bags across thirty-one bays for three years does not need anybody else to work out what that was worth. He is the only one who can put a figure on it and he is not going to give it to them.

---

On the Sunday of the first of September, at ten minutes past the hour, a man in his late twenties came out of bay nine carrying two bags, put them in the small silver one, got in, and drove out of that row without a word to anybody, and nobody said a word to him.

And at twenty minutes past the hour a man in his late fifties came out of the second bay carrying a bag of his own, which he could have left in there, and put it in the back of his own motor and drove out of that row by a different way from the one he came in by.

**HE SAT IN THE SECOND BAY FOR FOUR MINUTES ON THE FIRST OF SEPTEMBER AND SAID NOTHING AT ALL, AND THE OTHER MAN HAS SINCE PUT TOGETHER WHY HE STOPPED, AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS IT WRONG, AND THE ONLY PERSON WHO KNOWS ANY OF IT IS A MAN WHO SELLS IN THAT ROW AND MAKES A JOKE OF THINGS.**
