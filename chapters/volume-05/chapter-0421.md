# Chapter 0421 — A Gate

Wednesday the twentieth of June 2018, a kitchen and a back yard behind a short terrace, from about a quarter to ten in the morning till about half past five in the afternoon, in Tideglass.

**A MAN RE-HUNG A GATE IN A YARD BEHIND THAT TERRACE THAT HAD BEEN DROPPING SINCE THE FROST IN FEBRUARY, AND NOBODY ASKED HIM TO, AND HE DID NOT TAKE ANY MONEY FOR IT, AND HE DID NOT SAY WHY HE DID IT, AND THE MAN WHO LIVES IN THAT HOUSE HAS NOT ASKED HIM EITHER.**

That terrace is five houses and it is about ninety yard from a side street, and it is built along the side of a slope, and the back yards step down about two foot from the front of the terrace to the back of it, so that every one of those yards has a step at the back door and every gate at the back of those yards is a frame-and-ledge gate on a brick pillar and a brick pier.

The second house along is the man in his sixties' and he has had it since 1974 and he has the passage and the yard and the coal house and no other outside space, and he is seventy-one and has a bad hip, and that is the whole of what a person in this borough needs to know about him.

The gate at the back of that yard is a frame-and-ledge gate about four foot by three foot nine, painted the same dark green as the fence, and it is hung on a band and a hook on the pier side and on another one on the pillar side, and it is a heavy gate for what it is and it has never once been light.

---

He has been in that kitchen since about half past eight.

He is putting in a new sink and a new trap on a house that is going to a new tenant in the September, and he has done about six of them this year, and he is on his own in that house until about four, because the man in his sixties goes out on Wednesdays and does the same three things and comes back at about half past three.

He had the old sink off by about nine and the trap out and the new one in and the tail in by about half past ten, and he was standing at that sink with a spirit level on the draining board waiting for a joint to set when he heard the gate.

The gate has been going since about February. It has not been loud. It has been a scrape, about a foot of it, once every time anybody came through it, and there is nobody in that house but a man of seventy-one and he does not come through that gate more than twice a day, so a scrape at half past ten in the morning means nothing at all to anybody in that borough.

The man in his forties came through that gate at about ten to ten, with a tape in his pocket.

He was in that street for a completely different reason. He is doing the rainwater goods on the front of the fourth house along in the September and the surveyor wants a measurement off that terrace and the only place he could get it was off that kitchen, and the man in his forties has never met the man in his sixties and got himself let in that day by knocking on the front door at about a quarter to ten and saying his name twice and being let in by the man in his sixties in a dressing gown, who said yes and went out.

He went out of that kitchen door into that yard to get a line on the eaves with the tape off the chimney stack above the pantry, which is the only thing in that yard worth a measurement and the only reason anybody needs to stand in it, and the gate was across the bottom of that yard on the ground.

He got it up.

It took him about half a minute to get it up, and when it was up he stood and looked at the bottom of it on the ground where the pier hinge had come out of the pier and dropped about two inches, and at the top where the other hook had pulled out of the timber about an inch and a half, and at the bottom of the hinge stile, which is the stile on the pier side, and which was soft and dark for about eight inch up from the bottom and sounded wrong when he put his thumb on it.

It had been like that since about the twelfth of February, and it had been dropping and dragging and not catching, and the man in his forties looked at it and worked out in about four seconds that it had not been a frost at all, that the frost had only finished it.

He took the gate off its hooks and stood it against the inside of that coal house and went out of the front door and got his bag out of his own van, which is standing in the parade about a hundred and thirty yard away, and walked back, and shut that front door behind him.

---

He was in that yard for about three quarters of an hour and he did the whole of it and he did not say one word to anybody about any of it.

He took the rotten eight inch out of the bottom of that hinge stile with a chisel and a lump hammer and he did not hack at it, he cut the sound timber straight back, and there was about a foot of sound oak in that gate and the rest of the stile was frame oak and it took two new pieces about eighteen inch long that he had in the van because he has a box of oak offcuts that he has never once used for anything and has carried about five years.

He let those in with three-inch screws at the back and made the housings properly and put a length of three by one across the inside of the stile at the bottom, which is what stops a stile going at the foot.

Then he went to the pier with a lump hammer and a chisel and cut two new slots and set new hooks, a pair of wrought ones about four inch, because the old ones were the same four-inch hooks and there is no reason to change a thing of that size.

He filled the old slots with oak wedges and set them in, and he put new hooks on the pillar side too because the top one on that side was about to go, and he cut a new keeper and morticed it into the pier, and he set the gate up on those hooks about ten past twelve and it came down true and shut on that keeper with a noise like a door.

He did not adjust it to be tight. A gate that is set up tight in June is a gate that is dragging by August, and he set it so it swings through its arc without touching anything and closes on the keeper and that is the whole of what a gate wants.

Then he cleaned up the two bits of rotten oak he had taken out, which came to a double handful, and he put them in a bag and put the bag in his own van.

He was in that yard on his own for the whole of it. The kitchen window is at the back of that house, about nine foot inside the glass door, and it faces that yard, and it is a window that a man working at a sink has his back to, and there is a kettle on that draining board going.

The man in his thirties came out of that kitchen door at about a quarter past two with a cup in his hand and found the gate on the hooks with a new keeper and two new hooks and a new length of three by one visible across the bottom of it from the outside.

He stood on that step with his cup and took in the whole thing.

He had been in that house since half past eight. He had heard the gate go over at ten to ten and he had not gone out and looked then, because he had a joint setting at that sink and it was not his gate and it was not his yard.

He came back in and finished that kitchen and he was out of the house at about ten past four and he said, when he was going, "Your gate's all right now."

And the man in his forties was not there. He had been in that yard at about half past one, on his way back from a job two streets off, and had gone in through that back door with his own key, which he has had for six years and has never asked for and which nobody in that house has ever mentioned, and had done two more things in that kitchen in about ten minutes, and had gone.

He said it to a man in his own front room at about four that afternoon, and the man in his sixties said, "Was it," and that was all.

The man in his thirties has not gone near that gate since. He does not know what those two new hooks are made of, or that there is a length of three by one in the stile, or how long anybody spent on it, or that there is a bag of rotten oak in a van in a parade a hundred and thirty yard away. That afternoon the man in his sixties got back to that house at about half past three.

He let himself in and went straight through that passage to the kitchen, the way he does, and there is a pot on that stove that a woman comes in and does for him twice a week, and he did not notice anything about that kitchen and had no reason to.

He went out into that yard at about ten past four because it was still light and because that is what he does.

The gate was shut. It was on the hooks and it was closed and it was latched on a new keeper and there was no scrape in it, and he came up to it and put his hand on the top of it and pushed and it swung away from him and came back and shut and made the noise a gate makes.

He did not lift it. He did not look at the hooks or the pier or the bottom of the stile or the new length of three by one across the inside of it. He is not a man who looks at a gate.

He stood in that yard for a minute and a half and then he went in and shut the kitchen door and sat down. He has not asked. He will not ask. Nobody in this borough has been told that the gate works and there is nobody he would tell, and he has never in his life asked a tradesman in this borough to do a second thing for him and is not going to start, and the man in his thirties was in his kitchen all day, and the other one has not come near that gate again.

He does not know it was done for anybody. It was done for a man who had that yard and that gate and the run of them for thirty years and who is not in it, and who died in the second week of January, and who was in a crew with the man in his forties for about nine years in the 1980s and was the man they all used to ring.

And the man in his forties was one of four men in that house on the Saturday morning in the January when they took a fridge out through that yard and the fridge went through the gate backwards, and the gate came off its top hook and went over in the yard and everybody stood about looking at it, and the man in his forties put it back up on the same hook with the same screw he took out of it, in about four minutes, while the lorry was loading, because it was in his way and he was not going to stand about.

Nobody in that house saw him do it. Nobody in that street knew the man was dead by then. He did not say one word about it to any of the three men he was in that house with, and none of them knew which of the four had put it back, and none of them have ever mentioned that gate to him in five months.

And he has never gone back and looked at it. He went in on the Wednesday through the back door and did two other things in that kitchen and he did not go out into that yard while he was in the house, and he does not know that it is shut, and he does not know that it will be shut in the August and the December and the next February, and nobody is going to put that to him.

Nobody in this borough knows any of that except the man who has gone.

**HE WENT OUT OF THE BACK DOOR OF THAT HOUSE AT ABOUT HALF PAST ONE WITH A BAG OF ROTTEN OAK AND PUT IT IN HIS OWN VAN A HUNDRED AND THIRTY YARD AWAY, AND A GATE AT THE BACK OF THAT TERRACE HAS NOT DRAGGED SINCE, AND HE HAS TOLD NOBODY WHY AND IS NOT LIKELY TO.**
