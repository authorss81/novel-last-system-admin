# Chapter 0222 — What a Room Does

Friday the twelfth of February 2016, from about half past seven in the evening, a hall off a parade of shops in Tideglass.

**NINE PEOPLE CAME TO A ROOM THEY ARE NOT ON ANYTHING FOR, TWO PIECES OF PAPER IN ONE WOMAN'S HANDWRITING WENT ON THE TABLE, SOMEBODY ASKED FOR THE SHEET ON A WALL IN ANOTHER CITY, NOBODY COULD PRODUCE IT, AND THE ROOM WENT ON.**

The room is let out on Sundays to a group that meets every three months, and it is free on Sundays because the people who come work six days and cannot come at any other time, and it was free on the Friday evening in February because the woman who has the key to it was in it and does not charge anybody for the gas.

She had turned down a hall in the parade that charges four pounds a head back in November, out loud, to a man who had come to tell her he had a van, and nobody had asked her about it since.

About nine people came. They are not a committee and they have not agreed to be anything. They are people who have been in that room at some point in the last two years and have kept coming, and one of them has a key, and one of them takes the minutes, and the rest of them turn up, and nobody is required to come and none of them has been asked to.

**AND THE ONLY LATTICE NOTICE ON THE NOTICEBOARD OF THAT ROOM THAT WINTER, printed at about the height of a person of average height, and it defines nothing, and nothing on it defines *holder*:**

> **A HANDWRITING IS NOT THE THING THAT WAS WRITTEN.**
> **A COPY OF A SHEET IS NOT A SHEET.**
> **NEITHER OF THOSE IS A REFUSAL.**

Nell Ardent came in at about ten to eight with her bag and drank a cup of tea and said nothing for about twenty minutes, and then she put two pieces of paper on the table and squared them up to the edge of it, which is a thing about her.

"They are not the sheets," she said. "I want that said first, because I know what this room is going to do with them and I would rather it did it with the right object in front of it. I have never had a sheet. I have had a wall, and an office in a city on the other side of an estuary, and a plastic bag, and a fortnight, and a woman in a corridor who could not finish a sentence."

She put the first one down.

That is off the wall in a corridor. One line, six inches of type, four inches of white underneath where a second line has been taken off and not replaced. *Nothing is required of any person in order to use any part of this system.*

"That is the whole of what a town put on a wall that about two hundred people a day walk past, and it is a true sentence, and it is the best sentence I have read in about a year, and I have not got a single thing against it."

The second one was the same first line, in the same size, with a second line under it at the same size and the same space above. *A person has no standing in respect of anything recorded under this.*

"That is the office copy. Same day, near enough, and both of them carry the date the filters were done in a footnote, which is a footnote about a system that belonged to a company that has been sold twice since." She put her hands flat on the table on either side of them. "Two lines. Same type. Six inches apart. Printed by the same people on the same paper, and nobody in this room is going to be able to tell me which of the two the people were handed, and I have stopped expecting to be told, because being told would mean somebody in that building deciding to tell me, and there is nobody in that building with a department over any of it either, and I have got used to that and I do not like it."

A woman of about sixty-two, who has come to that room about fifty times and is on nothing at all, said the thing the room would have got to in another half hour.

"We could have that on our board," she said. "The top one. Not the other one, I don't know about the other one. Just the one that says nobody has to give anything. We could put it up in here next to the fire notice and people would read it and it would be true while it was up."

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

"I have got it here," said Nell Ardent, and put her hand flat on the first sheet without moving it. "I wrote it out in a corridor on the fourth of December with a pen off my own bag, and I compared the two with a ruler because that is what I do. And I would put it up. I want to say exactly why I am not going to, and it is not because of the second line and it is not because anybody in that town would mind."

"Which is what," said the woman of about sixty-two.

"Because it is my hand," said Nell Ardent. "That is the wall. There is a thing on a wall in a corridor in another city with about four inches of white underneath it where a line was taken off. And if I write it out and stick it up in a room over a shop, then in about two months the only version of it anybody in this borough can see is a piece of A4 in my writing with my initials in the corner, because I always put my initials in the corner. And then that is the sheet. And it will not be the sheet. And it will have been the sheet, because there will not be anything else."

The room did what rooms do, which is to go on, and the man of about eighteen who washes up in a kitchen at the back of the parade was in it and had been twice before and is not on anything and has not asked what any of it is about.

Mrs Obi, who is about thirty-six and has taken the minutes in that room in her own hand on a pad she takes home in a handbag for about two years, wrote that down, and nobody read it over her shoulder, and the pad went into the handbag at about nine o'clock and out of the door with her, and it is not in a field, and nobody in this borough could produce it if they were asked, and she was not asked.

---

The thing that was right about something small belonged to a young woman of about twenty who is at a college four days a week and does the other three in a shop and is not on a list, and it had nothing to do with any of it.

She had been looking at the board by the door since she came in, where the keys are on hooks, and there is a card above them that somebody wrote years ago in biro saying HOW MANY KEYS, and a number under it.

"That's six," she said. "It says six."

"There are seven," said the woman of about forty-two with the key.

"There are seven," said the young woman of about twenty. "And the sixth one is on the string, so if you counted hooks you would have said six and you wouldn't have been wrong about hooks." She put her hand up level with the board without touching it. "And that one has got no label on it and hasn't had one since I started, and everybody knows which one it is, because it's the only one nobody ever uses."

The woman of about forty-two looked at it for a while.

"Right," she said, and made more tea.

She did not write six on a card and change it to seven. She did not take the unlabelled key off its hook and put it in a drawer. She looked at the card for about a minute and a half and said "well" and made more tea, and the young woman of about twenty has not been asked about it since and has not mentioned it, and the card still says six, and there have been seven keys on that board for longer than anybody in that room has been coming.

**AND A SHEET ON A WALL IN ANOTHER CITY WAS ASKED FOR IN A ROOM IN THIS BOROUGH BY A PERSON WHO IS ON NOTHING, AND NOBODY COULD PRODUCE IT, AND IT WAS NOT A REFUSAL BY ANYBODY, AND A YOUNG WOMAN OF ABOUT TWENTY WAS RIGHT ABOUT SIX KEYS AND A CARD, AND THE MINUTES WENT HOME IN A HANDBAG, AND NOTHING IN THAT ROOM WAS MINUTED AND NOTHING IN THAT ROOM WAS RESOLVED.**
