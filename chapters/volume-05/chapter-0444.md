# Chapter 0444 — Half Past Eight

Wednesday the third of October 2018, beginning about seven in the morning and ending about midday, that parade and about a hundred yard of it, in Tideglass.

**A TRAY OF ROLLS WENT DOWN AT HALF PAST EIGHT AND NINE PEOPLE PUT THEM BACK AND NOBODY SAID ANYTHING, AND THE THING NOBODY HERE COULD PUT INTO WORDS A FORTNIGHT AGO IS THE SAME THING AT MIDDAY, AND EVERY OTHER PART OF THIS BOROUGH IS AS IT WAS.**

You can walk the length of that parade in a minute and a half. Down the east side, starting at the water: a bank, a premises that keeps a short day, a premises that does not, a premises with a place in it where a person stands to be served, and then six more, of which two have changed hands since April and one has not opened at all this year. Nobody put those six there for anybody. They are simply the six that are there.

At the top end, past the shop that shuts early, there is a small brick store about seven foot by nine with a plank door, a staple, a padlock and no window. It is opened at about half past six in the morning by whoever is on that round and shut at about midday, and it holds a barrow, a broom, a shovel, a coil of wire, a lead and a can of something orange. Nobody has ever slept in it and there is no bench in it and nothing is ever carried out of it in the evening.

---

Nine people were out on that stretch between about seven in the morning and midday, and not one of the nine had anything to do with the other eight.

Two of them work in the premises that does not close, the one the woman who works in a shop keeps, and were on the flags at eight, smoking. One is the man who works nights, up from the junction and away again past the top. One is a woman who came down about seven with a bag and went up again about ten. One is a man in his forties who came up about seven, was inside the short-day premises about two minutes, and then stood at the top for a quarter of an hour at a wall before he went back down. One is a man in his sixties who came up about nine and stayed at the front of the other one for a while.

The other three were a woman with a dog, a man who walked up in the middle of that morning without stopping, and a girl of about sixteen who let herself into the store with a key and came out of it holding a broom.

That is nine, and each of the nine came out for a private reason of their own, and not one of them had the faintest idea the other eight were under the same sky that morning.

---

At about half past eight in the morning she came out through her own front door of that shop carrying a tray.

It was a flat tray of a bit over two dozen rolls, and she was taking it out to the pavement because a van had been at the kerb and gone, and there is a space at the top of that parade where the crates are stacked on a Saturday, and she was going to put the tray on the stack and bring it in again at about ten.

Her heel went off the edge of the flag by the door. The tray went over. About a dozen rolls went down across the pavement, and a good many of them went into the channel at the edge of it, and the tray itself came down flat and did not break.

It took about half a minute for three people to be in it and about four minutes to finish.

The man in his forties, who was at the top end, came down and picked four rolls out of the channel. The man in his sixties, who was at the front of the shop, picked three off the pavement and two out of the channel. The woman with the dog picked three up and put them on the tray. The man who works nights, coming back down the parade, stopped, picked up two, put them on the tray and went on up the road. A woman who had been standing in the doorway of the shop that shuts early got six off the pavement, carried them in, and brought the tray out again. The girl with the broom had gone in, came out, and got two more.

A cyclist put a hand out, took one off the pavement and carried on.

They did not say anything to each other that is worth putting down. Two of them said excuse me. She said, about four times, "That's it, that's it," and once, to nobody, "Well."

The last two rolls were under the tray against the wall and she got those herself.

The tray went up on the stack at the top of that parade and she brought it in at about ten and put the rolls in, and they were all there and they were all the right way up.

Nobody was told anything. That woman has not mentioned it to anybody and will not, because there was nothing to mention. Neither of the two men at the front of it has raised it either, and nobody who was on that parade that morning will ever bring it up with her. The man who works nights was gone up the road before the tray was on the stack.

At about ten past nine that morning two women were standing outside the shop that shuts early, and one of them said a sentence.

It is one of the four that are going round. The count is the same as it was when this began: four, and no fifth for about two years, and one of them travelling a good deal further than the other three put together, and the remaining three not out of a mouth anywhere since the February before last. Put any two of the four side by side and a person gets nowhere at all, and no one in this borough has ever announced which question any one of them is trying to meet.

The other woman said, "Ah," and went in.

The woman who said it stood outside for a moment and then went in as well, and neither of them has been up there again.

The question behind them is where it always was, unanswered and unasked. Nobody put it to anybody else along that parade that morning. Four is still four. Not one of the four has been withdrawn by the person who is carrying it, and the person who said this one at ten past nine will say it again in about four months to somebody who has not asked for it.

There is a building by that water with a room at the end of a corridor in it, and a shelf in that room, and a book standing open on the shelf.

Nineteen entries and no more. The nineteenth is the last thing anybody wrote in it, and whatever that line says is what it says, and nobody has been up there to add to it since March. Ruled underneath, waiting, a strip of nothing the width of a pencil line.

The third of October found that strip of nothing one thousand four hundred and seven days old. It is not a number that anybody in this borough holds in their head. The room at the end of that corridor was locked on the Wednesday and it was locked on the Tuesday and it will be locked on the Saturday. Nobody walked up there, nobody put a mark on that line, and the rest of the page is as blank as the strip.

Ask any person living here to set down in words the one thing about this place that stands out, and you would get a shrug before you got the end of the sentence. It has not shifted since the middle of August. Nobody has raised it and nobody is going to.

**A GIRL OF ABOUT SIXTEEN WENT BACK INTO A BRICK STORE AT THE TOP OF THAT PARADE WITH A BROOM, AND AT MIDDAY SOMEBODY CAME OUT AND SHUT IT AND WALKED DOWN.**
