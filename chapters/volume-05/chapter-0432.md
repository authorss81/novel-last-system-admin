# Chapter 0432 — Since Before Christmas

Wednesday the eighth of August 2018, and the five months before it, one house on a side street off that parade, and one front room in it, in Tideglass.

**SHE HAS BEEN LIVING WITH IT SINCE THE FIRST COLD WEEK OF DECEMBER AND SHE IS THE ONLY PERSON WHO KNOWS, AND HE STOOD IN THAT ROOM FOR AN HOUR AND A HALF IN AUGUST AND LOOKED AT IT AND ASKED HER ONE QUESTION AND WENT ON HANGING THE PICTURE, AND TONIGHT SHE WILL LIGHT IT AGAIN.**

The house is at the end of a short row on a side street off that parade and it is the second one in, and it is a two-up-and-down with a small yard behind it, and the woman who lives in it has been there since the summer of 2016.

The front room is the room at the front. It has a chimney breast on the left-hand side going up to a chimney stack on the gable, and the flue from that fire comes down inside the breast and turns into a hood behind the breast and goes out through the stack.

Since the first cold week of December that fire has been bringing the smoke back into the room.

Not much. Not the way a fire behaves when a flue is completely stopped. It is a grey, low, sour sort of smoke that does not go up the chimney and does not come out into the room properly either; it comes out at the throat, spreads along the top of the breast, and goes down the far side into the room, and it is worst in the first half hour and it is always worst when the wind is in the east.

There is a bloom on the plate at the throat of that hood the colour of a thumbprint, and there is a black streak about fourteen inch long down the face of the breast just under the shelf of it, and the top three foot of the curtains on the window side of that room have gone a flat grey and have never come back.

She knows what it is. She has looked at the throat of that hood with a torch about four times. It is not a bird's nest and it is not a broken pot and it is not anything anybody could put a name to.

---

The whole of it, then, over about five months, and none of it to anybody.

In the first week of November a man came to sweep that flue. He came because she had telephoned a number out of a shop directory in the town eight miles off, which is the only way anybody in this borough finds a sweeper at all. He swept it from the top, brought a basket down the stack on a line, said it was clear, and charged her thirty pound, and she paid him in cash, and it was clear and it was thorough and she has never had any reason to think otherwise about it.

In the second week of November she lit that fire for about three evenings because the evenings had got dark, and there was nothing wrong with it at all.

In the first week of December the cold came in properly. She lit that fire on the first or second of December because she was cold in that room, and it was fine on the first night and on the second, and on the third night she sat in a settee with her back to the chimney breast and had a strange sweetish taste in the back of her throat and thought, that is the smoke, and got up and opened the top of that window about four inch and the smoke went out of it and the room was all right by the time she had made the bed.

On the fourth night she did the same thing. On the fifth night she did not bother, because she had got used to it, and the room was grey about half an hour and then it was not.

She has got that word about her house that other people in this borough use about their own, and she has never once said it out loud to anybody, and it is not that she has agreed to it.



The second week of January, a cough started. It is not a bad cough and it is not a cough that stops her doing anything. It is a cough in the morning when she gets up and a cough in the evening when she has been in that room for about an hour.

In the third week of January she took a bus to the town eight miles off and went into a chemist and said she had a cough, and the woman behind that counter asked her how long and she said since Christmas, and the woman behind that counter said since Christmas, and sold her a bottle of something and told her to go to her doctor if it was still there in a month.

She was not still there in a month. It is still there now.

In the second week of February there was a wet fortnight and the heating in that house did not come on until the sixth, and she dried her washing on a frame in the middle of that front room for four days because there was nowhere else in that house to dry anything. That fire was going the whole four days because that fire is the only heat in that room. She opened the top of the window about four inch on the first day and got used to the smell on the second and did not open it at all on the third and fourth.

At some point in that fortnight she moved that settee out about six inch off the chimney breast, because the smoke came down that side and the settee was in it.

She has never put it back. The six inch is where it is. It is not in the way of anything and there is no reason for it to be there and it has been there for six months.

In March she stopped using that fire for a fortnight because it had got to the point where she did not want to walk into that room and then she went back and lit it again, because it is the warm room and the other one is nine degrees colder and she is fifty-odd and she is going to be cold in the other one for the rest of her life.

In April her son rang on a Sunday, which he does, and she told him about the roof and about the yard and about a woman two doors down who is moving, and she had worked out about half of what she was going to say about that chimney by the time he rang and she said none of it, and put the phone down afterwards and thought about the other half.

In May a man came to fit a shelf in that room and was in it for about three quarters of an hour and did not look up.

---

On the Wednesday the eighth of August a man in his forties was in that front room for an hour and a half with a set of steps he had carried in himself.

She had asked him to hang three things. Two of them are hers and the third is her son's and it is a big one, about two foot by three, and it is a thing that wants two people on it and she has not got anybody and he had said on the Monday that he would do it on his own and that it would take longer, and she said that was all right.

He had the big one up at about ten to four and was standing on his own steps with his shoulder about a foot off that chimney breast, holding it with his left hand and getting a pencil out of his pocket with his right, and he looked down at the shelf of that chimney breast.

There was the black streak on the face of it and the bloom round the throat and the grey along the top of the curtains.

He looked, took it in for about a second and a half, and then moved the step about a foot and a half across so that he could see up under the hood, and he was up there about ten seconds, and then he came down and turned round and looked at it from the floor.

"You've had that fire going," he said.

She said, "Since the start of December."

"Mm," he said.

"Since before Christmas," she said. "I had it swept in November and it was clear. It was clear, the man said, and I paid him."

He did not say anything for about four seconds.

"Where does it come out," he said. "When it's going. Here, or at the front of it."

"It comes out along the top," she said, "and it goes down that side. That's why the settee's forward."

He looked at the settee, and at the six inch between the back of it and the breast, and at the black streak.

"You've been sleeping in here."

"I sleep in here," she said.

That was the whole conversation. It took about half a minute and neither of them raised their voice and neither of them said one word about what ought to be done about it, and he did not tell her to stop using that fire and he did not tell her that somebody could come and look at it and he did not say her name.



He went on hanging the picture.

He checked it twice and did it again and got it right twice, which is not something that happens, and he was on those steps for about fifty minutes in total. He put the two smaller ones on either side of the breast at about a foot three and about a foot eight, and those two came out the same height as each other and a different height from the big one, and that is because the big one is on the stack side and the other two are not.

She sat on a stool at the other end of that room with her hands in her lap for most of that and watched him and did not talk, and he did not talk.

At half past five he came down off the steps for the last time, put the steps by the front of the room, and said the price, and she paid him, and he went.

Nobody in this borough knows about that flue except the two of them. Nobody has been telephoned and nobody has been asked to come, and there is no report of it and there is nowhere in this borough that a report of it would go.

It has been eight months and there has been no accident and there is no fault and nobody is liable for anything, because what has been happening in that room is a draught and a fire and a hole in a chimney and a woman who wants to be warm, and there is no anybody in this city that can make that woman sit in the cold instead.

She will go in there at about half past seven tonight and put a light on and open the top of that window about four inch, because the wind will be in the west, and the fire will go, and she will sit with her back to a settee that is six inch too far forward, and she will be in that room for about three hours.

She has not decided to do that. She has decided nothing at all. A house that has gone quiet about what is happening inside it has not agreed to anything, and nobody has ever asked it.

**HE CAME DOWN OFF THOSE STEPS AT HALF PAST FIVE AND TOOK HIS MONEY AND WENT OUT, AND SHE LIT THAT FIRE AGAIN AT HALF PAST SEVEN, AND NOBODY HAS BEEN ASKED TO DO ANYTHING.**
