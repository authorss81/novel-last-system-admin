# Chapter 0219 — Not Worse

Thursday the twenty-eighth of January 2016, from about half past four in the afternoon, a parade of shops in Tideglass, and then a hall off the parade.

**HE HANDED A PIECE OF WORK TO A PERSON WHO HAD NOT AGREED TO BE ANYTHING AND HAD NOT ASKED TO HAVE IT, HE HAD RUN OUT OF THE THING HE WAS GOOD AT, THE OTHER PERSON DID IT A DIFFERENT WAY AND DID IT BETTER, AND NOBODY WAS GIVEN A TITLE OR A SEAT OR A FORM FOR IT, AND THE ONLY PERSON WHO SAID SO OUT LOUD SAID IT ON A FRIDAY AFTERNOON TO THE MAN WHO HAD DONE IT.**

The hall off the parade is free on a Sunday evening and it is free because nobody rents it, and the woman of about forty-two has had the key to it for about nine years, and she lets it out on a Saturday morning for a class and on a Wednesday for a mothers' group, and she is on no committee and there is no committee, and letting it out is done by a key and by a notebook she keeps in a drawer and by whoever turns up.

Two letters. Both of them are binding on her because both of them say that the room is hers to let for that evening, and both of them were written by a person who had a right to write it, and one of them is dated the seventh of January and one the eleventh, and they are for the same Sunday in February, and the first one says a christening lunch and the second one says a leaving party for about thirty people.

She had them both in a plastic wallet because that is what a plastic wallet is for.

"They're not cancellative," she said, when Jonas Mercer came in. "That's not me. That's not even me saying that, that's the woman at the letting place saying it, and she says one of them has to go and she won't tell me which one."

"You've asked her which one."

"I've asked her about four times since Christmas and she says it's the one with the later date on it, because that's how it works with a shop, and it isn't how it works with a shop, it's how it works with a hire agreement, and this is a hall with a key on a hook and a woman who has never in her life been wrong about a room." She put the wallet down. "And then I thought of you."

"Of course you did."

"Well, you were the one who told me in November about the square, so."

"I never told you about the square."

"You were in my room in November and you were in a mood," said the woman of about forty-two, "and I am not going to argue with a mood in my own doorway. Can you tell me which one's the right one."

"No," said Jonas Mercer.

He said it standing up, in about a quarter of a second, and she had not expected it, and Jonas Mercer has said yes to about four people a week for a year and seven months and has never once said no to one who wanted something off him.

---

**AND THE ONLY LATTICE NOTICE IN THE BACK OF THAT SHOP THAT AFTERNOON, printed and fixed above the till at about the height of a person of average height, and it defines nothing, and nothing on it defines *holder*:**

> **TWO THINGS CAN BOTH BE TRUE OF A ROOM.**
> **ONE OF THEM CAN STILL HAVE TO GIVE.**
> **NEITHER OF THOSE IS A RULING.**

He had been able to answer that question until about nine o'clock on the morning of the eleventh of November and not one minute after it, and there were two people in this city with a department over anything to do with it and he was not going to ask either of them, and he had not told her any of that and was not going to now.

"I've been the person who says which of two things outranks the other," he said, "since the fifteenth of August. That's it. That's the whole of what I've been for. And I can't do it now, and I am not going to be able to, and there is nobody I am willing to ask, and it has been seven weeks and I have not said that out loud to anybody and I am saying it now to a woman with a plastic wallet because otherwise she will keep asking me."

The woman of about forty-two looked at him for a second and then said, "Well," and picked the wallet up and held it out to him, and he did not take it, which she had not expected either.

"You take it," she said. "I have asked you. You're the one. Go on."

He did not take it. He said, "No. You keep it. But I am going to take the question to somebody who is better at it than I am, and I am going to be wrong about how he does it, and that is going to be fine."

---

Tomas Renn was behind the counter at the shop at the end of the parade with the shutter half down and a carrier bag of stockings he was putting away, and he is a shop steward, forty-four, and he has been on the other side of a counter answering that exact question since he was nineteen.

Jonas Mercer put it on the counter without preamble, which is a thing Renn noticed, because Jonas Mercer does not put anything down without four sentences first.

"The seventh and the eleventh of January. Same Sunday in February. A room. A woman with a key to it. Both letters say the room. She wants to know which one wins and she has been told the later one and she does not believe it, and she is right not to believe it, and I cannot tell her, and I am not going to be able to, and that is the whole of it."

Renn did not pick it up.

"Have you asked the two people who wrote them?"

"No."

"Then you have not got a question about the dates," said Renn. "You have got a question about the room. Those are two different things and the first one is a date and the second one is a person, and you have never once in your life been able to leave the first one alone."

He put the stockings down.

"Which one wants thirty people and a fuss," he said, "and which one wants twenty and a table?"

"I don't know."

"Right." He took the wallet off the counter and handed it straight back, unopened, and that was the moment Jonas Mercer understood that Tomas Renn was not going to be careful with this at all, and was going to be extremely careful with it, and that these were the same thing. "Because if it's the thirty, then this is about a date and the date wins and she's got no problem, and if it's the twenty, then it's about a room and it's got nothing to do with a date at all. And I bet you know which one it is and you didn't want to say, because you'd have looked like you'd been in a mood about it again."

The woman of about forty-eight was in the shop at about twenty past five that evening and the woman of about twenty-one was in on the Saturday morning, and Renn saw both of them and asked both of them one question each, and it was the same question.

It was not *which letter is yours*. It was *what do you want the room to be like at four o'clock on the Sunday*, and it took the woman of about forty-eight about four seconds to answer and it took the woman of about twenty-one about four minutes.

And then the two of them did what two people do. They came to the room on the Thursday before together, unasked by anybody, and they worked it out between them on the spot, and the rule they made was not about a date at all. It was that the room is the first one whose name is on the card by the door, and the card has no rule on it and no heading and no date, and it is a piece of card with lines on it and a string through the top of it, and the first one's family came to the room first because the mother rang the woman with the key in December, and the second one has never been in that room and did not know it was a card.

The first letter is in a drawer in a house four miles away and the second is in a plastic wallet in a parade of shops, and neither was cancelled, and both are still binding, and the room is on the first one's night.

"You were right," said Jonas Mercer, when he was told, in the parade, on the Friday, to Renn, who had not asked him to come and had not mentioned it.

"I know."

"No, I have to say it properly," he said. "You were right about it and I was wrong, and I have known I was wrong since about half past four on Thursday and I sat on it for two days because I did not want to hand it over, and that is what I want to say the actual words about. There is nothing I can put next to it. I have got a notebook and there is nothing in this in it, and there is nothing in this on any wall, and if there were a column somewhere I would be the man standing next to it saying that a shop steward did it, and there is no column and you are not on anything and nobody is going to be told."

Renn looked at him.

"You've gone over there," he said. "You've been up the estuary. What did you want?"

"To see what the other shape looks like," said Jonas Mercer.

"And what is it?"

"It's a man," said Jonas Mercer. "It's a man of about eighty-six and five things and a room with a sheet on the wall that says his name, and it is the best of everything and I have not got a sentence for it and I have been trying for a fortnight, and I am not going to say it to you at half past five in a shop because you are a shop steward and that is not a shop."

"Then don't," said Tomas Renn, and went back to the stockings.

Jonas Mercer stood in the parade for a while after that with the shutter coming down over the shop next door, and a woman he has never spoken to went past with a bag of shopping and did not look at him, and the room is on the first one's night, and the card by the door has no heading on it and no date and no rule, and behind that shutter Tomas Renn was doing a shop's Friday for another two hours and it was no more difficult than a Friday.

**AND A DELEGATED THING GETS DONE DIFFERENTLY AND NOT WORSE, AND THAT IS THE THING HE CAME BACK ACROSS AN ESTUARY TO LEARN AND DID NOT EXPECT TO LEARN IT ON A THURSDAY AFTERNOON IN A SHOP, AND HE DID NOT WRITE IT DOWN, AND HE WENT HOME AND DID NOT GET ON A BUS.**
