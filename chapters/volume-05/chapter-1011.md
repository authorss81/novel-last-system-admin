# Chapter 1011 — Whether It Is Right

Undated in this draft.

**THE SECOND BEDROOM AT NUMBER TEN HAS A BED AGAINST THE WALL AND NOTHING ON IT.**

The second bedroom at number ten is a narrow room with one window in the gable end and a bed against the left-hand wall, and the bed is an old iron thing on four legs with a sprung wire base and a woollen mattress on top of it that has a striped ticking on it and nothing else on that. It was made up before the sister went and it has not been slept in since, and there is a coat on the newel at the foot of it and a bag on the floor beside the bag, both of them still packed, which is the state a house is in when somebody who has been away six weeks has come back and has not yet done anything about the fact. The frame is true to the wall it runs against along its whole length and out of true against the window wall by about an inch at the head, so it does not sit parallel to the skirting, and anybody who has ever laid a floor or hung a door in that room has been able to see it from the doorway.

There is a thing about that room that a man sees in the first half-minute and a woman would not, and it is that the bed is under the window in the gable end where a person cannot get round either side of it, so that a person wanting to look at a bed has to stand at the door and look along it. He had done that four times in three weeks, on no occasion that required it.

He came up to that house with the sentence ready. He had had it ready since the middle of the afternoon in the rain and it had been getting longer, and he was aware while coming up the frontage that it had got longer, which is what happens to a thing that has been carried about. She was not in the hall. Her coat was not on the hooks. She was in the front room with her sleeves turned back, and she had her own chisel and a block of wood on the boards in front of her and the sill open along its whole length and a piece of sacking wedged in the corner of it, and she turned round when she heard him in the passage.

"Before you open your mouth about that job," she told him, "go and look at what I have done to your sill."

"I have not looked at anything yet."

"I know you have not, and I have had two days and a lump bar and nobody to tell me and I have made it worse." She stood up off her heels. "So go and look at it. And then tell me whether it is right, because I have been asking two people and one of them was a man from the yard about a price and the other one was my sister, who has not been in this house since before she went up the water."

---

He went out to that front room and got down on one knee in front of the sill so that his eye was level with the bottom of it, and stayed there while she talked, which is what he is for.

He looked at it for a good while and he did it the way he looks at everything, which is from the ends inwards and without touching anything until he has to. What she had done was cut a wedge out of the sill with the point of it instead of taking the whole of the soft corner out in one piece, and the wedge was about the shape of a carrot and about about a foot long, and it had gone in on the line of the old one and had taken six inches of sound wood with it at the far end, and where it had gone in there is now a hole you could put a hand into and daylight is coming through into the room off the yard.

He put his hand flat on the middle rail of the bed frame and pushed down on it and took his hand off and pushed down on it again, which is a thing he does with a bed when a bed is in the room and he has to wait for a woman to finish talking, and it told him nothing except that the wire had gone and the frame is true and the room is what it was.

"No. It is not right."

"That is what I wanted." She did not sit down. "What does it want?"

"Two days, and a new piece about four foot of hardwood, and the reveal cut back to a true line and not to the ragged edge of what is left there, and a board across the opening, and about eight pounds more than the figure I gave you, and a great deal of it at a time when I cannot get hardwood out of my own store because the four foot on my rack is stock and stock is not mine." He went on: "And you have cut into sound wood to get at soft wood, which is the one thing you cannot get back, and daylight is coming through that hole tonight and will be coming through it in the morning."

"It was going to come through in a fortnight."

"In a fortnight it would have come through at that corner and gone round the putty. Tonight it is coming through a hole." He straightened up. "I have not been unkind about it and I am not being unkind now. You did the only thing anybody does the first time, and you have told me about it before I found it, and there are men on this street who would have covered it with a bit of matchboard and kept it quiet."

---

"Then you will put it right," and she put it to him as though she were handing him a sack.

"I will put it right."

"Not when I have money for it in the spring. Now, before my sister is in this house and can see it." She picked the sacking out of the corner and put it under the hole on the floor and weighted it with the chisel so that nothing came through in the night. "The same tin. It is thinner than it was and it will be thinner still, and I have never once in this house had a thing come out of that tin that anybody could see, and there is going to be a new piece of hardwood in the window of this front room with a man's job on it that is not the first one, because the first one is going to look like what it is, which is a woman with a chisel."

He stood where the light from that window came across the boards, with his hands shut hard in his pockets, and did not put the sentence to her. Nobody will ever know he had it ready, and she never had any idea of it, and the not-putting was not because she was standing in the room. He had it. He had had it since the rain. It came to the front of his mouth while she was weighting the sacking under the hole and he put it back, and he put it back because of what had happened in the coal shed a month ago and not because of anything that had happened in the last ten minutes, and he is honest enough with himself to know the difference between those two things and not honest enough to enjoy knowing it.

The bed in the room behind him stands against the left-hand wall with the mattress bare and the wire gone, and it is the only thing in that house that nobody has asked him about and it is square to one wall and out of true to the other, and he has been in and out of that room four times this week looking at a frame he had no reason to look at.
