# Chapter 0712 — The Bag Set To One Side

Tuesday the twenty-second of March 2022, in this borough. She is in her van and at a lock-up from first light until dark and the whole of the day is a bag set to one side and a drawer kept shut.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES MOVES A BAG OF RAGS WITH SOMEBODY ELSE BLANKET IN IT WITHOUT OPENING IT, KEEPS A SHUT DRAWER UNDER THE PASSENGER BENCH SHUT, AND SPEAKS TO A MAN AT THE LOCK-UP ABOUT A TAPED BOX WITH A BAGGED CORNER AND ABOUT A DRIPPING TAP.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is in her van and at a lock-up all day. A man at the lock-up is at the doors at the middle of the day. She speaks to him at the doors.

---

She starts with the van before there is any light in the yard.

The white van is at the kerb with the frost on it, white on blue, wipers lifted. She opens the side door, runners loud in the cold, and looks in without getting in, breath showing. The bolster is on her own board with the drawer under the passenger bench shut with the leads and the screws in it, shut since the middle of November. The box is behind the bench under a bag of rags since the twenty-second of October with a number on its label she read twice and wrote nowhere. The bag with somebody else blanket in it is in with her own rags since the twelfth of November. She moves the bag to one side to get at the broom, lifting with her knees, setting it down square, without opening it, knot tight, blanket inside unseen. She sets it back after, square where it was, and the setting back is a thing that is finished.

She stands at the heap with the bagged corner in front of her and thinks about it while the doors are open and the road is empty. A bowl under a bath tap at number twelve, emptied at first light and at dark. A kettle with a furred spout in a house she cleared last winter. Both go on whether she looks at them or not, and neither of them is hers, and she has found that the easiest kind of thing to stand next to.

---

She drives to the lock-up in the grey and opens up.

The lock-up is cold and square with a heap at the back under a sheet, padlock stiff, doors dragging. She sorts what is hers from what is not, bag by bag, lifting each to feel the weight, and sets the bags in a row, necks tied, labels out. The taped box with the bagged corner is on the heap where she saw it on the seventh of January, with the folded bit of bag over its corner where the damp came through. She saw a heap box with a bagged corner then and left it. She leaves it now.

She does not open it. If she opens it she has to ask whose it is, and asking in a cold lock-up with the doors wide is asking out loud, and she has spent since October not saying things out loud, which is a habit and not a virtue and has never once broken.

---

She eats in the cab at the middle of the day with the doors locked.

Bread and cheese on her knee with the pad on the dash. She writes nothing while she eats. The figure for the job sits in her head, four hours, no invoice, plain. The shut drawer sits under the passenger bench at her elbow. She lays her palm on the front of it without opening it, feeling the woodgrain through the dust. Shut since the middle of November. Bolster on the leads inside. Screws with it. Nothing in and nothing out.

She thinks about the number on the box behind the bench while she eats, read twice, written nowhere. Black on white, figures neat. She could have written it on the pad. She did not. The not-writing sits with the not-asking, and both cost nothing yet and will cost something when they cost it.

---

The man at the lock-up comes at the middle of the day with keys and a list.

He looks at the row of bags and at the heap under the sheet.

"That box been there long," he says, nodding at the bagged corner.

"Since January when I saw it," she says. "Longer by the look of the tape."

"Open it."

"It is not mine to open. I read a number twice off a box in my own van and wrote it nowhere, and I saw a step ladder with tape and chalk in a back room and said out loud I could not tell whether it was the same one. Opening what is not mine is how a ladder gets lent without a number and left in a porch with no note on it."

The man nods and writes nothing down.

---

They drink tea out of paper cups with their backs to the cold, steam short in the air, cups double-wrapped. The row of bags stands square. The heap sits sheeted.

"What would you do about a drip," she says.

"What drip."

"Bath tap. At number twelve. White bowl under it. Emptied at first light and at dark."

He looks at her over the cup.

"Mend it."

"She cannot ask since before Christmas. Mending it wants hands and there is nobody to keep the frame while she goes up the fixed four rungs beside the hatch to see the cistern that weeps at the far side of a joist. So the water keeps coming and the bowl keeps filling, round and white, waiting where it was not meant to stay."

He lets that sit, because there is nothing to say to it.

---

She is in the van with the side door open before the light goes.

She takes the pad out and writes the figure for the job on it and puts no line for anything else on it. The two cuts on both palms are shut now, plaster on one and not on the other. The shut drawer under the passenger bench stays shut. The bolster stays on the leads. The box stays behind the bench under the rags. The bag with somebody else blanket stays set to one side without being opened.

She borrowed a step ladder on the twenty-ninth of November and left it in an open porch on the third of December with no note on it and has not thought about it since, except she thinks about it every time she sees a ladder. The folding one is folded under a window in a front room at number twelve with tape round the second rail and chalk on the third tread. The fixed four rungs are beside the hatch at the same house. The fibreglass one the lamp man took off his van in the gateway on the twenty-fourth of December is back in his van. The folded one in a back room in a semi in a street she has never worked in has the same two marks and she could not tell. No fifth is staged and none is needed.

---

She sheets the heap at the end of the day with the man watching.

The sheet is grey canvas, stiff with cold. They lay it over the heap together, corner to corner, and tuck the edges under the boxes. The taped box with the bagged corner sits under the sheet with the folded bit of bag over its corner. She squares the sheet over it without lifting the bag. Lifting it would show the damp through the tape at the corner. She leaves it.

"That is done," the man says.

"That is done," she says. "Bags in a row. Heap sheeted. Box left. Drawer left. Kettle left. Ladder left."

She picks up the pad and looks at the figure for the job. Four hours would not cover it. No invoice covers it. Put down plain. The two cuts pull when she closes the pad. Both palms. Plaster on one and not on the other. Shut now.

---

She sweeps the lock-up floor at the end of the day with a stiff broom from behind the doors.

She sweeps round the bag row and under the sheet edge and into the corners by the doors. The dust comes grey in the low light. She tips it in a bag and ties the bag. The floor shows the tyre marks where the van stood. She sets the broom back behind the doors. The shut drawer in the van outside stays shut. The bolster stays on the leads. The box stays under the rags. The bag with somebody else blanket stays set to one side.

She stands at the doors with her hands in her pockets and looks at the sheeted heap. The water runs somewhere in the pipes over the lock-up, bead and run, bead and run. She thinks about the bowl while it runs, round and white under a bath tap, emptied at first light and at dark. She thinks about the kettle with the furred spout. She thinks about the bucket cold on a yard slab. All three go on whether she sweeps or not.

---

She counts the bags at the end of the day in a row by the doors.

Six bags, tied square, names none. She lays her palm on each in turn, feeling what gives and what does not. Soft things. Hard things. Things that rattle. Things that do not. The heap sits sheeted behind them with the taped box under the sheet. She does not lift the sheet to look at the bagged corner. Leaving it sheeted is a thing that is finished.

---

She oils the lock-up doors at the end of the day with a rag from the van.

The hinges scream when the doors move. She rubs the rag along each hinge, top and bottom, working the oil into the pins. The doors swing easier after, though they still drag at the sill. She wipes her hands on the rag and puts it back in the van. The man watches from the doors with the keys in his hand.

"Better," he says.

"Better," she says. "Hinges oiled. Floor swept. Heap sheeted. Bags counted. That is the day."

She looks at the sheeted heap while the doors swing. The taped box sits under the sheet with the bagged corner. The water runs in the pipes over the lock-up. The bowl fills on the other side of the borough. All three go on.

---

At dark she locks the lock-up and gets in the van.

She sits in the cab with the engine off and looks at the shut drawer without opening it. The bowl fills in a bathroom under a landing on the other side of the borough. The kettle sits furred on a worktop in an empty house. The bucket sits cold on a yard slab. All three wait, and the water keeps coming, and nobody is asked why, not once.
