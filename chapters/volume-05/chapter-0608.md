# Chapter 0608 — The Tables

The Saturday of the fifth of December 2020. It runs from about ten in the morning to about five in the afternoon. In this borough.

**HE HAS BEEN STANDING AT THE DOOR OF HIS MOTHER'S HOUSE ON SATURDAYS SINCE BEFORE THE SUMMER AND HE CARRIES THE KEY AND HE CARRIES A CHAIR, AND TODAY HE CARRIED A SECOND CHAIR OUT OF THE DOOR AS WELL, AND THE WOMAN WHO HAS BEEN IN THAT HOUSE FOR MOST OF A DECADE IS AT THE WINDOW WHEN HE DOES IT.**

He is a man in his late twenties. The house is his mother's and she is not in it and has not been in it since before the summer, and the woman inside is in her late sixties and has the care of it and does not live in it.

---

The key has been his since the March and he has not used it since the September, and he turns up at about ten on a Saturday and puts the key in and finds that the woman has changed the cylinder at some point in the summer without telling anybody, and that he cannot get in.

That is not a thing anybody has done to him. The woman inside is sixty-eight and lives there and it is her door.

He has been standing at that door about seven times since before the summer.

He knows what the house is like. That is the thing about the key.

He knows there is a leak in the roof of the back extension that has been there since about 2018 and that he has had a ladder in the garage for it since the March and never gone up. He knows the boiler in the bathroom is on the third setting and should be on the second, and that his mother had it on the third because she liked the water hot. He knows the sill in the front room needs the bottom corner re-seated and he could do it in a morning. He knows the gate at the back sticks and has to be lifted while you shut it, and that his mother used to put a brick against it so she did not have to, and the brick is still there.

He has never seen any of it since before the summer.

What he has instead is a map of the house that he carries, and he carries it the way the key is a key and not the way a person carries a fact, and he came to the door this morning with the key in his hand and the chair under his arm.

Here is what happened at about ten.

He put the key in and turned it and it went as far as it goes and no further, and he took it out and put it back in again and did the thing that people do with a key that has been changed, which is to turn it slightly further than you would, in case.

And the door opened about two inches and stopped.

And the woman said, "It's locked. You'll want to knock."

And he said, "I'll knock."

And she opened the door about a foot and stood in the gap.

And he said, "I'm not stopping."

And she said, "I know."

And he said, "I brought a chair."

And she said, "There's two chairs in that room."

And he said, "There's two chairs. They've been in that room since she was in it, and one of them has got a cushion on it and the other one hasn't."

And the woman said, "It's a chair."

And he said, "I'll carry it round to the garage."

He carried it round to the garage in about twenty steps through the side gate, and put it in there at the side of the ladder, and came back.

He was in the front room with the woman for about twenty minutes and they did not talk about the house.

What they talked about was that the woman on the second floor over the road has had a chest infection and the woman had taken her a casserole on the Tuesday, and that the man who does the bins had changed the day again, and that the road is doing something on the Friday that neither of them knows what it is.

He has been in that room seven times and every one of them has gone like that. There is a rule and neither of them has ever said what it is.

Here is what he thinks the rule is. He thinks that the woman came into that house to be somewhere, and that she did not come to be somewhere that gets looked at, and that he comes on a Saturday, and that a woman who is sixty-eight and doing this job alone and who has never once asked him for anything is not going to say *will you look at the roof* in a front room.

So he does it in the garage. He took the sill off the front-room window before he came out, in about ten minutes, with the chisel out of the bag, and she stood in the room and watched him do that as well and asked him nothing about it either.

He was in the garage at about half past twelve with the sill up on the bench and the ladder out with one foot on the bottom rung and the brick in his hand.

The sill is a length of painted wood about the width of the door and the corner at the left-hand end had come away from the frame behind by about a foot before he had it off, and it is the kind of job that takes most of a morning if the paint has gone hard on the joint and half of it if it has not, and the paint on this one has gone hard on the joint.

He got the old paint off with a chisel and a hammer and made a mess of the top of the bench and put his hand down for somewhere to put the hammer and put it into the paint tin that was there for such a thing and it had gone over his glove.

He stood and looked at the mess.

He got the new piece of bead out of the bag he had in the car and got it cut to length on the old offcut on the bench and got it right, which he was able to do because he has been measuring that frame in his head since the March.

And he got the bead in and got it level and got two screws in and then stopped with the third one in his fingers.

Here is why he stopped, and he stood in that garage with the hammer and he went through it, and it is the whole of it.

The third screw is the one at the far end, at the right-hand side, and when the sill goes back in it goes through the sill and into a piece of wood behind the frame which in his mother's day was a stud and is now, since they took the wall out in about 2016 and put a skin over it, a piece of a joist that is not in the right place.

He found that out in the June and did not do anything about it. He drilled for it, hit something that was not a joist, filled the hole, and went home, and it has been sitting in his head since the June.

So that screw will go in about halfway and then stop, and there will be a face on the front of it, and it will not hold a sill over about twenty years of weather, and he knows that, and he has known it since the June, and he can do the thing that would make it hold, which is to shift the bead along by about an inch so that the last screw goes through the frame where there is solid wood instead of into the joist, and he could do all of that on this bench in ten minutes and then he would have to put it back.

It is not a long job and he knows exactly how long it is.

He stood there with it.

What he went through, in this order:

One: the bead is cut and there are two screws in the near end of it and there is a third screw in his pocket and there is a chisel mark on the top of the bench, and the sill is sitting on the bench instead of sitting on the frame in his mother's front room, and if he leaves it now then that corner will come away again, and it will come away with everything the weather has done to it on it, and it will be worse to do than it is now.

Two: if he does it now then he has moved a bead on his mother's front-room sill that he has been thinking about moving since the March.

Three: doing it now means there is no reason to come back to this sill. And the sills in this house are not the only thing in this house.

And he put the third screw in the pocket of his coat and put the hammer down and put the ladder up against the wall and came out of the garage and shut the gate, and it stuck, and he had to lift it and shut it, and the brick is where it has always been.

---

The woman was at the front window when he came round the side.

He came up the path and she was standing at the front room window looking out at the front garden and the railings, and the curtains were open behind her and the room was lit.

He stopped on the path.

He has stood at that door seven times and she has not once stood at a window while he was at it.

And he thought, on the path: she is not watching me go.

And he stood there about three seconds and then he understood what she was doing, which was that she was standing where the light was, so that if anybody on that road looked at the house and saw somebody in that window they would see somebody in.

He went home at about five.

The chair is in the garage. The sill is on the bench with two screws in it. There is a third screw in the pocket of his coat that has been in his pocket since half past twelve and is now on a table in his own kitchen under a stack of post, and the frame in his mother's front room has a filled screw hole in it about a foot from the left-hand end.

And he is not going to fix it this month or next month, and he is aware that what he is not going to do is not a delay, because he has already done everything except the last piece, and has done the last piece as a thought, and knows exactly how long that is.

**HE CARRIES THE KEY THAT WILL NOT TURN AND A CHAIR FROM A ROOM HE IS NOT ALLOWED TO KEEP, AND HE HAS GONE ROUND TO THE GARAGE AND GOT THE SILL HALF RIGHT AND STOPPED WITH THE LAST SCREW IN HIS POCKET BECAUSE FINISHING IT WOULD HAVE LEFT HIM NO REASON TO GO BACK, AND THE WOMAN INSIDE THE HOUSE STOOD AT A LIT WINDOW WHILE HE CAME ROUND THE SIDE.**
