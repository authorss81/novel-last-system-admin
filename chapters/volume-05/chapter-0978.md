# Chapter 0978 — Under The Propped Rim

Undated in this draft.

**A GALVANISED BATH STANDS UPRIGHT ON ITS RIM AT THE SHED END WITH ONE BRICK UNDER THE OTHER.**

The bath stands over on its rim where the shed meets the wall at number twelve, with its foot against the brick and its head away from it, and nobody has shifted it since before the wall was last pointed. It is an ordinary galvanised bath of the kind that came out of a kitchen in a house that no longer stands, and it is about five feet long and two feet wide and a foot and a quarter deep.

Turned on its side like that, it is resting along the length of one of its two rims. About a foot in from the near end, that rim has lifted a couple of inches off the flags and is being kept off them by a single brick laid flat across the space, and the brick has been doing that job long enough to have taken the print of the rim on its top face.

The rim that is down is bedded in its own grit and stands about a quarter of an inch into the flags where the yard has settled beneath it. Between the down rim and the lifted one the shell of the bath curves away, and that curve, closed at both ends by the brick and by the bath's own foot, is a space a hand can go into and nothing else can.

The galvanising on the rim has gone white and thin where a foot gets on it and is bright still where the roof water runs across it, and along the underside of the lifted rim the zinc is damp from the flags all night and dries by the middle of the day. The brick under it is old and soft at its arrises and damp through its whole depth in wet weather.

The inside of the bath is dry and pale and carries three coats of paint of different colours, the last of them a green that has gone chalky. Nothing has been put in it and nothing is going to be, and the bottom of the inside is clear of litter except for a leaf that came over the wall one autumn and is still lying where it fell.

The yard is laid in flags of the ordinary kind with a channel along the wall that takes the roof water of the house, and the shed stands at the far end of it with its doors open on a timber track and its felt along the eaves that runs about three inches short of the brickwork above it. The three inches are the reason for the damp line on the ground beneath that eaves and for nothing else.

A brick wall runs along the left of that yard with two courses on the top of it, and it is out of true where the roots have got under the footing and have come back again in the winter. The bricks of the coping are the colour of weak tea and have a bloom on the north side of them that comes off on a hand and goes on the hand's coat for a while after.

The coal shed takes up the width of the yard at the near end, with a timber lid over it and a wall of stacked logs inside the door. The lid is warped into a shallow bow by rain and sun and it does not shut at its far corner, so the logs at the top of the stack are grey with weather and the logs at the bottom of it are not.

Under the eaves of that shed the flags are dry in a strip about two feet wide along the drip line, and outside that strip they are not. The shed roof is higher at the front than at the back, which puts the drip line a little way out from the wall, and the woman who stands under it is on the dry side of that line with the roof over her. A bird bath on a short post stands in the middle of the yard and is not used, and the sandstone kerb along the edge of the flags by the gate is damp at the joint of every two stones and dry along the top where the sun gets at it.
---

The hand goes down over the lifted rim into the space beneath it, fingers first, and pushes inward and downward along the curve of the shell until the skin at the wrist will not pass and stops it there.

What stops the wrist is not the shape of the bath. It is the brick, which stands a little in from the lifted rim rather than at it, so that the hand has to go past the brick to get under the shell and cannot go straight at it. The depth of the space under the rim is about a hand's width, and the wrist arrives at the place where the brick begins.

Going past the brick, the hand finds the whole width of the space at once, and the depth of it is not the same along the run. At the end where the rim is still bedded, the space closes to nothing within a foot of the foot end, and at the other end it opens out to about a foot before the bath curves down to meet the ground again.

Under the shell the ground is not the flags. The brick was laid on a patch of earth that came up when the flags were lifted to put it down, and that patch has been tamped flat and has settled into a shape of its own, so that under the middle of the bath it sits about an inch lower than the flags either side. It is loose earth with the bottom of an old turf in it, and the hand can feel the edge of the turf with the pads of the fingers.

Nothing gives. The rim does not stir where the hand pushes against it, the brick does not roll, and the bath itself does not move an inch on the flags or sound at all where it is bedded. What is under the earth is the subsoil of the whole yard, which is the same firm stuff the wall is standing on. The hand comes out from under the rim with a smear of the old damp brick dust across the wrist, and the bath is left standing on the same brick in the same place, and the space under the rim is the same shape it was.

The three coats of paint inside the bath are worth what they are worth to whoever comes next to look at them. The bottom one is a cream, thin and covered with the grain of the galvanising so that it only shows in the hollows of it. Over that is a blue, thicker, cracked into squares along the curve of the bottom. Over that is the green, which has gone chalky on every surface the damp has had at it and is still bright where the last coat was put on too thick and never had to dry properly. Where the shell has stood on grit for thirty years there is a dark line all round the down rim and a paler one round the up, one for the side that gets walked over and one for the side that does not.

The bath was a bath. It had a tap on it once and a plug hole at the other end, and both of them are still there, the tap a stub of iron with the handle long gone and the plug hole a ring of rust with no plug in it. There is old lime round the stub where it was bedded in, gone hard and cracked away from the iron it was set into.

Where the shed felt stops short of the brick above it, there is a bead of old mortar in the joint that has come away from the brick along the length of about two feet and stands out from the wall in a shallow curve. Nothing has been packed into it and nothing will be, and the water that comes down the wall runs into the crack and out along it and never reaches the ground. The wall behind the bath is of old brick laid in English bond, and one course near the bottom has been pushed out of true by something that struck it from the yard side, which left the face of it a shallow bow that has not been straightened since.

The sister stands under the eaves of the coal shed with her hands behind the line of her coat, and she stays under the drip line and does not come out past it. The hand comes up out of the space under the rim about the time she arrives, and there is old brick dust across the wrist, and she looks at the yard and at the roof and not at the bath.

"There was a serial on that at the weekend."

"I did not hear it."

"It ran to two hours."

"Then I missed the last of it." The talk goes on to a cat that has been coming into the wash-house and has taken up with the coal shed, and whether it belongs to anybody, and the reply is that nobody has laid a hand on it and nobody has chased it off.

Her hand goes up towards the shed door for the latch on her way out and stops a good inch short of it, and comes back down to her side, and the shed doors stand open on their track where they have stood all winter.

The bath stands on the same brick on the same flags with the same hand's width of space under its lifted rim, and the brick will take the weight of it through another winter without moving.
