# Chapter 0821 — Cut Back To Where The Leaf Is

Monday the nineteenth of June 2023, along his own boundary at that unit from the middle of the morning until the shadow of that shed has crossed the whole of it, and a length of privet that has gone woody at the bottom is the whole of that Monday.

**A RUN OF PRIVET ALONG THE BACK BOUNDARY OF HIS OWN YARD AT THAT UNIT HAS GONE OVER THE FENCE AND LOST ITS BOTTOM TWO FEET TO WOOD, AND HE MEANS TO CUT IT BACK TO WHERE THE LEAF IS, AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN IS IN THAT YARD AT THE START OF THE DAY WITH A BUCKET AND DOES NOT STAY.**

That privet is about six feet of it along the back boundary of that yard, between the end of the block wall and the far corner, planted against the fence and never very much of a hedge.

It was planted as a hedge and it has never been one. It was put in about five feet apart and it has grown to about four feet high and four feet through in the middle and it has gone woody along the bottom two feet, which is what a privet does when it is planted and then ignored and then looked at once a year.

The bottom two feet of it have not got any leaves on them at all. They are stems, six of them, about an inch through, going straight up out of the ground and leaning over the fence behind them, and the leaves begin about two and a half feet up.

The whole of that hedge is on one side of the boundary, which is the fence, and the fence is close boarding with a wire above it, and the privet has gone up through both of them and over.

---

He is out at that boundary with shears, a pruning saw, a piece of sacking and a barrow, and he begins about as far in the morning as he can see to work.

That is a job about an hour and a half if you know where the cut goes, and he does not know where the cut goes yet, so the first hour of it is finding out.

A privet is not difficult to cut. It is soft-wooded and it cuts like anything else with a pair of shears, and it will let you cut it anywhere you like, at any height, at any angle, and it will not complain.

What it will do is tell you about it afterwards.

Privet is the only common hedge anybody grows that will not make a cut back into bare wood. Cut privet into old wood and it throws three or four thin shoots out of the cut point, all of them upright, all of them soft, and in two years the place where you made the cut is a tuft of five thin stems where there was one stem, and the outside of that hedge is a mess of uprights instead of a face.

Cut it back to the leaf and it makes one good shoot at each cut and closes up inside two seasons.

So the cut has to be at the leaf line, which is a line he has to find in six feet of hedge by looking at it, and the line is not straight and not even and not the same height at any two points along that six feet, because a hedge that has grown on its own for five years is not a shape anybody made.

---

The woman from two doors down comes into that yard about a quarter past the middle of the morning with a bucket.

She is at the near end, where the yard is about thirty feet wide and the paving goes up to the office cabin, and the privet is along the back boundary with a good twenty feet of grass between it and where she is standing.

She is not here for long. She fills the bucket out of the trough by the shed and she takes it out through the near gate and she does not come in out of the way at all.

He does not mention the privet and she does not ask about the privet, and there is nothing about the hedge in that yard that would tell anybody she had been in it.

He finds the line by going along the hedge in about six-foot stretches and putting his hand flat on it.

From the front, privet reads as a solid face, and from the front you cannot tell where the leaves stop and the wood begins, because the leaves of a hedge that has gone over in the middle come away from the wood behind them and hang across it, and the face of it looks full from six feet away and about four feet out.

With a hand on it you can feel it. There is a difference between a hedge that is leaves and a hedge that is leaves hanging over wood, and it is in the give of it, and it takes about a week to learn and it is not something anybody will explain to you.

At the wall end the leaf line is about two and a half feet up. Four feet along it is about three feet. Along the middle it is two and a half feet again and there is a dip in it where the hedge went over last winter and came back from the side instead of the top.

At the far corner it is down at about two feet and the corner is the worst of it, because that is the end of the run and the corner is the end of the light and everything at the end of a hedge has been fighting for what there is.

That corner is where he starts, because it is the worst of it and because a hard cut at a corner is easy to see and easy to judge.

He gets in behind it. There is about two feet of gap between the privet and the fence and he goes in on his knees with a pair of long-handled shears and cuts the six stems at about an inch below the leaf line, all six, one at a time, working round so that he is always cutting from the outside in and never cutting a stem he cannot see the foot of.

An inch below the leaf line is not a rule. It is what he does, and the reason is that a cut made exactly at the leaf line leaves a stem end sitting on the line where it can be seen from the front, and a stem end an inch below it is inside the hedge and gone by the time the new growth has come up round it.

Every one of the six cuts is a flat cut across an inch of privet, and the wood is pale and it smells like a cut green thing and it is wet in the middle even on a dry day.

Two of the six go through with the shears and four want the saw, and the saw goes in from the side because a privet stem will not take a blade from the top at an angle without the blade closing on itself.

He does the whole six feet in about an hour and a half with the corner done first and the wall end last.

The arisings go in the barrow.

A privet cutting does not rot down. That is the thing about it and it is worth knowing before you make a heap of one. Privet is full of tannin and it is a woody evergreen and it goes into a heap and stays a heap — it dries brown, it goes brittle, it goes into the ground and takes a year to get into the ground, and in the meantime it is a barrow of sticks that is in the way.

So the heap goes behind the shed at the far end of that yard, in the two feet between the shed and the boundary, where it will sit.

He does not take it out to the row. There is a skip at the end of the row for another trade and it takes what is put in the top of it and there is a man in this borough who would not put privet in it.

About an hour before the light goes off the top of the far corner he stands in that yard and looks at the six feet of it.

The bottom two feet are gone. What is left is a hedge sitting on a shelf of stems with nothing under it but six inches of gap and the light coming through the whole length of it.

And there behind it, for the first time since the privet was planted, is the fence.

He can see about four feet of it at the corner, and then it goes behind the rest of the hedge, and the four feet is enough.

It is close boarding on a gravel board and it is grey. The boards have gone the colour of a cigarette and they have gone soft along their bottom edges where they sit in the ground, and there is a wire above the boarding on the same posts, and about two feet of that wire is rusted through at the bottom.

The gravel board has gone at one end and the bottom board of the boarding above it is riding on the post. It is a fence that has been doing its job for twenty years with nobody behind it doing anything about it, and there is nothing wrong with it that a week and a bag of cement would not put right, and he does not do that week and there is nobody to ask him to.

The privet cut face is on both sides of him in that corner — one stem going up at a foot eight and one at two foot four, and about sixteen inches between them where the hedge has not closed up and will not until next spring.

He ties the tops of what is left of the hedge back onto the fence with the piece of sacking, because a cut privet left loose will have its new growth come up and go over, and the sacking is what stops that happening in a wind.

He does not tie it tight. Tight ties on a young shoot are a wire round a wrist, and by next July that shoot will be two thirds of the diameter it was.

He stands in that yard for a while looking at four feet of a fence he has not seen since the spring the hedge went in, and it is grey and soft along the bottom and a wire on it is rusted through, and the privet along the rest of that boundary is still standing over him from the place it started and will be bare at the bottom for about a fortnight before it puts a leaf out along those cut stems.

Six feet of it is cut back to the leaf and there is nothing else about that Monday, and about four feet of the grey fence at the corner is out in the open for the first time in five years, and a wire on it is rusted through.
