# Chapter 0805 — The Middle Bay Of The Path

Thursday the sixth of April 2023, in this borough. From the middle of the morning until the light is going she is out on her own front path at number nine, and three flags that have settled since January are the whole of that day.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES HAS THE MIDDLE BAY OF THREE FLAGS ON HER OWN FRONT PATH SETTLED INTO ITS OWN BED SINCE THE JANUARY UNDER A GALVANISED TUB OF IRON AND MEANS TO LIFT THEM AND RELAY THEM ON CHIPPINGS, THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING TAKES UP THE THREE FLAGS BEYOND THAT BAY ON THE SAME MORNING AND RELAYS THEM, AND WHERE THE TWO AREAS MEET THE JOINT BETWEEN THEM IS THE TRUEST LINE IN THE WHOLE NINE.**

Number nine, on that Thursday. She is in her late fifties. The front path runs about nine feet from the bottom of the front step down to the gate, and it is laid in three parts: one flag hard against the step, three in the middle, and three more on toward the gate, with a single flag by itself at the gate end that has always tilted to the rail and is not part of any of this.

The tub came off that middle bay in March and went up onto its shelf under the window, and the middle flag of that bay has not been looked at since the January.

The joint that ought to run between the step flag and the first of the three has opened about the width of a thumbnail at the house end and shut up again at the gate end, which is what a path does when the ground under the middle of it goes down and the two ends stay where they are.

She has a bar, a bolster, a lump hammer and a cold chisel, and she has the barrow, and there is half a tonne of chippings standing on the front garden where the bin usually goes, put down on Tuesday and left there because there was nowhere else to put them.

The spanner belonging to the man who does odd work is lying on the single flag at the bottom of the path, where he dropped it. That flag tilts to the rail and it is not part of any of this, and she steps over it every time she goes in and out.

She starts at the gate end of her three, because that is the end a flag can be slid out towards, and she goes along the whole run with the bolster, tapping each flag about where its centre is until the bolster goes through the joint.

They lift out whole. That is the good news about a path that has been laid properly and the bad news about this one: none of the three has broken and none is bedded in anything that has to be cut. They come up one after the other and go onto the grass of the front garden on the side away from the wall, and the bed underneath them is loose all the way to the bar.

She gets the bar in and levers the loose stuff out into the barrow. It is sand, mostly, grey and fine, and there is brick in it, and it comes out wet at the bottom of the hollow and dry at the top, which is the whole of the story of why those three flags are where they are.

Under the sand, at the four corners of each flag, there are the mortar dabs the flags were bedded on.

Three dabs to a flag, put in with the back of a trowel and never spread, which is what a flag is supposed to be bedded on. They are about the size of a saucer and an inch thick and they are as sound as the day they were put down, and they have not moved, and they cannot move, because they are floating on nothing.

You can put your thumb under the edge of one and it is still there, and it is as hard as a brick. What it is sitting on is the same brown soil as everything else.

The hollow each flag has made for itself is about a quarter of an inch deep at the deepest, and it is not round. It runs the length of the bay in one long dish with the middle of it down furthest, which is the shape of water finding a way along a path and taking the middle of it with it.

She rakes that out, and the last of it comes up with the bar, and the bottom of the hollow is hard enough to stand on.

Then the chippings.

She puts them in with the barrow and she spreads them and she levels them, and the levelling is the whole of the job and the reason anybody does it at all. A straightedge laid across the two outer flags and worked up and down until there is no daylight under it anywhere along its length, and a spirit level on top of that, and then a little more stone where it wants it and a scrape with the back of the bar where it does not.

The shelf under the window is above her shoulder the whole time she is on that side of the path, with the tub of iron standing on it, and she does not need it for anything and does not go near it.

---

The first flag goes back in the middle of that morning, and the second not long after, and by the time the third is out of the barrow there is a person walking up the front path.

She is coming along the path from the front of the house and she does not knock, and she is carrying nothing at all.

The woman of the house is on her knees at the gate end of her three with her back to the front of the house and a bolster in one hand. She hears the gate and she hears the boots on the flags, and then she hears the visitor say, out loud and to nobody in particular, "Right, I will take the far three." It is not a question and it does not get an answer. She does not turn round, because there is nothing about three flags in a front path that anybody has to be told about.

At the far end of the path, the three flags beyond her own have gone up by themselves since the hard weather, the middle one about half an inch proud of the two beside it, and there is a line of moss along the joints of them. The visitor looks down at that for a moment the way anybody looks at a job, and then she takes the bolster off the wall where it leans and goes down on her knees at the gate end of the far three and starts on the one nearest the gate.

The path is about nine feet long. The woman of the house is at one end of it on her knees and the visitor is at the other end of it on her knees, and between them is six feet of path and the single flag at the bottom with the spanner on it.

Neither of them says anything else to the other. There is nothing to say. It is not a conversation and it is not even two people working together. It is two people who can see a job and know whose half of it they are standing on.

She hears the bolster going at the far end. She hears it go four or five times and stop, and then she hears a bar go in, and then the sound of a heap of sand going into a barrow, and that is the whole of what she hears of the other half for about two hours.

She is in and out of the kitchen for the length of that afternoon with the back door on the latch, and she does not go out to the front while she is in there, because her hands are covered in grey sand and she does not want it on the door frame. Twice she stands at the kitchen window and twice the front of that house is out of sight of her.

She puts her third flag down, and she rakes the chippings out level along the top of it to the joint at the far side, because chippings that stop short of the edge are chippings that wash away the first time it rains hard, and she does it with the back of the bar so that there is no step where the stone meets the joint.

---

The visitor goes before the light goes.

There is no knock and there is nothing left on the wall and the barrow is where it was when the woman of the house came out of the house. The far three flags are down and the stone under them is black and wet and raked flat, and the line where they meet the three flags that were already down is a joint you could put a thumbnail into and not much more.

She puts a straightedge across the whole run, house end to gate end, with a spirit level on top of it, and moves the straightedge a foot at a time the whole way down.

There is one place where the straightedge rocks, and it is at the gate end of the single flag that tilts to the rail, and she was not going to touch that flag either.

The joint in the middle of the path — the line where the flags she laid meet the flags somebody else laid, on the same morning, with chippings out to the same level from both sides — is the one line in that run she has to look at twice to find.

She goes round the front of the house to the barrow and tips the last of it out on the grass where she has been putting it all day.

That is the heap that came out from under her three flags, and it is about four barrowloads of it and it takes up more ground than the bay it came out of.

It is not stone. It is not the brick and rubble and the blue engineering stone that a path is laid on. It is brown soil, dark and fine and full of root, and there are worms in it, and in the middle of the heap there is the shape of a boot sole pressed into it a long time ago by somebody standing on the ground before the path was ever laid there.

Whoever laid that path dug out nothing at all. They spread stone over the top of the ground and put six flags on it and let the weather find the join, and the ground under there has been going down and coming up again for thirty years, and in January she put a tub of iron on the middle of it and the tub found the softest part in the whole nine and sat on it.

That is the earth that was under her three flags, and it is on her front garden now in four barrowloads with worms in it, and the flags are not on it.
