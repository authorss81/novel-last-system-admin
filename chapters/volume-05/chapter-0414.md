# Chapter 0414 — Under One Leg

Monday the twenty-eighth of May 2018, beginning at about ten to eight in the morning and finishing at about half past one in the afternoon, a parade, and a hall at the bottom of it, in Tideglass.

**THE PARADE DID WHAT IT DOES ON A MONDAY, AND A MAN PUT A PIECE OF BATEN UNDER A LEG OF A TABLE IN A ROOM AT THE BACK OF ONE OF THOSE SHOPS IN ABOUT TEN MINUTES AND TOLD NOBODY, AND THE SAME PEOPLE WENT UP THAT STREET AND CAME DOWN IT AGAIN, AND NOTHING WAS ANSWERED.**

The parade runs about a hundred and ten yard from the bank end to the top. The shops are on the west side of it and there is a bus stop about two thirds of the way up, and the hall at the bottom of it is on a meter with the key in it, and it was shut all Monday, and a clock in that hall does the lighting itself at four, and nobody in that building was up to watch it do it.

The room this chapter is in is at the back of one of those shops and it is about thirteen foot by nine. There is a table in it, and a stool, and a heater on the wall with a bar over it, and a kettle, and about two inches of post on the floor round the skirting that nobody gets a brush to.

---

The man who puts shutters up was down that parade at about a quarter past eight with four of them up by about twenty to nine, and he was coming back up it at about half past ten with the rest to do.

A van pulled up outside the shop that shuts early at about ten past nine, put its hazards on, sat there about four minutes, and then went on up the road out of this borough. A woman went down the parade at about half past nine with a folded ladder over her shoulder and did not come back up it that morning. A man off nights came up it at about a quarter to one, went into a shop, and came away from it with a bag of compost.

About nine people were out on that parade in that morning. Most of them were no more than a few minutes on that pavement, and not one of them was holding anything on behalf of any of the other eight.

The man in his forties came into that shop at about eight for a box of washers, and the woman who works in a shop asked him, in about five seconds and in the ordinary way, whether he could put a trap on the outside tap while he was standing there, and he said he would do it now if the pipe was out, and the pipe was out.

The outside tap on that shop has been dripping since about February. The water goes into a bucket inside the back door and the bucket gets emptied into the same bucket, and nobody has chased it and nobody has reported it.

He had it in front of him for three quarters of an hour. He took the old trap off, cleaned the thread out with a wire brush, put a new one on, wound the new joint twice with hemp, tightened it, ran it cold, ran it hot, and wiped the back of that tap with a cloth, and filled the bucket in the yard and carried it round the front and put it under the tap again.

And that table has rocked since before Christmas.

It is a deal table four foot by two foot, standing on a painted steel frame with tubular legs, and that is the table the whole of that shop works on, and it rocks at the end nearest the heater because in about January somebody shoved that heater across and made its own against that wall.

It was in the corner of his eye the whole time he was at that tap, and it did not occur to him to think about it until he was standing up with his bag in his hand.

He got down onto the boards and put his hand flat on the top of it and pushed. That corner came up about a sixteenth of an inch and came down again. The other three corners stayed where they were.

One of the legs at that end is on concrete. The one opposite it is about two inch off the floor, and that frame is not a factory job. Somebody mended it once, with a short length of angle and a pop rivet.

He went out to his van and got a three-foot length of batten about one and a half inch by two and a quarter, which he had because he has always got three-foot lengths of batten in a van, and he planed one face of it flat and got it under that leg and knocked it home with the butt of a chisel and about four blows, and cut it off square with a bolster at the line of the leg, about a quarter inch proud.

Then he pushed the table again, hard, at the near corner, at the far corner, and in the middle of it.

It did not move.

He was in and out of that room in about ten minutes of the whole of it. He did not say one word about that table to anybody. The woman who works in a shop was not in that room at any point while he was in it, and she has not asked him about the table, and he does not know she ever stood next to it, and she would not tell her own self who put that batten there, and she is right about that as well.

She went into that room at about ten after a sheet of A4, because the counter had got busy and because a delivery was coming in at about midday.

She put the paper on the table and the table did not rock.

She stood there for about two seconds, laid her hand flat on the top of that table and pushed, and took her hand away, and looked down at a length of batten under one leg with the end of it cut off square and standing about a quarter inch proud.

She did not go and get anybody. He was four doors away at that minute and she could have gone and got him, and she did not, and she has not, and she is not going to.

That batten is still under that leg and that table has not rocked since, and neither of them will raise it, and the whole of what each of them knows about it is the half of it they were in the room for.

Four answers to one question have been going round that borough since about the autumn, and on the Tuesday before this one a man said one of them out loud at that counter to a man buying a bag of grout, and the man buying the bag of grout did not write it down and did not ask, and the man who said it has not said it again.

Set any two of those four beside each other and there is nothing to set them against, because they are not answers to one sort of thing. Not one of the four has been called back. Nobody has produced a fifth. Nobody in that borough has ever said out loud what the four of them are answers to, and nobody has written it down either, and it was not said this week.

There is one thing in that borough a person could not put into words if they were asked for it, and it is the same one it was a fortnight back.

A wire birdcage is standing under the hood of a gate that does not shut at the bottom of a lane, on its base, with its back to a brick pier, and it has been there since about a quarter to seven on the Friday before. The man that yard belongs to last went into it in the middle of May, before any of this, and he has not been back and he does not know what is up against his pier there.

There is a room in a block by the water whose door has never caught on its latch. Inside it there is a shelf, and on that shelf is a book, and that book has nineteen entries written in it, and the last of those entries has a space under it that has stood empty through one thousand two hundred and seventy-nine days.

Nobody went into that room on that Monday, nor on the day before it, nor on the day after that. One woman in this borough has ever had that book in her hands. She was not asked about it that week, and she did not go down that corridor and is not going to.

---

**SHE CAME OUT FROM BEHIND THAT COUNTER AT ABOUT HALF PAST ONE FOR A DELIVERY OF SIX SACKS AND PUT THEM AGAINST THAT WALL WHERE THEY GO, AND THE DAY WENT ON.**
