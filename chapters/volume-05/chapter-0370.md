# Chapter 0370 — The Barrow

Wednesday the twenty-second of November 2017, from about ten in the morning until about half past four in the afternoon, a shop off a parade, in Tideglass.

**A BUILDER'S BARROW WAS LEFT AGAINST AN ACCOUNT AT THAT SHOP ON THE TENTH OF NOVEMBER AND STOOD AT THE BACK OF IT FOR TWELVE DAYS, AND A MAN IN HIS THIRTIES CAME INTO THAT SHOP FOUR TIMES IN THOSE TWELVE DAYS AND STOPPED AT IT TWICE AND SAID NOTHING TO ANYBODY, AND ON THE WEDNESDAY MORNING OF THE TWENTY-SECOND A MAN IN HIS FORTIES PAID FOR IT AND TOOK IT OUT, AND ON THE WEDNESDAY OF THE SIXTH OF DECEMBER THE FIRST MAN WAS TOLD IT HAD GONE AND DID NOT SAY THAT HE HAD STOPPED AT IT TWICE.**

It is a builder's barrow with a squared front end and a tray about five foot long and a wheel at the front and a pair of handles at the back, and it is the kind of thing a man who digs and a man who lays both want, and it had a tyre on it that was new in the summer and a tray with no rust on the outside of it and one long scratch on the left handle where a scaffold has been dragged.

A man left it at that shop on the tenth of November against what he owed on the account, and the woman who works in a shop wrote a card out for it and put the card on the front of that tray with a bit of tape, and the price on the card was what he had asked for it, which was less than the shop could have got for it and less than a man could get for it anywhere else in that borough.

That shop does not want a barrow and has never had one, and the card has been on it since the tenth of November, and it has been at the back of that shop in a passage between two shelves where anybody coming in for a bag of cement walks past it.

---

He came in for a bag of cement on the Wednesday morning of the twenty-second of November at about ten past ten and the barrow was standing on end against a wall in that passage with a card on the front of it and the handles sticking out about a foot.

He looked at it for about two seconds and went on and got his cement and paid for it and carried it out to his pickup.

He had been in on the Monday of the thirteenth for a roll of tape and had walked past it then as well.

He came back on the Thursday of the sixteenth at about half past nine, and the barrow was still standing on end in that passage, and he stopped in the passage and put his hand flat on the tray and ran it along the side of it about a foot, and then looked at the wheel, and then went out again and did not say one word to anybody about it before he left or after.

He came in again on the Monday of the twentieth for a box of screws and stopped at it again, and he put his thumb under the edge of the tray and lifted it about an inch and let it down again and looked at the underside of the handles.

He did not say one word to anybody about it before he left or after, and he was in that shop four times in those twelve days and the woman who works in a shop has never had to say a word to him about the price of anything, because he did not ask her what it was and she did not offer it twice.

He was asked about that barrow by a man at a job in the middle of December and he said the shop had had one going and gave no more than that.

The man in his forties came in on the Wednesday of the twenty-second, a quarter past ten, for a coil of wire. The woman who works in a shop said she had a barrow going at that price, and he said what was the price, and she told him again what the card said.

He looked at the barrow in the passage for about a minute. He tipped it, once, forward, to get at the underneath of the tray, and put it back on its wheel, and looked at the front end, and said the front end was squared, which is a better thing than a rounded one, and she said yes, and he said the tyre was not bad, and she said it was new in the summer.

He paid for the wire and he paid for the barrow and she took the card off the tray and gave it him with the change, and it took about four minutes altogether.

Getting it out of that shop was the part of it that took longest, and it was done on the pavement.

It would go in the back of his pickup on its side with the wheel over the tailgate and the handles up against the cab, and it took the two of them about ten minutes to do it and neither of them said anything except the woman who works in a shop, who came out of that shop with her arms full and stopped and watched, and then went back in.

The man in his thirties came back along that parade about twenty minutes later with a coil of wire over his shoulder, coming back from a job at the top of the street.

The barrow was in the road at the kerb with the tailgate down and a man tying the front of it, and the man in his forties was about eight foot from him with his back half turned, and the man in his thirties walked past at the pace he walks past anything, and neither of them spoke, and neither of them turned round, and the man in his forties did not look up and the man in his thirties was gone before the tailgate was shut.

They were in the same yard off the Cleeve road on the Tuesday of the first week of December, both of them, for about two hours, and they did not mention the barrow.

---

She told him on the Wednesday of the sixth of December a few minutes before midday, when he came in for a roll of wire and a box of screws.

"The barrow went on the Wednesday," she said. "That man with the floor took it. I hope you did not want it."

That last part came out of her without her deciding to say it, and it came out because he was standing at the rack where the screws are and had stopped for a second, and she had noticed him stop, and she put the sentence in the gap.

He said, "No," and took a box of screws off the rack and put it on the counter and paid for it and the roll of wire and went out.

He did want it. He had been into that shop four times in the twelve days that barrow stood at the back of it and on two other mornings in that time he had walked round the back of that parade instead of going in, and there is nobody in that borough who knows that but him.

She does not know that he stopped for a second. She put that sentence in the gap because a gap is a gap, and she has gone over it since and she still does not know which of the two it was, and she is not going to ask him, because a woman who has asked a man whether he wanted a thing is a woman who has said out loud that she knew he was looking at it.

The man in his forties does not know any of that. He does not know the other man looked at that barrow twice, and he has never mentioned it to anybody, and he is not proud of having it, and he put it in that yard on the Tuesday morning and used it to move about nine tons of stuff by the end of the week, and it is the best barrow in that borough and he has not said so.

Neither of them has ever raised it, and there is no version of a conversation between those two men in which it comes up again unless one of them mentions the barrow, and neither of them ever will.

**ON THE WEDNESDAY MORNING OF THE TWENTY-SECOND OF NOVEMBER A BARROW WENT OUT OF THAT SHOP AND TIED DOWN TO A PICKUP ON A PAVEMENT OUTSIDE IT, AND A MAN WALKING DOWN THE MIDDLE OF THAT PARADE WENT PAST IT AT HIS OWN PACE WITH A COIL OF WIRE ON HIS SHOULDER AND DID NOT TURN ROUND, AND ON THE WEDNESDAY OF THE SIXTH OF DECEMBER HE CAME BACK INTO THAT SHOP AND SAID ONE WORD AT THE RACK.**
