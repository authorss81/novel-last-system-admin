# Chapter 0915 — Under The Loose Cap

Thursday the sixth of June 2024, at the low garden wall at number twelve while midges hang over the beds. The gap under a loose cap stone.

**SHE HAS A LOW GARDEN WALL AT NUMBER TWELVE WITH A LOOSE CAP STONE AND SHE MEANS TO SLIDE FINGERTIPS SIDEWAYS UNDER THAT CAP WITH PALM DOWN AND HER SISTER IS AT THE GARDEN DOORWAY FROM THE BEDS WITH A FOLDED SQUARE IN HER HAND.**

The low wall runs the length of the beds at number twelve, and the cap stones along its top have sat out four winters while the beds below have been dug over every spring.

They are caps of grey sandstone about fourteen inches long, with the tops rounded by weather and the undersides cut square, and the third from the corner sits a finger forward of the rest.

She wants the gap under that third cap, where the stone stands off its bed and the draught moves through.

A gap under a cap shows more than the top of it, because the top takes the sun and the underside takes what the wall is standing on.

So the fingertips go sideways under the wall cap palm-down.

The stone feels cool across the pads, and the underside drags where sand clings to it.

The fingers travel in about three inches and meet earth banked against the back of the wall, dark and close, with small white roots running through it and a snail shell wedged where the trowel left it in March.

She works the fingers left, then right along the bank, and the earth dips a third of an inch beneath the nails, then locks again as the hand circles.

What stops them is grit, sharp and pale, packed at the foot of the bank where the wall sheds its wash, with sand among it locking it where it lies.

In the end the cap at the corner does not sit on its bed along its length. It sits carried on its front lip and on a bank of earth at the back, with grit filling the mouth between, and the grit takes the rattle out of it when the wind gets over the beds.

Her sister comes to the garden doorway from the beds with a folded square in her hand and does not unfold it.

Square folded on her wrist, she occupied the doorway, glancing wall to beds to wall. Bean tidings passed between them, through along the warm wall, and by and by she snapped the square half open and refolded it, keeping it fast.

The square would be wanted up at the shed for the dust on the pots, though the reason passed between them in looks rather than in words, and she tucked it under her arm and went on up the path with it and left the doorway empty.

The wall below that cap was built in a week by the man who had the house before, with the stones laid in courses and the joints flushed, and the caps went on last with old lime gone hard underneath them.

Old lime gone hard keeps its shape under a cap the way new work cannot, because it has given up its water years past and now breaks before it bends.

Where the lime has broken under the third cap the break runs clean across the bed, and the cap has dropped a hair at the back and risen a hair at the front, which is why the draught gets under it.

A cap tipped forward like that spills rain off its front lip during storms, and the spill gutters a shallow trench in the dirt at the wall foot.

Wall-foot spate sheers away at the third cap, the jutting stone casting it beyond the joint.

So the forward cap guards its own bed by standing out of line, and the earth at the back takes the wash instead.

The sandstone of the cap feels rough where the weather has eaten the face, and the underside feels rough where the cutter marks remain, and the bed of old lime feels rough where it broke across.

Coarse stone sheds shower water in sheets, and every sheet strips a little loose sand off and leaves the tight grains.

A palm-roughened face is earliest clear after rain; its handled-smooth fellow nurses wet at the base till the sun climbs.

With its coarse crown south and its back to the beds, the third cap dries on the midday side first, which leaves moss in the north joint and the top bare.

Joint moss runs thick where banked earth stands highest on the cap and spare where lime pinches the mouth.

A moss pad two summers thick sits tufted there, yielding to thumbnails, defeating fingers.

Where the cushion has been lifted before it grows back thicker at the rims and thinner in the middle, which is the single way to tell an old cushion from a young one.

---

Once beds parch, wall chill mothers moss, and May logs the shift the whole run over.

Under a cap like that the drip off the coping above throws everything it catches to one spot, and everything that lands has to go somewhere.

It goes into the mouth at the back and stops there, because the cap overhangs and the bank beyond slopes away from it.

On the front side the cap sits square with the sky and the same rain runs straight over the top and off the lip, which keeps that side of the wall dry from above all winter.

The wall has been asked to do that longer than the fence beside it has been painted, and it has done it with no shift of its courses.

The corner cap has worn its bed down by a coin's thickness across three winters, and the soot smear that stood flush with the next cap after the shed rubbish fire rests a shade lower now.

Soot rinses away in showers within two summers, and this smudge has stayed faint and open for years because the cap overhangs it.

That smudge is the sole measure this wall keeps, and what it says is that the cap wears where it stands rather than wandering off it.

The root ends in the bank went soft over winter and will push again before July, and the soil they run in has not been turned since the fork broke its tine in March.

Woodlice run that mouth at night, and the sand at the lip of it stays loose enough to take a fingertip, and both go on working at a joint the eye passes over.

Woodlouse work in a damp mouth shows first as a pale bloom on the shaded face of the lime, and the bloom has not been on that back joint since the paint went on the fence.

Shed-door ledges butt their boards flush, wall caps butt their beds flush out of the wet, and path bricks butt soil flush.

The footing trench under that wall runs a spade deep and a foot wide, backfilled with rubble the builder barrowed from the path dig. Ivy roots from the fence corner have nosed into the backfill over the years, pale feelers that thicken into ropes where the earth stays damp. The ropes split fine earth aside and knot round small stones, and where a rope meets the wall foot it flattens and creeps upward into the joint. Old lime gone hard resists the creep where the joints stay pointed, crumbling only where pointing failed summers back. The failed joints breathe with the seasons, opening a hair in drought and closing in rain, and the ivy reads the breathing, sending fresh feelers into each opening.

She pulls the fingers from beneath the cap and rasps the dirt away on the bed timber, then studies the run from the path for its overhead aspect.

From above the caps look straight and even, with the third a finger forward and the tops catching the sun.

From the side the same run shows a low bank and a shadowed mouth, and the second of those tells more, because a wall stands on soil and not on sight.

A crown gone rough with weather throws the morning rain off in sheets, and each sheet leaves the faces a little cleaner than before.

After drought the third crown furs with dust, and rain rinses it bare again, each rinse carrying loose sand off and leaving tight grains.

She leaves the cap where the wall left it, the front lip on its bed and the back on its bank with grit in the shadow between, and the run will go down a hair into that bank every year under no weight but its own when the midges hang over the beds.
