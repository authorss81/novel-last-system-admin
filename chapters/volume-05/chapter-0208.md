# Chapter 0208 — The Crossing

Sunday the twenty-ninth of November 2015, from about half past six in the morning, a doorway in Tideglass, and then the landing, and then the water.

**FOUR PEOPLE WENT ACROSS THE ESTUARY ON THE MORNING OF THE TWENTY-NINTH OF NOVEMBER AND NONE OF THEM HAD BEEN IN A ROOM WITH THE OTHER THREE SINCE SEPTEMBER, AND THE FIRST THING THAT WAS SAID WAS SAID IN A DOORWAY BY A WOMAN WHO HAD SAID IN ADVANCE THAT SHE WAS GOING TO SAY IT, AND IT WAS A BOUNDARY, AND IT WAS NOT A QUARREL, AND NONE OF THE FOUR OF THEM IMPROVED IT.**

The doorway was the door of a house on a parade of shops and it belonged to nobody that morning, and Sanaa Iqbal was standing in it with her bag over her shoulder and her coat buttoned to the top, at about half past six, with the boat at ten past seven.

"I'm going to say a thing and then I'm going to stop talking about it for the rest of the day," she said. "I'm not going to be fixed. I know what that sentence sounds like at half past six in a doorway and I have thought about it being a bad sentence. I'm not going to be the reason anybody is talked out of anything, and I'm not going to be the reason anybody is talked in to anything either, and if I find out in three weeks that either of those things happened I will be extremely unpleasant about it and you can put that in your letter."

"You can put it in the letter," said Jonas Mercer. "I'm not going to argue with it in a doorway at half past six in the morning."

"I'm not asking you to argue with it. I'm telling you what it is so that you don't have to guess about it later and get it wrong." She shifted her bag. "And I'm going because there is a boat at about ten past seven and there are four of us on it and there is a room on the Friday, and I would have got in that boat if there had been three of us and I would have got in it if there had been five, and that is the whole of the reason and there is not a second reason underneath it."

She went out through the door and down the step and did not look back to see whether he had followed her, and he followed her.

They had not been in a room together since the middle of September, when two of them had argued in front of about thirty people and stopped, and neither of them had decided to stop.

Tomas Renn came at about ten to seven with a holdall and a folder under his arm and a flask, and he had brought the flask on purpose and would not say so. Nell Ardent came at about five to seven with a rucksack, no coat, and a paper cup of coffee from a machine that was on the parade for the building opposite and which she had to cross the road to reach. She gave it to Sanaa Iqbal and Sanaa Iqbal drank about a third of it and handed it back and said thank you, and both of those things happened without anybody in the four of them finding them difficult, which was itself the sort of thing that a room full of these four used to be unable to get through.

Nobody read anything out. There was no list and no roll and nobody said anybody's name in order.

The landing is a piece of concrete with a hut on it and a lamp that comes on at about six in the December dark whether anybody is on it or not. The boat came in at ten past seven, which is early, and it was the smaller one, and the engine was loud enough that anybody who wanted to say something on the crossing said it standing up at the rail.

It was flat and grey and about four degrees. There was a bucket that came loose under a thwart and went about the length of the deck and everybody watched it and nobody moved until a man got up and picked it up and put it back where it had been, which is how a boat behaves when four people are on it and three of them are watching a bucket.

A woman with a dog came on at the last minute and sat down. A man in a waterproof had a flat tray on his knees with nothing on it, newspapers, four, weighed down with a stone, and he was not selling anything and nobody asked him for anything, and he got off at the far side. Somebody had a newspaper going and it was the same one that had been going on the bottom deck that morning, and by the middle nobody was reading it.

About a quarter of the way over, Nell Ardent was sick over the side, quietly, with her back to everybody, and held the rail for a while afterwards with her eyes shut, and Sanaa Iqbal moved to sit where she could see her and did not touch her and did not go back to her own side of the deck, and Ardent opened her eyes after a bit and said, "Don't," and Iqbal said, "I wasn't going to," and that was all of it.

Renn waited until the water had gone out of everybody's ears and then said, "What's the room like."

"Nineteen chairs and a table the length of it," said Jonas Mercer, who had a note. "Paperwork on the table, printed, and a woman at the end who writes down what people say and does not argue with any of it. They are not a court and it is not a hearing and there is nobody there whose job it is to be disappointed."

"Who's the disappointment."

"Nobody. That's what I took from the letter. It's a group of three authorities and about six people who work for them and a great many people who use it." He put his hands in his coat. "And the water's fine, Tomas. I looked it up on the Friday and the water's the best thing on that river."

"I didn't say the water," said Renn.

"You said what's the room like."

"You were about to tell me about the water."

"I was."

They stood at the rail.

"There's a thing I should tell you," Jonas Mercer said, "and I have not told the other two, and I am telling you because you will find it out in about six weeks and I would rather you found it out from me. On the eleventh of August a document was made in this city. On the second page of it there is a line with a date under it and the line is empty. On Wednesday morning at nine o'clock the date came round and the document stopped being in force, all seven parts of it, at the same time, and nobody did anything and nobody was asked and nothing was cancelled."

The boat's engine changed and then did not change.

"Nobody asked you about it," said Tomas Renn.

"Nobody asked me about it. Nina Calder knows because I told her, and she wrote nothing down, and she is the only person in this city who knows. And the field — the column with the name and the hour called and the hour home and what the person was called for — is not empty, and nobody wiped it. It is a column in a book on a table in a building by the water, and it is not a field any more, it is nine months of somebody's handwriting."

"And you."

"I've got nothing to do about clause six," he said. "That's all. I have not had anything to do about it since the eighteenth of September and I have not got it now, and the reason I had it then was that it was in force to be stopped, and you cannot stop nothing."

Renn looked at the far bank for a while.

"That's not a resignation," he said.

"No," said Jonas Mercer.

"No," said Renn. "It is a thing that has been done to a person and he has not been told about it, and he has just told me about it on a boat, and I am not going to be the one who says the rest of it out loud, because if I do it will be in your mouth for the rest of your life and you will be a man who was relieved of something." He took the flask out of his coat. "Have some of this. It's terrible."

Nell Ardent had got her colour back by the time the engines dropped.

"I have got two questions for that room and neither of them is the one you are all expecting," she said, to the middle of the deck, at about ten past eight. "One of them is about a schedule with nine names on it, and one of them is about the fact that a document can be in force on the far side of an estuary and be correct on both sides and be a completely different thing to the people in it. And neither of them is a question I can put in a room, because I have no standing in that room and neither has anybody else on this boat, and that is the third question and it is the one I will not be asking."

"We're not on the agenda," said Jonas Mercer.

"No," said Nell Ardent. "You are not on the agenda, and I am not on the agenda, and Tomas is not on the agenda, and neither is she." Sanaa Iqbal did not look up. "There is a line on a piece of paper with our names nowhere near it. I read it on the Friday. I would like it minuted that I read it."

The boat came alongside at about ten past eight. A man in a peaked cap was on the slip with a rope and he wanted to know whether they were going as far as the works, and said, when Nell Ardent said she did not know, "It's the second one past the bridge," in the tone of a man who has said it before.

The four of them got off. The man with the empty tray was not there. The light was the same flat grey it had been on the other side and the air smelled of the works before it smelled of anything else, and Jonas Mercer got his bag off the deck and put it on the slip and stood there while the other three got theirs off, and that is the last of the twenty-ninth of November.
