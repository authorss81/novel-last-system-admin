# Chapter 0974 — Into The Flint Gap

Undated in this draft.

**THE FLINT BED BESIDE THE DOOR AT NUMBER NINE IS SET IN OLD LIME GONE PALE AND HARD.**

The door at number nine stands in a brick porch with a bed of flints set into the wall beside it, laid generations back in old lime by a mason who worked with what the fields gave.

The bed is about two feet square and set flush with the brick, with some thirty flints of different sizes bedded in old lime gone hard and pale. Each flint is dark and glassy where it broke and pale with cortex where it kept its skin, and each stands proud of the lime by a hair. Flint breaks with a conchoidal fracture that leaves sharp edges and smooth curves, and the break rings faintly when tapped with a nail. The cortex around each is rough and pale from the chalk where it lay, pitted with small hollows that keep dust. The mason who laid the bed knocked each flint to shape with a hammer, and the hammer marks show on three of them as pale stars.

Between two flints near the middle the old lime has fallen away to leave a narrow gap about the width of a fingernail and about an inch deep. The gap runs back between the flints to the heart of the bed, dark inside even at midday, with dust drifted into its mouth.

The flints around the gap are of different shapes, one square with a sharp arris and one round with a smooth face, and the gap between them narrows where the two come nearest. The arris of the square flint is sharp enough to catch the skin, while the round one is smooth and draws little.

Knapped flint needles the nail cold, glass-hard and glittering. Adjacent aged lime takes minimal warmth, dusting at a fingertip. Mouth grit yields no warmth to touch.

The old lime of the bed has gone hard through years and cracked along three joints besides the gap, each crack no wider than paper thickness. Old mortar still in place gleams wan on tenebrous flint, shedding filament shadows. Where it has fallen the flints show their sides, dark and damp where rain never reaches.

The brick around the bed is of kiln-fired stock with a dark skin and a softer heart, laid in courses that run true across the porch. The pointing there is old lime gone hard, fissured down one bond above the flintwork and showered in grit across the tread. Grit sleeps where it dropped, pallid on tread stone, unswept by any broom.

The step before the door is of stone worn hollow in the middle by feet, with the hollow cupping rainfall after weather in a shallow sheet. Stone steps wear hollow because feet strike the middle and avoid the edges, grinding the grain a little with every year. The hollow here is about an inch deep at its middle and runs the width of the step, smooth as glass along its centre and rough toward its rims. The rims stand sharp where the wear has not reached, square from the mason's chisel. The sheet dries from the edges inward, leaving a pale ring where the water stood. The stone around the hollow is smooth and draws little heat, while the hollow itself stays cool through the day.

Rain off the porch roof falls onto the step and splashes the bed, bruising the flints in bars that fade from the crown. Dawn dew glitters the knapped faces and quits by breakfast, the slit staying dry. Rooftop discharge dives down a corner downpipe, then films across the forecourt flags in a thin wash.

The forecourt flags were founded on sand, since scoured from under one arris to hollow the rim. Its void abides rainless and shingly; a venturing fingertip finds nether stone polished from the mould. The stone above vaults its void firm, humming under knuckles.

A hedge of privet runs along the front boundary, clipped each year to waist height and dense with leaves. Privet cut in summer throws pale shoots that darken by autumn, and the clipped face shows a thatch of twigs beneath the leaves. The thatch keeps spiders and dust through winter, and wrens work along it at dawn. The roots below bind the soil and keep the flags from spreading outward. Summer blades trimmed the privet; litter lifted, odd bleached sprigs stranded at the base. Woody shoots there stand iron-hard, taking trifling warmth, leafage yielding zero.

The road beyond the hedge runs to the church with grass verges on either side, and the verge opposite is worn where the bus stops. The wear there is of bare earth with grass at its edges, pale in dry weather and dark after rain. The post with the sign stands at the head of the wear, and the sign faces the road with letters that can be read from the far pavement. The stop itself is marked by a post with a small sign, and the sign there is pale with sun. The post stands upright in earth with stones packed round its foot, and grass grows between the stones.


Bus-stop signs fade fastest on their sunward face, ink bleaching to ghosts within summers. The sign across from number nine shows this exactly, its Sunday column legible only at dusk. The post beneath carries bills pasted and torn, layer upon layer, the oldest a corner of yellowed paper mentioning a fete. That corner survives because paste sunk into grain, defying scrapers. New bills cover it monthly, each paler than the last.

Privet berries hang black and shining through winter where clipping spares them. The hedge here was clipped post-flowering, so berries cluster only on the far side facing the church. Those berries feed one blackbird through January, the bird returning at the same hour daily. Its droppings stain the pavement violet beneath the roost, a mark the rain dilutes but never lifts.
---

The nail of one finger sets into the gap between the two flints and presses in as far as it will go.

Nail-led upright entry first, the slit refusing flat tips. It reads the sides by pressure and the back by touch, moving a little at a time.

Inward distance tells first. The nail reaches about an inch before it meets the heart of the bed, where the flints close behind and will not let it pass. At the mouth the gap takes the nail freely, and further in it grips the sides so the nail must ease past.

What hides behind tells afterwards. Through the greater depth old lime alone stands, rigid and pallid, scarred where the lost fragment tore free. Deep aft lurks a flint splinter spalled from squared arris, bridged crosswise, tissue-thin, keen. It stays sullen to top pressure, veering to side pressure till the round flint cheeks it.

The spall drinks warmth deep, razor-rimmed. Tread stone underfoot drinks cold slowly through the soles, dense quarry fashion. The lime flanking both takes what warmth the finger brings and gives none of it back, flouring to fingernails.

The nail ascends from the slit, grime beneath its rim, else clean. The slit stays as first found, the glass flake pinned where the press pinned it, a hair beneath its birth seat.

Neither flint stirs to pressure, lime bedding plus bed-mass locking each. Thumb load stirs neither a whisker; each sinks home anew.

Rain that splashes the bed does not reach into the gap except in driving weather, which is why the dust at the mouth lies loose while that within stays packed. Flanking privet hurls drips beyond the flintwork; the slit endures rainless but for cloudbursts.

---

The provisioner waits on the far footway at number nine beyond the carriageway, fronting the door with a carrier resting against one shoe.

The nail tip quits the flint slit and wipes itself on the coat hem while the shopper studies the porch from afar. Words are exchanged on whether the bus still stops by the church, and whether the shop keeps Sunday hours, with short answers crossing the road.

The provisioner keeps the far footway, crossing never, carrier at her shoe, carriageway yawning between. The flintwork bides seated, slit umbral and mortar blanched.

The flint wall keeps its lime and its gap through rain and sun, in case another winter should find the bed and test what is still sound.
