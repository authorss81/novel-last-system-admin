# Chapter 1029 — A Thumb Along The Top Tread

Undated in this draft.

**THE TOP OF THAT STAIR HAS A WINDOW IN IT AND NO DOOR OFF IT.**

That house has one stair and it turns at the bottom and goes up beside the left-hand wall of the passage, and the rise of it is short enough that a man in a coat has to go up it sideways. At the top of it is a landing you could not put a chair on, with a window in it giving on that street and no door off the landing at all and nothing ever stood on it except a woman who came up there to get out of the way of a house. The boards at the top are the same boards as the rest of the stair and they are worn in a band about four inches wide across the middle, and the whole of the rest of that landing is not worn at all.

She is the only person on that frontage who has ever come off that book and she came off it herself, at the end of the season, and what it took out of her was six weeks of her own work done up the river. Nothing has been put in front of that name since and nothing behind it either. She has a fortnight with nothing in it and a range in her kitchen that has gone out twice since the summer, and this is the one room in that house where nobody asks her anything.

He came up that frontage in the middle of the morning with nothing in his mouth at all, and he had not got a sentence in him when he started up the passage, and she was up there waiting before he was halfway along it.

"You have come up my stair with nothing."

"I have."

"Then this is not a visit. The last three times you came up this house you came with something in your mouth, and one of them was you standing in the way."

"I have come to stand here."

"You could stand in the yard. Everybody stands in the yard."

"I could."

She let that go past and came and stood on that landing facing him, and there was not room on it for the two of them to stand square, so they were both of them turned half sideways with their arms down and neither of them anywhere to go.

"Put out what you came to put out, and do not tell me you came to stand here, because everybody in this borough has an excuse and yours is the first one I have heard that was worth having."

"I have taken money off a woman at the back of that house and I have not done anything for it yet. I have asked a woman at number ten to be in a kitchen and she has not answered. I have cut four foot of deal off the foot of my own stack in a loft to make the tray the job wants, and the stack is not a stack any more. I have taken a plate off the corner of the pit in my own yard and left the hole open, and I have been up to that other yard this week and offered the whole of it to a woman who keeps a book, and she cannot put a fault anywhere."

He got all of that out on a landing with a window at the end of it and she heard all of it out without once putting a hand on the rail.

"And the money."

"The money is the easy half of it. I have not had any in my hand for ten years and I have now had it for three days and I have not spent a penny of it and I do not know what a man is for who takes money and does not spend it."

"And what are you going to do with your fortnight."

"The tray is made out of four foot of my own stack and there is nothing else I can finish."

"That is not a fortnight. That is two days and then a man standing in a kitchen with his hands in his pockets."

"That is all of it and I have not got a better answer."

She was quiet long enough that he thought she was going to let it go, and then she turned round and faced him again.

"I have six weeks on a river in my own name and I came home to a house my mother ran without me and I have not told anybody in this street what I did with them and nobody here is going to hear it from me. That is what I have got in place of an answer, and you might as well have that as anybody."

"Then you are the only person on this frontage who has done six weeks of work and cannot put them anywhere."

"I am, and I have not slept properly since the summer, and I have found out that a person with nothing to put a thing on loses more sleep over it than over the work itself."

---

"You have come up here and told a woman you cannot pay two men."

"Yes."

"Then let us be clear about what this is, because I have had two goes at working it out since you got to the bottom of my stair." She turned round on that landing and put her shoulder against the wall at the end of it. "Four times this house has had you in it since the spring and you have put one thing to me, which was what coming off a book gets a person, and you have never once come at me for anything else. Now you come up my stair with your hands empty and tell me the whole of what you cannot do. What am I to do with that."

"Nothing."

"You cannot want nothing. Nobody wants nothing. If you tell me on that landing that you want nothing off me you are telling me a lie, and you will have to go back down that passage with it."

"Then I want you to have been told," he told her, "because you told me in the spring what happens to a man who asks a woman for money, and you were right about all of it. You put it to me that a woman who agrees to it goes back into her pantry afterwards and feels it, and that neither of them ever puts the word about it again for as long as they keep working. I have been carrying that round for a fortnight. That is what I came up here with."

She looked at him for a while, and he put his thumb along the nosing of the top tread and walked it the width of that landing, and what he got was that band of wear about four inches wide and boards either side of it with nothing on them at all, and he took the thumb away and told her none of it.

"You have not asked me for anything," and then she stopped, and he knew from the way she stopped that she had heard herself do it.

He stayed on that landing until the light in the window had gone from grey to the colour of a wet road, and neither of them put a hand on the rail, and he did not ask her for one thing, and he got nothing out of the whole of it. What he had brought up that staircase was six different troubles and what he went down with was the same six, and the only thing that was different about him at the bottom of it was that a woman who has stood in that book since she was seventeen had told him about six weeks on a river and had not told anybody else.

She stood in front of a man who cannot pay anybody and let him empty the whole of himself out at her, and he has gone back down that passage with the lot of it still in her head and the wear on that top tread will be there in a fortnight, which is the only thing in that house that will be.
