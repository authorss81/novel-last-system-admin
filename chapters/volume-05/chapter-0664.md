# Chapter 0664 — In The Middle Of The Passage

Wednesday the first of September 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from about eight in the morning until about one in the afternoon and she is outside the front step of it for about two minutes of the whole morning, and the whole of it is a wire brush on a floor and a gate standing open on a road with nobody on it.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT WHO CANNOT ASK ANYBODY FOR ANYTHING DECIDES ON A WEDNESDAY MORNING THAT THE ONLY PLACE A THING CAN LIVE WHERE NOBODY CAN EXPLAIN IT IS WHERE EVERYBODY WALKS, AND PUTS A WIRE BRUSH ON THE FLOOR OF HER OWN PASSAGE IN THE MIDDLE AT ABOUT HALF PAST TEN IN THE MORNING, AND THEN STANDS HER OWN GATE OPEN ON A ROAD THAT HAS NOBODY ON IT, AND GOES BACK IN AND SHUTS THE FRONT DOOR BEHIND HER.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is on her own from eight in the morning until about one in the afternoon. Nobody comes.

---

Here is what she does with a Wednesday morning, and it is the same as every Wednesday morning since before Christmas, and it is eight o'clock, and it is a piece of toast at a table under a window, and a cup of tea, and the cloth on the table, and the green chair with the cushion on it.

And the only difference in that kitchen on the first of September 2021 is that she has not got a second thing to do afterwards.

---

And here is what she works out, sitting at that table, and it takes her the best part of two hours, and she gets it in the wrong order twice and has to go back and start it again.

First, what is actually wrong.

Not the ceiling. The ceiling is a real thing and it is a bad thing and it is going to be worse in the autumn, and she went up there on the Friday night and stood on a stool and heard a cistern weeping and she cannot get at it and she cannot ask anybody to get at it, and that is the fifth of six things on a piece of paper in pencil downstairs and it has a cloth in the bowl under it.

But the ceiling is not what she is thinking about.

---

What she is thinking about is the front room.

And the way she puts it, sitting at a table with a cup she has not drunk from, is this.

A front room is a room for sitting in. That is what a front room is. She has lived in that house since before the war and there has been a front room the whole time and it has had a chair in it against the wall by the window the whole time, and for the last year she has been the only person in that house who has ever sat in it, and in May of this year she said out loud in a kitchen in front of two people that it is the only room anybody sits in.

And there is a wire brush on the sideboard in it.

---

And a room with a thing in it that she cannot put a name to is not that room any more.

That is the whole of the decision and it takes her about four minutes and she gets it standing up in her own kitchen.

The front room was the one place in that house where she did not have to be anything. She could sit in it and there was nothing to be asked about and nothing to be thanked for and nothing to be given to anybody, and she had it for fifty years and she had it for every day since the May and it was the best thing she had.

And a wire brush on a sideboard in it is a question.

Not a question she can hear anybody ask. A question that is standing in the room waiting, every time she opens the door, for somebody to come in and find her in there with it.

---

And she cannot sit in a room with a question in it.

And that is not a figure of speech and she is precise about it in her own head, at about ten in the morning on the first of September 2021, sitting down again.

If she is sitting in that room and somebody comes in, then they will see a thing on the sideboard, and then they will have to say something, and she will have to be a person being asked about a thing.

And she has never been able to be that. She could not be that about a gas bill in an envelope with a lorry on it that has been on the shelf by that door since before Christmas and has not been ticked.

And so the front room is out.

---

And then comes the second half, and she gets this one in about ten minutes on the Monday and comes back to it on the Wednesday, and it is the reason she does what she does.

A thing has to be somewhere.

That is the whole of it. It cannot be on the sideboard and it cannot be on a shelf and it cannot be in the bin and it cannot be on the kitchen windowsill, and she has been over all four of those and rejected all four of those and she knows why she rejected all four of those and it is the same reason every time.

A thing on a sideboard is a thing you look at and think about.

A thing on a shelf in a passage is a thing you ask about.

A thing in a bin is a thing you decided was rubbish, and you cannot decide a thing is rubbish until you know what it is, and she does not know what it is.

A thing on a kitchen windowsill over a sink is a thing in front of your face all day.

---

And then she stops.

And she sits at a table with a cold cup of tea and she goes at it from a completely different direction, and it is the direction that gets her, and it takes about four minutes and it is the best thinking she does in the year 2021.

Every one of those four is a place where somebody can ask her about it.

That is what they have all got in common and it is why none of them works. Not size, not tidiness, not shame. Asking.

A thing on a sideboard can be pointed at. A thing on a shelf can be asked about from the doorway. A thing on a windowsill can be picked up and looked at while you stand there. A thing in a bin cannot be asked about at all, which is the only good thing about a bin, and you cannot get a thing to a bin.

She has spent a fortnight putting that brush in four different places in a house because there is not one place in her own house where a thing can be without a question.

---

And then she thinks about a floor.

And it is about half past nine in the morning on the first of September 2021 and she is sitting at a table, and what she thinks about is the middle of the passage floor between the front door and the kitchen, which is about four feet long and has a runner on it that came with the house and has not been lifted since before Christmas.

And a thing in the middle of a passage floor is not a thing anybody can ask about.

That is the finding and it is the reason she does what she does in an hour's time.

You cannot pick a thing up off a passage floor and look at it. You cannot point at it. You cannot say *what's that* about a thing that is under your feet, because by the time you have said it you have already stepped over it, and a person who has stepped over a thing has not asked about it.

A thing on a floor in a passage is a thing that gets stepped over.

---

And that is the first half of it, and the second half is the one she is not proud of and does it anyway.

She would like to be stepped over.

That is the sentence. She would like there to be a thing of hers in the passage of her own house that a person has to deal with on the way in, and that they cannot deal with by asking, and that they therefore have to deal with by doing something about it.

And she has never asked anybody for anything since before Christmas. Not a bulb, not a gate, not a pound, not a roll of tape. She wrote six things on a piece of paper and put them face up on a kitchen table and let people find them, and that is the only way she has ever managed to get anything done in her life, and it is the only sentence she has about asking.

---

And here is what the whole of it comes to, and she gets it at about a quarter to ten, sitting still, and it is the plainest thing she has worked out since the third of June.

She cannot ask.

And she has never in her life been able to ask.

But anybody can answer a question, and anybody can step over a thing, and there is one of those two things a person at that door has to do and it is not a favour and it is not a service and it is not money and it does not cost anybody anything.

---

And then she stops, because that is the danger, and she sits with it for about a quarter of an hour, and here is what the danger is and she gets it exactly right at about ten past ten in the morning.

A thing on the floor of her own passage is a thing she has put there on purpose, and anybody who comes in and sees it will know that, and there is not one person on that road who will look at a wire brush lying in the middle of a passage floor and think a woman of seventy-eight dropped it.

And if anybody ever says one word about it, then it is not a thing in the way. It is an ask.

And that would be the end of it, and the ask would be a wire brush, and she would have spent the whole summer turning a thing she could not explain into the first thing she ever asked a stranger for, and she would never do that again in her life.

---

So she has to get it right.

It has to be a thing in the way. It has to be a thing that is there because it does not have anywhere else to be, and she has to be able to look at somebody who finds it and have her face say nothing at all.

Which is the only face she has got.

---

And at about half past ten in the morning on the first of September 2021 she takes the brush off the sideboard.

And she does not wash it. There is a curl of old egg-coloured gloss in the crimp and a little grey dust in the ferrule and it has not been cleaned since the seventh of August and it is not going to be cleaned now, and that is the other half of why it is going in the passage: because a clean thing on a floor looks like a thing somebody has done on purpose.

---

And she carries it down the passage and she puts it down in the middle of the runner, about two feet inside the front door, with the handle across the walking line and not along it, so that you cannot step down the side of it without turning your foot.

And she stands there and looks at it.

And then she goes and gets the kitchen and washes the tea cup and puts it on the drainer and wipes the cloth flat on the table and pushes the green chair in and goes and stands in the front room doorway for about a minute.

The rush-seated chair is against the wall by the window. The sideboard has nothing on it. The ceiling in that room is white and it will not be white by the winter.

And she does not go in. She has not gone in since the seventh of August and she is not going to, and it is not the brush any more, and she has worked that out too.

---

And then, at about a quarter to eleven, she goes out of the front door.

---

She goes out of the front door and she shuts it behind her, and the front door is not on the latch and it has not been on the latch since before Christmas, and she shuts it anyway, because she is about to do something on the side of that house and a door left swinging in a wind goes.

And she goes round the side on the grass and past the coal shed, on the walked-flat line, and the grass is wet with the dew and there is about a foot of it where it has been walked and no more.

And she goes to the gate.

---

And the gate is standing where a gate stands.

And here is what she does with it, and it takes about twenty seconds, and she does it with her hands and not with a tool, because the drops only need a thumb and a half after the Saturday morning of the seventh of August, and she knows that because she was standing at her own kitchen window and watched it happen.

She puts her thumb under the top drop and lifts it out of its socket, and she takes the bottom one out with a finger and a heel of her hand, and she stands the gate back against the wall of the coal shed out of the way, and the drop bolts go in the pocket of the apron she has on.

---

And then she does not do anything else.

She does not go out through it. She does not stand in the gap. She does not look up and down the road.

She stands on the inside of her own gate with the path behind her and the road in front of her for about a minute and a half, and there is nothing in the road, and there has been nothing in that road at half past ten on a Wednesday morning since before Christmas, and there is no bus on it and no shop on it and it is not a road anybody has a reason to be on.

---

And here is the whole of what she has done, and she works it out standing there, and it takes her about a minute, and it is not what she meant.

She did not open that gate so that somebody could come up the path. She knows there is nobody coming, and she knew it when she opened it.

She opened it because it has not been left open.

And that is different from the front door, and it is the only difference in the whole of this account between her and any other human being, and it has taken her since before Christmas to get to it and she has got to it this morning for the first time.

The front door is open and cannot be shut and she lives with that and has done for a year. The gate is shut and can be opened and she has not opened it.

And there is no reason on earth for a woman to open her own gate at half past ten in the morning and stand it back against a coal shed wall.

---

And she goes back in.

And she goes in the front door and she shuts the front door behind her, because it is shut when she shuts it, and it does not latch and it never has, and there is no difference between that and a gate standing open, and she is the only person on that road who could tell you that.

And she is standing in her own passage.

And there is a wire brush in the middle of the runner about two feet inside the door, with the handle across the walking line, and she steps over it.

---

And that is the first time. That is the thing she does not expect and she does not tell anybody and she is not going to remember it later: the first time in this account that she has got past a thing in her own house by stepping over it, in her own front door, on her way to her own kitchen.

And there is nobody in the road.

And somewhere on that road there is a woman in her late forties with a drawer shut under a bench in a van, and a man in his early forties with nine pounds' worth of wire brush in an open drawer under a bench in a different van, and a woman in her early thirties who has knocked on that door once and handed a roll of tape over at the threshold, and a woman in her late fifties three doors down with a stool against a wall in a front room and a form from the council in a bag.

And none of them is coming today, and none of them has been told any of this, and the gate is standing open against a coal shed wall and the front door is shut and the brush is on the floor.

---

**AND A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS PUT A THING SHE COULD NOT EXPLAIN ON THE FLOOR OF HER OWN PASSAGE SO THAT THE NEXT PERSON WHO KNOCKS HAS SOMETHING TO STEP OVER OR SOMETHING TO ASK HER ABOUT, AND SHE HAS DONE IT WITHOUT BEING ASKED AND WITHOUT ASKING, AND THE ONLY THING SHE HAS DONE IN THE SUMMER OF 2021 THAT WAS NOT A LIST OF SIX IS TO ARRANGE TO BE ASKED ABOUT, AND THE GATE AT THAT HOUSE IS DONE AND STANDING OPEN ON A ROAD WITH NOBODY ON IT, AND THE SECOND THING ON THE LIST IS A ROOM SHE WILL NOT SIT IN WITH A THING IN IT, AND THE FIFTH THING IS A TAP WITH A CLOTH IN THE BOWL AND A CISTERN SHE CANNOT REACH, AND FOUR OF SIX IS DONE AND NOTHING HAS BEEN CROSSED OFF.**

(End of chapter 0664)
