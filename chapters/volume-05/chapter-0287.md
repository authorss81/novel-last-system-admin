# Chapter 0287 — The Gutter

Monday the ninth of January 2017, from about a quarter to eight in the morning, a first-floor landing in a building by the water in Tideglass.

**WATER CAME DOWN THE INSIDE WALL OF A ROOM AT THE END OF A LANDING ON A MONDAY MORNING BECAUSE A GUTTER AT THE TOP OF THAT BUILDING LET GO OF A LUMP OF ICE, AND A MAN PUT A LENGTH OF LEAD WHERE IT WOULD NOT DO IT AGAIN, AND A MAN IN HIS SIXTIES CAME BACK WITH A TROLLEY AND TOOK A WET CHAIR DOWN A STAIR.**

There had been ice on the top of that building since about the third and it had not gone off it, and the gutter at the back is the one that runs along under the eaves over the yard and the downpipe for it comes down the wall of the first floor and goes into a stub under the boards about two feet off the ground, and nobody has set foot on that stub since before the autumn.

The woman of about fifty-five had the key in her hand before the door of her room was open and she had to lift the handle up to get her shoulder past the frame, and the room was dry and the window shut and the light off and the water came in on her.

It came in about twenty past seven along the top of the wall in a run about a foot long, the way plaster lets water down a flat when the flat above is wet, and it went across the ceiling in a curve and down the corner and out along the skirting under the bed and into the box she had her shoes in.

She got the shoes out. She got the box of papers out of it and put it on the bed, which was wetter in about ten minutes, and she put the towel she had brought down off the rail and the towel did about what a towel does against a wall.

At about half past seven she went up the landing to the window at the end of it, which has never been painted shut, and looked out of it at the roof of the building across the yard, and could not see the gutter from where she was standing, and came back down.

---

By ten o'clock there was about a bucket's worth of it on the boards of that landing and it was coming in under the door of her room and going across the landing to the stair, and the woman of about fifty-five had it under control with a bowl and a piece of card and the two bath mats that came with the room, and at about ten past ten a man went past on the ground and looked up at the corner of that building from the yard, and then went on.

The man in the hi-vis was on a ladder over the front gutter of the shop next door at about half past ten, taking down a length of a run that had been sagging since the summer, and he had arrived over the bridge an hour earlier with the van and a box of tools and he was in this borough because that shop's landlord is in a town sixty miles off and does not come.

He came across the yard at about twenty past ten and put his head in at the door of that room and looked at the corner of the ceiling and went out and up the ladder round the side of the building instead, and came back down in about four minutes with a piece of ice about the size of two fists broken off a gutter and put it on the boards of the landing to show her what it was.

"That there," he said, "is what did it. Freezes, fills up, and then the whole weight of it lets go at once and it goes down one pipe. That pipe's on the inside of your wall because whoever put it in did it off an internal one and then faced it."

"Is it going to happen again."

"It'll happen again in about four winters," said the man in the hi-vis. "Not this week. This week it's empty and it can't do anything."

"Could you make it not happen again."

"I could put a length of lead along the whole of it for a figure and a half-day's time and it would not happen again in my lifetime or yours," said the man in the hi-vis, "and I'd have to be on a roof in February, and I've said to you what that costs and I'm not writing it on anything, and you'd have asked me in September if you wanted it and everybody in this building knows that anybody who wanted anything from anybody in this building has had to go and ask for it since the summer, and this is a length of lead, it is not a matter of asking anybody, it is a matter of a man and a ladder and a day."

"I have not got a length of lead in me," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"I never said I had," said the man in the hi-vis. "That isn't what I said."

He was back on that side of the building on the Friday of that week with a roll of lead, a dressed roll and a hammer, and he was on that roof for about three hours, and he came down and put the tools in the van and went up the parade without going in, and nobody knew he had been on that building at all except the two people on that landing and a man in his sixties who was in a car on the road outside at about half past twelve with his window down.

---

The woman in the room next door along, counting past the fire door, which stands open three inches with a chair against it, came out on the Monday morning about eight because her door was making a noise, and she stepped over about two inches of water on that landing and went back in and shut it to a hand's width and left it there.

The man in his sixties had been in that building before about ten in the morning on the Monday, having come off the road that runs along the bottom of it, and he went up to the first floor and put a foot on that landing and looked at the water and looked at the door of the room at the end and went back down.

He came back at about four in the afternoon with the folding platform trolley out of the boot of his car.

The woman's chair had four rubber feet and one of them had gone, so it had been sitting down at one side and taking water at that side, and the bedding on it was soaked through and the back of it was wet about a foot up. He took the bedding off it in the landing and put it in a heap, and he picked the chair up off its one good side, and he took it and the heap down the stairs, which were wet to the third step, and along the ground-floor passage and out into the yard, and stood it against the wall under the gable end about six foot from where the downpipe comes down, where the ground is high and nothing runs.

He put the bedding on the seat of it and put the two ends of the blanket over the back and left the rest.

The woman of about fifty-five came down at about ten past five on her own, in her coat, with her hair wet, and found the chair and the bedding where they were, and stood in the yard looking at the wall for a while, and there was nothing written anywhere about where a chair is to go, and nobody was there, and nobody came out and asked her why she was standing in a yard at ten past five looking at a chair.

She carried the blanket in. She left the chair against that wall, and it was still against that wall on the Tuesday, and it was still against that wall on the Friday, and it was still against that wall at the end of that month, and the ground under it had dried and gone hard and gone white.

**AND THE WOMAN OF ABOUT FIFTY-FIVE WAS STILL IN THAT ROOM ON THE MONDAY AFTER THAT, ON THE SAME TERMS, AND THE LANDLORD'S AGENT CAME ALONG THAT LANDING ON THE TUESDAY AFTER THAT WITH FOUR MATTERS IN HIS HEAD AND DID NOT GO IN, AND THE HASPIED SIDE OF THAT DOOR WAS WHERE IT HAD BEEN PUT AT THE END OF OCTOBER, AND NOBODY HAS SAID A WORD TO HER ABOUT ANY PART OF IT SINCE.**
