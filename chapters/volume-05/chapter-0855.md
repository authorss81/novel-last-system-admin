# Chapter 0855 — A Wedge Weighed In The Hand

Wednesday the twenty-fifth of October 2023, at the gate of number nine from the time the dew has come off the flags until the street has gone quiet, and the whole of that Wednesday is the third hole in a gate post.

**THERE ARE TWO IRON WEDGES AT THE GATE OF NUMBER NINE, ONE DRIVEN IN THE POST AND ONE LYING LOOSE ON THE FLAG IT CAME OFF, AND SHE MEANS TO WEIGH THE LOOSE ONE IN HER PALM AND PUT IT DOWN AGAIN ON THE SAME FLAG WITH THE SAME FACE UP, AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING STOPS AT THE FRONT WINDOW LOOKS IN THROUGH THE GLASS AND GOES ON.**

The gate is at the front of number nine, hung on the wall side of the opening, and it stands open on its stay because the leaf has gone over towards the road and will not lie square in the frame any more. The stay is an iron bar on a pin that comes out of the post and drops into a notch in the rail, and the end of it stands on the flags about a finger off the ground.

There are two wedges at that gate.

One is driven into the post, high up on the wall side, and it is the wedge that stops the foot of the stay coming out of its notch. It is going in about four inches and what comes out of the hole is flush with the wood. The other wedge is lying on the flag at the foot of the post, on the same flag it came off, and it has been lying there since the spring.

She looks at the loose one from the step.

It is the same grey as the post, and about the length of her hand, and it lies on the flag at the foot of a post with three old holes in the bottom of it. From the step a thing lying at the foot of a post is either a thing somebody dropped or a piece of the post that has come away. It is neither. It is a wedge, and she knows that before she has it in her hand, because of the shape. What she does not know is whether it is iron or stone with the shape of iron.

So she goes down and picks it up.

---

She does not pick it up with the fingers the way she would pick up a nail. She opens her right hand flat and slides it under, and the wedge drops about an inch and settles along the line of her palm with the point of it away from her wrist, and she closes the hand loosely on it and does not squeeze.

That is how a thing is weighed in a hand. Not on a scale. You let it lie on the flat of the hand and you turn the hand over and you feel where the weight is.

The weight is not in the middle of it. That is the answer, and it comes before anything else does. She feels it settle along the bottom of her hand in one place, about two inches forward of her wrist, and it does not spread. She tips the hand a little and the weight walks forward along her palm to where the point of the wedge is and gathers there, and the heel of it goes light, and she can feel the shape of her own palm go different underneath the thing.

A stone does not do that. A stone dropped into a hand is heavy everywhere at once, and where you put it down is where it stays heavy, and tipping the hand only shifts it as one lump. She has picked up enough stones off this path in sixty years to know the difference without thinking about it. This has an end.

She turns the hand back the other way and the weight comes back to the heel of it and gathers at the blunt end, and there it is all in one place again, and there is a difference in the middle of the palm where there is nothing over it at all.

Iron.

And there is a second thing that tells her, which is the grain of it. She has the wedge on its side across her palm and she can feel along the length of it with the pad of her thumb, and it is not smooth the way a stone that has been in a hedge is smooth. It is ridged. It was forged, and the face of it was brought to a taper with a hammer and the hammer left the metal in shallow bands running down it, and those bands are under her thumb now and they are about a quarter of an inch apart from end to end.

The point is blunt. She can feel that too. Where the taper comes to its end the metal is not fine, it is rounded over about the width of a thumbnail, which means it was never a wedge for driving into oak or into a stone post. It went in softwood, and it went in and out of softwood a long time, and the point of it is gone.

She thinks about that standing on the flags with her hand shut on it. A wedge driven into the post of a gate is there to be driven out again. You drive it in to stop a thing for a fortnight and you take it out in the spring and drive it in again in the autumn, and a wedge that has done that twenty times has a point like a spoon.

---

The woman who does her shopping goes along the front in the middle of the day with her bag on her arm, and slows at the front window and looks in through the glass at the length of the room, and goes on. She does not come to the gate and she does not look down.

She opens her hand and looks at the wedge lying along it and then goes down on one knee at the foot of the post, because there is only one thing to do with it and it is to put it where it came from.

Before she puts it down she turns it over in her fingers, which is not a second weighing but the last part of the first one, and what she wants to know is which way up it was lying.

It was lying on its side, and the face that was against the stone is the flat of it, which is the face with the taper rubbed pale and slightly dished along the bottom edge where it has been bearing on the flag grit and the grit has been bearing back. The face that was uppermost is the rougher one, with the hammer bands full and hard and the black of the flag ground into the hollows between them.

She puts it down on the same flag, on the same face up, because that is how it was lying and because there is no sense in turning a thing over on a flag and grinding the flat into it. It goes down about a hand's width from where it was and in about the same line, because a thing that lies on a flag for six months lies along the grain of the stone and not across it, and the line of it can still be seen.

Then she stands in front of the post with nothing in her hand and looks at the three holes.

They are in a line up the bottom of the post about four inches apart, and they were put there one after another over a good many years, by three different wedges or by the same three driven and pulled and driven again.

The upper hole has the wedge in it, and that is the hole doing something today.

The middle one is empty and it is rusted round the inside, and it is a round hole again now, and the rust inside it has the dark grain of the wet that has stood in it since it was last used.

The lower one is not a round hole any more.

It has been opened out. The front of the post at that hole was pushed apart and has partly closed again, and what is left is a slot with rounded ends and a lip standing proud on the left of it about the thickness of a thumbnail. A wedge put into that hole now would stand up out of the face of the post rather than into it, and it would be there in the morning and gone in the first hard night, because the only thing that keeps a wedge in a hole is the wood closing on the two flat sides of it and there is no wood left on the left of that hole to close.

She puts her thumb into it and knows all of that without being told, the way you know the depth of a step in the dark.

She does not fill it. There is nothing to fill it with that would stay filled. A wedge hole that has been opened out is a hole that has told the truth about what a wedge does to a post, and it will go on being a hole for as long as there is a post and a gate and somebody driving wedges into it.

There are three holes in the gate post of number nine, and the lowest one is the one no wedge goes into any more.
