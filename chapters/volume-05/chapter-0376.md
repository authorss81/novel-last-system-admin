# Chapter 0376 — A Tea Chest

Wednesday the thirteenth of December 2017, from about half past eight in the morning until about half past nine at night, a street in the middle of the town, and a shop off a parade, in Tideglass.

**A TEA CHEST WITH A LID, ABOUT TWO FOOT BY ONE, WAS PUT INTO THE CROOK OF A MAN'S ARM ON A STREET IN THE MIDDLE OF THIS TOWN ON THE MORNING OF THE THIRTEENTH OF DECEMBER BY A MAN HE HAD NEVER SEEN, WHO PUT IT DOWN AND KEPT WALKING AND SAID NOTHING AT ALL, AND THE MAN WITH THE TEA CHEST HAD HIS HANDS FULL AND COULD NOT HAVE STOPPED HIM EVEN IF HE HAD. HE HAS GOT IT. HE IS NOT GOING TO ASK ANYBODY WHERE IT CAME FROM, AND THE MAN WHO PUT IT DOWN HAS NOT THOUGHT ABOUT IT SINCE THAT MORNING AND WAS CARRYING IT OUT OF A HOUSE HE WAS CLEARING FOR A WOMAN WHO HAD MOVED.**

The street runs down off the parade to where it meets the bus station approach and there is a shop on the corner of it with a display of outside lights in it that have been up since October, and the footway is about six foot wide with a bus stop layby on one side of it, and on a Wednesday in the middle of December it is a place a man is going through, not standing in.

The man in his thirties came out of that corner shop at about half past ten with a roll of wire and a box of the clips that go with it, and he stood in the footway with his back to the wall to count what was left of his money, because the shop does not take notes over a certain figure and he had gone in with a lot more than he needed to take out.

He had the box of clips under his left arm and the roll of wire under the same arm and the money in both hands, and he was working it from one palm to the other with his thumb, and he was about half way through it.

The man who put the chest down came past him going the other way.

He was carrying it. That is all anybody could have said about him afterwards. He was carrying a tea chest under his right arm with his hand on the lid to stop it swinging, and he came past the man in his thirties at about twenty-five to ten on that footway and stopped for about as long as it takes to set a thing down, and he put the lid of it up off the man's arm where the roll of wire was, pushed the roll about two inch, and set the chest down in the crook of that arm on top of the box of clips, and took his hand off it, and carried on walking.

He did not say anything. There was no "here you are" and there was no "sorry" and he did not stop when he had done it. He went on up the street at the same speed with his hand now empty at his side, and he turned into the entrance of the yards at about the fourth shop along, and he was not in that street again that day.

The man in his thirties stood against that wall with a tea chest in the crook of his arm and a box of clips under the tea chest and about fourteen pounds in change in his hands.

He could not have stopped him. His right arm was pinned to his side by about thirty pound of wire and a box of plastic, and his left hand was full of coin, and the whole of the putting of it took about two seconds, and by the time he had looked up the man was four steps past him and going away at the same speed he had come at him.

He said nothing. He has never said anything about it, and he did not say anything that morning, and the only sound he made in that street was the coin going from one palm to the other.

He stood there for about a minute and a half after the man had gone into the yards. Then he went back into the corner shop and put the coin on the counter and asked for a box of screws as well, and the woman behind that counter gave him them in a bag and he went out and up the street with a tea chest under his arm and two bags in his hands.

---

He carried it about a hundred yards that way and put it down in the entrance of the yards, on the concrete, standing up against the wall, and looked at it.

It is a tea chest. Old pine, about two foot by one foot by one foot, with a hinged lid and a hasp and no lock on the hasp, and the corners are strengthened with flat steel at four corners and a strip of steel down each of the two long sides the way a box that gets carried about gets strengthened.

There are letters stencilled on the lid in white, about two inch high, and they have been painted over once and the paint on the lid is older than the paint on the sides. What is left of them is the top half of four letters and part of a fifth, and the first of them could be an E or a F, and nobody has ever worked out what it said and neither has he.

Inside there is a smell of cold tea and old paper and something with camphor in it, and there is nothing in it at all, and there is a ring in the bottom of it where something stood in one corner for a long time, and about an inch of grit in one bottom corner which came out in his hand in a grey heap when he tipped it in the yard.

He has not found out where it came from. He has put nothing to anybody, and he does not mean to, and the reason is not a good one and it is not a puzzle he wants working: to ask, he would have to stand in that street and say to somebody in a shop that a man he has never seen put a tea chest on him, and a man who has been given something he did not ask for has not agreed to anything, and he does not want it to be found out that he has got it.

It is in his front room.

It stands against the wall between the door and the board he has not put up since the summer, with the lid flat, and he has put a lamp on it, which he does not do, and a bowl on it, which is where the bowl is now. There is nothing in it and there is nothing on it and it is not a thing he has chosen to have.

He has had it since the thirteenth of December and it is still there, and it is still about two foot by one, and it will be there in a month.

---

The man who put it down came out of a house at the top of that same street on the Tuesday before, at about four in the afternoon, with a tea chest under his arm and about nine other things in two sacks, and he was working for a woman who had gone into a home at the end of November and whose son had asked him to empty the ground-floor rooms and put what was worth keeping in a van and take the rest to the tip.

He had a list. It is not a list anybody has asked to see, and it is his, and it was on the back of a fuel receipt, and on it in his own hand are nine lines and beside four of them he has written a word that means somebody might want that, and against one of the others, which is a tea chest, he had not written anything, because a tea chest is not a thing anybody wants and he had never in his life known a man who wanted one.

He put the tea chest on the landing of that house with the other four things he was keeping, and on the Wednesday morning he came at about half past ten and took the sacks out first, and then he took the four things, and he put three of them in the van and he did not put the tea chest in the van.

He came out with it because he was carrying it when he came out. He had it under his arm and his hand on the lid, and he came down that footway past a man against a wall, and he set it down in the crook of that man's arm because the man had a full arm and there was a space, and it took him about two seconds, and he did not stop and he did not say anything and he thought about a tea chest for no longer than it took to set it down.

He put it in the van on the Thursday morning, along with the rest, and the son of the woman whose rooms they were was not there, and the tea chest went to a house in a terrace two streets off, into a garage, where it is at the moment and has been since, and it is not that man's and he does not know it was in a street in the middle of this town for about three quarters of an hour.

He has not thought about it since. He has not thought about it once, and if it were said to him at that counter on that parade he would have to be told the whole of it before he knew what was being asked, and he would not remember where the man was standing.


The man in his forties was buying a bag of plugs at that counter at about ten past four that afternoon, and the man in his thirties came in about a minute later with a box of clips and a roll of wire and a bag of screws, and the tea chest was not with him, because he had left it at the end of his own street before he came in.

Nobody in that shop said anything to him that was not about what he had come in for, and he is not the sort of man a shop asks about a morning.

**THE TEA CHEST WENT INTO HIS ARMS AT ABOUT HALF PAST TEN IN THE MORNING ON A FOOTWAY IN THE MIDDLE OF THIS TOWN AND HE CARRIED IT A HUNDRED YARD AND PUT IT DOWN AGAINST A WALL AND EMPTIED IT, AND THE MAN WHO PUT IT THERE WENT ON UP THE STREET AND TURNED IN AT THE FOURTH SHOP AND DID NOT KNOW HIM AND HAS NOT THOUGHT ABOUT IT SINCE, AND THE STENCILLED LETTERS ON THE LID OF IT ARE STILL THERE AND HE HAS NOT TRIED TO WORK OUT WHAT THEY SAY.**
