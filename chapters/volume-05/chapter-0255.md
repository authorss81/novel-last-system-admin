# Chapter 0255 — The Detail

Saturday the thirtieth of July 2016, from about ten in the morning, a parade of shops in Tideglass.

**A FALSE THING SAID OUT LOUD IN A ROOM ON THE FOURTH OF JULY HAD A DETAIL ADDED TO IT ON THE THIRTIETH BY SOMEBODY WHO HAD NEVER MET THE MAN WHO SAID IT, AND THE DETAIL IS TRUE, AND THAT IS WHY NOBODY CAN TAKE IT OUT AGAIN.**

Jonas Mercer was under the awning of the shop at the end of the parade from about a quarter to ten, with a piece of paper in his coat and a biro, doing the sum on the back of his hand out of a habit that nobody had ever asked him to keep.

It came to six hundred and twelve. He wrote it on the paper and put the paper back in his coat, and the line it belongs to is under the nineteenth entry of a register in a building by the water, and it is blank, and he is not going to put it in front of anybody who is going to write it on anything.

The shutter on the shop went up at the minute it always goes up. The window card with the shop's own telephone number on it and nothing else on it was in the window where it has always been, and the woman of about sixty-five was behind that counter, and nobody went in and told her anything.

---

There was a queue outside the building supplies place by about half past ten, which is a Saturday thing and is the only thing about Saturdays that is predictable in this borough.

Three men from the firm that has three lorries and an office on an estate at the edge of town were in it, and the one at the back had a flatbed lorry on the road outside with the tailboard up and a load of blocks under a tarpaulin, and he was talking, and Jonas Mercer heard him say it from the far side of the parade without trying to.

"—it's the register. That's the one that'll get proved. Old one, borough's own. Anybody can be carried to it."

The man in front of him said which register.

"The one in the records," said the man off the flatbed, and waved a hand at the far end of the town in a way that meant *somewhere over there*, which is what people do. "He had it in a good way, that. The oldest dated thing this place keeps."

Nobody in the queue said anything about it. A man with a trolley said "Is that right," not to anybody, and went in.

Then the woman in front of the till — she had come in for a bag of post-fix and had come in a fortnight ago too — said:

"My mam kept the one before that."

"You what."

"The one before. Before that one. She used to do the dinners over the road at the school." She was pulling change out of her purse without looking up. "She said there was a line right at the bottom of the page where they stopped writing. Everything else on it was full up and then there was a line and nothing on it. She used to say it was the only honest thing in the building."

The man off the flatbed said, "See," and went back to his own end of the conversation.

Jonas Mercer wrote nothing down and had not taken the paper back out of his coat, and he stood in the doorway for another hour after that without anybody asking him anything, which was the ordinary Saturday of it and not a complaint.

By about half past twelve, two of the other lorries of that firm had heard it, and one of them was a different firm, and the man who was told by the second firm said: "You've been in a room, that."

"I haven't," said the man off the flatbed, and that was true, and nobody in that queue knew it and nobody asked.

The man behind the counter at the parade, who had said his own name quietly in a hall in July and had not said it loudly since, put the till figure off the dial and the next one on and asked the man off the flatbed, in the way you ask a man with a lorry outside:

"Where'd you get that?"

"A bloke in a hall," said the man off the flatbed. "Somebody put it right first time as well, apparently. Didn't want to be saying it was the other one."

The man in his sixties, who is a building estimator, was on that parade at about a quarter past two because he was measuring a shopfront at the far end of it for a firm who had not yet decided they wanted it measured.

He had a sandwich in its paper and a cardboard tube under his arm and he was eating as he walked, and the man behind the counter told him, at the door, that the register was the thing that was going to be proved, and then told him about the line at the bottom of the page.

The man in his sixties stopped walking.

"Whose?"

"Bloke off a lorry. It's in a hall. He says a man put it out."

"Right," said the man in his sixties.

"That's it, is it."

"That's what I've heard," said the man in his sixties, and went on up the parade with the tube under his arm, and he did not say it was not it, and he did not say it was.

The man behind the counter watched him go and said, to the man with the trolley: "Well, that's two of them, then."

---

The man behind the counter came up the parade about ten minutes later with a roll of receipt paper still in his hand and caught him at the end of it, and asked him the question he had plainly been carrying since two o'clock.

"About that line," he said. "Is it right."

The man in his sixties stopped walking and thought about it properly, which took long enough for the two of them to notice.

"The line is real," he said. "I can tell you that much and I have told you that much and I cannot tell you anything else."

"That isn't what I asked you."

"I know what you asked you." He shifted the tube under his other arm. "The line is the most honest thing in this borough's records and it means that about nine people in a room in April decided to write nothing down and left it showing. That is not the same as what the man off your lorry said it was, and I have not got a way of saying how it is different without telling you the other thing, and I don't have the other thing."

"Then say the line's not it."

"I can't say the line's not it," said the man in his sixties, "because the line is on the page and it is empty and it is going to be on the page after both of us. What I am telling you is that the two of them have got joined up and I cannot get them apart again, and I am not going to try, because the minute I try I have to say what that register does prove, and it does not prove anything, and I have not got a second thing to put in its place, and I told a room that in May."

He put the sandwich paper in a bin at the corner.

"And which half of it is yours," said the man behind the counter.

"I couldn't unpick that in a fortnight," said the man in his sixties, "and it is in three trades and a school and a queue outside a shop by now, and I am not going to start, and that is the answer to that question and I have given it."

The man behind the counter stood in the middle of the parade with the roll of receipt paper going soft in his hand.

"Somebody could go and ask the woman who keeps it."

"A body that can't make anybody do anything can't be told what it's going to do," he said. "And the same card says it isn't asked. They're on the wall in the hall on Carrow Lane, both of them, and I have read that card about as many times as anybody in this borough."

**AND THE MAN BEHIND THE COUNTER WENT BACK DOWN THE PARADE AND WROTE THE PRICE OF A BAG OF CEMENT ON THE BACK OF A RECEIPT AND PUT IT UNDER THE TILL, WHICH IS WHAT HE DOES WITH EVERYTHING, AND THE MAN IN HIS SIXTIES WENT ON UP THE PARADE WITH THE TUBE UNDER HIS ARM, AND THE SANDWICH PAPER WENT IN THE BIN AT THE CORNER.**
