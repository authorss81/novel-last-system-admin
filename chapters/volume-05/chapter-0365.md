# Chapter 0365 — A Day and a Half

Wednesday the first of November 2017, from about half past seven in the morning until about half past nine at night, a house at the end of a row off the Cleeve road, and a yard behind it, in Tideglass.

**THE JOB IS A CEILING IN ONE BACK ROOM AND IT SHOULD TAKE A DAY AND A HALF AND IT TAKES FOUR, AND THE MAN DOING IT FINISHES IT AND GOES HOME, AND HE IS THREE DAYS LATE FOR THE ONE BEHIND IT, AND ON THE DAY AFTER HE FINISHES A MAN SHE HAS GOT HOLD OF TAKES THE WHOLE THING DOWN AND PUTS IT BACK UP BY HAND IN A DAY, AND NEITHER OF THEM SAYS ONE WORD ABOUT ANY OF IT TO ANYBODY.**

The house is the last one in a row of nine that comes off the Cleeve road and there is a yard behind it about twenty foot by thirty, with a brick wall along the far side of that and nothing behind the wall but a field and a ditch and a line of thorn.

She has lived in that house since before the summer. There is a man and a girl in it and the man is away five days and the girl is at college four days a week, and since the middle of September she has been in that back room with a table and a chair and a laptop and a heater, and it is the only warm room in the house.

He came at the end of September and looked at it for about ten minutes and said a day and a half. She asked him what it would be and he said the same again, a day and a half, without going back to the drawing, and she noticed that he had given her the figure the way he had given it to somebody else in the summer.

The ceiling in that room is joists and boarding and a coat of nothing much. He was to take the boarding up, put two more joists in, put insulation between, and board over the lot.

---

He started on the Monday at about half past seven and he had the boarding down and the old insulation out by about ten, and the two new joists were in by about one, and he had the first sheet of the new ceiling up by about half past four.

Then he stopped, because the joists in that room are not where anybody told him they were.

They are four by two, and they run the short way, and the one over the outside wall is doubled and has a pad of oak on the side of it about three foot long where a girder once came down through this room and was boxed in and the boxing is still there under the plaster of the wall.

He put a pencil mark on each joist where it crossed the wall and the marks came out at eighteen inch and not at the sixteen he was working to, and the sheet of ceiling he had cut did not reach from one to the other with a bearing at each end.

He could have cut a narrower sheet and made it up. He did not do that either.

He got down off the ladder at about ten past five and went and sat in the front room for a while with the drawing on his knee, and then he went out to the van and got a length of timber out of it and cut it in the yard with a saw on a block of wood, and carried it in and scribed it to the face of the doubled joist, and made a packer out of the offcut, and fixed two lengths of extra joist either side of the pad and a short one across the head of it, and got back on the ladder at about nine o'clock and did two feet of the ceiling before he called it a day.

The woman of that house was in the front room the whole of that time with the laptop shut in front of her, and she was on the landing at about half past nine when he came down off the ladder to get his coat, and neither of them said anything about it.

He came back on the Tuesday and did about a sheet and a half. On the Wednesday he did the last of it and the second joist was a week in the building and had warped a little at the end and he had to take a quarter inch off the top of it with a plane on his knees on the landing, and the planing went fine and the ceiling went up fine, and he got it finished and taped at about half past nine on the Wednesday night.

On the Thursday morning he was there at about half past seven to skim the joints and the screw heads, and it took him until about a quarter to three, and he was finished a few minutes after that, and he swept up, and the rubble went out to the yard in nine sacks.

He put seven of them along the brick wall at the back of the yard, where you could not see them from the house, and the other two he put by the gate because the bin men were late.

He has not been able to take them away. She asked him whether he wanted the rest of them left where they were until he came for them, and he said there was no hurry with them, and that was on the Thursday, and she has not asked him about them again.

The nine sacks of plaster and boarding and old insulation are still along that wall in November, under a tarpaulin he left on them, and they are the first thing anybody sees when they go into that yard, and everybody in that house has walked round them about six times a day since the Wednesday.

He was three days late for a floor in a terrace about half a mile off, and the whole of the three days was in that back room, and the man who laid it waited for him on the Tuesday morning until about half past ten and then started himself, and finished the last four square feet on the Thursday afternoon without him.

He did not say anything about that either. The man who laid it has not mentioned it to anybody, and it is in a book of hours that nobody has ever asked to see.

---

He came back to that house on the Monday because he had left a canvas bag of fixings on the shelf in the front room on the Wednesday before, and it had a box of plugs in it and a roll of tape and his own bit holder, and it was worth walking half a mile for.

He came up the yard at about ten in the morning. He could see the back room door standing open across the yard from the gate and he could see the ceiling in it before he was at the door.

It is a different ceiling. The sheets are smaller, the joints are wider, and there is a bead of white across two of them about the width of a thumb, and it is not a skim, it is a bead, and a bead is what a man puts on because it is quicker than a skim and it is the same colour.

The floor under it is the same floor. The light fitting is the same light fitting, hanging where it hung.

She was in the doorway of that room with a cup in her hand.

The man who had put that ceiling up was in that yard at about half past ten on a step at the far side, cutting a length for something, and he had come up on the Friday morning and had it down by about lunch and had it back up by about six and had gone, and there is no man in this borough who could say what he charged her for it except her and the man in his forties and nobody has asked either of them.

He put his bag down in the front room and picked it up again and went out through the front and up the yard. She was still in that doorway. The man in the forties said, "Was there a phone in here."

"There is a socket," she said.

"Right," he said.

He did not look at the ceiling. He did not ask her who had been in, and she did not tell him, and neither of them said anything at all about the two inches of new plasterboard eighteen inches above where his own ceiling had been on the Wednesday morning, and the door of that room was open the whole time they were both in the yard.

**HE CAME OUT THROUGH THAT FRONT DOOR WITH A CANVAS BAG HE HAD NOT HAD ANY REASON TO COME BACK FOR, WALKED DOWN THE YARD PAST NINE SACKS HE HAD PUT THERE HIMSELF, AND WENT OFF DOWN THE ROW, AND THE BAG HE CAME FOR WAS IN HIS HAND AND SO WAS A SPOT LEVEL HE HAD ALREADY TAKEN HOME ON THE THURSDAY.**
