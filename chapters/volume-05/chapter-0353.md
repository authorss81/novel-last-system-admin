# Chapter 0353 — Nobody Has Ever Made a List of It

Wednesday the twentieth of September 2017, from about ten in the morning until about ten at night, a hall on a meter, a shop off a parade, and that hall, in Tideglass.

**A WOMAN SAID THE SAME SENTENCE TWICE, FOUR DAYS APART, ONCE TO A MAN WITH A PAD IN HIS BAG AND ONCE TO A MAN WITH A PAPER UNDER HIS ARM, AND IT WENT OUT OF HER THE SAME WAY BOTH TIMES, AND ON THE WEDNESDAY EVENING THOSE TWO MEN WERE IN A ROOM TOGETHER AND NEITHER OF THEM EVER SAID ANYTHING ABOUT IT.**

The hall is on a meter. It is open three days a week and a Sunday is one of them, and there is a meeting on the Sunday about half the time and tea either way, and the man who does that hall has the tables out by a quarter to.

The woman of about fifty-five goes twice a month when there is a meeting and once a month when there is not, and she has been going since before the summer, and the reason she goes is that the room is warm and the walk is the only walk she does.

She comes out of that block at about half past one and walks the length of a street and crosses at the lights and then goes the whole way down the parade, and it takes her about half an hour, and she has done that walk about thirty times since the summer and has never once been asked about it by anybody in this borough.

She arrived at about half past two on the seventeenth of September and stayed until half past three, and she was at the back of the room for the whole of the meeting with her hands in her sleeves, and she said nothing in it, and afterwards she put her coat on over the back of a chair while the other eight were going out and the man who does that hall was taking the cups through to the kitchen.

The man in his forties was at the far end with a cloth in his hand doing the top of the table, and he does that, and there was nobody else in that room and the man who does that hall was through a door with a tap running.

"That building," said the woman of about fifty-five.

He did not stop what he was doing with the cloth.

"Nobody has ever made a list of it," she said, and she said it in the ordinary way of a person saying a thing that has been in her head for a while, and not in the voice of a person who has come in with something to say.

He said nothing for about four seconds.

"No," he said, and went on with the cloth.

And that was all of it. He asked her nothing, and she told him nothing, and he has never been up that stair and has no reason to and is not going to, and the only things he knows about that building are what he has been told by other people in a shop, which is that a woman lives at the end of the first-floor corridor and that nobody has any idea what is behind a wall in it.

The work notes are in the bag at his feet with the elastic still round them, and there is very little on any of the pages, and he did not open the pad, and he did not put a hand near it, and it has not been open on that staircase and is not going to be.

---

On the morning of the nineteenth the man in a fleece was waiting at the top of that street, twenty minutes late for a bus he had every day of the week, with a paper folded under his arm that he had not opened and had been carrying about like that since Saturday.

She came past him at about ten to ten with a bag and stopped, and there was nothing about her that was unusual and nothing about stopping unusual either, and the two of them had spoken before, in a road, about a sheet of iron and a woman with a bag and nothing else.

"Nobody has ever made a list of it," said the woman of about fifty-five.

The man in a fleece looked at her.

"Of what," he said.

"That building."

"That building's not got anybody in it that's in a list of anything," said the man in a fleece. "Nothing in that building's in a list of anything, and there's nobody in it that's been asked for anything, and there's nobody up that stair with a book. I've been past the bottom of it a hundred times and I've never once seen anybody go up it with a pad under their arm."

There were three other people at that stop and none of them heard it. A woman with two bags was about fifteen foot off with her back to them both, a man was standing with a newspaper folded the way that man folded his, and a young one was sat on the wall end with his feet up.

The bus came at about ten past ten, and he got on it, and the doors closed, and that was all of it.

He did not ask her what she meant it about. A man who asks a woman of about fifty-five what she means by that at ten to ten in the morning at a bus stop is a man who has decided she is talking about a thing that needs him to understand it, and there is no arrangement in this borough for that and there is not going to be one.

He has not asked her. He has not asked anybody what she meant. What he took from it, and what he has, is that there is nobody keeping anything on that building and therefore there is nothing anybody in it has to do, and that is a thing he has believed about that street since about June and it saves a man a great deal of walking.

She cannot say which of the two she meant. She said it to a man with a pad in his bag and to a man with a newspaper under his arm, four days apart, and it came out of her the same way both times, and she has not given it a thought since the middle of that Tuesday afternoon, and no one here is ever going to put it to her and she would not answer if she were.

---

The hall on Carrow Lane on the Wednesday evening had a table out and the urn on and eight of them, and the man in his forties was in early as he is and the man in a fleece was in at about half past eight with his hands in his pockets, and the man at the end of a street was in at about a quarter to nine with a cup and had said nothing to anybody for the first ten minutes.

The two of them had been in that room for about a quarter of an hour before either of them said anything, and they had both had a cup, and they were on the same side of the room within four feet of each other, and neither of them knew what the other had been told on Sunday afternoon and neither of them knew what the other had been told on Tuesday morning, and they were both told it and neither of them was going to say so.

The man at the end of a street put a thing to the table at about half past nine and it was about his own Saturday and not about anything in that room.

"I had a barrow up at the top of the Cleeve road," he said. "A man came through a gate with nine lengths of something on it and set them down in the middle of the yard, not where that yard stacks its timber but out in the open, and the man with the barrow went off up the road without a word about where he was going, and they lay out in the open for about two hours, and then a man walked them over one at a time to the wall they go on and got on with what he was doing. I sat in my car and watched the whole of it and I have not given it a thought since until now."

Nobody put anything to that. The man in his forties said that a man with a barrow is not going to say where he is going, and the woman who works in a shop said that was true, and the man in a fleece said the man with the barrow would have gone wherever barrows go, which is wherever he has to be next, and the man at the end of a street said that was about it, and it went no further than that and it was not a question and nobody answered it.

Then the man in his forties and the man in a fleece were on their own by the urn with about half an hour gone since the last of the Sunday, and the man in his forties said the tea had gone cold again, which it had, and the man in a fleece said, "It is always cold again," and that was the whole of the twenty minutes.

Nobody in that room said one word that night about a sentence that a woman of about fifty-five said twice in one week to two different people in two different places, and she was nowhere in that hall on the Wednesday, and she is at the end of that corridor now with a jug in each hand, and nobody has asked her and nobody is going to.

**A MAN AT THE END OF A STREET WENT OUT OF THAT HALL AT TEN AND WALKED DOWN THE ROAD TOWARD A PARADE WITH HIS HANDS IN HIS POCKETS, AND THE TWO MEN WHO HAD BOTH BEEN TOLD A SENTENCE WENT OUT FOUR MINUTES APART AND DID NOT WALK TOGETHER.**
