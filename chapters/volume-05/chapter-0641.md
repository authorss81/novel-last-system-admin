# Chapter 0641 — The Two Lies In One Kitchen

Wednesday the nineteenth of May 2021, in this borough. She is on the tiles of her own kitchen for about twenty minutes and on her own settee from about a quarter past ten until about half past twelve, and there are two people in her front room in between, and the whole of it is one chair and a tin on a top shelf.

**SHE STANDS ON THE FOLDING CHAIR TO GET A TIN DOWN AND THE LEG SHE WAS NEVER PROPERLY FIXED GOES, AND A MAN WHO BUILT A STOOL IS IN THAT KITCHEN INSIDE TWENTY MINUTES, AND A WOMAN WHO BROUGHT THE CHAIR IS IN IT A LITTLE OVER AN HOUR AFTER THAT, AND A WOMAN IN HER LATE SEVENTIES ON HER OWN SETTEE TELLS TWO LIES IN ONE AFTERNOON, ONE TO EACH OF THEM, TO KEEP THEM FROM FINDING OUT ABOUT EACH OTHER.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. He is a man in his early forties who has not been on that road since the Wednesday of the fifth of May. She is a woman in her late forties who has not been on that road since the Friday of the fourteenth.

---

Here is what she has been doing since the Wednesday of the fifth of May, and it is worth putting down first, because everything else is built on it.

She has been swapping them.

The green folding chair comes out of the corner by the dresser in the morning and goes to the table. The stool goes onto its side against the wall by the sink. At about seven in the evening the green chair goes back against the dresser and the stool comes out and goes to the sill.

Every morning and every evening. Since the fifth of May. For nobody.

Nobody came up that path between the fifth and the fourteenth. The woman in her late forties sat on the Friday in a chair that had been put out for the man. The woman in her late seventies sat in a chair that had been put out for the woman. Neither of them knew it. Neither of them mentioned it. And at the end of that Friday the woman in her late seventies had to go and get the stool out again at about ten o'clock at night, because she had eaten sitting up and she was not going to eat standing.

There is a tin on the top shelf of the cupboard over the sink. It is the tin with the good biscuits in it, or it is the tin with the ones she cannot get anybody else to eat, and it is on the top shelf, and she has not been able to reach it since before Christmas.

There is a chair in this house for getting to the top shelf. It is not the folding chair. There is a wooden chair in the passage with a back on it and it is the right height.

And standing at the sink on the Wednesday morning with the cloth in her hand, she decides she is not going to get the wooden chair.

If she goes over with the wooden chair then there is a chair on the floor and a woman on the floor and a house somebody has to come to. There is a woman in her late forties who comes up that path with a spanner. There is a man with a white van. If one of them has to be here inside half an hour, then she will have to stand in her own kitchen and be examined and be asked whether she is all right and say she is all right. And then the chair is a story in this road, and a story about a woman of seventy-eight on the floor of her own kitchen is the end of the road for a woman of seventy-eight.

And the folding chair is right there. It is in this kitchen, with the cushion on it, because it is a Wednesday morning and she swapped them at seven.

---

Here is what she does, and it took about ten seconds and she thought about every part of it.

She takes the cushion off. She opens the chair out, and it is stiff, and she can hear the hinge when she does it, which is the iron, which is the piece of angle iron the woman in her late forties put on with a bolt through it in a bad mood on a back step in April.

She folds the cloth over the back of a chair and moves the table against the wall under the window, because a table in the middle of a kitchen is in the way of a chair, and she has not moved that table since before Christmas.

She puts the folding chair on the table.

She gets on the table on one knee and gets the tin down off the top shelf with her fingers, and it is not at the back, it is exactly where it is always at the front, and she has it in her hand.

And then she stands up on the chair with the tin in her hand, because you do not come down off a chair one-handed, and the leg goes.

There is a small dry knock in the timber of the table, a foot above the floor, and the chair goes sideways and the table goes with it, and the tin goes across the room and lands on the tiles by the oven, and she lands on the tiles.

The table legs go one way and she goes the other, and the green chair is under her, and she knows it is the green chair because the top of it is by her left ear and she can see the green from where her face is.

Her wrist goes into the gap between a table leg and the frame of the oven. That is all. It is not broken, she knows that inside about a second, because it is not the pain that does that, it is the heat coming off the oven on the inside of her arm.

She lies there. And then she gets herself onto her back knees, and then onto her feet, using the oven, and she gets to the settee in the front room, and she puts a bag of frozen vegetables on her wrist out of the freezer.

It is about ten past ten and the house is very quiet and she has not shouted and nobody has heard.

That woman is coming. Not today, because it was a Friday last and it is a Wednesday today. But she is coming, and it will be a Friday, and it might be this one.

And there is no version of *hello* that covers a table against a wall and two marks on the floorboards and a tin by the oven. So she will have to say something about the floor before the woman can say anything about the floor, and it has to be small, and it has to be true, and the only small true thing in this house today is the leg.

The leg came out. The leg is out. Four words, and none of them a lie.

And then the woman in her late forties will be on her knees on the tiles with a spanner, and she will say *that's the pin, that's not my iron*, and there will be a conversation about the pin.

And in that conversation, if she gets anywhere near the corner by the sink, the stool will come up.

She hears the van at about twenty past ten.

That is not an assumption. That is a white van she can hear from the front room, and she has not heard it on that road since the fifth of May, and she knows the difference between this van and every other van that comes up that road, and she is not going to write down why.

Her first thought, in about half a second, is that it is a Wednesday.

Her second thought, a second later, is that he has been on that road four times since February and has never once come on a Wednesday.

And her third thought, about two seconds after that, and it is the one she has to sit with for the next half hour, is that he has come up this path to ask whether the stool is all right, and could not ring because there is no number, and so he has come, and she is going to have to lie to him as well.

---

He knocks at about half past ten. Two knocks and a pause and one more, which is not her knock and which she is now going to be at for the rest of her life.

She does not get up. She is on the settee with a bag of frozen vegetables on her wrist and she cannot get to that door in the time it takes him to decide, and a woman of seventy-eight who has had a fall and is not answering a door is a woman somebody telephones about.

He knocks again. And then she hears the thing a person does outside a door when they have decided to look through the letterbox, and she says, in her own front room, to nobody, "Go on then."

He comes through the door because the latch was not on. It is never on. He has never noticed and she has never thought about it.

He is in the passage and then he is in the kitchen and then he is on one knee beside her settee with his hand out, and she says, "Don't lift me."

And he says, "I'm not lifting you."

And she says, "Good."

And he says, "Are you all right."

And she says, "I've not broken anything."

And he says, "Which arm."

And she says, "The right one's the oven. The left one's the table."

He looks at the two of them, and she watches him do it, and he is quick, and she is impressed in a way she did not expect to be.

And he says, "Do you want a hand getting up."

And she says, "I'll do it."

And he says, "Right."

And she gets up, on her own, in about a minute and a half, and he does not offer again, and she is grateful for that in a way she cannot put on paper.

And then she asks him for the stool. That is the pivot of the afternoon and it takes four words.

And she says, "Can you bring me the stool in."

And he says, "The stool."

And she says, "The little one. By the sink."

He goes and gets it, and brings it in, and sets it down about two feet from the settee, and does not come any nearer, and she sits down on it.

And the thing goes through her at that moment, on a stool a man built at a bench in a unit off an industrial estate, with a bag of frozen vegetables going soft on her wrist.

Somebody came up this path this morning to ask after a stool. And somebody else came up that path the Saturday before last and left a chair. And they are five days apart and neither of them knows the other exists, and she is sitting in the middle of it.

She is a quarter of an hour from the end of the road with a spanner in her coat.

Here is the whole of what happens next. It takes about an hour, both of them are decent, and it is the worst hour any of the three of them has had.

He hears her at the door. He is in the front room and she is in the front room, and he looks at her and she looks at him and neither of them says one word, and in that silence each of them has about four seconds to do the same sum, and the sum comes out the same.

Then the woman at number seventy-eight says, from the front room doorway, in a voice two sizes too big for the room, "Both of you sit down. There's tea in the pot and I made it before."

And it is the best sentence anybody says in this borough, and neither of them ever finds out what it was for.

They sit in that front room for about fifty minutes and they do not talk about the table and they do not talk about the leg and they do not talk about the two chairs.

And what happens is that the woman at number seventy-eight tells two lies in about four minutes, and it is the first time in her life she has told two in one afternoon.

The first one is to the man.

And she says, "That's the woman from the council about the damp. She came on the Friday about the corner."

And he says, "The council."

And she says, "They've been round about the corner behind here all through April. That's not your business, love. Don't you go interfering."

And the woman in her late forties, who has not been inside a council office since she was at school, sits on a chair and takes it. She takes it because a woman of seventy-eight has told a man to keep his nose out, and that is a thing you take.

And the second one is to the woman in her late forties.

And she says, "That's the man who did the kitchen at my daughter's. He's a good one. He came on his own time, which you don't get anybody to do now."

And the woman in her late forties looks at a man in his early forties sitting in a front room in a good jumper, and she has never seen a man sit like that and not be at work, and she says, "Oh," and that is all she says.

And then she says, "Is the table all right, do you want me to look at it."

And the woman at number seventy-eight says, "Never mind the table."

---

He goes first, at about a quarter past twelve, and he leaves his card on the table by the door, which he has never done, and which is a thing a man does when he has decided he is going to be in a house again.

And on the pavement he stands with his hands in his pockets and works out four things in about twenty seconds, and two of them are that there is no council woman about the damp behind that house and that the woman in her late seventies has not got a daughter, and he has checked both, and he does not use either, and he cannot work out why he has not.

And he gets in the van and thinks, plainly and without any feeling about it, which is the part that ought to worry a man: *she is covering for somebody and it is not me.*

---

She follows at about half past twelve and goes straight up the road to the parade without stopping and is in her own kitchen at about twenty past one, and puts the spanner on her table and stands in her own front room.

Two lies, in one afternoon, in her own front room, about two people who have each done something for her and neither of whom has been asked for anything and never will be.

And on the parade at about twenty past one she works out that both of the two of them went out of that house believing she is standing in front of one of them on behalf of the other, and that neither of them has any idea who, and that she has not the faintest idea herself, and that this is the arrangement now and it was made by a woman of seventy-eight on a Wednesday afternoon while she was on a settee.

And the first lie cost her a woman. She gave the woman in her late forties to the council, which is a thing that woman would have been furious about if she had heard it. She heard it. She took it. And the taking is the worst of it, because she took it because a woman of seventy-eight told her to keep out of it, and that is a thing a person does for an old woman and does not do for a lie.

And the second one cost her nothing at all. She gave a man in his early forties to a daughter she has not got, and he was not in the house to be contradicted.

And the difference between the two is that the first was for somebody in the room and the second was for somebody out of it, and she worked that out in about a second, and it is the most useful thing she has thought since before Christmas.

And then: three stories in this house now. The man who came for the fridge. The council about the damp. The man who did my daughter's kitchen. And the one that comes first is the oldest and it is the thinnest, and it is the one that will be hardest to keep, because she made it up first, when she did not have to.

**SHE SWAPPED A CHAIR AND A STOOL TWICE A DAY FOR NIGHT AFTER NIGHT FOR NOBODY, AND THEN STOOD ON THE FOLDING CHAIR TO GET A TIN DOWN AND THE LEG CAME OUT, AND A WOMAN IN HER LATE SEVENTIES ON HER OWN SETTEE TOLD THE COUNCIL ABOUT THE DAMP TO ONE OF THEM AND TOLD THE OTHER ONE THAT THE MAN DID HER DAUGHTER'S KITCHEN, AND BOTH OF THE TWO OF THEM WALKED AWAY THINKING SHE WAS COVERING FOR SOMEBODY, AND IT IS NOT FOR EITHER OF THEM, AND SHE HAS THREE STORIES IN ONE HOUSE NOW AND THE OLDEST IS THE THINNEST.**

(End of chapter 0641)
