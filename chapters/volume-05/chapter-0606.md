# Chapter 0606 — The Number on the Back of the Envelope

The Wednesday of the twenty-fifth of November 2020, from about half past one in the afternoon until about six in the evening, in a house in this borough.

**SHE HAS BEEN CARRYING A NUMBER SINCE BEFORE THE SUMMER AND SHE HAS WRITTEN IT OUT ON THE BACK OF AN ENVELOPE IN PENCIL IN A DRAY, AND TODAY SHE PUTS THE ENVELOPE ON THE SHELF IN THE HALL, AND THE HALL IS WHERE SHE KEEPS EVERYTHING SHE HAS MEANT TO DO.**

She is a woman in her early fifties. The number is a mobile one and it belongs to a man in her early fifties who was her husband's partner for most of a decade and who is not her husband's partner now and has not been since before the summer.

It has been in a drawer in the kitchen since she wrote it. On Saturday she moved it out of the drawer in the kitchen, because she had been going into that drawer most mornings since the June for something that is never in there any more, and because a drawer you go into as often as that becomes the place you keep things you cannot look at.

So it went on the shelf in the hall, and the shelf in the hall is where she keeps everything she has meant to do.

The envelope is one of a bundle of about two hundred that came with a mattress she bought in the June, and the back of it has a corner that is not printed on and the pencil goes into it well. She wrote the number with the number of the house on the front of it and then the number of the flat, and then, because she had the pencil out and had not decided yet, she wrote the word *ask* and put the number back.

That happened on the Wednesday of the twenty-second of July and she has been carrying the knowledge of what it says ever since without once putting it down, and she has not rung it.

Here is why, and she has the reasons in a sequence and has had them since the July.

The first is that she will get on well with him. She knows that because she has watched her husband do it for most of a decade from the other side of a table. She will get on well with him, and he will be kind to her about her husband, and she will like him, and the liking will be the problem and not the reason for the call.

The second is that if she rings and he is decent about it then she will have to say what she wants, and what she wants is money, and her husband took the money.

The third is that if he is not decent about it then she will have to say what she wants to her husband.

The fourth is that she does not want either of them to happen, and she has worked out since the summer that not ringing is not the same as not deciding. That is the thing she has got to. Not ringing has become an activity in its own right and it is quick and she is good at it.

---

Here is what today was.

She had been at her mother's at half past one and had got back at about ten to two, and her husband came in at about ten past two with a bag from the supermarket and put it on the kitchen table and went straight through to the front.

Then she said, "Have you heard from him?"

And he said, "Who?"

And she said, "You know who."

And he said, "Not for a bit."

And she said, "Right."

He was standing at the table unpacking. He said, "Why?"

And she said, "Because of the money."

And he said, "Right."

That was the entire conversation and it was over in a few seconds and neither of them raised their voice.

He said, "You want me to ring him?"

And she said, "No."

And he said, "All right."

And she said, "I'll do it. I've got to do it. I'll do it and I'll ring you in."

And he said, "All right."

And he went through to the front and she stood in the kitchen with the carrier bag in her hand until she had put it down, and then she went into the hall.

The envelope was under a folded tablecloth on the second shelf, which is where she had put it on Saturday, which is where it had been under three other things since Saturday.

She put it back under the tablecloth and then she stood looking at the shelf.

And then she did the thing that is actually the shape of the day and not the conversation: she moved the shelf.

It is a shelf she put up herself in about 2011 when the hall was redone, and it has been the shelf for everything since then, and everything on it is a thing she has meant to do. There is a lamp with a flex that twitches. There is a toaster somebody gave her that she does not use because it takes a slot too narrow. There is a roll of wallpaper in a cardboard tube from a house she decorated herself in about 1998. There is an envelope from a school about something in 2004. There is a hair dryer with a plug that has been wrapped in tape since about 2012.

And there is now the envelope with the number on it.

She got a chair and dragged it over to the shelf, which is a job, because the shelf is over a cupboard and the chair is under the stairs, and she lifted the chair up onto the floor with her foot so she could get at it without moving the table in the hall.

She was on the chair. She got the folder of photographs out from behind the wallpaper tube, which is a folder with a rubber band round it and about two hundred photographs in it, and she put it on the shelf beside the envelope, and she looked at the two of them side by side.

Then she sat down on the chair, which came down about four inches because it is that kind of chair, and she looked at the two of them.

She has not decided anything. The two facts are that a folder of photographs is on the shelf in the hall and that an envelope is on the shelf in the hall, and the shelf has about nine other things on it that have been on it for years.

Here is what she thought about, in the sequence and not in the sequence she would tell it in.

She thought: this shelf is a thing I built and it has become a place where things go so that they are in the house.

She thought: the reason that is useful is that it is not a hiding place. A hiding place is where you go and look. This is not where you go and look.

She thought: that is the whole of what I have arranged, and I did not arrange it, and it took me from before the summer to now, and it is the only thing in this house I have got right on purpose.

Her husband came back through at about half past four.

And he said, "You've moved the photographs."

And she said, "They're on the shelf."

And he said, "Why?"

And she said, "They were in the landing drawer."

And he said, "Right."

He looked at the shelf for a moment and then he said, "That's a lot on that shelf now."

And she said, "It is."

And he said, "You're not going to ring him."

And she said, "Not today."

And he said, "You'll ring him on the Saturday then."

And she said, "You'll have told me what Saturday is by then."

And he said, "Well. You'll have picked a Saturday."

That is the closest either of them has come to it and it was a joke about Saturdays and neither of them laughed.

Here is the last of it.

She cooked the dinner and they ate it and he did the washing up and she dried, because that is how they have done it for about twenty years.

At about six she went into the hall for her bag and the chair was still in front of the shelf, and the folder was on the shelf where she had put it, and the envelope was under the tablecloth where she had put it back.

And she did not move the chair. She left it there and she went to the bus stop with her bag and the chair stayed in the hall with the photographs and the number on it, and it will be in the way every morning until she puts it back under the stairs, and she knows that, and she has decided that the chair being in the way is a thing she is prepared to have in the way every morning.

**SHE PUT A NUMBER WRITTEN IN PENCIL ON THE BACK OF AN ENVELOPE ON A SHELF IN THE HALL WHERE SHE KEEPS EVERYTHING SHE HAS MEANT TO DO, AND SHE PUT A FOLDER OF PHOTOGRAPHS BESIDE IT, AND SHE LEFT A CHAIR IN FRONT OF THE SHELF ALL EVENING, AND HER HUSBAND SAID YOU'LL RING HIM ON THE SATURDAY AND SHE SAID YOU'LL HAVE TOLD ME WHAT SATURDAY IS BY THEN.**
