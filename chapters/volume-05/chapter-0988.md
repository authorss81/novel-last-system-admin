# Chapter 0988 — The Partition And The Decision

Undated in this draft.

**A PARTITION OF BREEZE BLOCK STANDS BETWEEN THE FRONT ROOM AND THE BACK ROOM AT NUMBER TEN.**

The partition at number ten is four courses of breeze block, eight blocks to a course, laid in a line across the house between the front room and the back one, with no gap anywhere along the run and no bond into either side of it. It was put in when the house was made into two, and whoever laid it bedded the blocks in mortar that was mixed too wet and has since gone hard as a stone, and set the first course out of level on both sides at once so that the whole run leans about three-quarters of an inch out of true with the joists it stands on.

He came along to look at it in the morning and the two sisters are in the front room with their coats still on and the front door standing open behind them into the passage. One of them is the woman of the house, in her early thirties, and the other is her sister, and they have both been in the room long enough to have taken their coats off and put them on again.

"It's not bonded," he tells them, tapping the face of it with the back of his knuckles. "That's mortar and that's a wall that has been stood up rather than built. It wants down."

"We know it's not bonded. It's been down twice already."

"Then it doesn't want down twice." He goes along the run with his thumb in the open joint at the second course, where the joint is the width of a thumbnail and the block above has begun to lean out over his thumb. "Who put the offer to you?"

The sister of the house does not answer that at all, and the other one answers it.

"I did. My sister's had a place offered on the crew that goes up the river. Six weeks, same as the sheet you read at number twelve. She's been told to have an answer in and she hasn't."

"So you want me to tell you whether to take it."

"I want one person to get it out loud and I couldn't care which one. I've asked our mother and she cried at me. I've asked him —" a movement of the chin at the man of the house, "—and he hasn't looked up off the paper once."

"Which paper?"

"You've not looked up off it since you came in."

He puts the rule down on the sill, which is the whole mistake, and stands in the middle of the room with his hands hanging at his sides and finds that he has used up the thing he came in here for and has not got to the end of it.

"I can put the six weeks in front of you in a minute." "I know what the sheet at number twelve is for and I know what the crew's for and I can put both of them straight inside a minute, and I'll do it now if you want it. What I can't do is tell you to go."

"You can. Everybody can. That's how the whole thing works."

"That's how it works for somebody who isn't asking you."

"Then don't."

The front room at ten is about eleven feet across with one window in the far wall and a door to the stair that nobody uses, and the partition stands across it in a line of pale blocks with the light from the window going flat over the top of it and stopping there. The top half of the wall casts no shadow at all on the floor in front of it, because the light in there is nearly level, and what that means is that anybody standing on this side of it at four o'clock in the afternoon would be standing in a bright room with a shadow of their own head going up a wall that is not doing anything.

---

He takes it down in the day and it comes apart like something that has only just been stood up. The blocks lift off the bed joint with a bar and one knock and he stacks the sound ones by the door and breaks the rest, because a breeze block with a knocked corner is no use to anybody and there is nowhere on this ground to put a heap of them. It came down in the order he did it and the mortar at the bed joint was soft enough to lift with a chisel and hard enough to break the arris of the block above it, so that half the labour of taking it out was breaking up good block afterwards.

The wall was bedded all round and not bonded along the run, and it is the oldest thing there is and he has taken out a dozen of them. Under the bed joint at the bottom the floor is bare boards with the dust of years in the joint against the skirting, and the skirting itself has been let in and out along its length by most of a quarter inch at each end, which is what a wall does when it is taken out and put back.

The two sisters were in the front room when he started and they were in the front room at half past two when the last course came off, and neither of them had put a hand on anything. He could have asked either of them to help and did not, and that went into the day along with everything else.

Under the head of the wall at the picture rail there was a row of picture hooks on a wooden strip, three of them, all in a line about nine inches apart, and the strip has been screwed to the lath and not to the plaster, and when the wall came away it came away with the strip still on it and the strip came off the lath with about a foot of lath on the back of it. He has put that at the top of the heap where the other blocks are and taken the screws out of the strip with the point of his bar, and put the screws in his pocket, because there is nowhere on this ground to put a handful of screws and they are not mine to leave in a strip.

He went back to them near four. They had not moved the stack and had not gone out. The sister of the house was standing in the gap he had made in the middle of the house, looking north into the back room and then turning round to look at him, and her face was the face of somebody who has been standing in a room for four hours waiting for a thing to happen to somebody else.

"It's down." He turns round to her without stopping. "The opening's the width I told you. I'll take the beam out on Tuesday unless you want the front room shut first."

"We want the front room shut."

"Then I'll put the beam in and close it up the same day, and it'll be a fortnight's wait for it."

---

He got his bag off the step at half five and the sister was still in the doorway of the front room with her arms across her chest.

"You've been in this house all afternoon and you haven't asked me one single thing," she told him. "That's what I was waiting for. That is the reason I came to you and not to somebody else."

"You asked me to decide and I wouldn't."

"You could've."

"And if I had, you'd have gone up the river for the rest of your life on a Tuesday afternoon because a man you had known ten days told you to, and never once in your life would you have asked yourself why." He did not have an answer for that and she went back in and shut the door, which is not a thing a person shuts a door at in a house where you have been all afternoon. He picked the lath and the bar and the folding rule up and put them in the bag in the order he keeps them in, and the new opening in the front room of number ten stood square and out of true with the joists by three-quarters of an inch with a foot of pale broken block at the bottom of it, and nothing about the woman's decision had moved an inch of it in either direction.
