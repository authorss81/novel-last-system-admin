# Chapter 0220 — The End of a Shift

Tuesday the second of February 2016, from about half past six in the evening, a hall off a parade of shops in Tideglass.

**SHE SAID IT IN A CORNER BY THE DOOR IN PLAIN WORDS AND HE ACCEPTED IT IN ONE SENTENCE AND DID NOT ASK WHY, AND NEITHER OF THEM WAS THE REASON THE OTHER WAS IN THE ROOM, AND IT WAS NOT A REPAIR AND IT WAS NOT A RUPTURE AND IT WAS ALSO NOT NOTHING.**

There were about nine people in the room and a gas heater on the wall that everybody had their back to, and the woman of about forty-two who has the key was in a chair by the switch, and there was a pot of tea on the table that had been going since about half past four.

Jonas Mercer had come in at twenty past six to ask her one question, which was whether the room was free on a Friday evening in the middle of February, and she had said yes, and had said it the way she says most things, which is before he had finished, and had not asked why he wanted it, because the answer to that question is always no and everybody in that room knows the answer to that question is no and about one person in five asks it anyway.

Sanaa Iqbal came in at about half past six. She had been on since two and she had done nine hours of it and she had a cup of tea in her hand before she had got her coat off, and she was not there for anything, and Jonas Mercer had not known she was going to be there and had not arranged it and had not come early because of it.

He had been coming to that room at the end of her shift for about six weeks.

Nobody had asked him to and nobody had told him to, and there was no arrangement and no list and no note of it anywhere, and the first two times he had come at six she had said *what are you doing here* and he had said *sitting in a warm room*, and after the third time nobody had said anything, and the last three weeks he had been coming at ten past six, which was about ten minutes before she finished, which was not a coincidence he had arranged and was not a coincidence she had arranged either.

**AND THE ONLY LATTICE NOTICE ON THE WALL OF THAT ROOM THAT WINTER, printed at about the height of a person of average height, and it defines nothing, and nothing on it defines *holder*:**

> **A ROOM FILLS WITH WHOEVER IS IN IT.**
> **TWO PEOPLE IN A ROOM ARE NOT A REASON FOR EITHER.**
> **NEITHER OF THOSE IS AN ARGUMENT.**

She got him in the corner by the door, which is about four feet wide and has a rack of leaflets in it, at about twenty to seven, with her back to the room, and she said it once and she said it in the voice she uses for things she has already thought about for a long time, and Jonas Mercer turned round and faced the wall of leaflets.

"I am going to say a thing and it is not about the work. You have been in this room at about ten past six on a Tuesday for six weeks and you have not been asked to and you have not asked to and I have let it be a way for the two of us to finish a day, and I want to stop it. It is not September. It is not anything you have done. It is that about nine people in this building have worked out that the last ten minutes of their afternoon is now a thing that happens, and two of them have started saying good evening to me when you come through that door, and they are not allowed to arrange their afternoon around me and they are doing it, and I am not going to be the reason a room of nine people arrange the last ten minutes of anything."

"Right," said Jonas Mercer. "I won't come at six."

He said it in one sentence. He did not ask why. He did not say that he was sorry, because that would have been a way of putting himself in the sentence she had just said, and he did not say *can I explain*, and he did not say *is there a version of it*, and he did not ask whether it was the man in the doorway in November or this, and he did not stand there arranging his face, and Sanaa Iqbal watched him not do four or five things, and said nothing, and put her cup down.

"Right," she said, and went back to the table, and he stayed in the corner for about half a minute and then came and sat down at the other end of it, which is where he has sat.

---

The room did what the room does. A woman of about eighty-nine had brought a shoebox of photographs and about nine of them were looked at. A man of about eighteen, who washes up in a kitchen at the back of the parade six days a week and is on nothing, said that the tea had gone the colour of a wet cigarette, and the woman of about forty-two said it had been that colour since about five, and nobody made more tea for another half hour because nobody wanted to be the one who admitted the tea was bad.

At about a quarter to eight the photographs were still going round, and Jonas Mercer looked at about six of them and could not tell anybody in the room who was in any of them, and said so, twice, and laughed at himself both times, and the man of about eighteen said "that's the whole idea, mate" and went back to his tea, and the woman of about forty-two laughed too, which she had not been going to.

At about ten to eight somebody asked him about the room in February, which is what he had come in to ask about, and he said what he wanted it for, and it was about ten minutes of talking, and the woman of about forty-two agreed to it, and wrote it in the notebook in the drawer, and not on the card by the door, because the card is a list of names and a Friday in the middle of February is not a name, and there has not been one on that card in nine years.

Sanaa Iqbal left at about ten past eight, and he left at about ten past eight, and neither of them waited for the other and neither of them said goodnight to the other in the room, and they were on the same pavement for about two minutes and then one of them went north and one of them went west, and that was the whole of it.

He did not write it down. It was not a thing to write down. It was a woman in a corner by a rack of leaflets saying a true sentence about her own life to one person, and it was said once, and it was not said to anybody else, and nobody improved it, and nobody in that building has any idea it happened.

**AND HE WENT HOME UP WARD STREET AND DID NOT THINK ABOUT IT FOR FIVE DAYS, AND ON THE SATURDAY HE CAUGHT HIMSELF SITTING IN A CORRIDOR THINKING ABOUT IT AND HE SAT THERE UNTIL SOMEBODY OPENED A DOOR, AND HE DID NOT WRITE IT DOWN AND HE DID NOT GO AND FIND HER, AND ON THE TUESDAY HE WALKED PAST THAT PARADE AT HALF PAST SIX AND CARRIED ON WALKING, AND SHE FINISHED HER SHIFT AT EIGHT AND CAME OUT ON HER OWN.**
