# Chapter 1024 — Three Treads And An Open Door

Undated in this draft.

**THE PASSAGE AT NUMBER TWELVE HAS A STAIR ON THE LEFT AND A DOOR OPEN AT THE BACK.**

The passage at number twelve is about four feet wide and runs from the front door to the back door of that house with the stair on the left and the door of the front room on the right and nothing else in it, no table and no chair, so that it is the coldest place in that house and the one room everybody in it goes through four times a day. The door of the scullery at the far end stands open on its hinge, and from the bottom of that stair you can see straight through it to the wall behind the range, and the wall carries the mark of the water the same way it has carried it for years, a foot high, with a soft edge to it where the paint has let go of the courses under it.

The hearth under that range is set back about three inches from the front edge of it on the left and flush with it on the right, which is not level with the top of the range's own front rail and never was, and what comes off the wall behind that range runs down the wall and off the hearth and into the corner where the slab edge is, because the edge of that slab does not run true with the wall it is set against and never has.

He came in at the front door with a figure in his head that he had worked out on the flags between number ten and number twelve, and it was the wrong figure, and he had brought it anyway because he wanted to see what she would do with a wrong one.

She was standing in the passage in her outdoor coat with the scullery door open behind her, and she had not been in the coal shed, and he could tell that by the fact that she had not asked him about anything else.

"I have come for money."

---

"You have come to ask me what I think that wall is going to cost you."

"Yes."

"And you have brought a figure."

"I have brought a figure and I have brought it because not once in forty years has a man of my kind asked a woman what anything costs him, and I did not know how to begin, and I have been working at that figure since number ten and it is wrong."

"Whose wrong one."

"Mine. It is a builder's figure and it is what the work is and it is nothing to do with what it costs me, and nobody of that sort stands in this borough with anything behind him, and I am that man, so no figure of that kind exists, which is why I have brought you one." He put his hands flat on his coat pockets where the other money was. "Do not take mine. Tell me what it costs you."

She stood in that passage and worked at it. He stood and let her get on with it and it took as long as it takes to boil a kettle, and she did it without a pencil and without going out of the passage.

"Then here is what I have got, and I want you to hear all of it before you answer, because I have been working at it for two days and I do not want my half of it played at. That wall has been wet since before I married into this house and it is going to be wet next winter as well, because you have told me it can be freed and not mended, and I have had to sit with that for two days. And a fortnight of a man's time on a range and a flue and a tray is the thing I have got to pay for, and it is nothing like a small thing to me and nothing like a big thing to you, and I am not going to be the woman who makes a man put into words what a thing costs him."

"It is the second money I have taken off that frontage in ten years and the second I have taken anywhere at all."

"Then put it back in your pocket and do not let me make you smaller."
They arrived at it between them. She went into the front room and got the coins out of her apron and did not do it behind her back, and she put them into his hand, and he put them out on the table one at a time so that she could see every one of them go down, and then he turned round and put them all back in a heap on that table and took nothing.

"You have not taken it."

"I will take it when I start. Not one minute before, and not a coin of it now, because there is nothing done yet that anybody could point at, and the first money you ever hand a man ought to be for something in your kitchen that you can stand in front of and look at." He put his hands back in his pockets. "And I want you to bring it to me yourself, and I am aware of what I have just done to you and I am asking it anyway."


There is a thing about that passage that a man who has gone ten years without asking anybody for anything finds out by standing in it, and it is that the whole of it is four feet wide and both of them are standing in it, and neither of them can get away from the other without going through the room where the page is on the table. She came out of the front room and he did not go down the passage to meet her. She came towards him and he stood where he was and let her come, which is the whole difference between a man who puts a question to a woman about money and a man who never does.

He thought about what the woman at nine had told him on a stair about the price of it, and about what she had told him about the two men who would not take a shilling off her, and he thought about the tin on the deal table at number ten and about a woman of the house at that number who had got a figure out of herself over two days with nothing to hand and nobody in the room, and who had then made him take the money in front of two people so that he would find out what it cost.

The two of them arrived at the same figure by opposite roads, and neither of them would have got it alone, and he is aware that a woman on that frontage has now worked out what a man like him is for and has put a price on it, and that the price is in coins on a table in her front room and is not going to be paid until a range comes off a wall in a scullery at the back of her own house.

On his way out he got as far as the bottom of those three treads and stopped, with the scullery door standing open behind him and the light off it on the flags.

"What is your name," she asked him. "I am going to be telling my daughter who has been in this house for six weeks."

She had asked him that on the morning she came back, in the front room at twelve, with the sash up four inches in his hand at that window, and he had stood at that window and given her nothing. He gave it to her now, standing in a passage with his back to the light, and it took about as long as it takes to get it out, and he did not put it into her hand on a piece of paper and he did not ask her for anything with it.

She let it lie where it fell. She moved her head once and put nothing on paper, and neither of them put the word about it again, and nothing else happened to it at all.

Then she went up the passage and got her coat and went out of that front door on her own two hands, and she did not shut it behind her.

She has not been in the coal shed at the back and she has not put the four words on the inside of its wall to him, and she is not going to, and neither is he.

What she does not know is what she is going to ask for when she gets to that yard, and she has gone out of that door with a figure in her apron and a man's name in her head that she got in a passage for nothing.
