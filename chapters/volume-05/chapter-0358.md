# Chapter 0358 — Which Way It Opens

Thursday the fifth of October 2017, from about a quarter past six in the morning until about ten past seven, a parade, a passage at the top of it, and a shop off it, in Tideglass.

**THE MAN WHO PUTS THE SHUTTERS UP ON THAT PARADE HAS DONE THE SAME NINE SHOPS IN THE SAME ORDER SINCE BEFORE THE SUMMER, AND ON THURSDAY MORNING THE MAN IN HIS FORTIES WALKED DOWN THE SAME PASSAGE HE HAS WALKED DOWN SINCE HE WAS TWENTY-SEVEN AND A DOOR CAME TOWARDS HIM THAT HAS ALWAYS GONE THE OTHER WAY.**

The man who puts shutters up gets to that parade at a quarter past six on most working mornings of the year, ladder over his shoulder, and he has been doing it since before this borough had a name for the parade.

He does the third shop, the fourth, the fifth, the second, the sixth, the eighth, the seventh, the first and then the one at the top, and that one is last because it is the one that takes longest, and he has not changed that order since the year before last and is not going to change it for anybody.

He is at the second one by about ten past six. He has the shutters up on the first four by about half past six and does the last five on a different day of the week.

The man in his forties was on that parade on the Thursday because the man who puts shutters up asked him to hold the top of a shutter while he got the bottom hook in, and that takes a man about four minutes a shutter and there were two of them.

He has stood on that ladder and held the top of that shutter about thirty times since the start of the year and it is the only work anybody has ever asked him to do for nothing.


The man who puts shutters up does not talk on a round. He has done the same nine shops in the same order for a very long time and he does the work and he says good morning where a man says good morning and he has never once asked anybody anything, and there is nothing about Thursday morning that would make him ask a man in a passage anything either, because he would not have seen him.

The two of them were finished at about a quarter to seven and the man who puts shutters up shouldered his ladder and went down the parade and the man in his forties walked behind him as far as the corner and then turned up the street.

The passage is between the second shop and the third and it goes through to a yard behind, and it is about four foot wide and about thirty foot long, and there is a door at the far end of it into that yard, and there is a stone ledge down one side of the passage at about hip height where men put their bags down.

The man in his forties has gone to the bottom of that passage about a dozen times since he was twenty-seven. He has a job in that yard about twice a year. He has never once gone through that far door, because there is nothing past that far door, because it goes into a yard that is reached from the road as well.

He has put his hand flat on the face of that door and pushed it about nine times in his life.

---

On the Thursday morning it came.

He was on his way down that passage at about ten past six with the ladder across his shoulder and he put his hand on it and pushed, and it came towards him about eight inch and stopped against his chest, and he took his hand off it and stood in the middle of that passage.

There is nothing on the other side of that door worth a man walking out of a passage to look at. There is a yard about thirty foot by twenty, with a concrete floor and a wall on two sides, and along the wall there is a stack of something under a sheet of plastic, and the sky is the width of the yard and about a foot above the top of the wall, and the light at the bottom of that passage is exactly the colour of the light that comes off that yard.

He knows that. He has stood in enough doorways to know that a door with a bit of sky on the other side of it lets a different light in than a door with a wall on the other side of it, and this one had a wall on the other side of it in June and a sky on the other side of it this morning, and there is no way on this earth for a man standing in that passage at a quarter past six to know which of the two is true.

He had gone down that passage on a job in the winter and on a job in the summer and twice for nothing at all. The last of those was the Tuesday before last, when a man he knew asked him to go and look at a heap in that yard, and the heap was not in that yard, and he stood at the bottom of that passage for about ten minutes saying so. The door went then. He put his hand on it and pushed and it went away from him into the yard and the leaf made a noise like a door does.

He stood in that passage for about a second and a half with the ladder on his shoulder.

He looked at the light at the bottom of that passage for a while, long enough that a man coming the other way would have had to go round him.

Then he went back up the passage and out into the parade and did the other shutter, and he was back on the second one at about half past six and the man who puts shutters up was on the first and neither of them said anything about it.

The man who puts shutters up did not see him go down. He had the bottom hook of the first one and his back was to the passage, and the passage is four foot wide and dark at that end in October, and a man standing still in a dark passage at a quarter past six in the morning is not a thing anybody is going to see.

He has not been back into that passage since. He has been in and out of that yard twice since, both times from the road side, and the far door is where it was.

Nobody in that parade knows that a door at the bottom of that passage has changed and nobody ever will, and there is nothing on the outside of that door or the inside of it that says which way it opens, because a door is a door and it is either propped or it is shut and this one is shut.

---

At ten past seven he was in that shop buying a roll of tape, and the woman who works in a shop had her back to the room with a receiver against her shoulder.

He waited about three minutes. She turned round with the receiver still against her ear, said "one moment" to nobody, and put it down, and he asked for the roll of tape and she went to the rack for it.

"You are not one of the early ones," she said, coming back.

"I am not one of anything," said the man in his forties.

She put the tape in a bag and he gave her the money and picked it up, and the only other thing said in that shop that morning was the weather, and it was said once each way and did not amount to more than that.

He did not tell her about the door and she did not ask him about the door and neither of them had anything to say about a door.

At about ten past seven he was at the top of that parade with a roll of tape under his arm and the shutters were up on eight of the nine shops and the one at the top was the one that takes longer, and he went down the passage with the roll of tape in his hand to cut it off against the edge of the ledge, and he went down the passage and he did not stop in it and he did not go through the far door and he did not put his hand on it.

He cut the tape off against the ledge and went back up.

**HE CUT A ROLL OF TAPE OFF AGAINST A STONE LEDGE AT THE TOP OF THAT PARADE AT TEN PAST SEVEN, AND A DOOR FOUR YARD BEHIND HIM IS SHUT AND OPENS THE OTHER WAY, AND HE IS THE ONLY MAN IN THIS BOROUGH WHO KNOWS THAT IT IS NOT THE DOOR HE THOUGHT IT WAS.**
