# Chapter 0440 — Ten Past Two

Friday the fourteenth of September 2018, and every day of the four weeks before it, one corner of a building off Marsh Lane, in Tideglass.

**FIVE PEOPLE WENT OUT TO THE SAME CORNER OF THAT BUILDING AT ABOUT THE SAME TEN MINUTES EACH AFTERNOON FOR THE BEST PART OF A MONTH, AND NOT ONE OF THE FIVE KNEW THAT THE OTHER FOUR WERE DOING IT, AND IT WILL BE THERE AGAIN TOMORROW.**

The corner is the south-east one, where the gable of the stair block comes out past the end of the flats behind it and makes an L of about nine foot. In the afternoon the sun clears the roof of the low building across the yard at about ten past two and puts a wedge of light on the tarmac of that corner, and from about half past three the wedge is gone and the corner is in the shade of the gable whatever the weather is doing.

It is the only place in that yard where there is any sun at all until the middle of the afternoon. It is also the only place where a person standing there is not on a window.

---

The man in his thirties has been in the top flat of the end building for two years and he eats in it, and he does not like eating in a room on his own in the middle of the day.

He goes down at about ten past two. He goes down because in the cold of his first year here he went down at four o'clock on a Sunday, found the corner cold, and went up again, and worked out after a while that the light is the only thing about that corner that changes, and it changes at ten past two.

He sits on the low wall under the gable with his back to the bricks and eats off a plate and stays about twenty minutes, and he has done that most days since the middle of August. He reads the same four pages of a paper over again and does not know that he does.

---

The woman on the first floor has her stair and her own door and a landing nobody uses.

She goes out at about ten past two with a cigarette she has not smoked in the house, because she does not smoke in the house, and standing in a doorway is not standing anywhere. She goes round to the corner and stands with her shoulder against the gable, in the part of the L that is out of the wind, and finishes it, and puts the packet and the stub in her pocket and stands there another two minutes doing nothing at all, and then goes in.

On the days it is raining she does not go out. On the days it is cold she does not go out. On the days she has not got a cigarette she does not go out, which is about two days a week, and it is not that she has decided not to.

The man in his sixties is on the ground floor of the middle building and he goes out at ten past two every day without exception, and has since before any of the others.

He takes a cup with him and stands with his back against the wall on the other side of the L, facing the yard, and drinks it in about four minutes, and then stands there a while longer, and then goes in.

He does not sit. He cannot get down low and up again without putting a hand on the wall, and he has worked out that if he puts a hand on that wall it is a thing people can see from a window, and he would rather not.

He is out at that corner in weather that would keep a younger man in, and there has never been anybody else standing on it when he has come out, on any day, since April.

The woman at the end has a plate and a fork and takes her food out there because the light is better on that side of the table than on any other side in that flat.

She goes out at about ten past two and eats in about nine minutes and takes the plate in and washes it when she gets back to the sink, which is on the other side of the flat, so it stays out of her way for an hour. She has been doing it since the hot weather and she will go back inside in a fortnight. She is not a person who is out of the way. She is a person with a plate.

She has looked round that corner perhaps a dozen times since the middle of August and there has been nobody on it.

The fifth one has a van and no particular hour, and he has been at that corner at about ten past two on three days a week through September, and he stands, and looks up the gable, and puts his hands in his pockets, and goes in again.

He is not a smoker and he is not eating, and there is nothing there he will sit on. He comes out because on that Wednesday and that Thursday and that Friday he has been in a van on the same yard since before seven in the morning, and this is the ten minutes where he can stand up straight.

The first time he came round that corner there was a woman standing in the L with her back to the sun and he nearly went back. He stood about eight foot off and looked at the gable for as long as it took her to finish, and then he went in, and he has thought about it once since.

He has not mentioned it. There is nobody in that yard he speaks to, and he is not going to start with a woman on a first floor because he came round a corner.

None of the five of them has ever seen another one of them at that corner.

That is not a thing any of them arranged and it is not a thing anybody agreed to. Four of them started inside their own front doors because of something about their own morning, and the fifth started because of a van. The wedge of light comes round that gable at ten past two whatever any of them does, and five separate afternoons at about ten past two have ended up at the same nine foot of wall with nothing between them but the fact of it.

There is nothing there. There is no list of it anywhere, and there is nobody keeping it, and there is no way of finding out about it except standing in that corner. The one person who has ever stood in that corner who was not one of the five saw a woman from eight foot off and said nothing and went in.

The light comes round that gable again at about ten past two tomorrow, and about half of them will be out, and nobody will count.

**A WOMAN WITH A PLATE CARRIES IT IN AT TWENTY TO THREE AND WASHES IT AT THE OTHER SIDE OF THAT FLAT, AND A MAN WITH A CUP STANDS WITH HIS BACK TO A WALL IN AN L OF NINE FOOT, AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS ANY IDEA THE OTHER ONE IS THERE.**
