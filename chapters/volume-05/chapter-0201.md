# Chapter 0201 — The Drawer

Friday the thirtieth of October 2015, from about half past three in the afternoon, a canteen in a building in Tideglass.

**THE HATCH ON THAT CANTEEN GOES UP ON A FRIDAY AFTERNOON FOR ONE MAN AND ONE PERSON PUTS IT UP, AND THE ONLY THING IN THIS BOROUGH THAT COULD BE CALLED A LEVEL OF SERVICE IS A PRINTED SHEET THAT WENT ROUND NINE BUILDINGS IN JUNE WITH NINE LINES ON IT, EVERY ONE OF THEM SAYING *no requirement stated*, AND IT IS IN ALL NINE OF THOSE BUILDINGS STILL, AND NOBODY PUT A NAME ON IT.**

The canteen is on the ground floor and it shuts its hatch at two. It has shut its hatch at two for about nine years. There is a hatch and a counter and about nine tables and a woman of about sixty-four behind it called Beryl, who has worked there for six years and was on the counter before her, and who is not on anything, and there is no document in that building with her name on it.

A man in overalls at the end table had been asleep with his head on his arm since half past two and had not been asked to go home. Jonas Mercer had been in the canteen since about half past two and had eaten, and was on a second cup of tea he did not want, because there is nowhere else in this part of the city to be at half past three on a Friday, and because on Friday afternoons the canteen is where the borough's one public room is.

A man of about thirty-one comes in at about ten to three every Friday and has done since he was at the college, and he is a man who wants his tea and is in no hurry, and at about half past three Beryl told him the hatch was shut and he said he would come Monday, and she said the hatch was open.

That is the whole of the correct thing. She opens the hatch on Friday afternoons for a man of about thirty-one who comes at ten to three, and nobody asked her to, and nobody can, and the kitchen staff had gone at two, and she does it herself with a mop and a bucket and about six minutes of a gas ring, and it costs her a half hour of her own time on a Friday, which is the most expensive half hour in this building and is not in any budget because there is no budget for it and no line for it.

**AND SOMEBODY SAID THE SENTENCE THAT IS ALWAYS AVAILABLE IN THAT KIND OF ROOM, WHICH IS THAT SOMEBODY OUGHT TO WRITE IT DOWN, AND IT WAS A MAN WHO WANTS HIS TEA AND IS IN NO HURRY, AND A WOMAN OF ABOUT SIXTY-FOUR BEHIND A HATCH THAT SHUTS AT TWO SAID NO IN ONE WORD.**

Jonas Mercer had eaten in this room on and off since the middle of May. He had watched that hatch go up twice, and both times it had been the woman doing it and nobody else in the room, and both times he had thought *there is a thing that happens here* and had gone on drinking his tea, because that is what a thing that happens is. It happens, and it goes on happening, and a room can tell you that it happened and cannot tell you when it will happen again, and that is as much as anybody gets anywhere in this borough and it is not a thing anybody can be shown.

"You should write that down," said the man of about thirty-one.

"No," said Beryl.

"Somebody ought to. It is years since you started opening that hatch on a Friday. It is not a big thing, but it is a thing, and if somebody came along in five years they would not know."

Beryl put the bucket under the counter and stood up.

"You are right," she said, "and I have thought about it, and here is what happens. If I write that down, then there is a piece of paper in this building that says a thing was done on a Friday, and then this is a building where things get written down, and I have worked in a building where things got written down and it took about a year and a half for the writing down to be somebody else's job. And then there is a man upstairs who wants to know how many people come through that hatch on a Friday, and there is no way of telling him and I have to say so, and then there is a piece of paper in a drawer in here with my writing on it about me being here on a Friday, and there is not one part of that I would be able to explain afterwards."

"You could write it without a name," said the man of about thirty-one.

"I could. I have thought about that too." She looked at him. "And then it is a piece of paper about a hatch that anybody could have done, and in nine months somebody will ask who did it, and I will either say it was me, which puts my name in a second thing, or I will say it was nobody in particular, which is a lie about a Friday, and I am not going to sit in a canteen on a Friday being careful about a lie."

The man of about thirty-one said, "Fair enough," and drank his tea, and Jonas Mercer, who has not asked anybody a question since the twenty-fifth of February, said nothing at all, and she was right on every part of it, and he had been asked nothing and had offered nothing.

**AND THEN SHE DID SOMETHING NOBODY HAS DONE IN THIS BOROUGH FOR NINE MONTHS, AND SHE DID IT ON PURPOSE, AND SHE PAID FOR IT HERSELF.**

At about ten to four she got a sheet of paper out from under the till. It was the back of a bread delivery docket with the printed side turned down, and there was a corner of it still stuck to the next one off the pad, and she tore it off, and she put it on the counter flat, and she got a biro out of the drawer under the counter, which has no lock on it and never has had one, and she wrote on it, on the back of a bread docket, in the middle, in a hand that went over the print:

*Fri. hatch open. till 6.*

Five words. It was not the right thing to write. There was no date on it and no year and no clock time and no hatch number and nothing in it about a Friday in October, and it said *hatch* and not which hatch, and it said *till 6* and the truth is that she shut it at about half past four, and the last stroke went through the word the printer had put underneath it.

She read it. She did not tear it up. She folded it in half once and put it in the drawer under the counter and shut the drawer, and that drawer has no lock on it and has never had one, and there are about nine other things in it that are not a list.

"Never mind," said the man of about thirty-one.

"That is right," said Beryl. "Never mind."

Jonas Mercer was the only other person in the canteen and he saw her do it and she knew he saw her do it, and neither of them said a word about it, and he is not going to ask her about it, and he is not going to tell anybody about it, and there is nowhere in this city he could hand it to even if he had.

And that is the first time in nine months that a correct thing in this borough has come with a piece of paper on it, and the paper cost somebody something, and the somebody was the person who did the thing, and the piece of paper is in a drawer in a canteen with five words on the back of a bread docket and no name on it and nothing in it that anybody could be shown, and no list of people who cannot be found has been built in this borough in nine months and one has not been started.

**THE ONLY LATTICE NOTICE ON THE CANTEEN WINDOW THAT AFTERNOON, printed at about the height of a person of average height, and it defines nothing, and nothing on it defines *holder*:**

> **A THING DONE ON A FRIDAY IS A THING DONE ON A FRIDAY.**
> **A NOTE ABOUT IT IS A NOTE ABOUT SOMEBODY.**
> **NOTHING ON THIS NOTICE IS HELD AGAINST ANY PERSON.**

The man of about thirty-one left at about four. Beryl washed the counter and the bucket and the gas ring and turned the sign. Jonas Mercer sat on with his cold tea until about half past four and then walked out into a street in Tideglass on a Friday evening with the drawing on more walls than it was in September and nobody in this city able to say how many, and the river down two feet since the rain, and five weeks of correct things in this borough and not one of them on a piece of paper except a bread docket in a drawer that has no lock on it under a canteen counter that nobody asked about and never will.
