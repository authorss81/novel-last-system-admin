# Chapter 0281 — Sand

Saturday the seventeenth of December 2016, from about ten in the morning, a shop that shuts early on a parade in Tideglass, and a kitchen at the back of that parade.

**A MAN SHOVELLED SAND AT ONE BACK STEP IN JULY AND TOLD THE WOMAN WHO RUNS THAT SHOP, AND HE SHOVELLED SAND AT ANOTHER ONE IN THE SAME WEEK AND TOLD A WOMAN WHO WORKS IN A SHOP, AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS MET THE OTHER, AND A THIRD PERSON SAID IT OUT LOUD IN THE MIDDLE OF A SHOP ON A SATURDAY MORNING WITHOUT KNOWING HE WAS STANDING THERE.**

There was a breakfast on at that shop on the Saturday before Christmas and the kitchen at the back of the parade had done it, and a lad of about eighteen was at the dishwasher in that kitchen, and the man in his thirties was cutting bread at the back of the shop, because the woman who runs that shop does not do her own shop on a Saturday and has not since about 2011.

He had been in that shop since about half past eight, and he had asked for a bag of cement in July, and he had shovelled sand at the back of that shop on the Thursday of the second week of July, because there is a soft patch at the back of that shop where a lorry was in and turned with a load on, and the shop's floor is a slab that was poured about 1998 and is not going to move for a load of sand.

Three barrowloads, and the sand came out of a builder's merchant over the river because she had no account and he had a card.

That was the whole of it, and she had not mentioned it since July, and on the Thursday of the second week of July he had also been at a house at the top of a street off Marsh Lane where a slab was lifting at the back and there was a gap under the edge of it about the width of two fingers, and he had gone down there on the Wednesday with the same card and shovelled six barrowloads into it and tamped it with the back of the spade, and he had told the woman who was in the kitchen of that house about two months after that, in a different week altogether, in the middle of September, and told her that it would hold.

---

There was no queue at all until about half past ten, and the first person through that door was the woman who works at the yard at Sarn, and she had been coming in on a Saturday for the best part of two years for a paper and a jar of something, and she had never once been asked anything by the woman behind that counter.

"You've had that man in the back, haven't you," she said. "Shovelling. About the time it was warm."

"Aye," said the woman who runs the shop. "July. He did it in a morning and he wouldn't have a drink of water out of the cold tap and I made him one out of the hot and he had that as well. What of it."

"He was at mine," said the woman who works at the yard at Sarn. "In July. Same week, near enough. And I've only just found out from Julie, and Julie only found out in about November when I asked her why her back step was level."

The woman who runs the shop had a hand flat on the top of a crate of tinned tomatoes and she had not moved.

"Well," she said.

"Well what."

"Well, he's stood right behind you," said the woman who runs the shop, "with a bread knife in his hand, and he's heard the whole of it, and if you want to say anything else to him you can say it to his face."

The woman who works at the yard at Sarn said, "I beg your pardon," and the man in his thirties put the bread knife in the block on the counter and did not come round it.

"You shovelled at mine," he said.

"At yours?"

"In July. Behind the shop. A soft patch where the lorry's been in and turned. Three barrowloads, and I wouldn't have a drink of water and she gave me a hot one, and I've been back once since and it's still level."

"I've not been shovelling anywhere," said the woman who works at the yard at Sarn. "I've had a man in about the second week of July and I did not ask him what he was called, because I am not going to stand in a yard asking a man what he is called, and he was not doing a great deal of harm, and I have thought about it since and I have never rung anybody."

"Which second week," said the man in his thirties.

"Second week. Wednesday or Thursday. It was the week it was about thirty degrees and my own bin men were off sick."

"I was at yours on the Thursday," said the man in his thirties. "And I was at the top of Marsh Lane on the Wednesday, in the same week, at a house where a woman called Julie lives, and she works in a shop in a place with a name off Corrance Lane, and there is a slab at the back of that house that was lifting, and I put six barrowloads in the gap under it, and I have been back twice since and I have not seen it move."

"And you told her."

"I told her in September," said the man in his thirties. "I told her in September because I was in her kitchen and it came up. I did not tell her in July because I did not know how she'd take it and because I did not want to be stood in a yard on a Wednesday explaining myself, and that is a bad reason and I know it is a bad reason."

The woman who runs that shop had not said a word through any of that. She came round the counter with a bag of a pound of small onions in it and set it on the counter in front of the woman who works at the yard at Sarn, and turned the top of the till towards her.

"That'll be ninety-one," she said.

After that the woman who works at the yard at Sarn went out at about a quarter to twelve with her paper and her jar, and did not go to the top of Marsh Lane, and did not tell anybody at the yard, and did not say one other word about it to anybody, including the woman she got it from.

The woman who runs the shop did not go up the street either, and did not ring, and did not go and stand in anybody's kitchen and say a man shovelled at my back step in July.

At about half past twelve the man in his thirties went up the street on his own with a bit of wood in his hand and left the shop, and the shutter of that shop came down at one as it does, and a lad of about eighteen came out of the kitchen at the back of the parade with the last of a tray on it and set it down on that counter, and nobody said anything to him about any of it, and he did not ask.

**AND ON THE MONDAY A WOMAN WHO LIVES AT THE TOP OF MARSH LANE ASKED THE WOMAN SHE LIVES WITH WHY THERE WAS SAND IN THE GAP UNDER THE SLAB AT THE BACK, AND WAS TOLD THAT SHE HAD LIVED THERE SINCE THE SPRING AND HAD NO SAND IN THE GARDEN, AND NEITHER OF THEM RAISED IT AGAIN AFTER THAT, AND THE SLAB WAS STILL DOWN ON THE THURSDAY.**
