# Chapter 0339 — Three Letters Off a Sign

Monday the twenty-fourth of July 2017, from about half past nine in the morning until about half past twelve, the low end of Cleeve Street, in Tideglass.

**A SHOP AT THE LOW END OF THAT STREET HAD THREE LETTERS COME OUT OF A SIGN OVER ITS WINDOW IN THE SECOND WEEK OF JUNE, AND A MAN SAID AT THAT SHOP DOOR THAT HE WOULD HAVE THE THREE MADE AND PUT BACK BEFORE THE END OF THAT MONTH, AND IT IS THE TWENTY-FOURTH OF JULY, AND ON A MONDAY MORNING HE SAID IT FOR THE FOURTH TIME TO A WOMAN WHO HAD HEARD IT FROM TWO OTHER MEN, AND NEITHER OF THEM SAID IT THE WAY HE SAID IT, AND HE LET IT STAND.**

The shop shuts at about half past three on a weekday and has shut at about half past three for as long as anybody in that street can remember, and it is a shop that sells bread and offal and a thing called a plain cake, and the woman who runs it has run it for nineteen years and she is not on anything.

The sign over that window is a strip of pressed aluminium about fourteen foot long and it carries the name of that shop in cut letters about four inches high, and every letter is on it in its own fixing, and three of them came out of those fixings in the second week of June and went where three letters go.

They went on the roof. The woman who runs that shop found the first one on the flat roof at the back of the shop, the second one in the gutter by the side door, and the third one was not found and has not been found. There are three holes in that aluminium about the size of a pencil going through, with the rims of the fixings still round them and the paint gone in three circles, and rain comes through the three of them into the shop and lands on the front edge of the counter about four inch inside the glass, and there is a plastic bucket on that counter under the middle hole and it has been there since the second week of June and the woman who runs that shop empties it into the sink about four times a day and does not put it on the floor, because a bucket on the floor in that shop gets trodden.

A man had said in the road outside that shop in the second week of June that he would have those three letters made and back in before the end of the month, and there are four people alive who heard him say it.

---

He came up that street at about half past nine on the Monday morning with his bag and stopped about ten foot short of the shop and stood in the road and looked at the sign.

He has looked at that sign four times since the middle of June. He has stood under it and read it. The name of that shop has three gaps in it about the width of a finger each, and from the far side of the street it looks like a name with three letters out of it, and from the road there is no telling which letters have gone unless a person already knows the name, and everybody on that street knows the name and none of them can say.

He put his bag down on the kerb. He has a pad of work notes in the bag with the elastic still round it and he did not take it out.

"Aye," said the woman who runs the shop that shuts early, from inside the glass, and she was wiping the counter at the other end and had not stopped for him.

"You've got a bucket on that counter," said the man in his thirties.

"I've got a bucket on that counter," said the woman who runs the shop that shuts early. "Since June. And I am going to have a bucket on that counter every day until the summer's out, and then it will be a bucket on that counter every day the rain comes, and nobody has said one word to me about the bucket, and I have not said one word to anybody about the bucket, and I am telling you about the bucket because you have been stood in that road looking at my sign for a minute and a half."

"I am going to have those three letters made and put back in that sign before this month is out," said the man in his thirties.

She stopped wiping the counter.

"You said that in June," she said. "You said it at this door and I have had a bucket on that counter since the week you said it."

"I am saying it again," said the man in his thirties. "That is the third time. You can count them, there is no reason why you should not count them."

He said it before she had asked him anything and she had not been going to ask him anything, and he heard himself do it, and it took him about as long as it takes to put a bag on a kerb.

He is on nothing in this borough. He has not been on anything since a kitchen in Corrance Lane in June. He has a pad in that bag with about four lines used in it in two years, and there is a line on that pad for that shop and that sign, and he has never opened it on that street, and he did not open it on that street that Monday morning, and a pad of work notes is a thing a man carries for a year without ever needing the last page of it.

---

The woman with a clipboard came down that street at about a quarter to twelve on her beat.

She carries a pencil on a string and a sheet of stiff paper in a plastic wallet, and what is on that sheet is everything a street puts in a walking person's way, and it is the only thing in this borough that anybody has ever written her name on, which is nobody, and about two thirds of it is done.

That sign was the thing she stopped at. She stopped at it because it is a sign with three letters out of it in the middle of a run of shops where nine of the fourteen signs are complete, and she stood under it and counted the holes from the pavement the way she counts posts and gaps.

He was standing in the road about ten foot off with his bag at his feet.

"That sign's had three letters out of it since June," said the man in his thirties. "I'm going to have them made and back in before the end of the month."

The woman with a clipboard hooked the pencil over her ear and looked at him.

"You've said that before," she said.

"That is the fourth time I have said it out loud," said the man in his thirties.

"A man in a fleece made a point of it to me at the shelter at the top of that street about three weeks ago, and said that a man had told him he was going to have that sign done."

"That is me," said the man in his thirties.

"And the man who does that hall on Carrow Lane said the same thing to a man in his forties in June, and the man in his forties did not say the name and neither did the man in a fleece." She took the pencil from behind her ear. "What I have heard twice is that somebody is going to have that sign done. That is what two men have said to two different people and it is the version of it that has gone round this borough. Not the letters. The sign."

"Does it matter," said the man in his thirties.

"It matters to me," said the woman with a clipboard, "because I have got thirty-one things on this sheet and I have got a habit, and what I have got in my head now about that shop is that somebody has given them something. And what you have said to me this morning is that somebody is going to make three letters. Those are two different facts and one of them is worth more to that shop than the other one, and I have no way whatever of telling you which one of us is right, and neither of us ever will."

"I have said three letters four times and I am going to do three letters," said the man in his thirties, and then he shut his mouth, because he had heard the end of that sentence go out into the road and stay there, and it was the fourth time that sentence had gone out of him in a month and a half and he had not put one of the three things in his hands on the ground.

She wrote nothing on that sheet. There is no column on that sheet for a man's word and there is not going to be one.

"I am not going to write a word of that down," she said. "I have got about two more lines of room on here and I am going to use them both on a stand and a grating, and if a man in this town says he will do a thing in the road that is a thing that happened in the road."

He said that was fair and she went on down the street and did the next four.

He stood in that road for another few minutes with his bag at his feet and the bucket visible through the glass on the counter at the other end of that shop, and the man in his forties has not been up that street since the middle of June and the man in his sixties has not been either, and the woman who runs the shop that shuts early emptied that bucket at about ten and again at about half past ten, and there are three holes in an aluminium sign at the low end of that street and nobody in this borough has done anything about them.

**A PLASTIC BUCKET WENT UNDER THREE HOLES IN A SIGN ON THE TWENTY-FOURTH OF JULY AND WILL GO UNDER THEM AGAIN TOMORROW, AND A MAN STOOD IN THE ROAD BELOW IT AND SAID FOR THE FOURTH TIME THAT HE WAS GOING TO HAVE THREE LETTERS MADE, AND DID NOT.**
