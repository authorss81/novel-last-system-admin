# Chapter 0782 — Three Lengths Of Dead Hedge

Saturday the thirty-first of December 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house and yard at number ten from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a gap in the boundary hedge closed properly, and a barrow that does not go through it.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES HAS A GAP IN HER BOUNDARY HEDGE ABOUT THE WIDTH OF A BARROW AND MEANS TO OPEN IT UP PROPERLY SO THE BARROW WILL GO THROUGH IT, HER SISTER CLOSES IT WITH THREE LENGTHS OF DEAD HEDGE AND FOUR STAKES, AND THE BARROW DOES NOT GO THROUGH AND HAS NOT GONE THROUGH SINCE.**

She is a woman in her early thirties at number ten on Saturday. Her sister comes through the gate in the morning and goes out into the yard with her.

---

The back door shuts true on its latch. The drawer under the worktop is shut with the hammer, the screwdriver and the folded sheet with three lines on it in it, and it is not opened. There is nothing on the line. The ply is still standing against the shed front fastened to nothing, the felt above it is still off the boarding at the top corner, and the gravel sack is where it was put in May. The three boards laid down the path in October are still down it and the water is still going off the low end into a hole it has made at the corner of the shed. The scaffold board in the stile of that shed door is still standing proud of the face of it, and the beech over the roof is bare.

The gap is in the hedge along the bottom of the yard.

The hedge is on the boundary and it has been there longer than the house. It is a proper hedge, not a fence — beech, mostly, going over at the top, thick enough in places that you cannot get your hand into it. It runs along the bottom of the yard and turns up the side for about twenty feet and stops at the shed.

There is a gap in it.

It is at the corner where the bottom run turns up the side, and it is about the width of a barrow, and it has been getting wider since the summer. What happened is that there was an old gap there and she barrowed out through it — branches, a bag of rubble, the trimmings off the beech when it came down in October — and the barrow caught the live wood at the two sides of it every time and the gap opened up.

She has a spade, a billhook, a piece of batten and a bag of post pegs, and she means to open it up properly.

She means to cut the two ends of the live hedge back square, dig two post holes about the width of the barrow apart, put an oak post in each one, and stretch three wires between them at the heights the hedge is on the rest of its length, so that it is a gap with wires in it and a barrow can go through it and the hedge can be tied back to them and grow through.

Her sister comes through the gate in the morning with a mallet and four stakes under her arm.

---

"You are not making a gateway in that hedge."

"I am making a gateway in that hedge."

"You are making a gate with no gate on it, and putting it in the wettest corner of your ground." Her sister stops by the gap and looks through it. "There are no posts in that hedge anywhere along the bottom of it. It has been a hedge for a hundred years and it carries itself. You will put two oak posts in and you will wire across them and you will have made the one place on this boundary that can be seen through, from the lane and from the front of the house, from the road."

"Then it is a barrow hole."

"Then it is a barrow hole with a fence in it."

---

"You have been barrowing out through it."

"Since the summer."

"So how much have you got left to barrow out."

She does not answer that straight away, and her sister does not wait for it.

"What is the ground under that gap like."

"It is ground."

"It is the wettest corner you have got, because it is where the bottom run turns up the side, so everything that comes off that side of your yard goes down to that corner and stands in it." Her sister puts the toe of her boot in the gap at the bottom and pushes it under the leaf mould. "You are going to put two post holes in that. You will get them down as far as you can in a wet December and you will fill them with what you have, and one of them will be loose by the spring and it will lean with everything else, and the day a barrow catches that post it will be out."

"So I cannot have a barrow through there."

"You can have a barrow through there in about four years, if you want it that badly, and you will do it properly with a gate on it and a footing and a hard floor and a strip of stone across the front of it so the wheels have something to run on." Her sister is looking down the length of the bottom run. "You are not doing it this winter. That is not me saying no. That is me saying a gate is not a hole with wires in it and a hole with wires in it is not a gate."

---

So they close it.

Her sister digs it out on the near side with a spade and gets three lengths of dead hedge up out of the thick part of the bottom run, well back from the gap.

They come up whole. That is what dead hedge is: a length of the hedge that died at the bottom in the shade years ago and has been in there ever since, with its roots off it and two or three uprights grown through the middle of it. It comes out of the ground like a fence that has been lying down, and it is the best thing in the yard for what they want.

They lay them across the gap.

One on the bottom, lying along the line of the old hedge. One above it. One above that. And they weave the uprights — the ones that grew through the dead wood — in and out of the live hedge at each side of the gap, so that it cannot be pulled off the old one.

Four stakes go in. Oak, cut from the hedge itself off the top where it has grown out at the side, and they go in at an angle on the far side of the hurdle, one at each end of the bottom length and one at each end of the middle length, driven with the mallet and cut off flush.

"It is tied in as well."

"It is tied in as well, and it will grow. That is the whole of a hedge, that is what a hedge is made of, and if you stand back from it in a year there will be no way to say where that gap was."

---

It is the best-done thing in that yard.

She stands at the side of it and looks along the bottom run and cannot find the join. The three lengths of dead hedge are the same colour as the live hedge above them once you are a yard back, and the uprights are woven through the old wood and the new in the same way they are everywhere else along that run, and the four stakes are cut off flush and are going to go the same colour as everything else.

It is a hedge.

---

The barrow does not go through it.

She does not believe that either, and she gets the barrow off the wall where it has been standing since the summer and puts it at the gap and tries it.

The barrow goes a foot into the gap and stops.

It stops because the hurdle is there. That is what the hurdle is for. The bottom length of the dead hedge is lying on the ground across the whole width of the gap and it is pegged down at both ends, and the barrow cannot get over it because it is a length of fence lying on the ground and not a strip of stone.

She lifts it. That is nothing, you can lift a length of dead hedge with one hand, and she lifts it and pushes the barrow through.

She gets the barrow through that way.

Then she puts the length back and does it again going out, then loads it and does it coming in, and each time the barrow goes through on the second go and not the first.

"You have to lift it."

"You have to lift it."

"That is not a barway."

"That is not a barway. There is no barway here." Her sister is standing at the far end of the gap on the next door's side of it, which she has not done before today and did not ask to. "And it is going to be worse and not better, because that dead hedge will knit into the bottom of the live one and in a year it will be one piece of wood you could not break with a barrow, let alone push one through."

---

They stand in the yard and look at it.

The barrow is on the near side. The gap is closed and the barrow does not go through it and is not going to, and the only way anything goes between that yard and the next door's is round the front of the shed and out through the gate like everything else has always gone, and she has a bin of stuff in the barrow that wants to go out and there is nowhere to put it but round the front.

Her sister picks up the mallet and the four spare stakes and gets them under her arm.

"You will want that gate," she says, "in about four years, and not this year."
