# Chapter 0820 — Face Up

Tuesday the thirteenth of June 2023, in her own yard at number ten from after the middle of the day until the light has gone off that wall, and six slabs standing on edge against it that have begun to slide are the whole of that Tuesday.

**SIX SLABS ARE STANDING ON EDGE AGAINST A WALL IN HER OWN YARD AT NUMBER TEN AND THEY HAVE BEGUN TO SLIDE, AND SHE MEANS TO PUT THEM DOWN FLAT ON TWO BATTENS OUT OF THE WET, AND HER SISTER COMES THROUGH THE YARD GATE WITH A FOLDED BLANKET AND PUTS IT DOWN AND GOES OUT AGAIN THE SAME WAY.**

That wall is the boundary at the back of that yard, and it is about nine feet of old brick with a coping on it, and six slabs have been standing on edge against it since about the spring.

They were not put there by anybody in a hurry. They came off a delivery about two years ago and there were more of them than six, and they were set out along that wall on edge because a slab laid flat in the wet greens the underside of it and the only place in that yard where a slab can be put down and be dry is against a wall.

They have begun to slide.

On edge, a slab is a wedge. The paving at the foot of that wall falls about an inch in six feet towards the corner, because all the paving in that yard falls towards the corner, and a slab stood on that fall is a slab standing on a slope with nothing to stop it.

The bottom edges have gone out about an inch and a half and the tops are leaning over the wall by about that much, and the two nearest the corner are out about three inches and are going over towards the corner at a fair rate now.

If they go over they will not be picked up again. A slab that has fallen flat on a fall lands on its face and chips two corners and the chip is through to the stone, and there is no way of making that face look any other than it is.

The other thing about a slab on edge is the noise it makes when it goes, and she has heard it four or five times in two years.

A slab on a fall is not going anywhere until the day it goes, and on the day it goes it does not tip. It slides the whole length of the bottom at once, about half an inch in a slow scrape, and it lands on its bottom corner with a noise like a drawer being shut in another room.

She has never once found where the noise came from. It is a noise that happens at about half past four in the afternoon in that yard and there is nobody to hear it but her, and it has happened maybe four times in two years, and every one of them she has gone out and found one of those slabs out further than it was the last time she looked.

---

She has the slabs out of the way by the middle of the afternoon.

They come off the wall one at a time on edge, the same way they went on, and she walks each one back to the far end of that yard where there is a stretch of paving about eleven feet long under the eave of the shed that does not get any sun at all after about the middle of the afternoon.

There is a patch on that wall the width of the shed and about four feet up that has been dark since February. It is not mould and it is not damp coming through the brick. It is condensation, off the warm side of that wall, on a north side, where the temperature on the inside of the brick drops below the point where the air coming off the warm side of the room can keep it up, and the dew comes out of the air on the inside face of the brick and runs down the outside in a streak.

That patch is why the slabs were put against that wall and not against any other, and it is still there.

Two battens. They are offcuts, both of them about four feet long, one three inches wide and one two, and they came off something and they are not straight but they are straight enough for what is going on them.

She lays the first one and then the second one about a foot and a half from it, and she stands a slab down on the two of them.

---

She puts it face up.

Then she puts the second one face up, because that is what she has always done and because there is a reason for it.

Here is the reason, and she has had it since she was about nineteen and it has never once been tested.

A paving slab is not flat. It is made in a press, and the press puts a fall into it on purpose, about a quarter of an inch from one edge to the other, so that when a slab is laid as a path it sheds the water instead of standing in it. The face that goes up in a path is the face with the fall running off it. The face that goes down has the fall running the other way, which is to say it runs to its own edge and ponds there.

A slab laid the wrong way up ponds on its own face, and everything that ponds on a slab in this borough ends up as a black mark that never comes off.

So a slab goes face up.

And the way you tell which face is which, without anything, is this. The face that was in the press is close and smooth and cold under the thumb. The face that was against the mould has a skin of fine sand on it from the mould, and it is gritty, and it is the one that feels as though it has been handled by a great many people.

She turns the third one over. Then she turns it back. Then she puts her hand flat on it and slides it across, the way anybody slides a hand across a table to find whether it rocks.

And then she stops, and she stays stopped for about five minutes, and what stops her is that the third slab has no fall in it at all.

She has been looking for it since she was nineteen. You do not find it with a level, because a slab lying flat on the ground has nothing to be level with — the ground is the only datum there is and the ground is what it is — so you find it with your hand, and her hand says that slab is flat from edge to edge in both directions.

She gets down on one knee with her cheek about six inches off the stone and looks along it from the end.

Nothing. And she takes the straightedge out of the shed and lays it on the slab and puts the spirit level on the straightedge and it reads the same at the two ends, which tells her what she already knows and nothing else.

That slab is cast, not pressed. The outside edges of it are rounded where it came out of a mould and there is nothing across the middle of it of any kind, and the arris round the edge is a soft roll and not the sharp little chamfer a press leaves. Somebody poured that one in a tray, and a slab poured in a tray has the same thickness at the four edges, because a tray is a tray.

There is nothing to get wrong.

She lays it face up anyway, and that is the whole of the decision and she makes it standing up.

She lays it face up because the face that was against the mould is the gritty one and the face that was the top is the smooth one, and that is still a fact about the slab even when there is no fall, and because there is one way of laying a slab and she has one way of laying a slab, and the rule that gave it to her was not wrong. It was right about every other slab she has ever laid and it is right about most of the slabs anybody lays in this borough, and there is no second rule to reach for when the first one does not apply.

She picks the grit off the face of it with the back of her thumbnail, which takes a while, and lays it with the others.

Her sister comes through the yard gate with a folded blanket over her arm about an hour after that and puts it down on the paving.

The blankets get damp in that yard in a wet week and they never come right afterwards, and this one has already been through one.

"You can put them on that if they're going to be out," her sister tells her. "I'll take it back tomorrow."

"Thanks," she tells her, and puts it down over the two battens and the slab on them, and her sister goes out through the yard gate the same way she came in.

There is nothing else about that from either of them. The slabs are not the sister's business and the blanket is the sister's business and neither of those two things is said.

The rest of that Tuesday is four more slabs on the same two battens, which means six inches of overlap on the run because the battens are four feet and the slabs are about twenty-two inches by eighteen, and the last one on the end of the run is hanging a foot off the end of the batten with nothing under it.

She deals with that by putting a third batten under the last one, cut to nothing from the bottom of a pallet, and by laying that last slab the other way up so that it goes across the two instead of along them.

It goes across. She has done it the other way round on purpose and she has not decided that for any reason that would survive being written down.

The wall behind all six of them is that north wall under the shed eave, and the damp patch on it is where they are, and while the six slabs have been standing on edge against the other wall the patch has been there doing nothing.

They go up at four in the afternoon, not at the top of the wall, about a foot below the coping and a foot above the paving, and there is air under them and air round them and the sun goes along the top of that wall in June and gets right under the coping.

She stands in that yard about an hour before the light has gone off the top of it and looks at the patch on the wall where it has been all year.

The middle of it is still dark. The edges of it are going pale — not light, not dry, just less dark, about an inch in from the outline of where it has been, and the streak below it where the dew has run is a brown line on the brick that stops at the middle of the wall.

That patch has been getting a little bigger every year since that shed went up and it is not going to go away because six slabs are lying flat against the bottom of it, and she knows that, and she put them there because that wall is the driest place in that yard and because they had to go somewhere.

The blanket is over three of the six and the other three are bare and they will not be wet at all. The battens are offcuts and one of them is not straight. All six of them are face up.

It will be another winter before she finds out whether that was the right way round.
