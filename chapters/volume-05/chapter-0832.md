# Chapter 0832 — Cut At The Level Of The Ground

Sunday the thirtieth of July 2023, along the side wall of that house at number nine from the middle of the morning until there is no light on the grass, and four feet of privet that is going with the path is the whole of that Sunday.

**THERE ARE FOUR FEET OF PRIVET ALONG THE SIDE WALL AT NUMBER NINE AND SHE MEANS TO CUT IT BACK, AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING PASSES ALONG THE FRONT AT THE KERB IN THE MIDDLE OF THE DAY AND DOES NOT COME IN.**

That privet is along the side wall on four posts with wire between them, about chest high, with the bottom two feet gone to wood where the light never got in.

It is going with the path when the path goes.

It has been cut twice in two years and cut the wrong way twice, because the first of those cuts went with shears into the outside of it, and the outside is where privet has leaf, and privet cut back to a bare face puts out two shoots where there was one. It is chest high now because of the two times somebody put it right, and the bottom two feet are wood because the inside of a hedge never gets light and the inside of a privet is dead a foot and a half in.

She starts with shears and a saw and a sheet on the flags to catch the cuttings, working from the front to the back.

The tops come off first, down to where the leaf is thick. Privet cut to a bare stem puts out two where there was one, so she cuts to leaf and no lower through the middle of the work, leaving green on every stem she leaves.

That rule is for the mass and not for the stems. Everything above the bottom two feet is a job of judgement about how full a hedge wants to be, and every cut in it can be gone into again next week with a pair of shears and nothing lost but the cuttings. Cut a privet bare halfway up and in three weeks there are two shoots where there was one and four where the cut went through a stem with two shoots on it. It grows back thicker. That is not a fault in the plant, it is what it does.

So the tops come down about a hand into the leaf and the sides come back to the wire and the front and the back go to about the depth of the wire and no further, and there is green from end to end of it and the shape of a hedge with nothing in front of it.

Then the sides, back to the wire, a little at a time, with the sheet pulled along as she goes.

The shears go in about a foot at a time and the cut comes out in a length on the sheet, and the clippings from a privet are wet and green and they smell of the cut and they stick to everything they touch. She pulls the sheet along with the toe of her boot as she goes, and the sheet ends the morning in a long green roll four feet across, and half of it will go on the compost and half of it will want to be somewhere else.

Here is the whole of the decision, and it comes at the end with her knees on the sheet.

**The last cut is a different cut and it does not have a rule in it that anybody can get wrong by eye.** Everything above can be got wrong by eye and put right again, a branch too high or too low, a side too full. The cut at the ground cannot be got wrong by eye because there is only one place for it, flat to the soil, and it is the only cut on a privet that has one answer.

The snag is the whole reason. A privet left on a stump a quarter of an inch high has a flat top on it that keeps water in the middle of the face, and the water goes into the wood and rots the cut off, and in two winters the stump is a hole with bark round it. Left cut on the slope, the water runs off the face and there is nothing to rot behind. Left cut at the level of the grass and not one inch above it, the cut is under the grass and out of sight in a fortnight and no one can see a cut they cannot see, which is the only kind worth having.

So the last four are at the foot of the four stems, and she pulls the sheet round her knees and puts the saw on the first one low against the soil with the blade angled a few degrees so the face comes off with a downward slope on it.

She puts the saw to the foot of the first stem and cuts across, and the stem comes away clean with a pale face showing. Then the other three in turn, each one low and flat, until the four posts stand in a row with wire between them and nothing else.

Each face is about the size of a thumb and it is pale green and wet and it is the colour of a cut apple left out. It is out of the sun and off the ground by the end of the afternoon and it will be a brown ring inside a fortnight and grey in a month, and the grey face with nothing above it is the right way for that stem to finish.

The woman who does her shopping passes along the front of number nine in the middle of that Sunday and stops at the kerb to look at the heap on the sheet, and does not come in through the side gate. She does not offer and nothing is asked of her.

“The bus did not come,” she tells her from the kerb.

“Then you will be late for your dinner,” she tells her, and she walks on without another stop.

The side gate has stood open across the path on its two wedges through the whole of it and the spanner on the flags by the step has not moved, and there is a woman at the kerb on a Sunday afternoon looking at a heap of green cuttings through a gate she did not go through, and that is the only thing either of them knows about it.

---

The four posts are left in the ground where they were put in, and the wire and the ties are left on them, and the cuttings go in the barrow in two loads.

The posts are two by two at the corners and they sit shallow in the ground, and a privet that is going with a path should have come out whole with its root plate, and a post left in the ground is a post that will be an obstruction in the middle of a path and a thing to find when the path is dug.

She puts a finger down the gap between a post and its hole and can get it to the second knuckle, and the wood at that depth is grey and going, and there is no root on it at all because a privet this size is growing out of one stem.

Leaving them in is a decision and not an oversight, and she made it in about as long as it took to put a finger in the gap. Taking them out means digging four holes two foot deep in a bed two foot wide against a wall, in a bed she has just taken the turf off, for four posts that have nothing in them. Putting them back is not a thing anybody can be asked for. So they stay, and the wire stays on them, and the fact that they are in the bed is a thing she will be able to read off the ground in the spring if anyone ever wants to.

The front wall of that side bed has the low spot at the door end where the water goes, and the privet stood four feet along it and will stand four feet along it, and the weed that comes up through the gap between post and soil comes up in the spring whatever is done about it.

She knocks the last of the cuttings off the sheet into the barrow and folds the sheet over the barrow, and the green comes off it in a line on the flags where it was wet.

When there is no light left on the grass she stands at the wall and looks down it. Four feet of stub is in the ground along the side wall with the cut at the level of the ground and nothing above it, pale faces to the sky, and the four posts are still in it with the wire on them and the ties on the wire, and there is nothing else in that bed at all.
