# Chapter 0637 — The Stool

Thursday the twenty-ninth of April 2021. He is in a workshop off an industrial estate on the far side of the parade from about eight in the morning, and the man he works across from is at the bench at the other end of the unit and asks him one question at about half past four, and the whole of it is one stool and the working days behind it.

**A MAN WHO MAKES THINGS HAS DECIDED TO BUILD A STOOL FOR A WOMAN HE HAS SPOKEN TO TWICE, AND HE HAS DECIDED ON A STOOL RATHER THAN A CHAIR SO THAT IT CANNOT BE CONFUSED WITH ANYTHING, AND HE BUILDS IT HEAVIER THAN IT OUGHT TO BE, AND A MAN AT THE BENCH WITH HIM ASKS HIM WHAT IT IS FOR AND HE GIVES A REASON THAT IS NOT THE REASON.**

He is a man in his early forties. He has a bench, a bandsaw, a planer that came with the unit, a dust extractor he put in himself, and a lot of clamps that do not match. He does kitchens for people with money and people without, in that order, because that is the order they come in.

---

On the Monday he was on a first-floor conversion ten doors up from her, and he was on a tower in the road across the Monday and the Tuesday, and on the Monday he was at gutter height, level with the top of her kitchen window, at about ten in the morning, for about four minutes, working out which of two brackets to use.

And the kitchen window had a green chair in it.

There was a green folding chair in that kitchen on the Monday, standing in the middle of the floor, not against a wall, not in a corner, standing in the middle of the floor the way a chair stands when somebody has put it there to look at. And he noticed it, and he looked away, and then he looked back for about a second, because a chair in the middle of a kitchen floor is not a thing you see every day.

And a woman of seventy-odd was standing at a sill with a plate on the sill.

And he was on a tower in the road at the height of her window with a bracket in his hand and a hundred and twenty feet of road between him and that kitchen and he could not have asked her a single question about it if he had gone up that path, which he was not going to do, because he has spoken to her twice and both times about nothing, and one of the two was at a gate and one of the two was in the road.

Here is the two times, and he has not told anybody them, and he gives them to himself when he is trying to work out what he is doing.

Once, in the February, about the post. It had not come. He said it had not come, because the second post comes at about four and it had not come by the time he was going, and she said, "They've not come since Christmas," and he said, "They'll come," and she said, "They won't," and that was the whole of it.

Once, at the end of March, about the roadworks. The pavement outside the parade was up and she came down the road on a stick to look at it and he was on a ladder outside number twenty-eight, and she said, "Is it going to be a while," and he said, "Not long, love," and she said, "You said that Monday," and he said, "I did," and she laughed at him.

That is the whole of it, both of them, and he has never once asked her a question, and that is not an accident, and he has never once asked her a question because every question he has about her is about the room, and the room is where she is standing up, and if he asks about the room then he is in the room.

---

So the stool, and not a chair, and here is the arithmetic of that, done on the Sunday in his own kitchen with a pencil and a bit of paper he did not keep:

A chair is for a person who is sitting down somewhere else at the moment. A chair belongs in a room with a table in it. If you turn up at a house with a chair and a woman of seventy-odd is standing at a sill, what has happened is that you have been in her kitchen, and you have looked at where she stands, and you have worked out why, and you have handed her the answer.

A stool is not that.

A stool is a thing you can give a person without either of you looking at anything. A stool goes in beside a fridge. A stool is what you sit on to reach a cupboard. A stool is a thing a room has, not a thing a person is.

And the second reason, and this is the one he would not write down: there is already a chair in that kitchen and it is green and it is new and it was in the middle of the floor, and he does not know where it came from and he is not going to find out, and if he turns up with a stool then there is a woman in a kitchen with a chair she has not got a story for and a stool she has not got a story for, and that is two things to be strange about, and one is enough.

He picks the ash out of the rack and it is good ash and it is not the ash he wanted, because the ash he wanted went in February, and he stands there working out whether to make the seat from ash and the legs from something else, and he makes it all ash, because a stool in two woods looks bought.

Tuesday: he rips the seat blank. Two hundred and ninety by two hundred and ten by twenty-two, which is the seat and no bigger than it has to be, and he gets the grain running the right way across the short side, and he gets it first time.

Wednesday: he works on the legs and gets his angles wrong twice and has to start the second one again, and the second one is better than the first one and he keeps both because the first one is a good leg.

Thursday morning: he puts them together dry and stands it on the bench.

Here is the thing about a stool, and he knows it and everybody who makes furniture knows it, and it is that a stool is not a chair with the back taken off. A stool has to be *lower*, because a stool is for a person who is on their feet from morning to night, and a stool at table height is a stool for a child. So the seat goes at about twenty-three inches off the floor and not at thirty.

And a stool has to be harder to move than people think it needs to be, because the one thing a person does with a stool when they are not sure they want it is push it out of the way with a foot.

So he uses ash for the legs and oak for the four rails, because oak is heavier than ash by about the amount you would want, and he lets the rails be an inch and a half by three-quarters instead of an inch and a half by two-thirds, which is the normal thing, which takes about ten minutes longer and is the difference between a stool that gets pushed to a wall and a stool that is still where you put it when you come back to it.

He gets it right about half past ten and it is the best thing he has made since February, and he knows it while he is gluing it.

And then he gets the weight wrong.

He has it on the bench at about half past three and it is coming out of the jaws of the clamp and the glue has had about ten minutes, and he lifts it with two hands under the seat and it comes up and he can feel the whole thing.

It is too heavy.

Not a lot. About a stone. But he can feel where the extra is, and the extra is all in the rails, and he has put the extra in the rails because the rails are what stop a stool being pushed away with a foot, and he has built a thing that cannot be pushed away with a foot and also cannot be picked up by somebody with one hand.

And the person it is for is a woman in her late seventies who eats standing up, and she will not pick this up with two hands, and if she cannot pick it up she cannot move it, and if she cannot move it then she cannot get it out from under the window where it is going to stand, and if she cannot get it out from under the window then the damp patch in the corner of that floor is going to be behind it for good.

He stands on the bench with it in his hands and does the thing he has done four times since Christmas, which is the thing where he cannot let a thing go.

He puts it down and takes the top rail off, and he planes it down by an eighth of an inch, and glues it back, and then the second rail, and then the third.

And it is lighter by about half of what it was, and it is still the best thing he has made since February, and he can put one hand under the seat and lift it and swing it about.

And he knows exactly what he has done, and he knows the two facts do not sit together, and he knows which of them he is going to tell the bench.

---

The man at the other end of the bench is a man in his mid thirties and he is building a set of drawers for a shop in the parade and he has been at the bench opposite for a bit and they have worked in silence for most of it, which is the correct arrangement.

At about half past four he puts a piece in the vice and says, "Is that going out today?"

And he says, "Tomorrow."

And the man says, "It's nice that."

And he says, "It's all right."

And the man says, "That's ash, that."

And he says, "That's ash."

And the man says, "What are you doing, a stool?"

And he says, "I'm doing a stool."

And the man says, "For who?"

And here it is, and he has it ready, and he has had it ready since about half past ten, which is the tell, and he knows it is the tell.

And he says, "It's for a mate. Down the road. He's done a kitchen himself and wants something to sit on at the end of it."

And the man says, "Right."

And he says, "I couldn't tell you."

And the man says, "All right."

And the man goes back to the vice.

And that is the last of it. He does not know whether the man believed him, and the man did not look up when he said it, and a man who does not look up when he is told a joke is a man who has decided to be polite about it.

Here is what he has at about five, and he is not going to write any of it on a drawing up the side of the bench where he puts everything else, and he is not going to tell the woman.

One: a woman of seventy-odd at number twelve eats standing up, and he has known it for a while, and he is not the first person in this borough to know it, and he has not asked and is not going to, and if he does not ask then nobody ever has.

Two: there is a green chair in that kitchen that was not there at the end of March, and he does not know where it came from and he is not going to find out, and he has built a stool he did not need to build, and the stool is the right thing, and the fact that it is the right thing is the difficulty, because the right thing only exists if the problem exists, and the problem is a woman of seventy-odd standing at a sill with a plate on it and nobody in this borough ever standing in that kitchen and asking her why.

Three: and this is the one he will not look at directly. Somebody has already been in there. That is what the chair is. Not a man from a skip and not a husband, because a husband would be seventy-odd too and there is no husband. Somebody else has already been in that kitchen, and has already seen the sill, and has already done something about it, and has gone, and has said nothing to him, and will not, and will go on saying nothing.

And he has not even met them. He will not meet them. He is going to go up that path on the Wednesday evening, after work, with a stool under his arm, and put it in her kitchen and go out again, and he is not going to knock on number ten or number fourteen or any other door on that road, because if he does that he will find out who, and if he finds out who, he has got a choice to make about whether to mention that somebody else has already been in that kitchen.

And the choice is one he has already made, which is that he will not mention it to her either, because she has not mentioned it to him, and a man who lays a stool beside a stranger's chair in a kitchen at half past eight at night has not given anybody a seat.

He sands the stool down and oils it and stands it on the bench overnight, and wraps it in a blanket off the pile at the end, because a thing like that left in a van in this borough is not a thing you leave, and because a stool that still smells of oil is a stool nobody sits on. Then he goes home at half past five and does not switch the radio on.

And then he sits on his own bench, which is a bench and not a seat, because there is no room in this unit for a chair, and he has thought about that about once, and it is not tonight.

---

**HE PICKED THE ASH ON THE MONDAY, RIPPED THE BLANK ON THE TUESDAY, GOT HIS ANGLES WRONG TWICE ON THE WEDNESDAY AND PUT IT TOGETHER DRY AT ABOUT HALF PAST TEN IN THE MORNING, AND AT HALF PAST THREE HE TOOK THE WEIGHT OUT OF THREE OF THE FOUR RAILS, AND AT HALF PAST FOUR A MAN AT THE BENCH WITH HIM ASKED HIM WHAT IT WAS FOR AND HE SAID IT WAS FOR A MATE WHO HAD DONE HIS OWN KITCHEN, AND HE HAS HAD THAT ANSWER READY SINCE HALF PAST TEN IN THE MORNING.**
