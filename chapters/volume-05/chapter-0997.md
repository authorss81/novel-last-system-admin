# Chapter 0997 — She Wants To Pay Him

Undated in this draft.

**TWO IRON BED FRAMES STAND AGAINST THE WALL ON THE SECOND FLOOR LANDING AT NUMBER TEN.**

The upper landing at number ten is a piece of floor about the width of the stairs with a window at the end of it and three doors off it, and the stair from the front room comes up to it in one flight with a turn. The two iron bed frames are stood against the wall under the window on their sides, frame to frame, and they have been stood there since Tuesday morning when the sister carried them up herself from the back room while he stood at the bottom of the stair and was told twice not to come up and help.

She is in the front room doorway with the door she came through open behind her. She is in her early thirties and she has a cloth over her shoulder, because it is a day off and a cloth is where a cloth goes at a weekend, and she has not asked him anything yet and she has not looked at the frames.

"You have not asked me a single thing about what I want."

"No."

"That is the fourth time this month and I have stopped keeping a list." She came out into the landing and put her back against the frame of the door. "I want them out of my front room. I want the front room open. It has been shut all summer and it has my sister's bed in it and her bed was made up on Friday and I have not gone in there since Friday and I am not going to until the two of you are back."

"Who are the two of you?"

"You know who the two of us are." She looked at him, and he had to stand there and take it. "The two who went up the river. My sister, because she was asked and she took it, and the woman over at nine, because she put her own name down with her own pencil. So there is nobody in that house but me and there is nobody in this one but me, and I am the one person here who is neither up a river nor at the bottom of one, and you have not once asked me how I am."

"I asked you on Tuesday whether you wanted me to put the front room beam in and close the wall up."

"That is not the same as asking me how I am and you know it is not the same, because you did not ask me the first one either. You put three things in front of me and let me pick one." She turned round and put both hands on the top rail of the frame nearest the window. "I am not going to pick one this time. I want them out. I want the room open. The list is those two items and there is one more, and that one is this."

He has taken iron frames apart before, about nine of them, and every one of them was somebody else's dead relation and the job came with a look at it and no conversation. He has never in his life been asked for anything by a woman who wanted him to do a thing, and the difference between being asked and being offered is a difference he has been able to feel for about a fortnight and has not once been able to act on, and here it is on a morning in a doorway with a cloth over a woman's shoulder. This one he looks at for a while before he answers, because it is a half-corner bed with a brass knob on each post and the head of the bed is not on the head of the frame, it is at the foot end, so that whoever slept in it slept with their feet at the headboard, and the slats are original and there are seven of them and the middle one has gone.

"Nine of these slats want to come out anyway," he tells her. "The middle one is snapped at the bed's weight and the other six have the sag that comes of six people."

"Seven, and one of them slept in it sideways for a year with a broken leg, which is why the frame is the wrong way round and my grandmother would have thrown it out the back door." She laid her hand flat along the top rail. "I am not throwing it out. I want it in the yard." He takes the frames apart on the landing and puts the feet through the window opening on their sides so that nothing has to be turned in a passage, which takes about twenty minutes for the first and rather less for the second, and she does not help and does not stand in the way, and at one point she goes down and comes back up with a cup of tea in each hand and puts one on the floor by his boot and makes no remark about it. Then she gets to the thing.

"I want to pay you for that."

"I don't want paying for it."

"I know you don't. That is beside the point, and you are going to let me get to it anyway." She crouched down and took the cup off the floor and put it on the window sill and stayed down there with her elbows on her knees. "I have never asked anybody for anything in my life. Not one thing, not since I was old enough to want things. So when I want something I have to make it into paying somebody, because that is the shape it comes out in, and I have thought about how to ask you for this job for two days and this is the only way I could get the words out of my mouth. So I am going to pay you, and it will be in my hand to you, and you are going to take it."

He looked at the seven slats of the second frame on the floor and then at her, down on her heels by the window sill.

"All right."

That was the whole of the transaction and it took about nine seconds, and after it neither of them spoke for a while. She put the money out of a fold of paper and not out of her hand, which meant she had put it aside on Friday morning before he was on the frontage, and he understood that when he saw the fold. He carried the two frames down and out to the yard and stood them against the shed wall with the headboards crossed, and when he came back up she was in the doorway with the door standing open behind her, which is the first time since Friday that that door has stood open.

The landing boards run in a line along the wall from the foot of the stair to the window, and they are worn into a shallow dish about a quarter of an inch deep in the middle from thirty years of women coming up that stair with their hands empty, and apart from that dish the run of them is even end to end, which is not a fault in the boards and is the only mark on that floor that anybody put there on purpose.

What has gone is not the money. What has gone is the reason he was able to walk into four houses without knocking, which was that he had never once taken a shilling off anybody, and every woman who let him in did it on the understanding that he came and did what needed doing and neither of them mentioned it, and on that morning a woman put coins into his hand off a folded square of paper, and that understanding is finished and he has not the least idea what he is in those houses for now. The two iron frames are stood in the yard at number ten against the coal shed, headboards crossed and brass knobs facing out, and the front room at number ten stands open behind its own door with the dust of a week standing in a stripe along the boards where the light comes in, and he has money in his pocket for the first time in this street, and the square of folded paper it came out of is still upstairs on that table with nothing on it at all.
