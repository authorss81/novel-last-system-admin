# Chapter 0683 — A Lad Puts It Down And Does Not Knock

Monday the twenty-second of November 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from first light until dark with one lad on her path for about forty seconds and nobody else, and the whole of the day is a box coming back to a table it left in October.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT FINDS A LAD SHE HAS NEVER SPOKEN TO PUTTING THE PARCEL ON THE GRASS INSIDE HER OWN OPEN GATE WITHOUT KNOCKING, BRINGS IT IN AND PUTS IT ON THE LEFT OF HER OWN KITCHEN TABLE WHERE IT HAS BEEN SINCE THE TWELFTH OF OCTOBER, AND THE ENVELOPE ON THE RIGHT OF THAT TABLE DOES NOT MOVE.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is on her own all day. One lad speaks to her for about forty seconds at her own gate and asks her one question and gives her no explanation of where it has been.

---

She sees it from the kitchen window at about half nine and she does not go straight out.

She stands at the sink with the cloth in her hand and looks at the gate, which is standing back against the wall of the coal shed out of the way with both drop bolts in the pocket of her apron, and there is a lad on the grass inside the gate with a box by his foot.

He is about twenty-five in a hood and he is not the lad from the top of the road, and he does not knock.

---

She goes out.

"Sorry."

"That's all right."

"Did you bring that for me?"

"It was on your path."

"It was."

He shifts his weight and looks at the wall for a bit.

"There was one of them on the top of the bin the other week and it went down with the recycling because it went in the bin," he says. "So we found out and we're not putting them out no more. This is the last one."

"Right."

"They're not ours." He says it as though that is the end of it, and it is the end of it, and she does not ask him what the box is and he does not offer.

"Thank you."

"No, it's fine. Just — if one comes, it comes on the path, not on the bin."

She stands there.

"Right," she says.

And he goes out through the gate and off down the road, and he does not look back at her, and she stands in her own gateway with her hands empty for about a minute before she goes and picks the box up.

---

It is lighter than it was.

That is the first thing and it is the first thing she would say to anybody if there was anybody in that house to say it to. She lifts it off the grass with two hands and it is not the weight it was on the day she carried it out to the coal shed, and it is not the weight it was in the shed, and whatever has happened to it happened over the autumn and the box got lighter.

She carries it in and stands in her own passage with it, and the brush is in the middle of the runner with the handle across the walking line, and she steps over it right foot then left and goes through to the kitchen.

---

She puts it on the left-hand side of the table, where the cloth usually is, and where it has been since the twelfth of October.

The gas envelope is on the right of that table, face up square with the edge of the table, pencil side up, six things on the back in pencil and nothing crossed off, and there is no pencil in that house and there has not been since before Christmas, and the second list on the front of it is in another woman's hand and its top corner is gone, taken by tape, and neither woman can write on that paper again.

She puts the box down to the left of the envelope and the envelope does not move, and the box is not square with the edge of the table, and she does not square it.

The table stands dead flat on a shim planed and sawn flush under the window-side foot with the leg back in dry and a card on the joint and no glue, and it does not rock with two things on it, and a man put that in in the summer in about a quarter of an hour with a woman watching and neither of them saying anything.

---

She looks at the label.

The tape is off the top corners and the card of it has gone soft and come away from the folding, and the print on the label has gone into a grey smear across about half of it, and there is a number on it, and it is not a house number.

It is a reference, in the middle of a label, above a line that says *sent*, and the date beside that line has gone into the grey with the rest of it, and there is a second date underneath in a different hand, a hand that is not the printed one and not hers, with a number and a street on it.

She reads it twice.

It is a street she has never been to and she knows by the shape of it, and there is a number at the end of the street that is not twelve.

Under the printed label there is a second label, stuck on the old one and gone over the top of it, and that one is handwriting — a biro, hurried, on the gummed strip that comes off a roll — and on it there is a street and a number and, at the end, something that is either a name or a house.

Somebody has put a forwarding strip on a parcel that was already in the wrong place.

She looks at it for quite a long time.

---

What she is sitting at the table with is this.

The thing came to this house off a van on the twelfth of October for a woman at number twelve. She was here when it came and she took it in because the lad who brought it lived at number fourteen and it was easier than making a scene on her own path.

It went on this table and then it went into the coal shed behind the open gate on Monday the eighteenth, and it was gone by the Saturday, and there is a clean rectangle in the coal where it sat.

And it has come back on the footpath from a van that is not the same van, from a lad who is not the same lad, with a second address written on the gummed strip on the label.

Which means it was not mislaid. Mislaid things do not get a second address. Things that get a second address are things that somebody in an office decided to send somewhere else, and that somebody did it on a strip of gummed paper in a hurry, and the strip has gone brown round the edges and the biro has run a little where it crossed a fold.

---

She does not put that together and then act on it. That is what she would have to do and it is the one thing in this whole business that she cannot do, because the whole of it from the start was a box on a table and a woman who could not say who had sent it.

She sits at the table with the box in front of her and does the thing she does at that table, which is to look at a thing and work out what it is, alone, and get about ninety per cent of the way there, and then stop, because the last ten per cent is a telephone call or a knock and she cannot do either.

What she has got is this: two streets, and a box in a van belonging to somebody who has an account with the house it was sent to, and a coal shed with an open door, and a gate.

The gate is on a road with nobody on it. The coal shed is eight feet from her kitchen window and she cannot see into it from where she stands. The front door has not latched since before Christmas. The passage light has been on since June.

A parcel went off that coal in October and a parcel came back, and nothing in between was locked.

She sits with the cloth over the old damp mark on the table for a while, and the cloth is the one off the oven rail and it is not quite dry.

---

She does not open the box.

That is the decision and she makes it in about a second and a half, standing at the table with her hand on the lid, and she makes it for the same reason she did not write a telephone number on the side of it in October, which is that opening it turns her into the woman who opened it, and she does not know what she would have to be afterwards.

She puts a clean cloth over it and squares that.

---

She goes round the side afterwards with her coat on, because she has not been out there since the Saturday and the coal is still in the bucket.

The shed door is open the way it was. The padlock is hanging on the hasp where it has hung since before Christmas. The coal has a rectangle in it and the rectangle has been rained on since Saturday and there is now a dark clean shape in the middle of the dust where the rain has not got to.

She looks at it and then she goes and gets the shovel and puts a bucket of coal in the bucket by the range and shuts the shed door behind her, which is the first time she has shut that door since before Christmas.

She does not lock it. She could put the padlock on the hasp in about a second and the key is on a hook behind her own back door.

She shuts the door and comes in and stands in her own passage with her hand flat on it, and the brush is in the middle of the runner and the passage light is on, and both of those are exactly as they were before she got up.

She thinks about the lad on the grass.

He was about twenty-five and in a hood and he did not knock, and she has lived on that road for fifty-one years and about four people have ever not knocked at her door, and one of them put a box on her grass on purpose and told her where to find it, and that is a piece of manners.

She has not worked out how she feels about it. What she has worked out is that a lad who does not knock has made it her job to open the door, and that a woman who has not opened her own front door for anybody since before Christmas got up and went out and opened it this morning because a box was on her grass, and that the box was the reason and the box was not hers.

---

She empties the bowl under the bath tap at midday and at night, with the folded cloth in it, and puts it back, and the tap goes on dripping.

She looks at the brown mark on the bathroom ceiling and the mark has a hard edge and the middle of it is darker than the outside of it, and she puts her thumb to the edge about a quarter of an inch in and it comes away with grit on it, and she wipes the grit on the cloth and the grit goes in the weave.

The passage is over the front room. She worked that out on a Friday night in the summer with a torch and has not been up the stairs since.

There is a pale patch on the ceiling of the front room just inside the door and she has not sat in that room since the sixteenth of July, and the water is coming down out of a roof space she has not been in since the spring at the far side of a joist where a cistern is weeping, and she cannot turn the supply off because she does not know where the pipe comes up and never has.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS BEEN TOLD BY A LAD ON HER OWN PATH THAT THE PARCELS ARE NOT THEIRS AND THAT ONE CAME ON THE BIN AND WENT IN IT, AND HAS READ A SECOND STREET AND A SECOND NUMBER ON A LABEL IN A HAND THAT IS NOT HER OWN AND HAS WORKED OUT THAT A MISLAID THING DOES NOT GET A SECOND ADDRESS, AND HAS STILL NOT OPENED THE BOX, AND HAS SHUT A SHED DOOR FOR THE FIRST TIME SINCE BEFORE CHRISTMAS AND NOT LOCKED IT.**
