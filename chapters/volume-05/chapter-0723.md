# Chapter 0723 — The Number Was On The Machine And There Is No Pencil In This House

Sunday the eighth of May 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number twelve from first light until dark and at her own gate at the end of it, and the whole of it is a message she has listened to four times and a number she cannot write down.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT FINDS A MESSAGE ON HER OWN MACHINE THAT SHE DID NOT ASK FOR AND THAT A STRANGER PUT THERE, HEARS A TELEPHONE NUMBER SPOKEN TWICE, CARRIES A PEN OUT OF HER OWN KITCHEN DRAWER AND PUTS IT ON THE GAS ENVELOPE ON HER OWN TABLE AND DOES NOT WRITE THE NUMBER DOWN, TELLS HER SISTER OUT LOUD ON HER OWN STEP THAT THE TELEPHONE IN THIS HOUSE RINGS FOR PEOPLE WHO DO NOT LIVE HERE, AND WHEN HER SISTER SAYS SHE COULD RING IT FROM HER OWN HOUSE SHE SAYS NO.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight. She is in her own house all day. Her sister is on her step at the end of the day. She speaks to her there.

---

She does the bowl at first light and does it again at dark and that is the whole of what the tap is.

Then the kitchen, under the window, on the green chair with the cushion on it, cup in both hands, cold by the time it has warmed her palms.

The taped box is on the left of the table under the cloth. The gas envelope is on the right face up with six things on the back of it in pencil, nothing crossed off, and the pencil that wrote them is not in this house and has not been in this house since before Christmas.

Four of six.

She does not do anything about it yet.

---

The light on the machine is on.

She sees it from the kitchen doorway at about the middle of the morning and she crosses the passage and steps over the brush and goes into the front room.

The room is as cold as it has been since Easter. The sixty-watt bulb is on out of the daylight. The rush chair is against the front wall. The fire is where a fire is.

And under the window, folded against the wall where it has been since the seventeenth of December, is the step ladder, four treads shut true, tray down, tape round the second rail, chalk on the third tread. She comes round the foot of it to get at the table and she does not touch it.

She presses the button.

---

The woman has left a message and it is short and she plays it four times.

It begins, "Hello, I've rung you twice, I hope that's all right," and it is a bright voice and it is not frightened, and it is the voice of a person who is doing a job.

"I'm after whoever's been dealing with the upstairs at that house," she says. "The man hasn't come and I've got a deadline on the Friday. If you can't get him, can you ring me back on this number — I'll say it twice — it's zero one seven ... it's zero one seven, six three, two, dash, four one nine eight."

Then: "Thanks. That's it."

And it stops.

---

She plays it again straight away, and the third time she has the pen in her hand.

That is what happens: on the third time she goes in with the pen in her fist and the machine going, and she is ready for it, and the woman says the numbers slowly, twice, and the whole of the message takes the length of the kettle coming to the boil.

And then the message ends.

And she stands there in the front room with a pen in her hand and a ladder at her back and nowhere at all to put the number, because the only paper in this house is on a table in the kitchen and she is not going to carry a number through a house in her head on the offchance of keeping it in her head for the length of a walk.

She goes back to the kitchen and puts the pen down on the gas envelope and squares it along the edge of it and leaves it there, and then she stands and looks at it, and then the envelope is still on the right of the kitchen table with a pen lying on it and nothing written on the back of it anywhere.

---

She plays it a fourth time to be certain, and it is the same number, and she writes nothing.

Not because she cannot. Her hand is not bad and her eyes are all right and there is a table in front of her.

She does not write it down because she has understood in the meantime, standing at her own table with a pen on an envelope, that a number written on the back of that envelope would have to go somewhere.

She would have to get it to a telephone. She would have to ask her sister to use her telephone, or she would have to use her own, and there is no number of hers anybody can ring, and there is no number of her own either, because the line in this house is the one the machine is on and it comes in on a name that is not hers, and there is nobody in this house she could ask to ring a stranger on her behalf, and if she wrote it on that envelope and put the envelope in the cupboard the number would sit in a cupboard for the rest of the spring and go out with the paper at some point in a bin somebody else put out, and it would very nearly be the end of it, which is that a woman who had rung this house twice would never know that she had rung it.

So she has the number in her head for as long as it takes to walk from the front room to the kitchen table, and then she has not got it.

And the machine will put another message on top of it. That is what the machine does. She has read about that in a leaflet in the summer and she has never tested it and she is not going to test it.

She puts the pen back in the drawer and pushes the drawer shut and stands in the doorway with the machine quiet in the room behind her and the number going out of her head by the time she is back at the table.

---

She does the whole of the rest of the day in her own kitchen.

She warms a cup and does not drink it. She wipes the sill with a damp cloth in one pass and the damp comes back at the edge where the water sits. She squares the cloth over the taped box and puts her palm flat on it. She looks out of the window at the gate and the lamp on its bracket and the road with nothing on it.

At the middle of the afternoon the machine goes again in the front room and she stands in the kitchen doorway and lets it ring and it goes for a good while and then stops.

She does not go in.

---

Her sister comes at the end of the day off the front path with her bag over her arm and stands on the step with the door wide behind them.

"You've not had your door shut all day," her sister says. "I heard that thing go twice more."

"You did."

"And you're not going in."

"No."

Her sister looks at the doorway behind her, at the passage, at the floor she has to step over to get there, and then at her sister.

"There's a message on it," her sister says. "And it's not just a wrong number, because a machine doesn't keep a message for a wrong number."

That is the exact thing, and it is said on a step in the cold, and it is the first true and useful sentence anybody has said in this house about anything since before Christmas.

---

"There is a telephone in this house," she says, "that rings for people who do not live here."

Her sister does not answer straight away.

"Who?"

"I don't know. A woman about an upstairs. She's rung twice."

"Then ring her back and tell her."

That is the sentence. That is the whole of what is left of this morning and it is nine words long.

And she does not answer it.

She stands on the step with her arms round her and looks at the front path and the road beyond it, and her sister waits, and the waiting goes on long enough that both of them know it is not going to come, and then she says:

"I can't ring her. I haven't got the number. It was on the machine and I didn't write it down, because there's no pencil in this house."

---

Her sister says, "There's a pen."

And she says, "I know there's a pen."

And then neither of them says anything for a while, and the draught comes off the back of the house and across the step between them, and the lamp at the end of the road burns amber over a gate and steady through the cold.

Then her sister says, "You could ask me to do it."

"No."

"You could ask me to ring it on my own."

"No," she says, and she means it, and it is the only no in this conversation that is not the whole truth, because the truth under it is that if her sister rings a stranger from a telephone in this house then a stranger is being told a thing about this house by somebody who is not her, and she cannot have that, and she could not say why to anybody.

---

She sees her sister off at dark and then she goes out to the gate herself with the apron on.

Her own gate is still propped back where she left it in the autumn, flat against the brick of the coal shed, and the two drop bolts she keeps in her apron have not been anywhere near it since the first of September and are where they have been since before Christmas. The padlock is in the hasp and the padlock is open, as it has been since the first of September, and she does not put it shut, and she does not go near the shed door, and the key is on its hook inside the back door.

She looks at the lamp on the bracket. It burns out in the daylight all summer and burns all night in the dark, and it has burned since June, and nobody has ever come about it.

---

Her sister has not gone. She is standing at her own gate off down the road with her bag still on her arm and not going in, the way she stands when she is giving somebody time to say something.

She watches from behind her own gate for as long as it takes to be sure of it, and she does not call out, because there is nothing to call out about. There is a number in a front room behind her that her sister has not got, and her sister has a half of one, and neither of them is going to say the rest of it standing in the cold.

After a while her sister looks up the road at this house and cannot see anybody in the doorway, because the hall light is off and she has not put it on for a woman who was not coming in.

Then the sister goes in, and a light goes off somewhere off down the road, and it is dark here.

Behind her the front room is dark and the machine in it is dark, and the ladder is under the window where it has been since before Christmas, and the pen is in the drawer where she put it back this morning, and her sister has gone into a cold kitchen having been offered one thing and told no.
