# Chapter 0996 — As Far As The Back Step And No Further

Undated in this draft.

**THE SCULLERY AT NUMBER NINE HAS A YARD DOOR STANDING OPEN OFF THE YARD.**

The scullery at number nine is a room the width of the house with a stone sink in it under the window and the yard door at the far end, and it is the coldest room in that street because the pipe came out of the yard wall where it runs under the doorstep and nobody has had the money or the reason to do anything about it since the winter before last. The door she uses is the yard door and she leaves it open, all weathers, on the theory that a scullery wants air, and it has stood open every day since the summer. He has come up the frontage to use it and been into the kitchen at the side the last three times he came, and both times the woman was standing in the kitchen with her back to the sink and a cloth in her hand, and both times she had not asked him a question about herself and he had not asked her one.

Both times she had told him what she wanted before he had it out of his bag. That is the thing that has happened to him since the spring and he has not decided yet whether it is a thing that happened to him or a thing he arranged.

The street door is shut and the hook is up under the staple and the staple is rusted and the hook has not been up there since before the spring, so that the only way into this house at all is the way she comes in herself, and he has not used it and is not going to use it today. He puts his hand flat on the inner face of the yard door where the damp has taken the paint off in a band at the height of his palm, and the door leans away from him about the width of two fingers at the top, and he takes his hand off it.

---

The flags run in a line from that door to the sink and they are as near even along that line as a hundred years of a woman washing things in a hard place can leave them, and there is a dish about two feet by two and a half of the flags that sits about a quarter of an inch low at the sink end and takes everything that comes off the roof, which is why the door is open and why the floor of the front room is damp in one corner in a wet week. He found it in the summer when he was in the front room and made no remark about it then, and he has thought about it perhaps twice since, and both times he thought about it standing somewhere else.

The floor of the room she was born in is a different thing entirely and not a dish. It is the joists themselves, one of them, and one of them has come down at the far corner, and the boards over it have gone with it, and there are nine of the boards on that corner that have a gap under them you could lose a finger in. He found that in the autumn and told her it wanted nine boards and two joists and a day and a half, and she asked him what it would cost and he told her, and she did not have it, and he did not do it, and she did not ask him to do it. She has gone up the river on the second of the two lines and will be six weeks gone, which is longer than she has been away from that house since she was a girl, and the hook is up, and the front room is dry, and the back room has nine boards over a joist that has come down.

In the autumn she took the figure off him without a pencil. She gave back nine boards, two joists and the best part of two days, in the voice of a woman quoting a price out of a shop, and then she went to the dresser and opened it and stood looking into it long enough that he had time to put his bag down and pick it up again. What she took out at the end was not the money. It was a tin, and the tin went back into the dresser with the same number of coins in it as before. Then she told him the floor would keep until the weather turned and that it would be worse than it was now after that, and that was the whole of the conversation and it lasted about four minutes.

He walked out of it and down the frontage and stood in the middle of the street and understood that he had just been handed a floor to keep by a woman who could not pay for it, and that he had thanked her for it. He has not been thanked for anything in his life and he thought at the time that he had been thanked for that, and he has been going on thinking it for ten days.

He stands in the scullery and he puts his bag down on the flags at his feet and he does not go any further into the house. That is all he does there.

It takes him about two minutes to make the decision and the two minutes are spent on the flags and not on anything else. He has come up this frontage four times in ten days and each time he has told himself the reason, and the reason has never been the floor. The reason is that he has made a decision about himself since Friday and has not told anybody, because the decision is not a thing you can put into words in a woman's own kitchen without her hearing it as a gift. The decision is that he will not begin anything this season that nobody has asked him for.

It is a good decision. He has believed it for two days. It is also the reason he has never been on a book, because a book does not deal in men who have an offer in them, and he has had offers in him since he was twenty and has spent thirty years making them, and every man he has ever worked beside is on that book and he is not, and there was never a day when anybody sat down and decided that he should be left off it. He did it to himself, four times a week, for thirty years, in about nine hundred small free gestures, and none of them cost anything, which is exactly why there are so many of them.

He picks the bag up off the flags and puts the strap over his shoulder and stands for a moment in the middle of that cold little room listening to the roof, which is doing nothing, because there has been no rain since the middle of the morning yesterday. If she were in the house he would not be standing in here like this. He has understood that about himself while he was making the decision, and it is the only thing about the decision that gives him any comfort, and he is suspicious of it, because a rule that pays you for the thing you wanted is not a rule.

---

He goes back out of the yard door and along the frontage at the speed of a man who is not late anywhere and does not particularly want to arrive, and the strip of light off the scullery window comes out across the flags behind him and stops in the middle of the yard where the shade of the wall takes it, and the yard door is still standing open off that yard the way it has stood open in every weather since the summer, and the house behind it is shut at the front with the hook up, and the nine boards over the dropped joist in the back room are exactly where he found them in the autumn and will be there when she comes back down the river in six weeks.
