# Chapter 0807 — The Run Along The Front

Thursday the thirteenth of April 2023, in this borough. All of that Thursday goes on a run of scaffold board along the front of that unit, and a line that has lifted and gone wavy with the frost is the whole of it.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES HAS A RUN OF SCAFFOLD BOARD ALONG THE FRONT OF THAT BUILDING'S HARDSTANDING THAT HAS LIFTED AND GONE WAVY WITH TWO WINTERS OF FROST AND MEANS TO TAKE IT ALL UP AND RELAY IT ON A BED OF CHIPPINGS, THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN TAKES UP THE OTHER HALF OF THE SAME RUN ON THE SAME AFTERNOON, AND WHERE THE TWO MEET THERE IS ONE JOINT BETWEEN TWO PEOPLE'S WORK THAT IS STRAIGHTER THAN EITHER HALF, AND NEITHER OF THEM EVER FINDS OUT.**

The unit, on that Thursday. He has run the place for years and nobody at that address thinks about how old he is any more. It is one shed of a place with a roller door on the frontage, an office cabin in the corner of it and a yard behind it, and there is nothing in the yard but a bench and a length of oak on it. The hardstanding runs along the front of the building for about twenty-two feet between the roller door on one side and the return of the next building on the other, and the whole of it is boarded over with scaffold board laid in two rows.

Two rows of scaffold board, nine feet long and nine inches wide, laid end to end along the frontage with the long side of each board along the run. Eleven of them in all. The ends of scaffold board are banded in steel, a strip of angle iron round each end about a hand's width back from it, because a scaffold board is carried on its end and the end is what fails.

The run has been down two winters. It is wavy in both directions, up in the middle of each board and down at the joints, and the joints at the two ends of the frontage are open wide enough to put a thumb in.

It lifted because of what is under it.

There was never anything under it but a bed of sand, and the sand is on the hardcore of the old hardstanding, and the hardcore has been washed out from the middle of the frontage where the falls cross it, so the frost gets at sand on nothing and lifts the whole run about an inch and lets it down again, and two winters of that is what a wavy run is.

He means to lift all eleven, dig the middle of that frontage out, put a proper bed of chippings on it and lay them back on that.

He has a bar, a bolster, a spade, a wheelbarrow, a straightedge and a spirit level, and he has a yard's worth of chippings tipped at the far end of the frontage that came on Monday.

He starts at the roller door end and works along.

Each board lifts off the bed with a bar at one end and a kick at the other, and they go onto the render beside the office door where they are out of the weather, and there are eleven of them and the whole of the run is bare hardstanding in about forty minutes.

Under them is what he expected. The sand bed is about a hand deep and it is grey and wet in the middle of the frontage and dry at the two ends, and under the sand in the middle there is not hardcore at all. There is rubble and there is soft stuff and there is a hollow where the water goes, and he can push the bar in there to below the head of the spade and it goes further.

He digs the middle of it out to the bottom of the hardcore and barrows it away, and the bottom of that hollow is the one place on the whole of the frontage where you cannot get the bar to go through.

Then the bed goes in, in two goes, and it goes in while the barrow is full and it is raked flat and it is rolled with the roller half buried because that is the only roller there is and it weighs about what a man weighs.

A straightedge is laid along the run and worked back and forth on it until not a ray of light shows anywhere under it, and the spirit level sits on top of that, and where it wants more stone it gets more stone and where it is high it comes off with the back of the bar.

That is about three hours and it is the whole of the preparation.

A scaffold board is not a paving flag and it does not want to be jammed down.

It is bedded into the chippings and worked down with a kick of the heel until the chippings come up around its edges and stop it coming up again, and the first one is always the worst, because the bed under it is exactly the right depth in one place and a finger too deep in another and you find out where by putting your weight on it.

He works it down and packs chippings along both edges with the back of the bar and puts the heel of his boot on the middle of it, and it takes up about a quarter of an inch and stays where it is.

They go fast after the first one. Two, three, four, and by the middle of that morning he has laid five of them and raked the chippings out level along the open edge of the fifth, the way you rake sand out to a line so that the next board has something to bed onto.

He goes in about the middle of the afternoon for the second barrow.

The wheelbarrow is the one with the bad tyre and it will not go over the lip of the yard without lifting, and there is nothing else that will carry half a yard of chippings, and he is not going to do it with a bucket.

He brings it in through the roller door, which comes up without a sound and goes down without a sound and has been doing both since he cleaned the guides, and he stands the barrow under the window with the boards leaning against the wall above it, and he fills the wheelbarrow from the pile at the far end of the frontage in about ten goes over about forty minutes.

While he is inside, the woman from two doors down lifts the last board off the far end of that run.

---

She had come along at the middle of the afternoon because that is the time she comes, and she had come with her own bolster and a bar and a short piece of timber she uses for packing.

She does not knock. The roller door is down and the office window is dark and there is nobody out on that frontage, and she goes to the far end of the run where the boards have lifted worst because that is the end where the water goes, and she puts her bar in under the end board and lifts it off, and the sand comes up with it in lumps and she rakes it clear with her own foot.

The boards go onto the ground on the grass side, banded ends up, and she digs the sand out of the far eleven feet of that run and barrows it into a heap on the grass where the ground is soft and it does not matter.

There is rubble under it too and a hollow about the depth of a spade blade in the middle of it, and she fills the hollow back in with the rubble broken down and puts the chippings on top of it about a hand deep and rakes them flat and rolls them.

She lays six boards to his five.

She starts at the return of the next building and works toward the roller door, and the fifth one she lays butts the open end of his fifth with about a quarter of an inch of slack in the joint, and she does that because a scaffold board that is jammed end to end with another one in frost is a joint that opens.

The end of her fifth board and the near end of his fifth board are both banded in steel, and the bands are about a hand's width back from each end, so there are two steel bands sitting face to face across that joint with eight inches of steel against steel between them.

She packs chippings along the edges of the six and kicks the heel of her boot down the middle of each one until it comes down about a quarter of an inch and stays there, and she does the last one and it goes down easier than any of the others because the bed she has just made is the best bed on the frontage.

She goes at about five, and the roller door is still down, and she puts her bar and her bolster against the return wall where she took them off and goes home.

She has not seen him and he has not seen her and there is no knock and nothing to explain afterwards.

---

He comes out at about five and the sun is off the frontage and there is a cold place along the last six boards where her half of the run has the night air under it and nothing between it and the ground, which is the first time since the summer that any part of that frontage has had air under it, and he looks at the whole run from the roller door and it is down and straight and it is all one level from the office wall to the return.

What he has not done is raked the chippings out along the joint.

He gets a barrow and he rakes the chippings out from his own side to the open edge of the fifth board and shakes them down the last inch so they sit tight in the joint rather than proud of it, because chippings standing proud of a joint are chippings the first hard rain takes off and into the frontage.

He rakes about a foot of stone along that joint and packs it down with the back of the bar and takes his bar out of the gap and puts the bar against the wall.

Then he walks the whole run.

Twenty-two feet of it, from the roller door to the return, with a straightedge laid across the boards and a spirit level on top of it, moved along a foot at a time, the whole way, in the last of the light.

There is no daylight under the straightedge anywhere along its length, including the eight inches in the middle where his fifth board and her sixth lie end to end over the two steel bands. Those eight inches are the only part of the run that cannot crush and cannot lift and cannot go wavy again in a hard winter, because there is no bare arris of wood anywhere in that joint to be lifted by frost. The joint itself is the straightest line in the whole twenty-two feet and it is straighter than the boards on either side of it, and the stone under it goes on without a break from one edge of that run to the other. He stands with the straightedge lying across it for a while and does not find anything wrong with it, and he leaves it exactly as it is.

The eleven boards are stacked against the wall by the office door, banded ends out, and the oak is still on the bench out in that yard with its shoulder out of the square, and the store door is standing open all winter behind its yard of brush strip, and the near gate comes home on the palm of one hand as it always has.
