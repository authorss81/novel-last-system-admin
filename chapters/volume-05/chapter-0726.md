# Chapter 0726 — The Chalk On The Top Rail

Wednesday the eighteenth of May 2022, in this borough. He is at his own unit from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a pair of gates that are paid for and a run of units that will not come true.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES SWEEPS THE SHAVINGS FROM UNDER THE FRONT RUN AT HIS OWN UNIT, LOOKS AT THE CHALK NUMBERS ON THE OAK GATES LEANING AGAINST THE BACK WALL, SPEAKS TO A LAD WITH TIMBER ABOUT WHERE TO PUT IT, AND LATCHES THE UNIT GATE AT DARK WITHOUT RE-CHALKING ANYTHING.**

He is a man in his early forties at his own unit on Wednesday. A lad with timber meets him at the gate near midday and he speaks to him out there.

---

He begins with the front run and it behaves as it did on Monday, which is to say it does not behave at all.

Across its six foot the top length stands out from the three beneath by near a quarter inch. He has been at it since March. He has shimmed it and planed it and taken a shaving off and put it back, and it is proud now by what it was proud by in March.

He stands out at the far end and looks along the frontage with his hands down, and you cannot see it, and anybody who stands where he is standing and looks along that wall can see it.

He sets the plane aside on the bench and leaves it there.

---

Then he gets the broom from behind the bench and sweeps under the run where the shavings have sat since March.

It is a small job and he does it properly, edge to edge, into a pile and into the sack by the bench. The sack is half full of oak dust and pine curls and he ties it off and sets it by the door for the bin men.

While he sweeps he thinks about taking another shaving off the top run, and then he thinks about not taking it, and the not taking wins, because a run that has been planed twice and shimmed once and is still out by the same hair is telling him something.

He leaves it proud. He will live with a proud run that is paid for rather than spoil one that is paid for by working it thin.

---

At the back wall the oak gates lean on their batten where he put them before the winter.

The chalk numbers are still on the top rails, soft with dust, his own hand from the winter. He wipes a thumb across one figure and the chalk smears and comes back, and he leaves the rest alone.

He does not re-chalk them. Re-chalking a gate that is tried and paid for is the act of a man who thinks it is going somewhere, and these gates are going nowhere until a man with hands to spare comes with a ladder.

The posts are in the ground at the cottage. He put them in himself. They will keep.

---

In the middle of the day a lad comes to the gate with timber on his shoulder and asks where to put it.

"Round the side?" the lad says. "Or in?"

"In," he says. "Against the bench, but not against the gates."

The lad brings it in and stacks it plank by plank, and he watches and moves one piece that would have leaned on the chalked rail.

"That run still out?" the lad says, nodding at the front.

"A quarter over six foot," he says. "Since March."

"And the gates?"

"Paid and tried," he says. "Waiting on hands."

The lad nods as if that means something to him, and then he is gone with the chit signed, and the yard is quiet again.

---

He spends the rest of the afternoon at the bench with the shoebox and the scrap of scribing card.

The card has twelve on one side in his own pencil. He takes it out and looks at it and puts it back in his shirt pocket with the twelve out, the way he has carried it all winter.

The pencil is behind his ear. He takes it down and sharpens it with his knife and puts it back behind his ear, and that is the only sharp thing he does all day.

The drawer under the bench stays shut. It has been shut since the sixteenth of November with a brush in a bag inside it, and he does not open it, because there is nothing in there he needs for a run that he has decided to leave alone.

---

At dark he walks the unit from the bench to the door and back, switching off as he goes.

The two-tread step stool stands in the corner by the paint where he left it after the eighteenth of April, folded, with dust on the top tread. He looks at it and leaves it there. It is not what a man needs for a quarter inch over six foot, and he knows that now from having stood on it.

He pulls the unit gate across and it latches first time, and he tries it twice out of habit and it stays latched.

He gets in the van and sits a moment with his hands on the wheel and looks back at the unit with its gate latched and its lights off, and the gates inside it leaning where they were at first light, with the chalk still on them and nothing new on them, and he drives away with the scrap still in his pocket and the pencil behind his ear.
