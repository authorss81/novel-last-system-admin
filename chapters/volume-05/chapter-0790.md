# Chapter 0790 — The Rubber On The Kerbstone

Tuesday the thirty-first of January 2023, in this borough. The whole of that day goes on the ramp of her own lock-up and on the stone at the head of it, and a strip of rubber goes over the edge of that stone.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES HAS A STONE LAID ACROSS THE TOP OF HER RAMP MAKING A STEP ON IT AND MEANS TO TAKE THE STONE OUT, THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK WILL NOT, AND BOLTS A RUBBER NOSING OVER THE EDGE OF THE STONE INSTEAD, AND THE TROLLEY RIDES UP OVER IT WITHOUT STOPPING AND THE TWO GOES BECOME ONE, AND SHE STOPS PULLING IT, AND THE DAY SHE DOES IT COMES OFF THE TOP OF THE RAMP ON ITS OWN AND GOES ACROSS THE BAY.**

She has been in her late forties for a good while and the lock-up is hers, and the woman who books work is already in the yard when she comes out of the office, standing at the top of the ramp with her arms folded, having come in an hour before through a lane gate that stands open all day.

---

Inside, the roller door is down and has been down since the middle of January, and the switch that works it is on the wall by the office door where it has always been. There is a strip light over the bench and one of the two tubes in it went out in November and the other one is going. The office window looks out on that yard and has not been cleaned since the summer. There is a calendar on the wall behind the office door with November still open on it, because nobody in that building turns a page. There is a coat on a hook by that door that came off the van and has not been on a hook since the van came, and an empty drum lying on its side in the corner behind the roller door.

Outside along the wall the four pallets have been standing on their edges since October with a stack of blocks down the middle of every one, and every one of those stacks is wet right through in the middle. The trolley is away against the far wall with nothing on its deck and the back legs up in the air. The drawer in the bench is shut. The box behind the bench is where it stands. And there is a crescent of grit trodden hard into the floor there, from the end of the rack across to the wall, that was swept to in the autumn and never taken out. The van is in the bay nose to the back wall with its tray up under the sill and a length of green conduit cut off square at both ends lying under the front of the tray bed, and neither of them looks at it.

The kerbstone is across the top of the ramp.

She has been looking at it since Christmas and getting more tired of it. It is a dressed kerb about as long as the ramp is wide and about as deep as a kerb is, laid across the top of the ramp on a bed of sand, let into notches cut in the sound concrete either side, and kept down by two lengths of flat bar bolted down into the ramp. Its top edge stands about two fingers proud of the concrete either side and is a true line from one end of the ramp to the other. Everything that goes into that bay goes up in two goes.

Her plan is to take it out.

She has a bar and a lump of brick and a spanner, and she means to unbolt the two flat bars, lift the stone out with a bar and two battens the way it came in, dig the notches out of the concrete at both sides with a bolster, and fill the trough that is under it with rubble so that the ramp goes up in one run again the way it went up until that kerb was laid.

---

"So you are taking it out."

The woman who books work is standing at the head of the ramp with her coat on and her arms folded, and has been standing there long enough to have read the whole of the top of it.

"I am taking it out. It is a step on a ramp. Everything in this building goes up in two goes because of a piece of stone."

"Then put the trough under it back the way it was."

"That is what I am going to do."

"That is what you are going to do and it will be worse than it is." The woman comes down the ramp a yard and stands in the trough where the kerbstone was. "Do you know what is under this stone."

"Rubble and sand."

"Rubble and sand that has been there since the autumn, packed down into the bed the kerb is sitting on. And under all of that is the channel that was cut in that concrete before the stone ever went over it. That channel is about as deep as your thumb and it runs the width of the ramp, and the two of them together are what the bed is." She puts her heel in the rubble and grinds it. "And you are going to pull all of it out and put rubble you have barrowed off a yard in its place and pack that in with the back of a shovel."

She turns her heel over in it and looks at him. "And then a wheel comes up that ramp, and the wheel is doing something on the way up it, and the water coming down it in a storm is doing something on the way down."

"What is the wheel doing."

"The wheel is bouncing, because nothing under it has any key in it, and a bouncing wheel on a fall walks." She takes her heel out of the rubble. "And in about two years you will have another band across your ramp about as deep as your thumb and you will be back here again, and this time you will not be able to put a stone over it, because there is nothing left on your yard wall to put one over it."

---

"Then it stays."

"It stays." The woman comes back up to the top of it and stands on it. "And you can stop wanting it out, because it is not the thing in the way. The thing in the way is the edge of it."

"The edge of it."

"Your trolley comes up a fall and hits the front of that stone and stops dead in front of it, and the whole of the weight of your load arrives at two inches of stone in about a fifth of a second." She puts the toe of her boot against the top edge of the kerb. "And stone cannot take that. Stone takes it about four times and then the arris goes. It is not the middle of that kerb that wears out, it is the edge, and it is the edge that stops your load, and every one of those four goes takes a piece of the edge with it."

"What do you want on the edge."

"Something that takes it instead."

---

Then she gets the rubber.

It has been rolled up in the corner of the office since the autumn, when a tipper came in on a job and left a scrap of its offside mudguard, which she meant to keep and did not. It is a strip about nine inches wide and a quarter of an inch thick of flat black rubber on a thin steel backing, and it has a moulded bead along its edge about the thickness of a pencil where the steel gives over to the rubber.

She does not lay it on top.

She gets the steel backing up against the top edge of the kerb and folds it over the edge so that the rubber face is what comes up out of the ramp and the backing goes down the far side of the stone, and she trims the two ends of it so that they mitre round the ends of the kerb and finish against the two flat bars, and she drills through the rubber itself in three places on each side of the top of the stone and screws the backing down into the kerb with four screws and then into the two flat bars with four more.

The rubber stands about the thickness of a coin proud of the stone at the top of the ramp, and the bead of it stands a little over that where it comes over the edge, so that a wheel coming up the fall comes onto rubber a fraction before it comes onto stone.

"It is a nosing," she says. "That is what a nosing is. It is not there to make it go. It is there so that the edge that stops your load is not the edge of the stone."

"It looks like a bit of a tyre."

"It is a bit of a mudguard. And it is bolted through into the stone and through into your own bars, so it is not going anywhere, and when it wears through in the middle of it you will see it and put another one on, and the kerb underneath it will still have its arris." She stands back. "That stone is the only nose that ramp is ever going to have. Now put your trolley on it."

---

The trolley rides up over it without stopping.

She loads a sack against the back rail and gets it up on the handle from the yard, and the front casters come onto the fall about a yard out and the back legs hang as they have hung since November, and then the front of it comes onto the rubber and there is a moment where she thinks it has stopped, and then it does not.

It goes over the stone in one movement. The front casters climb the rubber, come over the edge, drop about a finger onto the flat bars beyond, and the back of the frame comes off the concrete and lands with a bang, and the sack goes forward against the back rail and stops there, and the trolley is in the bay.

She has not pushed. She has walked it.

They do it four more times with four different loads, and it goes up in one movement every time, and it never stops at the top.

"It is one go."

"One go." The woman who books work is not smiling. "Go on then. Stop pushing it."

"I have not been pushing it. I have been walking it."

"You have had a hand on that handle every time and you have been shoving about the amount a child shoves a pram." She steps off the kerbstone and off the rubber. "And there is nothing on it now to push against, so get on with your day."

---

And she does stop pulling it.

It goes in about a fortnight. First she finds out that she is getting up the ramp before her hand arrives at the handle. Then she is going up beside it with her hands full and the handle untouched. Then she is going up behind it with the sack on the deck and her mind on something else, and the trolley goes up by itself.

And it is not until the middle of February that she realises she has been going up that ramp for a fortnight without touching it, and that the reason is a strip of rubber about the thickness of a coin on the edge of a piece of stone, and that the thing she has been shoving for eleven years has gone.

---

And then, one afternoon, it comes off the top of the ramp on its own and goes across the bay.

It is a Tuesday and it is nothing of a day. She is in the office with the door open and there is a load coming in, and she comes out with her hands full to find the trolley already at the top of the ramp going up without her.

The back legs of it are hanging about a yard from the top, as they hang. That is the whole of it and it has been the whole of it since November. A trolley with its back legs on the ground has something behind it that drags and something that slows it. This one has nothing behind it at all, and the kerbstone that used to be the thing that stopped it is now a strip of rubber with a coin on the top of it.

It comes over the top and its casters run out onto the floor of the bay with nothing to stop them, and it goes across that bay at a run with the sack standing up against the front of the deck, and it comes up against the side of the van about nine feet out and stops there with the deck still going a few inches under it.

She is standing in the doorway of the office with the load in her hands and she watches a trolley she did not touch cross her bay and put itself against the side of the van.

That stone is the best nose that ramp has ever had, and it has a strip of rubber on it that does not need a hand anywhere near it, and there is nothing anywhere on that ramp now that will stop anything.
