# Chapter 0533 — The Thing He Was Always Assumed

The Tuesday of the third of December 2019, and about two hours of it, on three steps at the side of a building in this borough.

**SHE ASKED HIM IN THE FEBRUARY OF 1994 TO COME AND SIT IN A HOUSE WITH HER FOR THREE HOURS, AND HE SAID SEVEN O'CLOCK, AND HE DID NOT COME AT SEVEN AND DID NOT COME AT ALL AND DID NOT SEND WORD, AND FOUR MONTHS AGO A THIRD MAN SAID ALOUD, WITH BOTH OF THEM STANDING THERE, THAT HE HAD ALWAYS ASSUMED IT WOULD BE THE FIRST MAN, AND ON THE TUESDAY THE THIRD OF DECEMBER HE SAID IT AGAIN IN THE SAME VOICE.**

The man is of about fifty-two. The woman is of about fifty. The third man is a man in his late forties and has known the first of them since 2008, mostly through work, and he is not a fool, and he has no idea what either of the two of them has just heard.

They are on three steps because the ice is on the middle one and the top one is dry. The steps go down off the front of a building to a path that runs along the back of a row, and there is a woman in that row on Sunday who is moving to a smaller place three miles off, and the two of them have been going to help since the middle of November, and the third man came along on the first Sunday in September and has been three times since.

The ice is the reason the three of them are still standing about at ten past seven instead of having gone. It came in at about six, out of a sky that had been black since four, and it has not been a heavy one, and it went on the step and on nothing else.

---

What happened in the February of 1994 is this. The woman's mother was in a house in the town, and a man was going to the house at about eight to look at something at the top of it, and she did not want to be in the house with that man when he came, and she was twenty-five and living in the same borough, and she asked the man to come and be there from seven.

He was working. He was working on a job that was a good hour and a half the other way and he had the job until about six, and he was not going to be back in this borough before half past seven at the earliest, and she knew that when she asked him.

He said he would come at seven.

What he did was finish the job, and drive back, and go to a place on the sea front about four miles the other way from where she was, and stay there until about ten, and then go home. He has never told anybody that and he is not going to, and there is nobody alive who knows it except him, and he has worked out since that there were about six people in the borough who could have told her where he was and that not one of them was ever going to.

She sat in that house from about half past seven until about ten. The man came at eight and was in the house for the best part of three quarters of an hour and was perfectly decent about it, and she was perfectly decent back, and at about twenty to nine he said he was going to leave her be, and she said that was all right, and he said if she wanted him to stay he would, in a way that was worse than if he had not, and she said no, that was all right, and he left.

What she has done in the twenty-five years since is to leave it alone. That is the entirety of what she has done about it, and she has done it perfectly, and there is no part of it that anybody could put a finger on. She has mentioned him to other people in a way that shows she is fond of him. She has never once said any of it to him, not in 1994 and not since, and on the two occasions in twenty-five years when the subject of that house has come up between them she has changed it herself and done it well.

And there is the other half of it, which is hers and which she has never taken to anybody. For twenty-five years she has assumed he did not come because he did not want to. That has been bearable and in a way it has even been useful to her, because a man who did not want to is a man whose wanting is a thing she can measure herself against, and she has been able to get on with everything else.

**SHE HAS HAD HIM WRONG FOR TWENTY-FIVE YEARS AND HAS NO IDEA THAT SHE HAS, AND HE HAS KNOWN SINCE THE WEEK AFTER THAT SHE HAS, AND NEITHER OF THEM WILL BE THE FIRST TO SAY ANYTHING.**

The second of August was the day the two of them took an old man who lives at number nineteen a long way across and back, and it is a long day if you have to be in two places at once, and the two of them had been in his front from about nine in the morning. At about half past three the third man arrived on foot, which is the other side of the borough, with one of those flat things on wheels of his own, and said, without being asked, that he would take him back.

And in the way between the two front rooms, with the woman four feet away behind him carrying a parcel he had not asked her to carry, he said, to the man, "I'd have thought that was you."

He said it the way a man delivers something he has turned over a hundred times. He meant the driving. He meant the last hour of it, when a man of seventy-eight has been in a car since nine in the morning and there is somebody standing where the traffic stops who will take him, and he meant it as a compliment, and it was one, and the man said nothing at all and went out to his own car and started it and did not say one word for the rest of the afternoon.

Four months. The man has carried it for four months and has not put any of it to a living soul, and he is not a man who says things, and there is nobody he could say it to.

Here is what is in it, so far as he has been able to look at it himself. A sentence said in a hallway in about 1994 that he has never repeated. A sentence said in the same kind of place in August 2019 that he was not the first to make and that was not even about him. And the difference between the two, which is the whole of it, is that one of them was a thing he said and one of them was a thing that was said at him, and there is no way known to him of telling anybody that.

What he also has, and cannot get past, and will not be telling anybody: it is nearly always him. That is what he has worked out in four months. If a woman in this borough needs somebody in a house, it is nearly always him, and it has been nearly always him since he was twenty-six, and nobody has ever had to think about whether he would come. They simply know. The third man knows. The woman knows. Everybody who has ever had to decide knows, and they decide before they ask, and the deciding takes about a second and it takes no effort and it is the only thing about him that anybody in this borough has ever relied on.

And in the February of 1994 it was him, and he did not come.

---

They are still on the steps at about ten past seven and the ice has not gone and the third man is talking about a roof.

Then he says, "You'll want to get there before the rest of us."

He says it to the woman and he means the man, standing four feet away with his hands in his pockets, and he says it in the same voice he said it in August, and it is very nearly word for word what he said then, and the man is certain that the third man has no idea he has ever said anything of the kind about anything, and the man is right about that, and that is not the worst of it.

The man says nothing at all.

The woman says, "Nine's fine," and that is all she says, and she has not looked at either of them, and she has not looked at either of them for the best part of ten seconds, and the man knows that, and the third man does not, and afterwards nobody could have said what any of them were doing with their faces.

The man puts his hands in deeper and looks at the ice on the middle step and does not say one word, and the third man says, "It's a nice job, that, and it's only the once," and the man says, "Right," and the whole of what the three of them said in about a minute is now nine sentences, of which six are about a roof.

At about ten to eight the third man went down the steps and across the path and away, because he has a long way and because he always goes first. He put a hand on the woman's shoulder on the way past, in the way he does, and said "Sunday, then," and she said "Sunday," and he did not look back at either of them, because there was nothing to look back at.

The man and the woman stood on the top step for about four minutes after that.

The woman said, "I won't be any trouble."

"I won't be any trouble," the man said, because that is what he said on the first Sunday in September and on the second, and he had not planned to say it and it came out anyway.

And then she went down the steps, and there was ice on the middle one, and she went round the side of it on the grass, and he stood where he was, and he has not moved.

**HE SAID NOTHING AT ALL ON THE THIRD OF DECEMBER, AND HE WILL BE IN THAT HOUSE AT NINE ON SUNDAY MORNING AND WILL NOT LEAVE IT, AND THE THIRD MAN WILL HAVE GONE HOME AT TEN PAST SEVEN THINKING ABOUT A ROOF, AND NOT ONE OF THE THREE OF THEM WILL HAVE SAID A SINGLE WORD ABOUT ANY PART OF THE FEBRUARY OF 1994.**
