# Chapter 0926 — Behind The Brick Course

Thursday the eighteenth of July 2024, at the brick edging beside the front path at number ten with the morning still on the hedge. Behind the upper course.

**SHE HAS AN EDGING OF BRICKS SET ON EDGE IN TWO COURSES BESIDE THE FRONT PATH AT NUMBER TEN AND SHE MEANS TO LAY THE THUMB FLAT AGAINST THE UPPER COURSE AND SLIDE THE HAND SIDEWAYS BEHIND IT WITH THE OTHER FOUR FINGERS BENT BACK, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE BED JOINT UNDER THAT COURSE RUNS AND WHAT STANDS BEHIND IT.**

The edging was laid in a week in the spring of the path widening, and the path has been resailed once since while the two courses have kept their beds.

It is a double edging of hard blue brick set on edge the whole length of the walk, one course standing on the other, with the outer course standing a little proud where the ground has settled under it. She wants the bed joint, which is the line where the lower course ends and the upper one begins.

A bed joint is the one joint on a run of bricks that the eye passes over entirely. So the thumb goes along it and the hand follows behind. Brick feels cool and slick along the top arris and gritted down the side of it, and the mortar between them is gone to sand.

The thumb runs about three feet and comes to a place where the joint opens and does not close again. Beyond that line the joint is a quarter of an inch deep, with old mortar crumbled along its floor and pale grains sitting in it. The thumb presses down into the opening and it does not give at all, because what is under it is not sand but the footing course of a gate pier, laid flat and already three years gone.

---

The joint between two courses of brick on edge is a bed joint stood on its end, and it is the coldest joint on the walk, because it faces the ground and no sun gets into it.

Cold in a bed joint is why mortar in it goes first. The frost that gets into the pores of a bed joint in January comes out in March and takes the face off the mortar behind it, and the joint opens from the back while the front still shows square. The upper course of this edging stands about two inches proud of the lower one, which is not proudness but the way a brick on edge stands: two inches wide and three and a half deep, set down the slope rather than laid across it.

A brick set down the slope cannot shed water off its top, and water standing on the top of that course goes down into the joint on either side of it. It goes down one side and comes up the other, and the frost has that rise to work on every year.

The gate pier that comes up behind the joint is the oldest work on the walk. It is three courses thick laid flat, with a footing spread under it a course wider on each side, and the footing is what the joint has run into and stopped against.

Where a bed joint runs into a footing like that it does not end. It steps down onto the footing's top and goes along it, half as deep and twice as cold, and the whole of the ground under a path is decided by where those joints are put.

A double edging laid this way takes twice the ground of a single one, and it is laid in two parts: the footing course flat on the earth, and the two courses on edge above it. Where the earth settles the footing goes down with it and the courses above go with the footing, which is why the whole run tilts a little at the gate end rather than dipping in one place.

The tilt is four inches over the length of the walk. Water on the top of the course follows the tilt, and it always arrives at the low end, and the low end is where the joint has opened.

What the joint has opened into is a footing that runs on across the walk and comes up again at the gate pier on the other side. Two footings, thirty years apart, and the joint of the later one has stopped dead against the earlier one and has nothing further to say.

The bricks came out of a kiln two miles off and were sorted on the delivery for hardness, and the ones that could not strike a ring were thrown out. What is left is stone at the grain and not clay, and clay brick laid on edge in a joint like this one is already breaking down in its second winter.

The front arris of the upper course has been rubbed to a shine by six summers of a broom drawn along it and by nothing else at all. Under the shine the brick is still taking in water, because a rubbed face closes the pores at the top and leaves the face under it open. A rubbed face dries first and wets slowest, which is the whole of the trick of a rubbed edge: it keeps a film off itself by being burnished rather than by being kept out.

Rain off the hedge runs along the top of the course and finds the joint, and the joint takes the run without passing it on. Every shower puts the same two hundred grains into the same foot of that joint, and the grains are from the top course itself, worn off it by the water that made the groove.

The broom that rubs the arris is the same broom that scatters the joint, and both of those are the ordinary way a path edge is treated. The edge has stood sixty years because it is the sort of thing that costs nothing to put down and nothing to look after. The lichen on the shaded inner face of the edging is thin and grey, and it comes back edge-first after a wet spring and goes off from the middle outwards in a dry July.

A joint that has gone to sand cannot be pointed again, for nothing is left in it to point with. Sand in a bed joint is a record of a mortar that has gone, and the brick above it will go the same way once the frost has taken the arris off the top. The path was widened by taking up the old slabs and laying the same width of sawn stone instead, and the old edging that came up was stacked under the step, which is why the joint at the gate end is straighter than the rest of the run. The bed joint was not re-pointed when the path was done, because a bed joint between two courses on edge is not a face and does not look like one from the gate.

The bricks of the pier footing were never meant to be seen either. They were laid to spread the pier's load into the ground, and they were laid at a depth that the eye does not reach, and they are the only masonry on this walk that has had no rain on it at all.

Her sister is at the shed window from inside, the sash up to its head, a folded net in her hand that she has not shaken out.

Net in her hand, she stood at the opening and looked down the length of the edging, then at the gate, then along it once more.

"The hens have found the new bed."

"Then they have got there before the rain."

"I will take the net down and let them at it."

"Let them at it."

She put the net under her arm and went on down the shed path with the sash up behind her, and neither the run nor the ground got a word out of her on the way past the joint.

The brick edging stays where it was laid, the upper course a finger off the lower one, with old mortar crumbled along the line between them and neither the sand in its floor nor the footing course under it going anywhere.
