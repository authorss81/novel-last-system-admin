# Chapter 0352 — Nine Coats

Thursday the fourteenth of September 2017, from about eight in the morning until about half past four in the afternoon, a first-floor corridor in a block off the top of a street, and a shop off a parade, in Tideglass.

**THE SASH WINDOW AT THE END OF THAT CORRIDOR WAS PAINTED SHUT SOME TIME BEFORE THE DOORS ON THAT LANDING WERE LAST PAINTED, AND A WEDGE PUT UNDER IT IN THE LAST HOT WEEK IS STILL THERE AND THE WINDOW HAS NOT MOVED, AND A WOMAN IN A SHOP OFF A PARADE HAS WRITTEN A TIME ON THE BACK OF A LIST TWICE IN A FORTNIGHT AND CANNOT SAY WHY, AND NEITHER OF THOSE THINGS IS ANYTHING TO DO WITH THE OTHER ONE.**

The block is three storeys and it stands at the top of a street that comes off the Cleeve road, and it was put up in about 1908 out of brick that was not very good, and it has four flats and nobody has ever owned it in this borough that anybody can remember, and there is a woman in number three on that landing with a cat and there is a man in number two who has a van and there are two flats that have been empty since before the summer and the doors on them are locked and there is no carpet visible under either.

The corridor is about the length of a bath and a half and there are four doors off it and a sash window at the end of it.

The window has no opening in it at all. The two sashes are painted together where they meet in the middle, and the top sash is painted down onto the bottom sash, and the frame is painted over the horns of the lock, and there is paint about a quarter of an inch thick in the corner where the top sash sits, and the paint in that corner is not a single skin and it has not cracked, which means nobody has tried to open it in a long time.

There is a wedge under the top sash about two foot in from the left stile. It is a piece of offcut about four inch long sawn across the grain and it has been there since the last hot week, which was the last week of August, and somebody in that building put a knife in the gap and levered the bottom rail up about half an inch and pushed the wedge in.

The sash has not moved since. The wedge is still there and it has not gone in any further and the window has not come down onto it, and the room behind that window is number three and the woman in it has a cat and a chair and a radio and has been in it every day since February.

---

The man in his forties went up that stair at about ten in the morning on the Thursday to put a length of shelf in a kitchen in number two, and he was let in by the man with the van, who was going out and who said that the woman in number three would not want talking to and that he would be about an hour.

He had the drill out of his bag and a bit in it and the two brackets already marked, and the bracket on the far wall is under the window, and to get at it he stood a folding step under the window and put his hand flat on the bottom rail of the sash to steady himself while he set the bit into the plaster.

The bottom rail did not move at all.

He stood there with his hand on it for about four seconds and then he took his hand off and looked at it, and then he took the knife out of his bag and put it in the gap at the corner and levered the sash up about half an inch, and it came up as easy as anything, and the paint along that corner broke off in three pieces about the length of a finger and dropped onto the sill and into the dust on the sill.

The paint under the top one is a different colour from the paint over it.

The top coat on that sash, where he levered it, is off-white, and it is the colour of the doors on that landing, and it goes round the horns of the lock and along the meeting rail and down the stiles in one unbroken coat about the thickness of a postcard.

Underneath that, on the inside of the sash where it has lifted, is a colour that is not off-white and has not been off-white for a long time. It is a sort of drab green, and it is a thin coat, and it has gone over the join between the two sashes, which means it went on after the sashes were last opened.

The doors on that landing were painted about twelve years ago, and everybody in that building will tell you twelve years, because that is when the last of the doors was done and all four of them were done at once off the same tin.

So the sash was shut before the doors were painted, and the person who shut it painted it shut on purpose, and shut a window that opens in a corridor on the upper floor of a block nobody owns, and then left it.

He put the wedge back in and went and did the shelf, which took the best part of an hour, and he did not tell the man with the van, and the man with the van was not in, and he did not knock on the door of number three.

Nobody in that building is going to find out who did it. The building has not been anybody's job since before the summer, there is no paper about the window in this borough, and a man who wanted a window shut in a corridor where he did not live could have done it on a ladder in an afternoon in about 2005, and there is no way anybody in that street is going to put a year on it beyond the one the doors give it.

And the window is still shut. The woman in number three has a chair by it and a radio and has not opened it since before the summer and did not try to open it in the hot week, and the wedge that somebody put there in the last week of August is the only thing in that corridor that has moved in about a year.

The shop off that parade is the one with the counter, and the shutter comes down at about half past three with a bang that puts the window glasses in, and the shutter went up at about eight.

The woman who works in a shop has a list. She has had one since before the summer and she writes it on a Sunday night, on the back of a sheet out of a pad, and it is what the shop wants for the week and it is about nine lines and it is a list of nothing at all, and there is no column on it and no box and no heading and nobody in that borough will ever see it.

The Monday of the week before last it said: teashirts, brown paper, string, two boxes of bags, bin rolls, a bulb, a roll of paper towel, polish, and six pound of apples.

The Monday of this week it says: teashirts, string, bin rolls, a bulb, polish, and the same six pound of apples, and there is no brown paper and no bags because there was no bags, and that is the whole of what that list is for.

Underneath that, on both weeks, about four lines below the apples, there is a time of day written in the same hand in the same pencil.

TWENTY PAST SEVEN.

On the Monday of this week it is not in line with anything. It is not under the apples and it is not a price and it is not a delivery and it is not an opening time, because the shop opens at eight and the shutter goes down at half past three and the shop has never opened at twenty past seven on any day of its life or any other shop on that parade's.

She has not been able to say why she wrote it and she wrote it twice, which is a fortnight apart, on two different sheets, and the two sheets are the same size and the pad is the same pad, and the second one is on top of the first one in the pad and the first one is under it.

She cannot find out why she wrote it, and it cannot be established, because a list like that carries no day against anything that is on it and there is nobody in that shop or in that borough who would know what a woman writes at the bottom of a shopping list on a Sunday night, and she has not thrown the first one out and she is not going to.

---

The man in his forties was at that counter at about half past two that afternoon buying a length of four by two, and she cut it and he paid for it and put it under his arm, and he did not tell her about the window and she did not tell him about the list.

He was in the room at the top of that street for about an hour and she is in that shop for about ten hours a day and those two facts have never been in the same conversation in this borough, and there is no version of a woman behind a counter on that parade in which a man in forties mentions drab green under a sash window, and there is no version of a Sunday night in which a woman mentions the man in the corridor and the door that took twelve years and the paint that came before it.

**A WEDGE OF WOOD IS UNDER A WINDOW THAT WAS SHUT AND PAINTED OVER BEFORE FOUR DOORS IN THE SAME CORRIDOR WERE PAINTED, AND A TIME OF DAY IS WRITTEN UNDER A LIST OF APPLES ON TWO SHEETS A FORTNIGHT APART, AND A MAN WHO FOUND ONE OF THOSE THINGS BOUGHT SOME BOARD IN A SHOP AT HALF PAST TWO AND SAID NOTHING.**
