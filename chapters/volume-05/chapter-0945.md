# Chapter 0945 — Into The Pipe Bore

Monday the seventh of October 2024, at the clay pipe standing on end in the boundary wall at number nine with the first cold on the flags. Into a pipe's mouth.

**A FOUR-INCH CLAY PIPE IS SET ON END AS THE END PIER OF THE BOUNDARY WALL AT NUMBER NINE WITH ITS BORE OPEN TO THE AIR, AND THE FOUR FINGERS OF ONE HAND GO INTO THAT BORE AS FAR APART AS THEY WILL GO AND THEN CLOSE SLOWLY UNTIL THE PADS OF ALL FOUR ARRIVE ON THE CLAY AT ONCE, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE PIPE GOES AND WHAT IS ACROSS THE FAR END OF IT.**

She had seen the pipe from the kitchen window most of the summer and had not gone out to it. The reason she had not is that there was nothing at it that wanted doing.

It stands at the end of the boundary wall where the wall stops and the open strip begins, built into the second course down. The bricks are run round it on three sides and its mouth stands about a hand's width proud of the face of them.

It is an ordinary four-inch clay pipe of the kind that goes into a wall on end for a pier, because it is cheaper than dressed stone and stands a heavier load. A pipe is strong in a straight line and comparatively weak across one. A wall wants a thing that is strong in a straight line, so a length of pipe stood on end in the middle of a wall is doing what it was put there to do.

It is the newest thing on that wall by fifty years. The bricks on either side of it are the same bricks and were laid in the same summer, and they have gone the colour of a cigarette, while the pipe has gone a different colour altogether, a pale orange where the sun has been on the outside of it and a dark chocolate on the inside where nothing has been.

Four fingers go in as far apart as they will go. The bore is wider than they are spread, so the spread finds nothing at first, and the hand has to go further down before the clay comes near it at all.

Then the fingers close, slowly, so that the pads of all four arrive on the pipe's wall together and the width of the pipe comes off the closing of the hand instead of off the looking at it. A four-inch pipe is four inches across the inside, and four fingers spread are nearer five, and it takes a closing of about an inch before the clay comes in on the outside of the little finger.

The bore is round and very slightly out of true, so that it is wider at one height than at another by a difference small enough to be the thickness of a sheet of paper and large enough to feel under the pads. It is colder in there than the wall around it, because there is nothing in it and the air in a bore goes a long way down before it takes the temperature of the earth round it.

The clay itself is brittle at the rim, where the mouth has been chipped on the outside by a barrow or a boot and the chip has been left as it came away. It crumbles at the edge of that chip under nothing more than a fingernail and has been crumbling slowly since the wall was built, which is why there is a small heap of orange grit on the brick ledge below the mouth.

Inside, away from the rim, the clay is not brittle at all. It is smooth and dark and dry and it takes a print from the pad of a finger and gives that print back slowly, the way a piece of fired clay always does and never the way a piece of stone does.

---

A pipe on end in a wall is bedded rather than built, and being bedded is the whole reason the section has not moved since the wall went up. The brickwork is set on the outside of it and lime is run round the outside of it, and the earth under it is beaten down before the section goes in, because a pipe set on loose ground settles into it and takes its bond with the lime along with it.

So the section does not give at all. It is not loose in the wall and it is not loose in the ground, and a hand can push at the mouth for as long as a hand is willing to push and the clay will not come towards it by so much as a nail.

Inside the pipe, at about eleven inches down, the bore narrows and there is a face across it. That face is a flat stone the size of a spread hand, set on a ledge of lime that has gone hard and gone pale, and it is the stone a man puts at the bottom of an open pipe to stop anything going down into a wall that was not built to let anything go down into it.

To one side of the stone the bore carries on a further two inches. It closes in clay after that, which is the tail of the section, cut short and set into the ground under the earth in the bottom of the pier so that the earth cannot get up inside the pipe and bring half the garden in with it.

A cobweb is drawn across the mouth and across the upper third of the bore, from rim to rim, and it is the old one and not a new one. It has been in the wind all summer and is still there because a cobweb in a round pipe takes a straight wind square on and loses it again.

A wall like this one was built by a man with a yard of pipe and a yard of brick and no reason to buy stone. And the pier went in where the wall ends because a wall has to end on something.

The bricks are laid on their edges for the two courses at the top, which is the ordinary way of getting a wall of that height to stand straight under a wet autumn. The pipe is built into the thickness of the brickwork instead of into the end of it, so it carries the wall rather less than it is carried by the wall.

The bond round the pipe is old lime and not cement, and it has gone hard and gone out of colour. It stands a little back from the pipe at the top of the joint, and none of that is decay in it.

Old lime draws itself back from whatever it is set against as it goes off, and the gap it leaves is not a fault in anything. It is the reason a wall like that one can still be taken down by a man with a crowbar and nothing else.

A pipe laid on end is stronger than the brickwork round it in every direction that matters. Round it, the brick has to bridge four inches and it does it. Down it, the pipe carries four inches across and a foot and more downward without noticing, and the one weakness in the whole arrangement is the rim, which stands out in the weather on one side and takes everything a wall of that height takes.

---

The woman who does her shopping comes through the plank gate standing open in the boundary wall at the back strip. She is off the field and down the path towards the house, with a string bag over her arm and the last of the shopping in it, and she does not come up to the wall.

She asks whether the queue was outside at the shop or in. She gets an answer about the rain and about the hour of the day, and neither of them has anything to do with the pipe, and neither of them asks about the pipe at any point in it.

Then she goes on up the path with the bag against her side. The gate stands open against the wall behind her the whole way, and the fingers came out of the bore before she was within thirty feet of it.

The stone at the bottom is still a stone at the bottom, and the tail of the section is still two inches of clay. The ledge of old lime under it has not moved and is not brittle where it carries the weight, being a lime that has been hard for sixty years and takes a load now the way a piece of chalk takes a load.

The section is bedded in earth and lime and is as fixed in that wall as the brickwork on either side of it. Its mouth stands a hand's width proud of the face of the bricks, with a heap of orange grit under it and a cobweb drawn across it.

Eleven inches down there is a stone on a ledge of lime and the section is bedded in earth and lime all the way round it, and what a hand takes back out of that bore is orange dust and the print of brick on the inside of the wrist and nothing else whatever, so that the middle of that wall stays the inside of a pipe and the wall it stands in and nothing other than the wall.
