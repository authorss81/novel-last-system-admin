# Chapter 0718 — A Number He Did Not Write Down

Monday the eighteenth of April 2022, in this borough. He is at his own unit from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a run of units a quarter of an inch out and a telephone on the wall and a call he lets go off in his pocket.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES WORKS A RUN OF UNITS A QUARTER OF AN INCH OUT OVER SIX FOOT AND KEEPS A SCRAP OF SCRIBING CARD IN HIS POCKET WITH TWELVE ON ONE SIDE AND A PENCIL BEHIND HIS EAR AND WRITES NOTHING ON IT, ANSWERS A TELEPHONE RINGING A WOMAN WHO ASKS WHETHER HE HAS A STEP LADDER, SAYS HE HAS NOT GOT ONE, AND DOES NOT ASK HER WHY SHE IS LOOKING.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is at his own unit and on no other ground. A woman he has never met telephones him in the morning and a woman from a shop telephones him in the afternoon. He speaks to both of them on the telephone.

---

He starts at the bench before there is anybody else in the unit.

The gates are leaning against the back wall where he put them at the end of the month before last, oak, a pair, with the numbers chalked on the top rails in his own chalk. They are up off the ground on a batten so the grain can breathe, and they have been leaning there through the winter and they are still leaning there.

He does not start on the gates.

He starts on the run at the front of the unit, which is the thing he has been not finishing since March and which he works at first light because it is the only job in the building that will not wait for anybody.

It is a run of four units along a frontage and the top run of it is proud of the others by about a quarter of an inch over the whole of its six foot, which is the sort of thing you cannot see and everybody who stands in front of that wall and looks along it can see.

He has a scrap of scribing card in his shirt pocket with the number twelve written on one side in his own pencil, and he has had it since before Christmas, and it is the scrap he keeps for setting out on.

He takes it out and turns it over and the other side is blank, and he puts it back in the pocket, and the pencil is behind his ear where it has been since he came in.

---

He is not going to write anything on that side and he has not written anything on that side since before Christmas.

It is not a decision he made. It is that the number on the other side is twelve, and a card with twelve on one side and a job on the other is a card that somebody could pick up, and he would then have to say what the job was and whose it was, and he has never yet worked out how to say that.

So the numbers on the gates stay in chalk on the gates and the only time and labour on them is a card in a drawer at the cottage with a number on it, and it is paid, and it was tried in March, and the latch stile wants a pair of hands and a ladder.

He does not do roofs. He has never done a roof and he says so before anybody asks.

---

The mobile goes off at about the middle of the morning.

He takes it out of his pocket and looks at it and the number on it is not one he knows, and it is not with a name on it, and it is a mobile, and that is the first thing.

He lets it go off.

It goes again while he is on the step with the plane, and this time he answers it, because a number with no name on it in the middle of a Monday morning is either somebody who has rung the wrong thing or somebody who has been given this number by somebody he works for, and he would rather find out which at the start of a job than the end of one.

"Hello."

"Is that the joinery, is it?" she says. "Somebody gave me this number for a step ladder."

"Who gave you this number."

"I don't know, he just said ask him."

"Right."

"You've not got a step ladder."

"No."

"You do fencing," she says. "Somebody said you did fencing."

"I did a pair of gates in March," he says. "I haven't got a ladder."

"Oh."

And there is a question sitting right there. What she wants a ladder for. It is the only question in this call and it is the whole call, and it is a question he would ask a man without thinking about.

He does not ask it.

There is a reason and the reason arrives in his head before the sentence does, and the reason is that the moment he asks it, he is a man who has been asked by a stranger what a stranger is doing, and a stranger who answers is a stranger who has somewhere to put a man with a ladder and has not wanted anybody in the house.

So he waits for her to go on, and she goes on, and she is talking about a greenhouse pane that has come out of a frame, and about how she does not want to be up a ladder in the wind and does not want anybody in the house, and he listens to the whole of it.

And what he does with the answer is nothing. It goes into his ear and stays there and he never puts it anywhere, and the not putting it anywhere is the cheapest thing he does all day and it is the only thing he does all day.

---

And this is what he does while she is talking, which he sees afterwards and not at the time.

He takes the scrap of card out of his shirt pocket with his left hand and he has the pencil behind his ear and he has a stretch of her talking, long enough, in which to write six digits on the back of a piece of scribing card with the number twelve on the other side.

And he does not do it. He puts the card back in the pocket and the pencil goes back behind his ear and she is still saying something about the frame, and then she says, "Well, if you don't, I'll get somebody else," and he says, "That's it," and she says, "Right, thanks," and it goes dead.

There was time for it twice over. The card was in his left hand.

---

He stands in the unit with the mobile in his hand afterwards and works out why he did not write it, and he gets two answers and he does not like either of them.

The first is that he did not want her number. That is not true and he knows it is not true, because a woman with a pane out of a greenhouse is a person he could do a small job for on a Tuesday and there is nothing on at the unit this week.

The second is that he has had the card in his pocket since before Christmas with twelve on one side, and if he starts writing the other side then it is a working card, and if it is a working card then somebody could ask to see it.

And nobody has asked to see it. That is the thing. He has been careful for months with a card in his pocket and the care has not cost him anything at all, and it was not worth a job in March, and it is not worth a job in April.

He puts the mobile back in his pocket and goes and puts the plane down on the bench and lines it up with the edge of the bench because he lines everything up.

---

The telephone on the wall rings at about the middle of the afternoon.

That one he answers, because it is the shop's own telephone on the wall of his own unit and it is a telephone he is responsible for and it is the only number printed on anything he owns.

"Unit," she says. "Are you free tomorrow morning?"

"Who's asking."

"It's me, from Friday."

"Hang on."

He puts the shop's telephone down on the bench and he goes across to the racks with the list and he finds the day written at the top and then the shop's telephone starts going again behind him on the wall while it is lying on the bench, and it is going because the line went dead when he put it down.

He stands there with the list in one hand and the unit going behind him.

Then the mobile goes off in his pocket.

Two instruments, both sounding, and he can take one of them.

He takes the wall one and picks it up off the bench and puts it to his ear and finishes the conversation about tomorrow morning, and he writes tomorrow in the book on the bench, and he asks her about a hinge, and she tells him about a hinge.

And the mobile goes on going in his pocket the whole time and then stops, and when he puts the wall one down and looks at the mobile there is nothing on it but a number he does not know and no name and no message, because he does not have one of those that tells you.

---

He did not even have to make a choice. That is the part he keeps coming back to.

There was no moment where he stood and weighed the woman on the telephone about the greenhouse against the woman about the hinge. The wall one was on the bench and the mobile was in his pocket and he turned round and picked up the one on the bench because it was in front of him.

A number with no name on it lost to a hinge, and the hinge was about a door and the number was about a job, and both of them were real, and neither of them knows it happened.

He writes tomorrow in the book. He writes the day and the hour and the word hinge. He does not write the greenhouse, and there is a scrap of card in his pocket with twelve on one side and nothing at all on the other.

---

He squares the gates up against the back wall before the light goes, because they have been leaning long enough that a person coming in the gate in the dark could have them over.

They are up on the batten and he pushes them straight with his boot and looks along the top rails and the numbers are still in the chalk.

He has not been up that road since the middle of June and he is not going up it today. It is four streets and one gate and he does not do roofs, and the latch stile is right, and the work is paid for, and there is nothing to go up there for.

He looks along the run at the front of the unit while he is out there, from the far end, the way you stand to see a thing you have been working on.

The top run is still proud by about a quarter of an inch over its six foot. He has been at it since March and it is not less than when he started and it is not more, and on Monday it was very nearly the same as on the Thursday before that.

He shuts the gate and it latches, and the shed is not his and the cottage is not his, and there is a ladder in this borough in a room at the end of somebody else's road that he has never seen and has never been told about, and the woman on the telephone this morning was looking for one.

---

**What happened at the unit:** he worked a run of units that has been a quarter of an inch proud over six foot since March, and he let a call with no name on it go off twice, and he had a stretch of a woman's talking and a blank piece of card in his left hand and he did not write her number down.

**What he will not know:** that a woman on that call was asking after a step ladder, and that a folding ladder with a strip of tape round the second rail and a chalk mark on the third tread is folded under a window in a front room four streets away, put there on the seventeenth of December by somebody who cannot put it back and has never been able to say whose it was.

**What is still there:** the gates against the back wall, the posts in the ground at the cottage, the paid work, the latch stile that wants a pair of hands and a ladder, and the drawer under the bench with a brush in a bag in it, shut since the sixteenth of November.
