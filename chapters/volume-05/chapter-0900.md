# Chapter 0900 — Back Of The Hollow

Monday the eighth of April 2024, at the face of the bed at the end of the yard at number twelve and the task is the whole of that weekday. A hollow cut back into a face of soil.

**SHE HAS A BED WITH A FACE OF SOIL STANDING A SPADE DEEP AT THE END OF THE YARD AT NUMBER TWELVE AND SHE MEANS TO SPREAD THUMB AND FINGERS ROUND THE INSIDE OF A HOLLOW IN THAT FACE AND PRESS THEM OUTWARDS TO ITS SIDES AND HER SISTER IS AT THE FRONT WINDOW FROM THE GARDEN WITH A DUSTER IN HER HAND.**

That face has stood at the end of that yard since the bed was dug out, and the path beside it has been swept twice while the face has stayed where it was cut.

It is a face of dark soil about a spade deep, with the top turfed over and the sides going straight down to the path.

She wants the hollow halfway along it, where the face dips back in a smooth curve about the size of a hand.

It sits at elbow height where the splash off the path lands hardest, which is why the rain found it before any other part.

A dip in a bed face answers touch better than sight, because winters mark the face while one rain cuts the dip through them.

So thumb and fingers spread round the inside of that hollow and press outwards to its sides.

The soil inside is cool and firm, with small stones in it and roots cut clean across where they ran through when the face was straight.

The hollow goes back about three inches at the middle and nothing at the rims, with the back of it smooth where water ran over it and the sides rough where soil fell in.

What cut it is rain off the path. Water runs off the path in heavy rain and hits the face in one place, and each hitting takes a little more soil out of the middle.

Her sister comes to the front window from the garden with a duster in her hand and does not touch the glass.

She stood there with the duster over her wrist and looked out to the bed, and after a while she went along the front of the house to the kitchen door with the duster still in her hand.

She was told the window would want its runner oiled before summer, and she drew the duster along the sill and carried it through to the kitchen and left the glass where it was.

---

A face of soil stands only while roots lock it, because bare soil slumps in rain and turfed soil keeps true.

The top of this face is turfed with grass that has rooted down about two inches, and the roots go down like pins and keep the top edge true.

Below the turf the soil is bare to the air, with worm holes in it and old root channels going back further than the hand can reach.

Worm holes drain a face the way field drains drain a meadow, and a face with worms in it sheds water through itself instead of over itself.

That is why this face stands a spade deep without boarding. Worms have bored it full of pipes that take water down to the path footing before it can gather head.

A face without worms ponds at the top in heavy rain and then slips in one piece, with the turf sliding off like a cap.

Rain finds the weakest place in a face and works on it, and the weakest place here is where an old root rotted out last year and left a soft spot.

Water got into the soft spot and hollowed it, and the hollow caught more water, and each rain made it deeper in the middle and wider at the rims.

That is why the back of the hollow is smooth and the sides are rough. Water smooths what it runs over and soil falls rough where it drops in from above.

She spreads thumb and fingers again and presses out to feel how much the sides give.

They sink half an inch under hard pressure and then meet hard ground deeper.

What stops them is a mat of roots, going back into the bed from the sides of the hollow like fingers into a glove.

The roots are from the grass on top, grown down and sideways to find the damp the hollow catches.

Grass roots follow damp the way ivy follows a wall, and a hollow that catches rain will be ringed with roots in a year.

That ring keeps the hollow from growing wider, while the back of it goes on deepening where no roots reach.

Roots reach for damp first and food second, which is why the ring sits where the splash wets the sides and not where the bed feeds best.

A root in damp soil branches twice as thick as one in dry, because each tip splits when it finds water and splits again when it finds more.

That is why the mat at the sides is dense enough to stop a thumb, while the back, which dries between rains, is almost bare of them.

A hollow ringed like that lasts for years, deepening a little each winter and steadying each summer when the grass grows thick.

---

The soil of the face is firm where roots lock it and soft where water runs, and the hollow sits between the two, firm at the sides and soft at the back.

The back of it is dark and damp, with grit washed clean where the water runs fastest.

Grit washes clean in running water because the fine soil goes first and the coarse stuff stays, and each rain leaves the back a little stonier than before.

That is why the hollow will never fill on its own. Rain takes the fine stuff out and leaves the coarse stuff in, and the coarse stuff lets more rain in next time.

A hollow ringed with roots like that is stopped with a turf patch pinned over it on two pegs of hazel, the patch lapped across the rims so that it throws the first rains off instead of drinking them in.

Hazel pegs swell in damp soil and lock firm, while pine pegs shrink and work loose by May.

That is why hedging is pinned with hazel and not with offcuts, and why a turf patch on a face wants the same.

Turf throws water the way thatch does, and a patched face sheds rain while a bare one drinks it and slumps.

The turf on top is thick and tight, with the blades lying flat where feet brush it and standing up where they do not.

Turf cut from a path edge brings its own soil with it, about an inch of it, and the inch beds down onto the face in a week and roots through in three.

A patch cut thin curls up at the corners and lets rain in behind it, which is worse than no patch at all.

That is why a good patch is cut thick and left proud, with the grass long on it to throw the first rains.

The path below is swept clean, with the sweepings thrown back on the bed where they feed it.

Sweepings feed a bed because grit in them opens the soil and leaf in them rots down by winter, and each sweeping adds a little of both where the face meets the path.

She takes the hand out of the hollow and brushes the soil off on the face beside it, and reads the hollow from the path for what the cup declares.

From the front the hollow looks like a dark cup in the face, smooth at the back and ragged at the rims.

From the side it shows as a dip in the line of the face, deeper in the middle than at either end.

A dip in a face throws shade at noon and catches it at evening, which is why the back stays damp long after the rims have dried. Midges gather in that shade on still evenings and leave by dark as the soil cools.

The cup reads true from the path and the hand reads the back of it, and the two together say which part of that hollow will go first, the rear of it going down winter on winter while the sides stay where they are, and the sides are kept tighter by the roots than the turf above them ever keeps them.
