# Chapter 0361 — Two Figures on a Slate

Friday the twentieth of October 2017, from about seven in the morning until about three in the afternoon, a yard off the Cleeve road, and a shop off a parade, in Tideglass.

**A MAN COUNTED FOUR HUNDRED AND FIFTY-FOUR SLABS OFF THE TAGS ON THE BANDS AND A MAN COUNTED FOUR HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-SIX OFF THE STACKS THEMSELVES, AND BOTH OF THEM ARE RIGHT, AND BOTH FIGURES ARE ON A SLATE HANGING ON THE WALL OF THAT YARD IN TWO HANDS, ONE UNDER THE OTHER, AND NEITHER HAS BEEN RUBBED OUT.**

It is a yard behind two houses on a lane that comes off the Cleeve road, and it is not one of the yards with workshops at the back of it, and there is a concrete apron down one side of it and brick along the other and a stack of pipe against the brick. The way in is a pair of steel leaves about four foot high, and they are propped with a length of scaffold that somebody put there, and there is no sign on them and no lock on them and nobody in this borough has ever been given the use of that ground.

The slabs came into that yard on a lorry on the Thursday morning at about half past six and were put down in nine loads of about sixty, and there were about five hundred and something of them in that yard by about ten, and a pallet of four of them was left at the front by the driver and the driver went.

A slab of paving is about two foot by one foot and about two inch thick and about four stone in weight, and they come on a pallet in bands, and the pallet boards in that yard are the yard's own boards, and the bands are tied with a strap and the strap is sealed with a flat tin tag that has the supplier's number and a number of four on it.

Four is the band. A band is four slabs and a pallet is five bands and a pallet is twenty, and the tag on the strap carries the supplier's number and the number four, and the supplier's number says which load it came off.

Nobody in that borough weighs anything twice.

---

The man in his forties counted that yard on the Friday morning.

He started at the back and worked down, because you cannot count a yard from the front with a heap at the back of it, and he counted the tags, and he counted the slabs on the top of every pallet, and he got a number and wrote it on the slate that hangs on the wall of that yard where it has hung since before the summer.

The slate is about two foot by one foot with a hoop round the top of it and a nail through the hoop, and it is a thing men chalk figures on, and it has never had a heading on it or a rule on it or a name on it, and nobody has ever asked to see it.

He wrote his number on the top half of it and left the bottom half of it clear, which is what it always is.

The man in his thirties counted that yard at about midday the same morning and he is the man who laid about two thirds of what is on the ground and knows it and has a reason for every slab on it.

He counted the tags, because the tag on the strap is the yard's own record of what came off a lorry, and he counted the number on the tag, and he counted the slabs on the top of every pallet, and he got a different number and wrote it on the bottom half of the slate underneath the other one.

Neither of them is wrong.

The man in his forties counted every slab he could see, including four bands that came off the lorry loose and were never strapped and have no tag on them at all, and he counted those by putting them in fours on the ground, because you can count in fours off the edge of a slab and you can do it standing up.

The man in his thirties did not count those, because a slab with no tag on it is a slab nobody has said anything about, and he is not a man who counts things nobody has said anything about.

And there is a third thing and it is the reason the two numbers are where they are, and neither of the two men has ever mentioned it to anybody and the man who put the slabs in that yard is four counties away.

Those yard boards get used. A board comes off a pallet and goes back on a pallet of something else and the number of the load it came off goes with it, and about a quarter of what is in that yard at any time has a tag on it that belongs to a load that is somewhere else entirely.

There is no way in that yard to tell a good tag from a reused one. They are all the same tin, the same string, the same flat thing, and the number on one of them says a yard in another town.

---

They were in that yard together at about twenty past twelve on the Friday, and neither of them had ever been in it at the same time as the other before that.

"That is a lot for a path," said the man in his thirties.

"It is what came," said the man in his forties.

"You are not going to get it up in a week in this cold."

"I will get it up in two," said the man in his forties, and meant it, and it was right.

They looked at the heaps. The man in his forties put his thumb at the bottom of a heap the way a man does when he is looking at a heap and not at a number, and the man in his thirties said the base course wants doing first and that the man in his forties knew it, and the man in his forties said he did.

Then the man in his thirties said, "How have you got that one."

He nodded at the slate on the wall.

The man in his forties said, "I counted the tags and I counted them loose."

The man in his thirties looked at the slate for about three seconds.

"You have not counted the tags on that one at the back," he said, and went over and put his hand on a band at the back of the yard, and the tag on it was the tag that said a yard in another town, and he said, "That is a Fennick one. That came in on the second load and it has been on these boards since about March."

"That is a tag," said the man in his forties. "It is not a slab."

"That is my point," said the man in his thirties.

And that is where it stopped, because the man in his forties did not say that a reused board did not make the slabs wrong, and the man in his thirties did not say that a slab with no tag on it was not in the yard, and both of those sentences were available to both of them inside about half a second, and neither of them said either of them.

He put the band down where he had found it and they got on with the base course, and the two of them worked in that yard until about three o'clock and did not say another word about a number.


At about half past three on the Friday the man in his thirties was in that shop buying a bag of pointing.

"They have you short," said the woman who works in a shop.

"Short of what," said the man in his thirties.

"Of whatever it is you are laying. You have been back in that yard twice today and you have not been in a shop twice in a day since before the summer."

The man in his thirties said that the man in his forties was laying it and that he was on the base, and that a base takes a day whatever is on it, and she said that was a thing you could say about anything, and he said it was, and paid for the pointing and went out into the parade.

She has two figures. The man in his forties said his out loud at the counter in June when he put down a deposit on something else entirely, and the man in his thirties has not said his to anybody at all, and the only place in this borough that has both of them is a slate on a wall in a yard nobody has the use of, and it is not a record of anything and it is not anybody's.

The man in his forties has said his number to two people and the man in his thirties has said his to nobody, and there is nothing whatever in that yard that says which of the two is the count of what is in it.

**THE MAN IN HIS THIRTIES WENT BACK TO THAT YARD ON THE MONDAY MORNING AND LOOKED AT THE SLATE ON THE WALL FOR A WHILE AND DID NOT PUT HIS HAND UP TO IT, AND THE FIGURE HE WROTE ON THE BOTTOM HALF OF IT IN THE MORNING IS STILL UNDER THE FIGURE THE OTHER MAN WROTE ON THE TOP HALF OF IT IN JUNE, AND BOTH OF THEM WILL BE THERE WHEN THAT WALL COMES DOWN.**
