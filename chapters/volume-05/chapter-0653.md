# Chapter 0653 — Two Lists On One Piece Of Paper

Tuesday the thirteenth of July 2021, in this borough, and not in this borough, because the house is on the far side of the ring road. She is in it from about half past eight in the morning until about twenty to twelve, and the whole of it is a cupboard door, a tape measure and an envelope that took the skin off one of her lists and put it against another one.

**A WOMAN GOES BACK INTO AN EMPTY HOUSE TO PRICE A CUPBOARD DOOR SHE HAS ALREADY PRICED, AND THE ENVELOPE IS STILL TAPED TO THE INSIDE OF IT WITH ABOUT TWO INCHES OF TAPE TORN OFF WITH HER TEETH, AND WHEN SHE PEELS IT OFF IT TAKES THE TOP CORNER OF HER OWN HANDWRITING WITH IT AND LEAVES THE SIX THINGS ON THE OTHER SIDE AS LEGIBLE AS THEY EVER WERE, AND A MAN IN HIS LATE TWENTIES ASKS HER WHOSE THE OTHER SIDE IS AND SHE SAYS *IT'S MINE*, AND HE SAYS *RIGHT* AND DOES NOT ASK TO SEE, AND SHE TAKES IT AWAY ANYWAY.**

She is a woman in her late forties. He is a man in his late twenties. There is nobody else at that address.

---

She goes back on the Tuesday because the back room has to go after all.

That is the whole reason and it is not an interesting reason and it is the only reason.

The woman who booked it rang on the Monday morning and said the brother had taken the key back on the Friday night, and had taken the key to the back room with him, and was not answering, and could she do it before the end of the month.

And she said yes, and then she sat in her own kitchen for about a minute and worked out why she had said yes.

Because she had charged that job on the basis of a whole clear and a quarter of it had gone back in, and the eight boxes of the back room are still on the invoice as a take-away, and there is no line anywhere on the invoice that says a woman came in with a key at midday and put half of it back.

So she goes back. To price it properly. Which is the professional thing, and it takes twenty minutes of the three hours and a bit that are actually there.

---

She gets to the garage at about half past eight and the front door is off the latch because the front door is always off the latch in this house and she has never once reported that.

The back room is empty. All eight boxes were taken back out and the room is bare and there is a rectangle of unfaded carpet where the wardrobe stood.

---

And then she goes to measure the cupboard door, and here is what she is doing.

She has already priced a door. She told the woman who booked it about sixty pounds for a door and fitting and that was the fourteenth of June and she has not been out to look at it properly since, because the day she took the job she priced it from the outside, from the open door, and did not go into the garage.

You cannot price a door from the outside. You have to have the opening. You have to know whether it is a solid core or a hollow one, and the hinges, and the clearance at the top for a floor spring or none, and whether the frame is going to move when you knock the old one out.

So she goes into the garage with a tape.

---

And the door is where it was, leaning against the wall beside the frame it came off.

And there is a gas envelope on the inside of it, taped at the four edges with about two inches of tape, and the tape has gone yellow and there is a bit of it at the top left corner that has come away and taken the print with it.

---

And here is what happens to her in about a second, standing in a garage on the far side of the ring road with a tape measure in her hand.

Nothing happens to her.

That is the whole point and it is the reason she is going to be able to do the next half hour at all. She has been driving to this address since the Saturday of a table leg with a piece of paper in her coat, and she has not once let herself think about it, and she has been extremely good at that, and she is suddenly extremely bad at it.

---

She sits down on the step of the garage with the envelope in both hands.

And the first thing she does is try to get it off whole.

She picks at the top corner with a fingernail for about a minute and a half, and it does not come, because that is what tape does to a door that has had garage damp on it and then weeks of nothing.

And then she thinks: *take the tape off with the tape.*

She gets a roll of brown tape out of her bag, which she has had in every van she has owned since she was twenty-two, and she lays the roll along the taped edge, burnished, and pulls, and it comes off in one go.

---

And it takes the corner of her own handwriting with it.

And that is the end of the paper.

She has got a gas envelope with about half of the top left corner gone, and on the back of it there is a smear where her own room list has been half lifted off the envelope, and it is not clean, and you cannot read the corner of it, and the rest of it is perfectly legible.

---

And she turns it over and she looks at the side that is not hers, and it is the first time she has looked at those six lines with intention since the Saturday morning of the nineteenth of June.

She reads it.

*the gate*

*the front room*

*the window in the back room*

*the hotplate on the left*

*the bath tap*

*the passage light*

---

And she reads the back as well, and it is her own, and it is:

*back room — eight boxes, put back, nothing taken*

*hall — four boxes, loft*

*box room — two boxes, curtains off the rail*

*bathroom — one box, keep the green tiles*

*landing — four boxes, strip the stair*

*hoarding, thurs, eight*

---

And here is the thing that happens on that step, and it takes her about four minutes, and it is the whole of that morning.

She cannot use that piece of paper again.

That is the first thing. It is a practical fact and she gets to it in about ten seconds and she gets to it cold, and it is the reason the tape was torn and not cut, and the reason she put it there in the first place is the reason she cannot have it back.

If she writes on it again, the woman of seventy-eight's handwriting goes under hers. If she keeps it, it is in her van. If she puts it in a skip, a woman's list of six things goes in a skip.

And there is nowhere on this earth she can put a piece of paper that is somebody else's and her own at the same time.

---

And the second thing, which takes longer, is the order.

She sits on that step with the thing in her hands and she gives it the twenty minutes it deserves and she cannot make any sense of it and she says so out loud, in a garage, on the far side of the ring road, to nobody.

And she says, "Well, that's not an order."

---

Here is what she can work out, and she is a woman who reads a house for a living, and it takes her about ten minutes and it is the first honest thing anybody has said about that list.

She can work out that four of those six are the sort of thing a woman of seventy-eight on her own could get done by having one person in her kitchen who knew things. A window. A tap. A light. A stove.

She cannot work out why any of them is in the position it is in.

And she says that out loud as well, sitting on a step.

And she says, "That's not a list of jobs. That's a list of somebody."

---

And then she gets to the gate.

She has read it four times in a kitchen off the parade, on her knees on the tiles with a clamp standing up out of the middle of the table, and she has not once got past the first line, because the first line is not a job and every time she gets to it she goes somewhere else.

She gets to it this time and stays there.

---

Here is what the gate is.

It is at the front of that house on the front step and it swings in on the wrong side and it bangs at about four in the morning when there is a wind, and the woman in her late forties stood under it on the nineteenth of June and watched it do it and said nothing about it.

And she can do a gate. She is good at gates. She has hung about forty of them.

And she has got a card in her van with a mobile number and a landline on it and a symbol at an angle on it, and the man who prints that card does gates as well as kitchens.

And she works out, sitting on a step in a garage, exactly why she is not going to ring him about a gate.

---

She is not going to, and the reason has nothing to do with the gate, and she works that out sitting on the step.

The fourth of that list is a hotplate, and he put a new element in it himself, and he was in that kitchen for the better part of a morning with the two of them. And she has not been up that road since the nineteenth of June and she is not going to now, because if she walks in there with a piece of paper in her hand then she is a person who has come about the list, and she is not that person and she cannot be that person on a Saturday morning.

And the day she does that, he will come up, and he will be in that kitchen, and he will not know what to do because she will not be there.

And she cannot be there.

And the reason she cannot be there is that she cannot be in that kitchen and in her own van at the same time, and she has to be in her own van, because that is where the tape and the brown paper and the roll are.

---

And she sits on that step and says, out loud, to a garage:

"No."

---

And here is the last of the morning, and it is about twenty to twelve, and it is the reason the morning does not go the way she came for.

The man comes into the garage with a tape on his belt and a set of keys and about four minutes, because he is here about the front room floor, which the woman who booked this house is having up, and it is a twenty-minute job and he does these all day long.

He is in his late twenties. He has been doing it four years. He is very quick and he is very good and he does not want to be here for longer than he has to.

---

And he looks at the door leaning against the wall and says, "You're not having that door."

And she says, "I'm measuring up for one."

And he says, "You don't want a door."

And she says, "Why not."

And he says, "Because that's not the door's fault. That's the hinges."

And he gets down and turns the door over on its edge and looks at the plates and says, "Those plates are fine. That's solid oak plate, that's a door that's been on a wall since the war. Two new plates, about twelve quid, and it's the same door."

---

And that is worth sixty pounds to a woman in her late forties sitting in a garage, and it is the first money back she has had off that job, and she takes the twelve pounds off the invoice and she does not tell the woman who booked it how she got it.

---

And then he says, "What's that."

And she looks down and she has got the envelope in her hand and she has not noticed that she has still got it in her hand.

And she says, "Notes."

And he says, "There's writing on both sides."

And she says, "There's writing on both sides."

And he says, "Whose is the other one."

---

And there is about two seconds in a garage where she could say anything she liked.

---

And she says, "It's mine."

And he says, "Right."

And he does not say *let me see*. He does not say *who's the other one*. He does not even say *that's a lot of notes for a job*.

He says "right" and he goes and takes up the front room floorboards and does not look at it again, and he is gone by about twenty to twelve, and he will be in four other houses this afternoon and in four other houses this week.

---

And that is the last of the not-asking, and it is the one that matters most, and it is the one that has cost the most.

Four people have now been within arm's reach of a thing somebody else has written and not one of them has asked where it came from.

A man read six lines off a kitchen table and could not ask why they were in that order.

A woman in her early thirties stood in that same kitchen for an hour and a half and was told *no* in three seconds about a gate and did not ask why, and she never saw the paper at all.

A woman in her late fifties three doors away has not gone up that road since the morning she was handed a stool by a stranger, and she could go up it any afternoon of her life.

And a man of twenty-odd in a garage on the far side of the ring road was asked, directly, by a stranger, *whose is the other one*, and said *right*, and will never know.

---

She puts the envelope in the inside pocket of her coat at about twenty to twelve.

And then she rings him at about half past one from her own kitchen.

And she says, "I've got it."

And he says, "Right."

And she says, "The envelope. With the six things on it."

And he says nothing at all for about four seconds.

And she says, "I've been in a garage on the far side of the ring road with it taped inside a door and I could not get it off without taking the top corner of my own writing with it, and that was on the Tuesday, and it has been in my coat since the Saturday of the nineteenth of June, and there is now a bit of paper in this borough that two women have written on and neither of them can use."

And he says, "Right."

And she says, "And I cannot ask her what the order of it is. That is why I am ringing you and not her."

---

And then there are about four seconds on the telephone in her own kitchen.

And he says, "What order would you do them in."

And she says, "I don't know. That's exactly it. I couldn't work that out on my knee with a clamp on and I couldn't work it out on a step in a garage and I can't work it out now."

And there is about two seconds.

And he says, "Bring it round Friday."

And she says, "What for."

And he says, "Because I can't read anybody's handwriting off a description either, and because you can't put a list of six in a drawer and get the fourth one done, and because you are going to walk in there on Friday and give her a piece of paper you have had in your coat since the nineteenth of June and you are going to have to say where you got it."

---

**SHE WENT BACK TO PRICE A CUPBOARD DOOR AND CAME AWAY WITH A PIECE OF PAPER THAT TWO WOMEN'S HANDS ARE ON AND NEITHER OF THEM CAN NOW USE, AND A MAN OF TWENTY-ODD WAS ASKED WHOSE THE OTHER SIDE WAS AND SAID *RIGHT*, AND SHE HAS RUNG THE ONE PERSON IN THIS BOROUGH SHE WAS NOT GOING TO RING AND ASKED HIM WHAT ORDER SIX THINGS SHOULD BE DONE IN, AND HE HAS SAID BRING IT ROUND FRIDAY.**

(End of chapter 0653)
