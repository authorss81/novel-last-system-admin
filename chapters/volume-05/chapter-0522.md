# Chapter 0522 — Nine Minutes

The Sunday of the sixth of October 2019, and the only part of it that belonged to anybody was the half hour from a quarter past ten. One building in this borough, in Tideglass.

**ON A WEDNESDAY IN MARCH A GIRL OF FIFTEEN ON A PLACEMENT WAS STANDING UP CRYING AT THE FAR END OF AN OFFICE AND A WOMAN TOOK HER OUT, AND THAT WOMAN HAS TOLD IT ONCE AND A MAN HAS TOLD IT FOUR TIMES, AND THE TWO VERSIONS OF IT DISAGREE ABOUT WHAT SHE WAS CARRYING AT THAT MOMENT, AND NEITHER CAN GET AT THE OTHER. ON THE SIXTH OF OCTOBER THEY SHARED A ROOM FOR NINE MINUTES AND SHE SAID ONE SENTENCE.**

They work in the same building and they have worked in the same building since 2013 and nothing has ever grown between them, and in six years they have never once had a conversation that was not about the work.

Her name is a thing this account does not set down. So is his. They are a woman of about thirty-four and a man of about fifty-one, and both of those figures are hers and his alone, and neither of them is anybody anybody else in this borough can describe afterwards.

---

Here is her account, and she has given it once.

She gave it on the last Sunday in March, at a lunch, to a woman she sits near, and she gave it in about a minute and a half, and she has not mentioned it to anybody since.

There was a girl of fifteen on a placement in that office from the second of March. On the Wednesday the nineteenth the girl was at the far end with a stack of files on a low stand and the girl was crying, silently, standing up, with her hand over her mouth, and nobody else in that office had noticed for about a minute.

She noticed. She went down the length of that office and she said, in an ordinary voice, "Go and get your coat, we're going out." And they went out to the shop together and they stood outside it for about six minutes and the girl stopped crying, and they came back in, and the girl sat down at the far end and got on with the files, and at the end of that day the girl's manager came over and asked her why the girl had gone out at ten in the morning, and she said the girl had a headache and she had walked her down to the air.

What she was carrying at the time, according to her, was nothing. Her hands were in her pockets. She had been at her own screen.

Here is his account, and he has given it four times.

He gave it the first time at the end of March to two men in a car park and the second and third times in April and May to a succession of people who have since moved on, and the fourth time in July to a woman who has since stopped speaking to him about anything else, and he has never given it to her.

There was a girl of fifteen on a placement in that office from the second of March. On the Wednesday the nineteenth the girl was at the far end with a stack of files on a low stand and the girl was crying, silently, standing up, with her hand over her mouth, and nobody else in that office had noticed for about a minute.

He noticed. He went down the length of that office and picked up the phone on the way past her own desk, and spoke into it, standing up, for about half a minute, and then he put the phone down, and then he said to her, "You'd better take her out," and she said, "Take her out?"

And he said, "She's been stood at that end for a quarter of an hour and nobody's said anything to her."

And she said, "I hadn't noticed."

And he said, "Well, you'd better take her out, hadn't you."

And she said, "I was just going to."

And he said, "I know you were," and went back to his own desk, and she went out with the girl.

What she was carrying, according to him, was a bundle of files, about the thickness of a house brick, that she had taken off her own desk to have done while she waited for the girl to stop.

Neither of them has ever been able to get at the other account.

There is no copy of it. There is nothing in that office in writing. The girl has gone to college in another town and has not been asked about it and will not be asked. The manager of that office has said that in his recollection nothing happened on the nineteenth of March, which is true, because nothing happened in front of him.

And the two accounts cannot both be right, and there is no way of settling it that does not involve going to a girl of fifteen in another town and asking her to remember a Wednesday in March.

Here is the six of October.

Neither of the two had anything to do with why they were in that space at all. The room is at the bottom of that building and it is booked out from a list on a board in reception, and on the sixth of October it had been booked from a quarter past ten until half past twelve by two people who work on a different site and who were not there for the first half hour of it because they were held up somewhere else.

And the woman and the man had both been told to be in it at half past ten because it is the room that the other side always says to use, and they both had nothing to do before twelve, and so they were both in it.

They have been in that room together twice before, in six years, and both times was for over an hour and both times there was somebody else in it.

Nine minutes. He has since worked out that it was nine minutes because there is a railway thing on that wall that anybody in that building can read from the far end, and he looked at it when he came in and he looked at it when she spoke, and the two readings were nine minutes apart, and he has not told anybody that either.

She was standing at one end with her back to the wall. He was at the other end with a folder he had brought down and had not wanted.

At about four minutes in, she said, "Do you want the bigger one for the twelve o'clock."

And he said, "I don't know. I don't book it."

And she said, "I don't either."

And nothing happened for about two minutes.

And then she said, "I wasn't on the phone."

And he said, "No."

And she said, "On the nineteenth of March I wasn't on the phone and I wasn't carrying anything. I had my hands in my pockets and I'd been at my own screen."

And he said, "Right."

And she said, "I know what you said."

And he said, "I know what you said too."

And she said, "That's the only thing I came down here to say."

And he said, "All right," and glanced up at the thing on the wall, and then said, "Right."

And that is all of it. She said one sentence, and it ran to more than one, and he has not worked out since which stretch of it was the sentence.

The two people came in at twelve and the meeting began and both of them were in that room for fifty-one minutes, and the nineteenth of March was not touched once in any of those fifty-one minutes, and the business of the meeting was got through in fifty-one minutes and will not need doing again until May.

And on the way out she went ahead of him and he let her, and she held the door and he said, "That's all right," and she said, "It's nothing," and those were the only words the two of them exchanged in fifty-one minutes.

And the thing neither of them can do anything with is this.

She has spent a hundred and sixty-five days knowing that there are four accounts of one Wednesday in that building and that hers is the shortest and the only one given once.

She has known since the twenty-fourth of April that the other one is out there, and she worked that out not because anybody told her but because a man she sits near said one evening that a man in that office had been telling a story about a girl on a placement, and she did not ask which story, and she sat with it for a week and then two.

He has spent two hundred and one days knowing that she has not asked him, and that she has not once referred to it, and that if she had heard any of it she would have said one sentence and that there is no version of this in which she does not know that he has told it four times.

And now he knows that she knows, and she knows that he knows that she knows, and neither of them has any idea at all whether she has heard any of it or how many times it has been told, and that is the part the nine minutes did not settle, and it is the only part either of them came down there for.

**SHE SAID ONE SENTENCE IN THAT ROOM ON THE SIXTH OF OCTOBER AND IT RAN TO MORE THAN ONE, AND HE HAS SINCE DEDUCED FROM THE RAILWAY THING ON THE WALL THAT IT TOOK NINE MINUTES, AND HE HAS NOT TOLD HER THAT HE DEDUCED IT, AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS ANY IDEA HOW MANY TIMES HE TOLD IT.**
