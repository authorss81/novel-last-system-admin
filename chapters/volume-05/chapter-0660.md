# Chapter 0660 — The Room With The Thing In It

Saturday the fourteenth of August 2021, in this borough. She is in that house from about eight in the morning until the light goes off the front room, and she has been in that house every day since the Monday, and there is no other person in it on any of those days, and the whole of it is a wire brush that has been on a sideboard since the Saturday before and a room she does not go into.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT FINDS A THING ON A SIDEBOARD IN HER FRONT ROOM THAT SHE DID NOT PUT THERE AND CANNOT ASK ANYBODY WHAT IT IS FOR, AND SHE CANNOT PUT IT IN THE BIN BECAUSE SHE DOES NOT KNOW WHAT A THING IS BEFORE YOU BIN IT, AND SHE CANNOT CARRY IT INTO THE PASSAGE BECAUSE IT WOULD BE A THING SHE COULD BE ASKED ABOUT, AND SHE PUTS IT BACK ON THE SIDEBOARD, AND FROM THAT DAY SHE DOES NOT SIT IN THE FRONT ROOM, AND THE FRONT ROOM IS THE ONLY ROOM ANYBODY HAS EVER SAT IN.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is on her own all day. Nobody knocks.

---

She finds it on the Monday morning, and it is the Monday, and the finding takes about four seconds.

She has gone into the front room for the newspaper that comes on a Sunday and does not go there on a Monday, and the Monday she went in there because the light was better in there at eight in the morning than it is in the kitchen, and that is the entire reason and it is not a good one.

And there is a wire brush lying on the sideboard.

Ash handle. About nine inches. A crimp of stiff steel, splayed at the ends, and a curl of something in the bottom of the crimp the colour of a bit of old egg, and a smear of grey dust on the handle where somebody's hand was.

It is lying on its side on the sideboard with the handle pointing at the door.

---

And here is what she does about it, and it is worth setting down in order, because the order is the whole of her and it takes her until dark.

First: she is not frightened.

She stands in the front room doorway with the brush in front of her and the first thing in her head is not fear and it is not *who's been in my house*. It is the door.

The door is not on the latch. The door has not been on the latch since before Christmas, and there has not been a night since before Christmas when she has got up and gone to the front door to see whether it was shut, because you do not get up and go and look at a door that is not on the latch.

And so the first thing she works out, standing in that doorway on the Monday morning, is that somebody has been in her front room.

---

And that is as far as she gets with it, and she does not get any further for the rest of the day, and it is not a gap in her thinking. It is a wall.

Because the next thing is the question, and the next thing after the question is the answer to the question, and she has nobody to put the question to.

She cannot ring anybody. She does not have anybody to ring. The number for a man in his early forties is in a phone in a kitchen off a parade and she has not rung it since before Christmas and she is not going to start. The woman in her early thirties is three doors down and knows everything about that man and has not asked her one question since May.

And the woman in her late forties is not a person she can put a question to about a brush.

---

So she stands there.

And the brush lies on the sideboard, and it is nine inches of ash and a crimp and a bit of old egg, and there is nothing else in the world she wants to know about as much as she wants to know about that, and there is no way of getting at it that does not involve saying a sentence out loud to another human being, and there is no other human being.

---

Second: she tries to work out what it is for, on her own, standing up, for about half an hour.

And she cannot do it.

It is not a painting brush. She has had one of those in a house for fifty years and it has soft bristles and it is not stiff and it is not steel and it would not last a fortnight of washing up.

It is not a dish brush. She can see that. It has not been near a plate in its life.

It is not for a fire. She has not got a fire. She has not got anything with a fire in it since before the war and she does not want one.

And the thing it is actually for, whatever it is, is a thing that has to do with paint, or with a gate, or with wood, and she has got none of those, and she has not had any of those since before Christmas, and that is where the working out stops.

---

Third: the bin.

And this is the one that takes her until the middle of the morning, and it is the one she will think about again afterwards, and the reason she will think about it again is that she knows it is true and she cannot see how she knows it.

You do not know what a thing is before you bin it.

That is not a rule anybody taught her. It is just true, and it is true the way a step is lower than the one above it. You cannot decide a thing is rubbish until you have looked at it long enough to know what it is, and if you cannot tell what a thing is, then you cannot bin it, and if you cannot bin it, then it is not rubbish, and if it is not rubbish, then somebody brought it.

She stands in her own kitchen with a bag of household rubbish in her hand and works that out twice, from the beginning both times, because it is the only decision available to her and she is going to be held to it for the rest of the day.

She puts the bag down. She has not put the brush in it. She would have had to walk it out through the front room to do it, which is another thing, and she does not do the other thing either.

---

Fourth: the passage.

And the passage is where a thing goes. That is what a passage is. There is a socket on that wall by the door for the vacuum cleaner and there is a shelf inside the front door with about nine inches of clear space on it, and a brush is nine inches long, and the shelf is nine inches deep.

And she stands in the passage and looks at that shelf for about two minutes.

And the reason it does not work is not the size of it. The reason is that a thing on a shelf in a passage is a thing in the way.

Somebody comes up that path — and somebody always comes up that path, and she has not been able to stop it and would not want to — and a thing on a shelf in a passage is a thing they see on the way in. And then they will say something about it. And she will have to answer.

And she cannot answer.

---

And here is the thing about that, and it is the thing she has not admitted to herself since before Christmas, and it comes up in that passage on the Monday morning, at about the middle of the morning, while she is looking at a shelf.

She does not know how to be asked about things.

A woman who can be asked about things gets asked about things. She is a woman who cannot. Somebody knocks, and she opens the door, and whatever is said to her next, she has a face on and she can get through it because it lasts about nine seconds and the person is usually gone before it needs to be an answer.

But a thing on a shelf in a passage is a thing that is still there when the person comes back. A thing on a shelf is not a nine-second thing.

And if it is still there in a while, and they come back and see it again, then she is a woman with a thing in her passage that she cannot say where it came from.

---

Fifth: the kitchen windowsill.

And she tries it, and it is the sill over the sink, and she has got it up on the sill at about twenty past twelve, and she stands back.

And it is worse, and she takes it off the sill at about half past twelve.

It is worse because the sink is where she spends the day. That is the whole of the reason and it takes her a second to see it. She stands at that sink for a bit of bread in the morning and a cup of something in the afternoon and a sinkful of dishes at the end of the day, and a windowsill over a sink is in front of your face the entire time you are doing any of that.

And it would be there while she looked at it.

Not that it is frightening. Not that it is anybody's. She would look at it and there would be a wire brush on the sill by the plates.

And on the Saturday morning when a woman in her late forties stood at that sink with blood in the creases of both hands and washed it off with a bar of soap on a string, the sill was empty, and she knows that, and she was standing in that kitchen and she saw the sill.

---

And so it goes back on the sideboard.

And that is the end of the options. There are four places a thing can be in that house: in the bin, in the passage, on the sill, and on the sideboard. She has tried three and rejected three and the sideboard is where it is now and the sideboard is where it was on the Monday morning and it is where it will be in the dark tonight.

---

And here is the second half of the day, and it is the part that is not about the brush at all, and it starts at about one o'clock with something entirely ordinary.

The tap is dripping.

It has been dripping since before Christmas and there is a folded cloth in the bowl under it, and she empties the bowl at about one o'clock on the Monday and at about eight on the Monday night, and she has been doing that since before Christmas, and that is the fifth thing on a piece of paper in pencil on a gas envelope on her own kitchen table.

And four of the six are done.

The window in the back room is a tape sheet and a blanket folded on a mattress. The hotplate is a new element and it went off in the middle of cooking something on the seventh of July and it did not get hotter. The passage light is a bulb he fitted in four minutes on a Wednesday in June. And the gate is a gate that swings and does not come in on the path, and she has stood at her own kitchen window and looked at it and not gone out to it.

Four of six, and nothing has been crossed off.

---

And she gets the pencil out at about one o'clock.

She does not have a pencil.

She looks for one in the kitchen drawer where the string and the fuses and the tea bags live, and in the tin on the shelf, and in the pocket of the coat on the back of the front door, and on the string by the cooker where a pencil is not but a wooden spoon is.

And there is no pencil in that house.

And she wrote six things in pencil on a gas envelope in April on the table under the window, and she has not had a pencil in this house since before Christmas, and she cannot ask anybody for a pencil, and there is no shop at the end of that road.

---

And she stands in her own kitchen at about one in the afternoon with a gas envelope face up on the table in front of her, and four of the six things on the back of it are done, and she cannot put a line through one of them.

That is the whole of what a gas envelope is for. It is not for writing things on. It is for putting a line through a thing that is finished.

And she has got a piece of paper with a job on it and no way to close the job, and she has not been able to ask anybody for a pencil since before Christmas.

---

And then, at about half past two, she does the thing that decides the rest of the week.

She goes into the front room.

Not to do anything. There is nothing in the front room to do. She goes in there because it is half past two on a Monday in August and the light is in that room and not in the kitchen, and she has been doing that since the Monday.

And she stands in the middle of it with the light on the floor and the chair against the wall by the window, and the sideboard is against the far wall with the wire brush lying on it handle-first towards the door.

And she looks at that room for about a minute.

---

And then she works out what has happened, standing there, and it takes about four seconds and it is the plainest thing she has worked out since the third of June.

There is a thing in this room that she cannot identify and cannot get rid of and cannot account for.

And this is the only room in this house that anybody has ever sat in. She said so out loud, in a kitchen, in front of two people, on the Friday of the sixteenth of July, and nobody followed it up, and she has not sat in this room since the Friday of the sixteenth of July, and she did not work out why until this afternoon.

---

And the reason is not the brush.

That is the part she gets wrong for about an hour and then gets right at about half past three, and she is standing in the front room when she gets it right, and it is this.

If she sits in this room, then this is a room a person is in, with a thing in it, and she cannot say a word about the thing.

And somebody will come up that path. And they will come in. And the door is not on the latch. And they will see the front room with her in it and a brush on the sideboard, and they will have to say something, because you cannot walk into a front room past a seated woman and a thing on a sideboard and say nothing at all, and everybody in this borough has found that out.

And she will be a woman being asked about a thing in her own front room.

And she cannot do that. She has never been able to do that. She could not do it about a gas bill and she cannot do it about a brush.

---

So she does not sit in the front room.

That is the whole of what happens on the Monday, and it is the whole of what happens on the Tuesday, and it is still the case on the Saturday after that.

She eats at the table in the kitchen, under the window, with a cloth on it, on the green chair with the cushion on it, and she looks out of the kitchen window at the road and she can see the gate from there and the gate swings and does not come in, and she does not go out to it.

And there is a rush-seated chair standing against the wall in a dark room at the front of that house that nobody sits on.

And the front room is the only room anybody has ever sat in.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT WENT TO BED WITH FOUR OF SIX THINGS ON A LIST DONE AND NOTHING CROSSED OFF AND NO PENCIL IN THE HOUSE, AND A WIRE BRUSH ON A SIDEBOARD IN A FRONT ROOM THAT IS THE ONLY ROOM ANYBODY HAS EVER SAT IN, AND SHE HAS DECIDED THAT SHE WILL NOT SIT IN THAT ROOM, AND THE SECOND THING ON THE LIST IS THE FRONT ROOM, AND IT IS NOT DONE, AND IT IS WORSE THAN IT WAS WHEN SHE WROTE IT SECOND IN APRIL.**

(End of chapter 0660)
