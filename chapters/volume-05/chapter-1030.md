# Chapter 1030 — A Rail Back In Its Own Gap

Undated in this draft.

**THE SCULLERY AT NUMBER TEN HAS ITS BACK DOOR STANDING SHUT AND A GAP ALONG THE BOTTOM OF IT.**

That scullery is the little room between the back kitchen and the garden at number ten, about seven foot by five, with a stone sink under the window and a wooden sill across the doorway where the flags are, and the flags themselves run down towards that door instead of away from it. The back door is a plank door hung on two strap hinges with an unglazed light in the top corner of it that has not been glazed since before the war, and there is a gap you could get a finger into along the bottom of that door, because the bottom rail is split along its whole length and the piece of it has dropped about half an inch at the hinge side and the door shuts on nothing whatever.

The sill across that doorway stops a hand's breadth short of the jamb at the left-hand end of it, so that anybody coming in with a full pail down that step puts the water on the flags instead of over the sill, which is how the flags by that door came to be the one low place in a house that has no other low place in it.

He was in there with the door shut behind him and she came in at the back door with the garden gate going behind her and shut that, and she had her sleeves turned back.

"You have not come about my sister."

"I have not."

"Then you have come to stand in a scullery with the door shut, and the only person in this house who shuts a door on a man in a scullery is me, so get on with it."

---

"I have come to tell you two things, and the second one is the one I have come about. Your sister is going to stand in a kitchen at the back of number twelve when I begin. And I cannot begin on Monday."

"That is two pieces of news and you have given me the second one first, which tells me the first one is the one you have been carrying."

"It is."

"Then go on."

"I cannot start on Monday because that job wants two men under a range the size of hers and I am one man, and I have not got a second man, and I have not got money enough to bring one in, and this borough has nowhere at all that I can send for one." He put his hands flat on that sill behind him while he was at it. "And when I go and tell her that her sister is going to be standing in that kitchen when I begin, she is going to stand there and see a man not begin."

She did not answer for a bit. Then she went and turned the sink tap a quarter of a turn and shut it again, which is a thing a woman does in a scullery when she is thinking and has no other place to put her hands.

"That is honest. Now sit down."

"There is nowhere to sit."

"No. Now you will hear the other half of it, and I have not asked anybody for it before and I have thought about it for two days, so you will let me put it the way I have got it and you will not make it smaller."

"I want to know what my own back door costs me."

He looked at the door instead of at her.

"You want a figure."

"I want to know what it costs. You can put your hand through the gap along the bottom of it, and there is a woman who has done six weeks up a river standing in this borough who has not got a price for anything either, and it has been there since before the summer, and every time it rains a hand's breadth of that floor goes under the sink, and I have not mended it and I have not asked a single person about it in four years, and two men have come into this house in my life and neither of the two ever wanted paying and I sent both of them away, and there is nobody else at all I could go to." She kept her shoulder to the sink. "You have been into this house five times over since the spring and you have not touched a thing in it and you have not asked me for a penny, and I would rather be asked than have it done."

"That door has three things wrong with it and they are not one thing. It wants a new length of rail, because the old one is split the whole way along its length and has been since before the summer, and the jamb has to come back to take the new one. and the corner light wants reglazing after that. Two days of it with one man and a fortnight with two, and none of the three of us in this house has anybody at all to pay."

"Then what is it."

"I do not know. Not one figure in my own head in my life, and you know perfectly well that there is not, and I could go to that yard on the other side of this borough and get the two men who work there, and neither of those two would come to this house for nothing and both of them would come for money."

"You have not asked me what it costs me."

"I was coming to it. I asked you what it comes to at the back of that house in a passage and you worked it out over two days at a table on your own, and I have not been able to put a number on anything since. So I am asking you now, out loud, the same way I asked her."

---

She turned round from the sink and put the flat of her hand down on the stone beside it and left it there.

"I do not know," she told him. "I have never had to know. I have never paid anybody for anything in my life. I have had money off my mother when I was on that book and I have never had money enough to pay a man with, and if I had I would not know what the right amount was, and if I did know it I would not know what to do with the answer."

"That is a straight answer."

"It is the only one I have got and I would rather give you a straight one than a figure I have made up in a scullery to get rid of you."

He lifted the loose piece of that rail out of the gap where it had dropped, and turned it over in the light that came in off the garden door, and ran his thumb along the split from one end of it to the other and could feel where the wood had gone soft along the inside of the break. Then he put it back into the gap it had come out of, in the same position and at the same angle, because and that was all he had come into that scullery for, and it was all he was going to do in it.

She let him get as far as that gate before she caught the edge of the draining board with one hand.

"I am going to find out," she told him. "I am going to go and ask somebody what a thing like that costs a woman, and I have never done it and I do not know who to ask, and you did not put that in my head, you just walked into my scullery and asked me first, which is where all of it came from."

Standing in a scullery with a loose piece of rail back in the gap it came out of, he found that he had taught a woman in this borough to find out what a thing costs her, and when she does it there will be nothing on any page in this borough to show that he was ever in the room.
