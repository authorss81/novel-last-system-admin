# Chapter 0417 — The High End

Monday the fourth of June 2018, a yard off a side street, and a shop on that parade, starting at about a quarter to eight in the morning and running to about half past four in the afternoon, in Tideglass.

**A SHEET OF IRON CAME UP OFF THE WALL ON THAT LEAN-TO IN A WESTERLY ON THE FRIDAY NIGHT AND LET THE RAIN IN ONTO A STACK OF BOARDS, AND A MAN CAME INTO THAT YARD ON THE MONDAY MORNING FOR HIS OWN BUSINESS AND PUT IT DOWN PROPERLY, AND HE WAS NOT ASKED AND SAID NOTHING, AND THE ONLY PERSON WHO FOUND OUT WAS THE WOMAN WHO WORKS IN A SHOP, AND SHE DID NOT GO AND GET HIM.**

That yard is behind a row of six houses off a side street, and you get into it through a gate four foot wide that stands open because nothing behind it has had a gate on it since about 1990. It is about thirty foot by twenty, hardstanding for the first ten foot of it and concrete for the rest, and the boundary on the far side is the flank of a building in the next street, and that wall is about twelve foot high and it is the only good wall in that borough for doing this kind of thing to.

The woman who works in a shop uses that yard every working day. She has a key to that gate and has had it for about three years and the yard is not hers and is nobody's, and she takes her deliveries through it because it is twenty foot from the back of her shop and it is the only place a van can turn.

---

The lean-to is in the far corner of that yard and has been there since the third week of April.

A man in his thirties built it in three days for money, on a job he got off a man who owns the building behind that wall. It is about nine foot out from the wall and about nine foot high at the wall and seven foot high at the front, and it is four posts and two purlins and a wall plate bolted through into the brick, and the roof of it is nine sheets of corrugated iron about ten foot long laid up the slope with a lap over the wall and a lap at every sheet.

The three sheets nearest the wall were laid over a strip of roofing felt that was already about twelve years old when it went on, because that was what was on the site and it was a job priced by the day.

On the Friday night of the first of June there was a westerly on that side of the river for about an hour and a half, not a bad one, the sort that comes in behind a bright sky.

That sheet went up about three inch off the felt along about four foot of its length, and it stood up and slapped, and it did that two or three times, and then it came down again a foot flatter than it had been.

And rain came in behind it for two days, and there is nothing in that yard that would have told anybody about it.

He came in at the gate at about a quarter to eight on the Monday morning with his own van behind him.

He was in that yard because he is doing a shop next door and the shop has no yard of its own and there is nowhere else within a hundred yard to put a van, and he has been putting it in there for about three weeks and closing that gate behind him every time, which nobody has ever said anything about.

He was on his way in through the gate at a quarter to eight with the gate swinging and he saw the far corner before he had shut it.

There is a sheet of iron standing up off the wall on that lean-to with a gap under it about the width of two fingers, and the light was coming through that gap along the wall, so that he could see the line of it from the gate.

He stood in the yard with the gate still moving and looked at it.

Then he shut the gate and got his bag out of the van and did not go and tell anybody, and it never crossed his mind to look for the man in his thirties, or the man who owns the building behind that wall, and neither of those two is anybody in this borough could go and ask.

Here is what he did, and it took him about seventy minutes.

He got his own step ladder out of the back of that van. He got up on the top of it with his bag on the top step and took the whole of that top sheet off its fixings and lifted it clear, and the felt under the lap came away in pieces in his hand, and that is the whole reason it came up and there is no argument about that.

He raked the old felt out and cut two lengths of new out of a roll he has carried in that van for six years for exactly nothing in particular, and he dressed the wall side of the lap, and laid the new felt, and bedded a sawn length of three by one over the end of the sheet with two coach screws, and put the sheet back on the roof and drove it back on four fixings, and put three more in at the end where it had been standing up, because four was what it had and six is what it wants.

And then he looked along the rest of that roof, and the other three sheets were all in the same state, and their laps had all been sitting on the same perished felt, and he was already up the ladder, so he took those three off as well and did them the same way and bedded a second length of three by one along the whole of that head of the roof in one go.

He came down that ladder at about ten to ten, and he swept the pieces of old felt off the hardstanding into a sack and put the sack in his own van, and he put the ladder in his own van, and he put his bag in the front of that van, and he went and did a job that had nothing to do with any of it and never mentioned any of it again to anybody in this borough.

There is nobody in this borough who knows he was on that ladder.

The woman who works in a shop came through that gate at about ten past ten on the Monday with a sheet of paper in a tube under her arm.

She had been in that yard every working day for three years and she can see that whole lean-to from the door of her shop without turning her head.

She saw the stack of boards against the wall first. Those boards belong to the man who owns the building behind that wall, and they have been there since the February under a piece of hardboard, and about six of them had gone dark on the underside, and there was a bloom coming through the white paint about eighteen inch above them and running down two courses in a dark teardrop each.

She stopped about ten foot into that yard and looked up at the roof.

There was a length of new roofing felt under the head of that roof, and a sawn length of three by one running the whole width of it about four inch in from the top, and a sheet of iron lying flat where it had been standing up, and about nine bright new heads in three rows.

She stood there for about two seconds.

She worked out who it was in about two seconds and she did not have a single thing to do about that, and she did not go and get him, and he was in a shop off that parade at that minute buying a length of something and could not have come for about an hour anyway, and she has not gone and got him and is not going to. And then she looked at the far end, which is the end of that lean-to at the other side of the yard, and which she looks at every day, and which is where the water always comes in.

---

That far end is a return wall about seven foot high where the lean-to meets the next boundary, and it is hard against a fence and there is about four foot of dead ground in the angle with nothing on it.

The lean-to falls toward the yard, at about one in eight, which is right and which he has not touched.

And the concrete in that angle is about three inches higher than the head of the fall, and it has been higher than the head of the fall since the lean-to went up in April, and it was put like that by whoever laid it, and nobody has ever looked at it.

So the water that comes off that roof does not leave. It goes to the low point in that angle and stops there, and runs back under the bottom of the iron and along the foot of that return wall for about nine foot, and comes down the inside of it.

There is a bloom on that return wall about five foot up and about a foot across, and it is a different colour from the one over the boards, and it is not new.

It is in photographs of that yard that she took in the April on her own phone, and she has never shown them to anybody and nobody has asked her for them.

She looked at it on the Monday and she knew, standing there, that it had been there in the April, because she had put her own hand flat on that wall herself in the April and had said out loud, to nobody, that there was a wet mark on it.

He was at the other end of that roof. He was at the end that had the wind on it, and he was up the ladder for seventy minutes and he did every sheet on that head the way it should be done, and he did not once go to the far end, because from where his ladder was standing that corner is behind the run of the roof and he could not have seen it if he had wanted to.

That corner is still doing exactly what it was doing in the April. The rain that comes off the top of that roof will still come down the inside of that return wall for as long as that lean-to stands there.

Three people in this borough know that the head of that roof will now take a storm. The man who made it sound did not know that anything else about that yard was not sound. The woman who works in a shop knows both halves and has not said either of them. The man in his thirties will be in that yard again in the July for something else entirely and will not go under that lean-to.

**SHE STOOD ABOUT TEN FOOT INTO THAT YARD AND LOOKED AT THE WALL AND AT THE ROOF AND WENT BACK THROUGH THE GATE WITH HER TUBE UNDER HER ARM, AND THE TOP OF THAT ROOF WILL HOLD A STORM AND THE FAR END OF IT WILL NOT, AND BOTH OF THOSE HALVES WENT OUT WITH HER AND NEITHER OF THEM WAS SAID.**
