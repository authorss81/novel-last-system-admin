# Chapter 0298 — A Hundred and Twenty-Four Pounds

Saturday the eleventh of February 2017, from about half past eight in the evening, a hall off a parade in Tideglass.

**A SHEET CAME UNDER THE DOOR OF THAT HALL IN THE MORNING AND HAD A FIGURE ON IT AND TWO BOXES AT THE FOOT, AND SOMEBODY WROTE A NAME IN THE FIRST OF THEM, AND THE NAME WAS NOT A PERSON IN THAT ROOM.**

The hall off the parade used to be a shop and the counter at the front of it does not go anywhere now. There is a run of about thirty feet of clear floor behind it and then tables to the back wall, and the gas meter is on the outside wall of the porch, in a metal case with a hinged flap, and the woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall pays for that gas out of her own pocket and has done since about the summer, and most weeks there is a pound in it and some weeks a pound and a bit.

The card with a letting agent's firm on one face and a mobile number on the other is still on that table, and it has been there since the Wednesday before Christmas. The sheet with two hundred and sixty pounds on it went into the bin in January and the room knows that. The sheet with a hundred and fifty-five pounds on it has been lying face up on that table since the twenty-fourth of January and nobody has turned it over.

On the Wednesday morning of this week there was a fourth thing on that table, which is that a sheet of A4 had come under that door.

---

The man in his sixties was in that hall at about half past six in the morning because the woman with the key had asked him on the Monday to look at the outside light, which has not worked since about 2011, and had told him on the Monday that if a man were going to come and the meter were running, the light was the thing.

The sheet was on the doormat inside the door, face up and dry and not folded.

At the top of it was a firm's name, and it is not the firm on either of the other two sheets, and there was a reference and a date, and set against the words *emergency lighting and external luminaire* was a figure of a hundred and twenty-four pounds.

Under that were three lines. The first said that the work could not be carried out until a named person had given an account of the installation. The second said the account was to be in writing. The third said the account and the work were to carry the same date.

The bottom half of it was empty apart from a printed line along the foot with two boxes on it, the first for a name and the second for an account.

He read it standing up in the dark with the strip fitting over his head switched off, and then he put the light on at the switch by the door and read it again, and then he put it in his pocket and went and opened the shop next door and stood in the passage between that shop and this one for about twenty minutes, and then came back and shut the door and went home.

The sheet was on the table at about half past eight that Saturday night, face up, beside the other one.

---

There were nine of them in there. The man from the parade was in early. The man in his forties arrived in the middle of the evening with nothing in his pocket, which was a change. The man in his sixties was holding a cup of tea and the sheet. The man who works nights was at the door in his coat and had been standing there about ten minutes without taking it off. The woman of about fifty-five came in about a quarter past eight. The woman who works in a shop had her chair turned round so that her back was to the window.

"That came in under the door," said the man in his sixties, and put it down, and took his hands off it.

The man in his forties read it standing up at the end of the table, and then read the other one.

"A hundred and twenty-four," he said.

"For the light," said the man in his sixties.

"There's an emergency light over that door," said the woman who works in a shop, "and it has been on that battery since before I started coming."

"Then that is what wants testing," said the man in his forties. "Anything with a battery in it wants testing once a year, and those two battens over the foot of the stair want looking at every time somebody walks past them, and that outside light has been out since 2011, and there is your hundred and twenty-four."

Nobody said the word emergency out loud.

"So it's not a new thing," said the man from the parade. "It's the same paper twice with a different firm's name on the top of it. You get a sheet, you get a number, you get a line at the bottom, and there is no room in it anywhere for a person."

"It's a different firm," said the man in his forties.

"It's a different letterhead on the same paper," said the man from the parade.

The woman of about forty-two with the key had been at the door through the whole of that with her hand still in her coat pocket where her keys are, and then she came round the table and looked at the foot of the sheet.

"There are two boxes on that," she said.

"There are two boxes on that," said the man in his forties.

"And a name goes in the first one."

"A name goes in the first one and an account goes in the second," said the man in his forties. "And I know what you are about to say, so have it and get it over with."

She took the pencil off the ledge behind the counter. It is a stub with about an inch and a half of lead in it and the wood is chewed at one end.

She wrote in the first box the name that is over the door of the shop next door, in capitals, the way it is over the door, and she put nothing at all in the second box, and she set the pencil down on the table beside the sheet.

Nothing was said about it for a moment.

The man in his forties looked at what was in the box, and then at the counter, and then at the wall on the far side of the passage where the shop next door has its own meter, and he did not tell her she had no business speaking for that shop, and she stood there without a sentence with two halves in it.

"You know what that does," said the man in his sixties from the end of the table, in the voice he has. "It does not put a light in that porch. It puts a light in that porch on that shop's account, and the invoice goes to that shop, and the landlord of that shop keeps itself to a town sixty miles distant and has never once come to look at it, and the shop has got a company on the deeds of it, so the man from this firm writes to a company, and a company pays a hundred and twenty-four pounds for a light and has never been in this room, and this room gets a light and nothing on it."

"I know what it does," said the woman of about forty-two. "I have put that shop's name on a piece of paper and I shall not be doing it again."

The man in his sixties picked his tea up, drank it, and said that it was the wrong name and the right idea, which is a thing two people in a room can say to each other.

"That lad was in here on Tuesday night," said the woman of about forty-two to the man in his forties, without turning round, and she tipped her chin up the room the way she does.

"Lad," said the man in his forties, and looked over.

"He is the one who works nights," said the woman of about forty-two. "He was stood out in that yard looking at the side of a bath when I came past the gate on my way in at about seven, and he had his coat open, and he had been in since about six."

The man who works nights had his cup in both hands and did not take his coat off and did not put it on, and the room went on with what it was doing for a minute and a half, and the man in his forties did not ask the woman of about forty-two anything about having said it, and she did not explain it, and the man who works nights was not asked by anybody whether that was all right, and he drank his tea and left at about half past nine with his coat on and let himself out with a key out of a pocket of it.

The light over the middle of the table went at about ten and the two lights at the far end of it stayed on, and the woman of about fifty-five said she was going up the road for a bit of the fresh air, which is a thing she says, and the man in his forties said the two would last till about midnight.

**AND THE SHEET WAS ON THAT TABLE AT MIDNIGHT WITH A SHOP'S NAME IN THE FIRST BOX AND NOTHING IN THE SECOND, AND THE OTHER SHEET WAS BESIDE IT, AND OVER ON CARROW LANE THERE IS A BOILER SITTING IN A CHAPEL THAT HAS NOT BEEN LIT SINCE SOMEBODY DECIDED AGAINST IT, AND IT IS NOT ANYBODY'S TO GIVE AWAY AND NOBODY IN THAT ROOM WANTS IT.**
