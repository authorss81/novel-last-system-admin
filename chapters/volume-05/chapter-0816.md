# Chapter 0816 — Where The Colour Comes Up

Wednesday the thirty-first of May 2023, in her own back yard at number ten for most of that Wednesday, and a strip of gravel path that has gone over with moss is the whole of it.

**SHE HAS A STRIP OF GRAVEL PATH ABOUT TEN FEET LONG IN HER OWN BACK YARD AT NUMBER TEN THAT HAS GONE OVER WITH MOSS SINCE THE AUTUMN AND SHE MEANS TO TAKE THE MOSS OFF IT AND GET THE STONE BACK UP, AND HER SISTER COMES IN AT THE FRONT DOOR EARLY, HEARS THE FORK GOING AT THE BACK, AND GOES OUT AGAIN THE SAME WAY.**

Number ten, that back yard. It is about thirty feet deep and narrower than that at the bottom, and the path goes up the middle of it from the step under the back door to the gate at the far end, and it is gravel about two inches deep over a base that somebody laid in the year the extension was done, and there has never been a weed in it and there has never needed to be one.

She is on it the same morning with a garden fork, a wooden rake with iron teeth, a builder's sieve about eighteen inches across, a stiff broom and a barrow. She has done this to three other paths in her own life and she does it in the same order every time, which is the near end first and the far end left until last, because the far end is the end you are standing on when you find out how much of it there is.

The moss is not on the path. That is the first thing and it is the whole of what makes the job different from raking a path.

It has gone through the stone and gripped it, so the top inch of that path is not stone with moss on it any more, it is a mat about an inch thick of moss and dead grass and the grit bound together by both, and it peels up in pieces the size of a dinner plate if you put a fork into it and lift.

She starts at the gate end because it is the end nobody walks on first.

She gets the fork in about three inches and leans back on it and the mat lifts, and it comes away in one piece about the size of a shovel, and she turns it over on the path and shakes it and the loose grit falls out of it and what is left in her hand is a mat of green and grey with the small stones still caught in it.

That is the job. Ten feet of path and a mat about an inch thick over the whole of it, lifted in pieces and shaken and the stone dropped back on the path and the mat carried to the edge of it.

It is quicker than it sounds and slower than it looks. A fork lifted mat and a fork's width of path behind it, all the way up the yard, is about a foot of path and a good deal of stooping, and she does the width of it in about half an hour and does it again on the other side and then she has a yard of that path done and the rest of it not.

She does not look at the rest of it.

She looks at what she has done.

---

The stone in the first yard of that path is brown. That is the stone. It is a brown and buff grit, angular, and it has been under a mat for seven months and it comes out of the mat damp and clean and the colour comes up on it like the colour comes up on a wet stone.

Past the end of that yard the stone is grey.

Not dirty grey. Grey the way a stone goes when it has been in the dark and has not been wet through for a long time, and she rakes a bit of it about with the back of the fork and looks at it, and it is a different stone from the stone at the gate end.

That is where she stops.

The line runs across that path where the gravel stops and the base starts, and the base is not gravel. It is the stuff that was put down before the gravel, and it is a fine grey-brown material with no stone in it at all, and a fork pushed into that does not come back out clean, it comes back out as a plug and turns the whole thing to butter in the hand.

She puts the fork in at the line and pushes it hard, and it goes in about six inches and comes up as a plug.

That is the test and it is the same test every time, and it is why nobody scarifies a path by the depth of the fork and everybody scarifies a path by the look of the stuff that comes up.

A gravel path is a skin. Under the skin is not more of the same thing, it is a different thing entirely, and the job is to take the skin back to where the stone is and no further, and a woman who puts a fork in the base has not dug deeper, she has started a job with a different ending.

She puts the line back with the rake and takes her fork out of it.

The reason she is out on it at all is not that it looks bad. It looks perfectly fine from the kitchen window. The reason is that she put her heel down in April and went through the skin into the base and came up with grey stuff on the sole of her boot, and a path you can go through with a heel is a path that has stopped being a path and started being a mat, and everything about it works downhill from there.

The next rain is what finishes it. Moss on a gravel path does not kill the stone, and it does not starve it, and it binds the surface together better than a rake does. What it does is keep the surface wet from underneath as well as from above, because a mat of moss and dead grass is a blanket, and a blanket over a path keeps the base damp in a way a bare path never is, and damp base under stone is how a path goes to a slurry in a wet fortnight and takes the edge of the shed with it.

She knows all of that and none of it stops her getting the fork in, because getting the fork in is the part that can be done and the rest of it is a plane tree on the far side of the fence that has been dropping leaves on that side of the yard since before she came.

She has a barrow and a sieve and a fork and she has about four hours of light and that is the whole of what she has brought to it with.

---

The rest of the Wednesday is putting back.

She goes over the whole ten feet twice more, the second time across rather than up, because a path goes over in patches and one pass in one direction only finds the patches that lie in that direction.

She sieves as she goes. The sieve goes on the path and she rakes the loosened stuff across it and what stays on top goes in the barrow and what falls through is the stone, and it comes out of the sieve damp and brown and clean, and it goes back where it came out of.

The stuff that goes in the barrow is about a wheelbarrow and a half in the end and it is moss and dead grass and a lot of small stone and a certain quantity of silt, and it does not smell of anything in particular and it is not going to rot down into anything anybody wants.

She puts it in a heap at the bottom of the yard, against the boundary, out of the way of the shed, where she cannot get a barrow to it without turning the barrow round.

It is a heap and it will be a heap on that spot until it rots, and it will not go into anything, and there is nobody in that borough who takes a heap of moss and a barrow of silt.

And that is the whole of what that Wednesday is about.

What she puts back is not quite what came out, and that is true of the whole of it and worth saying plainly.

In the middle of the run, where the moss was thickest and where the mat had gone right down into the gravel and taken the top of the base with it, she had to go deeper than she wanted to. Not far. About half an inch into the base at the worst of it, in two patches about a foot across, and the fine grey stuff off the top of the base came up mixed with the grit and went back into the sieve and through the sieve with the rest of it.

Fine stuff through a sieve with an eighteen-inch mesh does not stay under the sieve. It goes through with the water and out of the bottom of it, and what goes back on the path is stone and the grey base mixed through it rather than stone on top of a base.

That is why the middle of that path is a different colour from the ends of it.

Where she scarified to the stone and no further, the stone came back on top and it is brown and it is the colour it has always been. Where she went half an inch too deep in two patches, the grey has come up into the top of the path and the middle of that run is a paler stripe about two feet wide from one edge of it to the other, and it is not going to go back to brown, because once the base is mixed up through the gravel on top of it nothing in that path will ever put the colour back.

It is a good path. She walks it with a heel on it and there is no soft place in it anywhere, and it is a foot better than it was at the gate end that morning. And there is a stripe down the middle of it that she put there herself, and it is going to be there for as long as that path is there.

She puts a little more of the sieved stone over the two patches, about an inch, because a path wants its skin and she had taken too much of it, and the stripe goes a little quieter under the extra stone and does not go away.

Her sister calls out from the house about the middle of the afternoon.

"Are you going to be out there all day?"

"Not all of it," she calls back. "Two hours, maybe."

And nothing else, and the fork goes on going up and down the path behind her, and her sister does not come out and does not ask to be helped and does not come out again.

She rakes the whole thing level at the end and sweeps it with the stiff broom, and the broom brings up a line of fine stuff along the edge where the path meets the grass, and she rakes that back in.

Then she puts the fork and the rake and the sieve and the barrow away, and she stands in the gate with her back to the yard and looks at the path from the gate end the way anybody would look at it.

It is brown at the ends and a paler grey stripe in the middle, and it is not a mat any more, and the stones of it are showing.

The heap of moss is against the boundary at the bottom of the yard where she put it, and it is not going anywhere.
