# Chapter 0792 — The Strip In The Head Of The Door

Sunday the twelfth of February 2023, in this borough. He is at the back of his own unit with the door of the store shut, from the middle of the day to the end of it, and the gap along the head of that door is the whole of it.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES HAS A QUARTER OF AN INCH OF DAYLIGHT ALONG THE HEAD OF HIS STORE DOOR AND MEANS TO FILL IT, THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN PUTS A LENGTH OF BRUSH WEATHER STRIP INTO THE HEAD OF IT AND SCREWS THE STOP DOWN ON IT, AND THE DOOR SHUTS WITH NOTHING COMING THROUGH IT, AND INSIDE A MONTH HE STOPS SHUTTING IT, BECAUSE THE STORE IS ONLY EVER OPENED AND NEVER SHUT.**

He is in his early forties, and the woman from two doors down comes through the roller door in the middle of the morning, which is the only roller door in that row anybody comes through.

---

The unit is cold from the floor up and he works in a coat. The roller door is up to its bracket and there is grit along the rail of it that nobody has been at since the autumn. Through the doorway the gate stands open across the gap in the yard with its latch hooked back over it, and the chalk on the leaf has gone down to two marks and a bare square, and the post it hangs off leans at the height of a man's hip when you push it, and the frame it is supposed to catch on has nothing on it whatever. In the middle of the grass there is the hole he filled in, and the fill is standing up out of it like a lid. At the back the cowl sits on its air brick and the brick under it is dark the whole way from the floor. A length of galvanised pipe goes into the side wall and comes to nothing, and that wall knocks hollow about the width of a hand behind where it was packed. The drawer under the bench is shut with the bagged brush down on the bottom of it. There are three tins of paint standing on a board against the wall and two of them have gone hard, and a coil of hose is hung on a nail with the hole the nail used to be in behind it.

The door of the store is shut and has been shut since January.

He got a new bottom rail into it then, and it shut on that rail and did not have to be lifted, and there has been a quarter of an inch of daylight at the head of it ever since — from one stile to the other, the whole width of it — and his hand goes into that gap flat to the second knuckle. He put a strip of newspaper into it in January to show somebody. It is still in his coat pocket.

That gap is the whole of what is wrong with that door.

His plan is a fill.

He means to take that quarter of an inch off the top of the head with a chisel and a bolster, get the head of the frame down by the thickness of a thumb where the leaf closes against it, and shut the door on it. There is a scrap of wood on the bench end and a chisel with it, and the door has been off its hinges twice in his life and can easily be taken off a third time.

---

"So you are taking that head down."

"I am taking that head down."

"With a chisel." The woman from two doors down has come about twenty feet into that unit and has stopped where she can see the head of the door from the side. "You have a leaf that shut on three hinges and it shut because the top of it was up hard against the top of that opening. You take that head down a thumb's width and you have got a leaf that does not reach its own frame."

"Then it shuts on a fill."

"Then what shuts on a fill." She turns round to look at the head of it. "You will put a piece of wood in there. And a piece of wood in there is a piece of wood in there. It will be the first thing on that door to go, and it will go wet, and then the draught will come back and it will go wetter, and you will be back where you started with a chisel and a bolster and a piece of something."

"Then what am I meant to do with it."

"You are not meant to do anything to the door at all." She is already at the bench. "That gap is not a hole. It is a gap, and the thing that is made for a gap is a strip, and a strip does not take up the hole. A strip fills the hole and takes up the movement in it."

---

Then she puts a brush weather strip in the head of it.

She has had the length of it since the summer. It is about a yard long and an inch and a quarter deep and an inch and a quarter wide, and it is a length of woven brush set in a rubber back, and the rubber back has a channel in the top of it that takes a screw.

She starts it at the hinge stile end and works it along the head of the opening, and she does not cut it and butt it. She runs it past the hinge stile a couple of inches and lets it turn down the back of the jamb a little way, and at the other end she runs it past the latch stile and down the back of that, so that there is brush in the corner as well as across the top, because a draught in that store has never once come in through the middle of the head of that door. It has come in at the corner where the leaf does not shut against the frame, and she says that to him while she is doing it.

Then she turns the brush up into the channel of the rubber with the back of a screwdriver, all the way along, so that the bristles stand up against the underside of the head and stand up into the corner of the two jambs, and she screws the stop down on top of the rubber, working from the middle outwards and going back to the middle again.

"It is a strip and not a filler."

"It is a strip and not a filler, and it is a strip because that head is going to move." She puts the screwdriver down. "That leaf rides on those three hinges and it will carry on riding on them whatever you do to the frame, and if you fill that head with wood you have made a fight in it, and a strip gives and takes back."

---

The door shuts with nothing coming through it.

He draws it to and the leaf comes over the rail and into the frame and the latch drops, and it comes shut with exactly the weight it came shut with in January, and then he opens it and shuts it again four times to be certain.

Then he puts a hand up over the whole length of the head and moves it along.

Nothing comes past his hand. Not a sheet of newspaper, not a thumbnail, not a draught off the yard that is thirty feet away. The brush makes a soft noise against the head of the door as it closes, and when he pushes it to it comes over the brush and goes down and latches, and the leaf itself does not move an inch higher than it did.

"You have got about a quarter of an inch there."

"I have got about a quarter of an inch of brush there and you have got a quarter of an inch of gap there," his visitor says. "The gap is not going anywhere. You put a brush in it and the brush takes the air out of it and the gap is still a gap, and that is exactly what it is for."

---

Inside a month he stops shutting it.

It goes in slowly and it goes in without a decision anywhere in it.

The first week it is shut because it is shut. The second week the yard is wet and there is grit blowing about in it, and shutting it keeps the grit out, and shutting it is what the door is for. Then it is warm for a couple of days at the end of that month, and there is nothing in that store to shut the door against, and he does not shut it, and then it is cold again and he does not go back.

By the middle of March that door of the store is shut about once a day and that is when it rains hard. It stands open the rest of the time, with the brush along the head of it and nothing whatever in between them but a quarter of an inch of daylight, and the frost off the floor of that store comes up and stands in the open doorway in the mornings and goes back down again when the sun gets on the roof, and there is nothing in that room to be spoiled by it, because what is in that room is boards offcuts and paint and a bag of sand.

And the strip is a strip and not a filler and it does its job and it will go on doing it. Behind that yard of woven brush there is that same quarter of an inch for the whole of that winter and the whole of the one after it, and nobody will ever shut it.
