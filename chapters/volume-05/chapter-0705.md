# Chapter 0705 — The Bowl Under The Tap

Monday the twenty-first of February 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house at number twelve from first light until dark and the whole of the day is a bowl emptied at first light and at dark and a ladder seen without going in.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT EMPTIES A BOWL UNDER THE BATH TAP AT FIRST LIGHT AND AT DARK, LOOKS AT A FOLDING STEP LADDER FOLDED AGAINST THE WALL UNDER THE FRONT ROOM WINDOW WITHOUT GOING IN, AND SPEAKS AT HER OWN BACK DOOR TO A WOMAN ABOUT A METER WHO DOES NOT COME IN.**

She is a woman of seventy-eight. She is on her own all day except for a woman about a meter at her own back door at the middle of the day. She speaks to her at the door.

---

She wakes before there is any light in the passage and goes to the bathroom under the landing in her coat with the apron over it.

The bowl is where she left it at dark, under the bath tap on the white china, with the folded cloth beside it. The tap has been dripping since before Christmas, a bead that grows and lets go and grows again, and the bowl takes it. At first light it is half full and cold to the palm. She lifts it with both hands and tips it into the bath and sets it back under the tap and squares the cloth beside it.

The table in the kitchen is dead flat where the window-side foot was planed and sawn and shimmed and put back in dry card joint with no glue. She sits at it on the green chair with the cushion on it and puts her hands round a cup.

---

She eats at the kitchen table under the window and does not go into the front room.

The front room is between the kitchen and the back, and the door to it stands open on the passage. From her chair she can see the edge of the doorway and the pale wall inside. She gets up after her cup and stands in the doorway with her hands in her apron pockets and looks in.

The folding ladder is folded against the wall under the window, four treads shut true, tray on top shut down, a strip of dark tape round the second rail and a chalk mark on the third tread. It went in on the seventeenth of December, put there and squared by a woman of about thirty. It has not been opened out and it has not been moved. The rush chair is against the front wall under the window and nobody sits in it. The stool she stood on in the summer is beside the sideboard with nothing on it.

She does not go in. The second thing on the back of the gas envelope is the front room, and the front room cannot be started round a ladder and the ladder cannot be moved and there is nobody to ask to take it away, and those are three facts and they are the same fact.

---

She comes back to the kitchen and squares the cloth over the taped box on the left of the table without lifting it, corner to edge, palm flat, and sets her cup square on the right by the gas envelope.

The box is the size of two loaves, taped at every edge, twice addressed, with a gummed strip carrying a street she has never been to and a number that is not twelve. It did not move in the ten chapters before this one and it does not move in this one. On the right of the table the gas envelope lies face up, square with the edge, six things on the back of it in pencil and nothing crossed off and no pencil in the house.

She runs a finger along the edge of the cloth without picking it up. The brush is on the passage floor on the middle runner, handle across, unwashed since the first of September. She steps over it on her way to the sink, as she has stepped over it since June, and does not bend for it.

---

She goes out to the yard at the middle of the day with the bolts in her pocket.

The yard is narrow and paved, with the damp showing round the drain. The coal shed door is shut with the padlock hanging open in the hasp. She lays her palm flat on the door and feels the cold come through. The key is on a hook behind her own back door. She does not take it off the hook and does not put it in the hasp, because the shed has been shut and never locked since the twenty-second of November and opened and shut again on the twenty-first of December and that is how it stays.

The gate stands back against the coal shed wall where it has stood since the first of September. She squares her shoulder to it and looks at the line of it. Gate done, four hours on the seventh of August, drops out back, swings not come path. The two drop bolts are in her apron pocket. She puts a hand in the pocket and closes her fingers round them through the cloth and takes her hand out again.

"You stay," she says out loud to the gate. "You are done."

---

She comes back in and goes to the bathroom to look at the bowl.

It has taken an inch since first light, white china with the water trembling when she sets her palm to the rim. The folded cloth sits beside it, square. The bead grows on the lip of the tap and lets go with a small ping. She watches it do it three times and squares the cloth with one finger and goes out.

In the passage the brush lies on the middle runner, handle across, unwashed. She steps over it, as the surveyor stepped over it and the lad stepped over it and the woman with the ladder stepped over it and the man with the lamp stepped over it. Nobody bends for it. Bending for it would mean washing it, and washing it would mean asking where to dry it, and she does not ask.

She stands in the doorway of the front room again with her hands in her pockets. The ladder is folded under the window. The rush chair is against the wall. The stool is by the sideboard. All three sit in the cold. She looks at the tape round the second rail and the chalk on the third tread and thinks about the woman of about thirty who squared it there on the seventeenth of December and did not go in after it. She could have asked her whose it was. She did not. The room waits round the ladder, and the bowl waits under the tap, and both wait where they were not meant to stay.

---

At the middle of the day there is a knock at the back door.

It is a woman with a folder and a meter card, come about the meter and the reading of it. She stands on the step with her shoes clean and her folder shut. The woman of seventy-eight opens the back door and stands inside with the door wide and does not ask her in.

"I have come about the meter," the woman says. "Can I see it."

"It is under the stair," she says. "You can see it from here."

The woman steps half in and reads it and writes it down. The passage light is on over them, as it has been since June. The lamp on the bracket over the gate is on out in the daylight, as it has been since the twenty-fourth of December.

"Do you want the number," the woman says.

"I cannot write it down," she says. "I have no pencil."

---

The woman with the folder looks past her at the kitchen table and the cloth and the box.

"Is that to go," she says.

"It is to stay," she says. "It came on the twelfth of October and it stays."

"Has anybody been about it."

"Nobody has been."

The woman nods and puts the folder under her arm. She does not ask about the brush on the passage floor and she does not ask about the ladder in the front room, because she has not seen either of them. What she has seen is a sink with a cup in it and a stove that warms and does not boil and a green chair with a cushion on it.

"Will you be here if they send about the reading," the woman says.

"I am here," she says.

---

The woman goes back up the path to the gate and the gate stands back against the coal shed wall where it has stood since the first of September, with the padlock hanging open in the hasp. The two drop bolts are in her apron pocket. The key is on a hook behind her own back door. The coal shed door is shut and never locked, the only lockable building on that road.

She shuts the back door but does not put it on the latch, as it has been since before Christmas. She goes to the bathroom and looks at the bowl under the tap. The water has come up past the line the cloth leaves. She leaves it where it is. It will be emptied at dark.

---

She gets the kettle going on the stove after the meter woman goes.

The stove warms and does not boil, as it has done since the element was put in. She sets the kettle square on the left ring and stands with her hands round it while it ticks, watching the thin steam rise and fade against the cold glass. The steam comes thin and pale and stops when she lifts it, leaving the chill standing on the lino. She pours what there is into the cup and takes it to the table. The cup warms her palms through the apron.

She looks at the gas envelope while she drinks, face up, square with the edge, pencil side up, six things on the back of it in pencil and nothing crossed off. Gate done. Front room not. Window done. Hotplate done. Bath tap not. Passage done. The second thing is the front room and the fifth is the bath tap and both sit there in pencil, uncrossed, with no pencil in the house to cross them. She runs her thumb along the edge of the envelope without lifting it.

---

She spends the end of the day at the kitchen window looking out at the yard.

The yard is narrow and paved, with the coal shed at the end and the gate standing back against it. The cistern weeps at the far side of a joist in a roof space nobody has entered since the spring, and the reason she cannot go up the fixed four rungs beside the hatch is not the dark and not the top, it is that there is nobody to keep the frame while she does. She knows it the way she knows the bowl will be half full at dark, because both of them go on whether she looks at them or not.

She thinks about the ladder in the front room while she stands there, folded and squared under the window, tape round the second rail. A woman of about thirty put it there on the seventeenth of December and squared it and did not go back. She could have asked her then whose it was and did not. Asking would have meant standing in that room with that woman in it and saying the words out loud, and she would rather have a room nobody can start than say them, and that is the whole of what is wrong with her house.

---

At dark she goes to the bathroom under the landing with her hands cold and empties the bowl.

She lifts it with both hands and tips it into the bath and sets it back under the tap and squares the cloth beside it. The bead grows on the lip of the tap and lets go into the empty china with a small ping. She stands and watches it do it once and then goes out and pulls the door to.

She sits at the kitchen table under the window and puts her hands round a cold cup. The front door is not on the latch. The passage light is on. The gate lamp is on out in the dark. The four of six stays four of six and nothing is crossed off and there is no pencil. The bowl is empty and will be half full again at first light, round as a moon in white china, waiting where it was not meant to stay.

"There," she says out loud to the empty kitchen. "That is done."

The tap answers with a bead that grows and lets go.
