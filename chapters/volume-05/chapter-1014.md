# Chapter 1014 — A Woman Back Down The River

Undated in this draft.

**THE FRONT ROOM AT NUMBER TWELVE HAS ITS SASH STANDING OPEN ON THE BOTTOM RAIL.**

There is one window in the far wall of that room and it looks out over the back yards, and the sash has been down on the sill since before the summer except for the two days in the autumn when somebody wanted light in there, and it was up four inches when he came in with the range wall in his head and the coal shed in it. The rail carries no curtain and has not carried one for years, and the rail itself is a length of iron screwed to the lintel with two screws and has nothing on it, and the reveal under the sash was cut true and square when the window went in about a hundred years ago and is out of true now by the thickness of a pencil at the top corner on the left, which is what a reveal does when the sill under it goes soft and nobody cuts it back. The room has no fire in it and has not had since before the summer, and the mother came down the river before the middle of the morning and has been in the house two hours and is sitting at the table with her outdoor clothes still on.

The sister had the page on the table in front of her when he came in, not hidden and not shown, laid out flat with the last line toward the chair her mother was sitting in. She did not get up. She looked at him once, which was the whole of the greeting, and there was nothing in the look that told him anything about what she was going to do, and he has spent six weeks learning to read the two women on that frontage and he could not read her at all this morning.

The mother had come down on the morning boat with a bag and had put the bag in the passage and had not unpacked it, and there was a wet coat on the back of the door and a hat on the table beside the page and neither of them had been put where they usually go. Everything in that room was two hours old and out of place, and he stood at the window with his coat over his arm being the third thing in a front room that ought to have been the first, and a woman who has come home to a house her daughter has been running for six weeks can tell which thing in it is a visitor and which is furniture.

He put his hand on the sash and took it up its four inches and kept it up with the flat of his hand on the bottom rail while the other two of them went on, and he would not let it go while they were looking past it. The sash weighs more than it looks and it does not want to stay where it is; it comes down about half an inch of its own accord every half-minute and he took it up again each time, which is a thing a man does with a window and not a thing he does with a conversation.

---

"You have had the range out."

"The range has been out since the summer."

"You have had the wall behind it. I could see it from the step with the light on it and there is a new patch in the paint and no dust in the corner of the slab where there was a great deal of dust." She laid her hands out on the table one on each side of the page and did not touch it. "So who has been in this house while I have been away."

The daughter answered that, in a flat voice with no shape in it at all, and she did not look at him while she was doing it, and it took a good deal longer than it should have. A man had come about the range in the spring and had not done it. He had come about it again at the back end of the summer and had not done it then either, though he had told her he would. He had come about a wall at number ten in the week before she went, had not done that either, and had done a different job there instead. He had put a page of six houses on that table in his own hand and had asked for a line against a book at the lock-up and had not been given one. Then he had asked her, in this room, to put her name at the bottom of that page instead of his own, and she had got in first and asked him for it, and he had given it her.

What she did not mention, and he could tell that she did not mention it, was the chalk. There are four words on the inside wall of that coal shed in a hand that is not hers and not his sister's, low down where the lime has gone off the courses, and a woman who has been away six weeks will find them the first week she goes in there with a lamp, and there is nobody alive who could tell her what they mean except the man standing at her window. The sister knew. He could tell by the way she had not looked at him.

The mother listened to all of it with her hands where they were and her outdoor coat on and her hat in her lap, and she did not interrupt once, and at the end of it she did not look at the page. She turned her head and looked at him instead, standing at the window with his hand on the sash.

"And you cannot tell me your name."

That is what she asked. Not the wall, and not the page, and not the six houses, and not the four words in chalk on the inside of her coal shed that will be gone by the spring. She asked him the one thing in that room that nobody could answer for him, and she asked it as though it were an ordinary question about a tradesman at a door, and she waited, and he stood at that window with a sash up by its rail that would not stay where it was put, and did not answer it.

"He cannot." From the table: "He has never once put his name to anything any of us could hand to anybody. That is what he is. And I gave him my name because I asked him for it and nobody asked me for a thing in thirty-one years."

There was a version of this morning in which he gave the mother his name. It had been ready since the coal shed and it was nine words long and it would have cost him nothing at all, and the reason he did not give it is not that he does not know his own name, which he does, and it is not that nobody on that frontage could then have given it, because the woman at ten could have and would have. The reason is that a man who hands a woman his name in a front room is a man who has been asked to stay, and there is not one house in this street where he wanted to stay, and he would have had to want to, and he does not, which is the first honest thing he has managed to put about himself since the summer.

Four things in five weeks from people who could not refuse him, and one thing this morning from a woman who had every right to, and he has answered every one of them, and nobody in this borough could put any of it down as work that was asked for.

The mother turned back to the table then and looked at the page for the first time, and she read the whole of the six lines, and she put her finger on the last one and left it there a while, and the finger went down the line and did not go across it.

Nothing on the rail over that window but the wind off the back yards, and it is coming in across the floor and over the toe of a man who has just been asked the one question in that house he cannot get out of.
