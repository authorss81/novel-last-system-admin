# Chapter 0897 — Under The Bench Boards

Thursday the twenty-eighth of March 2024, in the store at the unit under the bench along the wall for as long as the door stands open on the yard. What the boards of a bench rest on.

**HE HAS A BENCH ALONG THE WALL OF THE STORE AT THE UNIT WITH BOARDS ON TRESTLES AND HE MEANS TO SLIDE THE ELBOW SIDE OF THE FOREARM INTO THE LOW SPACE UNDER IT WITH THE HAND FOLDED BACK ALONG IT AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN IS AT THE NEAR GATE FROM INSIDE WITH A BUCKET AT HER FEET.**

The bench has stood along that wall since the store was set out, and the trestles under it have been moved twice while the boards have stayed where they were put.

It is a bench of three boards about eight feet long, with the middle board a little proud of the other two, and it stands on two trestles of softwood with the feet splayed out to the room.

He wants what is under the middle of it, where the light does not reach and the dust lies thick on the floor.

Under a bench the arm tells more than the eye does, because the boards carry the work on top and the rails carry the boards below.

So the elbow side of the forearm slides into the low space under the boards, with the hand folded back along the arm to keep it out of the way.

The floor under there is concrete, cold and smooth, with grit in the joints where the broom does not reach.

The arm goes in about a foot and stops on something firm. Not the floor. A rail, running across under the boards from one trestle to the other.

He turns the forearm a little and feels along the rail with the side of the wrist. It is a length of two by one, screwed up under the boards to keep them from sagging, with the screws up through the rail and into the boards.

What the rail sits on is air. It hangs between the trestles with nothing under the middle of it, and the boards hang on it the same way.

The woman from two doors down steps to the near gate inside the yard, a bucket idle at her shoes untouched.

"Is the store open till dinner?"

"It is open till the light goes," he said, "and then the door goes shut."

"Our boiler went off in the cold snap."

"It went off in March and comes on again by April," he said.

She tipped the bucket over with her foot and set it upright again, and stood at the gate with her hands in her sleeves while the wind came round the corner of the yard, and then she carried the bucket back up the yard upside down in one hand.

---

What the arm finds further in, about two feet from the front edge, is the back rail.

Not a second rail screwed under the boards. A rail of the building, running along the wall at the foot of it, about three inches high and set back an inch from the boards.

He gets the folded hand round it and feels the top of it with the back of the fingers.

It is a skirting rail, put there when the store was lined, to stop the boards of the bench from rubbing the wall.

The bench stands an inch clear of the wall along its whole length because of that rail, and the inch of air behind it is full of shavings and cut ends that have fallen down there over years.

A bench that stands clear of the wall will take a winter without rotting at the back. Air goes up behind it and keeps the backs of the boards dry, while a bench pushed hard to the wall sweats at the back and goes soft in two years.

That is why the backs of these boards are firm and pale while the fronts are dark with oil and use.

The trestles are firm where they meet the floor, with the feet spread wide and the crossbars low, and the concrete under them is worn smooth where feet have stood to reach the high shelf.

Softwood goes soft at the foot when it stands in the wet, because water climbs up the grain from the floor and sits in the end fibres.

These trestles stand on dry concrete with air all round the feet, so the ends are firm and sharp and show the saw marks still.

Concrete sweats in spring when warm air meets a floor that has been cold all winter, and the sweat runs to the lowest corner and dries there by dinner. The sweat beads like dew on the smooth pour and never on the rough patch by the trestle feet.

That corner is by the door, away from the bench, which is why the trestle feet have stayed dry while the doorway darkens each March and pales again by May.

The gap between the two ends of the bench and the walls is about a hand wide at each, and daylight off the doorway crosses the floor under the boards and reaches the far end of it by the middle of the day and stops there, and the line of it does not move from one week to the next.

He slides the arm back out a little and feels up at the boards from underneath with the fingertips folded back.

Shavings gather behind a bench because the broom throws them forward and never gets behind, and each year adds a layer until the back rail sits in a bed of curls that keeps it dry.

The middle board is thicker than the other two by about a quarter of an inch, which is why it stands proud on top.

It is a board out of another bench, put here when the first middle board split down the middle and was taken away for firewood.

A bench made of odd boards carries its load on the thickest one, and the other two ride on it the way slates ride on a batten.

That is why the rail under the boards is screwed tight to the middle one and only just touches the other two.

The screws are slotted and gone dark with oil, with the slots full of dust packed hard in them.

A trestle with splayed feet stands firm on uneven concrete where a straight leg would rock, because the splay throws the weight out to the corners and the leg finds its own level.

These two stand eight feet apart with the boards overhanging a foot at each end, which is the most an eight-foot board will carry without dipping in the middle under a man leaning on it.

The middle board split because it was the one every vice was screwed to, and each screw broke the grain a little more until the board opened along its length in a cold snap.

Softwood splits along the grain when the iron in it moves with cold and the timber round it does not, and a board split like that will not glue back to strength.

So the split one went for firewood and the spare one came off the rack by the door, where it had stood for two years with paint on one end.

Paint on the end of a board keeps the end firm while the middle weathers, because water gets into end grain ten times faster than into face grain, and a painted end throws it off.

That is why the spare board is pale on its face and dark on its painted end, and why it will outlast the other two by a year or more.

He takes the arm out from under the bench and sights along the boards from the doorway for what the line tells.

From the front the bench looks level and true, with the three boards running straight to the wall.

From underneath it is all air and rails, with the middle board doing the work and the rail hanging on it.

One of those is a bench and the other is a frame of rails in air, and the arm can say which face will outlast the rest of them, namely the nether one where the rail hangs free and the skirting takes the damp, leaving the bench to serve another year.
