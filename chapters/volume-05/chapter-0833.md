# Chapter 0833 — The Sound Stack

Wednesday the second of August 2023, under the kitchen window at number ten from about the middle of the day until the light has gone off that wall, and six feet of dry brick that nobody is going to want afterwards is the whole of that Wednesday.

**THERE ARE SIX FEET OF DRY BRICK UNDER THE KITCHEN WINDOW AT NUMBER TEN AND SHE MEANS TO TAKE IT DOWN COURSE BY COURSE AND PUT IT IN A STACK, AND HER SISTER IS IN THE KITCHEN WITH THE DOOR OPEN THROUGH THE DAY AND NEITHER OF THEM MENTIONS WHAT IS OUTSIDE.**

That wall is six feet long and three courses high under the sill, brick laid dry with no bonding, put up for a windbreak and never pointed.

Three courses is nine bricks to a foot of length and a half-brick at each end of each course, and that is about thirty bricks in the whole thing. She has done the count twice from the flags without touching any of them, because a wall that is thirty bricks is a morning and a wall that is a hundred is a day and a half, and the difference decides whether a barrow trip is needed in the middle of it.

It is not there to carry anything. It is there because the kitchen wall faces west and the low evening sun came straight in through that window onto the table, and a person built three courses of brick along the bottom of the wall with nothing behind them. There is no footing under it and there never was. It is a thing that stands up because it is not very heavy and it is not very tall, which is the whole of its engineering.

Nobody is going to want it afterwards.

The bricks are reused, which is the thing that makes the job worth doing at all. Anything that has been round a garden once and has been laid up again with nothing behind it is the sort of brick that ends up in a heap against a wall for want of anywhere better, and there are two such heaps on that side of the house at the moment.

She starts at the top with gloves and a bucket, lifting each brick with two hands and laying it on the flags in a row.

A dry wall comes down best course by course from the top, because every brick bears on the one below and the whole lot locks together. She works along the top course from one end to the other, easing each brick up and out, with dust coming off them in a puff.

A wall with no mortar in it has no key in it. That is the other side of the same thing and it is the whole trick. Every brick in a dry wall is kept there by the weight of the bricks above it and by nothing else, so a wall of this kind can be pulled down from the top with two hands and no tools at all, and it cannot be pushed over from the top because the moment the top is gone the middle has nothing to bear on and it walks out sideways.

So the top course comes off like a tray coming out of a cupboard. She goes along it with her thumbs under the upper edge and lifts and pushes it back and out, and the brick comes out without any noise except a puff of dust and the sound of the one below taking the weight.

By the middle of the afternoon the top course is down and the middle course shows, and here is what she has to work with.

There are four colours in that wall. Red and brown and a pale yellow and one almost black where it was near a fire. Colour is what the eye goes to first, and colour is nothing to go by. A red brick can be soft as cheese and a black one hard as iron, and the other way round the next time.

The reason is the clay and the fire and not the colour. Two clays burnt at the same temperature in the same kiln come out the same colour whatever they will be when you knock them. Brick that has been in a garden and been frosted and wet and dried about a hundred times is not the brick that came out of the kiln, and the frost goes into the face of it and takes the face off it in the weather without anybody noticing for three years.

The test is the sound and not the colour.

She takes each brick as it comes down and taps it on the edge with the back of another, and listens. A sound brick gives a short hard ring off the edge, clear, that she feels in her fingers. A soft one gives a dull thud with nothing in it, dead, that stops at the skin.

The tap is on the edge and not on the face because the edge is the piece the brick was made with. A brick rings or it does not, and where you ask the question makes little difference to the answer, and asking it on the edge gives a longer note and a longer note is easier to judge.

It is a rough test. She has heard a soft brick ring once and a hard one thud, and it happens with a brick that has a crack running off one face that you cannot see. What it does is sort out the ones that have gone, and going is the only thing she is sorting out, because the other bricks in that yard are going into a footing under nothing and a footing under nothing does not care.

One sound in four colours. The ring is the same off red and brown and yellow and black when the brick is good, and the thud is the same when it is not.

She stacks by sound. The ones that ring go in one stack on battens off the damp, in the order they came down, tight together. The ones that thud go in a heap by the shed for footing under nothing, where softness does not matter.

Thirty bricks and nineteen of them ring and eleven of them do not, and she rings each of the thirty twice because the first tap on a dusty brick gives a dull answer whatever is under the dust, so the dust comes off with a thumb and then the brick is asked properly.

Her sister is in the kitchen of number ten with the door open through the whole of that Wednesday, and the wall is under the window outside, and neither of them mentions it from either side. She can hear cups and the sound of a spoon on a pot, and none of it comes out of the door, and she does not take a brick inside to show.

“Your tea is on the side when you want it,” her sister tells her.

“Leave it there and I will come up when this course is down,” she tells her.

There is a wall coming down under the window of a kitchen with the door open and there is a person in that kitchen, and thirty bricks and nineteen answers and eleven of them go past the window in a barrow, and neither of them has said one word about the wall.

---

The bottom course comes up last, with soil on the undersides where they have sat for years, and she brushes them clean before she taps them.

The bottom course is the one that has been sitting on the ground, and the ground has come up to the bottom inch of it over however many years, and that inch is a different brick from the inch above it. She gets the spade under the whole course and lifts it in three sections on the strength of its own width, and the soil comes off the undersides in a slab that breaks where the frost has been in it.

She brushes them with a stiff brush on the flags and she gets the corners, and then she asks each one, and five of the bottom eleven thud and six of them ring, and that is a worse proportion than the course above and she was expecting it and would not have been surprised by anything else.

The dust and the brick dust and the grit off thirty years of faces is one grey colour and it goes over the flags and into the bed under the window, and the bed is not dug and the edging along it is not lifted.

She does not wash any of it down and there is nothing to wash it down with and she does not go out to the tap for anything at all through the whole of that Wednesday.

The nineteen go up on the battens against the wall and the eleven go round the side of the shed in four barrow loads and each one is laid on the flat and not on edge, because a footing under nothing wants the full face of the brick down and not a corner.

When the light has gone off that wall she stands on the flags and looks at what is there. Eleven soft bricks are laid along the foot of the shed in a line for nothing at all, and they are the ones nobody could have picked out of that wall by looking at it, and the ring of the other nineteen is still in the ends of her fingers from the last one she tapped.
