# Chapter 0696 — The Space Behind The Offcuts

Friday the fourteenth of January 2022, in this borough. He is at his own unit from first light until dark and the whole of the day is a pair of oak gates leaning against a back wall and a space behind a stack of offcuts where a ladder ought to be.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES MEASURES A PAIR OF OAK GATES LEANING AGAINST THE BACK WALL OF HIS OWN UNIT WITH POSTS ALREADY IN THE GROUND AT A COTTAGE, FINDS HE CANNOT HANG THEM ON HIS OWN FROM A STOOL, HAS A SCRAP OF SCRIBING CARD IN HIS POCKET WITH TWELVE ON ONE SIDE AND ROOM ON THE OTHER AND A PENCIL BEHIND HIS EAR AND DOES NOT WRITE ON IT, AND TELLS THE MAN AT THE BENCH OPPOSITE HE HAS LENT HIS LADDER OUT AND DOES NOT SAY TO WHOM.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is on his own all day except for a man at the bench opposite in the morning and a man who rings him at the middle of the day. He speaks to both of them.

---

He gets to the unit before the strip light has warmed.

The shoebox is on the top of his own bench where he cannot come in without seeing it, and the lid is on it, and the element is down inside in a bed of screwed-up newspaper, and it has been on that bench since the third of August.

The drawer under the bench is shut. It has been shut since the sixteenth of November and nothing has gone into it and nothing has come out of it, and the new brush is in a plastic bag flat along the back of it and the nine pounds he paid for it in the summer is still the only money he has ever put into that van out of his own pocket for anybody.

The stack of offcuts goes from the front bulkhead back to about the middle of that bay. The leads are along the other side with the dust sheets folded on top of them, the way they have been since June.

The space behind the offcuts is a folded step ladder's wide. It has been that shape since the summer. It is that shape now. There is nothing in it.

---

He stands in the doorway of his own bay with the extractor going and does not go in yet.

On the twenty-ninth of November, in a street off the ring road, on a Monday morning, he took a folding aluminium step ladder out of his van and lent it to a woman who clears houses. He did not say when it comes back and she did not ask. That was the whole of the arrangement.

Since then he has fitted a run of wall units off a packing bench and taken most of the day over it. He has set a top run a quarter of an inch out because he had to sight it from a stool. He has said he does not do roofs. He has not been up that road since the seventeenth of June.

The ladder went into an open porch on the third of December with no note on it. It went into a front room at number nine. It went round to number ten. It went up a road on the thirteenth of December on a shoulder. It went into the front room of a house at number twelve on the seventeenth, put there and squared by a woman of about thirty, folded, against the wall under the window.

It has not been opened out and it has not been moved and she did not go in after it.

He knows as far as the porch. The rest he does not know. He has not asked.

---

"You've not got a ladder."

The man at the bench opposite has a length of oak on the bench in front of him with a plane and a shooting board and a card with a pencil line on it, and he does not look up.

"I've not got a ladder," he says.

"You had one before Christmas."

"I've not got one now."

The man at the bench opposite runs the plane down the oak and looks at the shaving.

"You've lent it out."

"Aye."

"Right."

That is the exchange. The man at the bench opposite does not ask who and does not ask when, and he goes back to the oak, and there is a radio on low at that bench with the talk on it that neither of them follows.

---

He has a piece of scribing card in the shirt pocket of the coat on the back of the cab door.

It is the corner of a card about the size of a playing card and there is TWELVE on it in his own writing in a scribing pencil, and he wrote it at a tip off the ring road on the sixteenth of November because there was a taped box on the top of that heap with a street on it that he has gone past and a number on it that is a house at the end of a road, and he read a name off the other half of that label and put the box back down the way it was and folded a bit of bag over its top corner with his own tape.

There is room on the other side of that card.

He has got a pencil behind his ear. He could write a number on it and drive it down to the ring road and put it in the merchant's hand and the merchant would put it on a spike by the door, and if a woman in a white van ever went in and asked him about a ladder he would give it to her and she would ring him and he would have his ladder.

That is the whole of the thing, and it is a pencil and a card and a drive and a talk with a merchant who knows him.

He does not do it.

---

He has a very good reason and he is not going to say it out loud to anybody, and the reason is this.

The moment he writes a number on that card, that card is a thing he has given to somebody, and TWELVE is a thing he is doing something about, and he has not been up that road since the seventeenth of June and has gone past the end of it twice and stopped once and put his hand on a door handle once and taken it off again, and there is a woman at the end of that road who cannot ask anybody for anything, and a gate that stands open against a coal shed wall, and a tank in a roof space that nobody has ever been in, and a woman in a kitchen with her back to the glass.

He has done that twice now. He has put a card in a pocket and left it in a pocket. He has a number and a pocket and neither of them is a plan and both of them are better than a plan would be, because a plan is a thing that has a date on it and this has not got a date on it and can therefore not turn into one of those evenings where a man is sitting in a kitchen wondering whether he was right.

He puts the card back in the pocket and picks up the router.

---

The gates are leaning against the back wall of his own unit.

They are a pair of oak gates made since the summer, with a number chalked on the back of each one, and the posts are already in the ground at a cottage, and the man who wants them wanted them by the spring and paid up front and has the money down.

He goes to the back wall with his tape and his rule and his pencil behind his ear and measures the latch stile on the left gate and the hinge stile on the right one. The card he put in the latch stile lifts out in a while, because on the latch stile it lifts out, and he said so to a man on a shop telephone in October and the man said that is a thing, is it.

The gates are true. The posts are in the ground. Hanging a gate on a post that is already in the ground on your own wants a ladder, and you cannot do that from a stool.

He has not got a ladder.

He has a packing bench, the little stool he made himself out of two offcuts in the summer, and a stub of a folding stool that came off a skip in the summer. None of the three is four foot and none of the three is going to be.

He sights the gates against the posts in his head and puts the tape away.

---

The telephone on the bench rings at the middle of the day.

It is the man at the cottage. He has paid and he wants them by the spring.

"Are we still on for the spring," the man says.

"We are still on for the spring," he says.

"Have you got a date."

He looks at the space behind the offcuts. He looks at the stool. He looks at the card in his pocket with TWELVE on it and nothing on the other side.

"In the spring," he says, and puts no date on it, because a date would mean saying he has no ladder and saying he lent it and saying he cannot ask for it back without saying that he lent it, and he cannot say any of that on a telephone in a unit with the extractor going.

"Right," the man says. "You'll let me know."

"Aye."

He puts the telephone down.

---

And there it is, and it is the same shape as the shed with the felt gone at the front edge and the same shape as the bath tap with the bowl under it and the same shape as the front room with the ladder in it.

A repair with no date on it is not a repair. He did that to himself on the twenty-ninth of November when he lent a ladder out of a van and did not say when it comes back. And the woman he lent it to turned half a job down for the space and left it in an open porch because there was no third place for it. And a woman took it in out of the rain, and she is the same woman who carried it up a road on the thirteenth of December and put it down squared in a front room at the end of that road, where a woman of seventy-eight eats in a kitchen and does not go in.

Nobody in this borough has agreed anything. Everybody has been reasonable. That is the entire of what has happened to that ladder and those gates since November, and it is going to be exactly the same as it is now when he locks up.

---

He works the rest of the afternoon on a length of oak for a window-side foot.

He planes it and saws it and shims it dry in card joint with no glue, the way he did the table at number twelve in July, dead flat at six feet. The shavings come off in curls. The man at the bench opposite works on without talking. The radio talks on without either of them following it.

At the end of the afternoon he sweeps up. He puts the router away. He puts the tape on the bench. He looks at the shoebox on the top of the bench and does not open it. He looks at the shut drawer under the bench and does not open it. The stranger's brush is in the side pocket of the canvas bag in the footwell with the stone in the leather bottom of it.

The piece of scribing card is in the shirt pocket of the coat on the back of the cab door with TWELVE on one side of it and nothing at all on the other.

He has a pencil behind his ear.

He looks at the card for about as long as it takes to put the pencil behind the other ear, and then he starts the van and goes home, and the card is in the pocket, and the space behind the offcuts is a folded step ladder's wide, and the gates are against the back wall, and the only person in this borough who has ever written that number down is him, and he has written it on a scrap of card he keeps in a coat on the back of a cab door, which is not the same as saying it out loud.

---
