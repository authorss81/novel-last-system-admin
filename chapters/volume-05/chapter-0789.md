# Chapter 0789 — The New Stile In The Shed Door

Friday the twenty-seventh of January 2023, in this borough. Her own yard and her own shed at number ten from the time the frost is still white until it lifts, and one scaffold board standing proud of the face of that shed door is the whole of it.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES HAS A LENGTH OF SCAFFOLD BOARD LET INTO THE BOTTOM OF HER SHED DOOR AND STANDING PROUD OF THE FACE OF IT AND MEANS TO PLANE IT AND SCREW IT TIGHT, HER SISTER TAKES THE BOARD OUT AND LETS IN A NEW STILE AND A BOARD OF THE THICKNESS THAT BELONGS IN IT AND PUTS THE FELT BACK OVER THE SHED, AND THE DOOR SHUTS WITHOUT BEING LIFTED FOR THE FIRST TIME SINCE THE SUMMER, AND THE BARROW GOES BACK AGAINST THAT DOOR THAT AFTERNOON.**

She has been in her early thirties since long before she took number ten, and on the Friday her sister comes round the side of the house and in through the yard door at the back, and it is her own way or it is nothing.

---

The yard gate is off its top hook because she has never got round to it. Inside the back door there is a mat and a pair of wellingtons and nothing else, and the door behind her comes back to where it was without being lifted. The three boards she laid down the path are still down the middle of it, and the low end of that path is where the water goes and where it has made its own hole at the corner of the shed. The ply is where she left it, stood up against the shed front with nothing fastening it to anything. Nothing has been on the washing line since before she came. Along the bottom of the boundary the close boarding has a raking strut bearing on its far post and four turns of wire about the joint, and that run cannot be opened without cutting them. At the corner where the bottom run of the hedge turns up the side there is dead hedge and four oak stakes, and the barrow has not gone through it since before Christmas. The felt is over the shed with one corner of it standing clear of the boarding and it stays there.

The shed door is the problem and has been since the summer.

It is a boarded door with three ledges in a frame and it has hung in that frame since before she came. The beech came down in October and took the branch that was over the roof of the shed with it, and there is still a limb of it on, above that roof, bare and dead as a stick, and every time it rains or snows and the water melts off it, it comes down the face of the shed and across the door.

There is a length of scaffold board let into the bottom of that door. It runs the whole width of it, about nine inches deep and an inch and a quarter thick, and it is let into both stiles with its ends buried in them, and it stands proud of the face of the door by about three-quarters of an inch all the way along.

She put it in herself in the summer. The bottom of the hinge stile had gone soft the length of a hand and she could put her thumb into it to the second knuckle, and a scaffold board was what she had, and she let it in and screwed it and painted over the screws.

The water off that limb runs down the face of the shed and over the top edge of the board and into the two joints where the board meets the stiles, and the door has to be lifted at the bottom to shut, and it has to be lifted hard, and it is at the bottom of the door where she puts her foot.

Her plan is a plane and a screwdriver.

She means to take the proud three-quarters of an inch off the face of that scaffold board with a block plane until it is flush with the door, put two screws into the middle of it to draw the joints tight, screw them home, and be done with it.

---

Her sister comes round the side of the house in her coat and in at the back door of the yard and finds her out at the shed with the block plane in her hand.

"You are not going to plane that off."

"I am going to plane that off."

"You are going to take three-quarters of an inch off the face of the only thing at the bottom of that door that is not soft." Her sister stands at the shed corner. "Put your thumb under the bottom of the hinge stile where the board crosses it."

She does. Her thumb goes in about as deep as a nail head goes and stops.

"Now put your thumb in the same place on the other side of the board."

It does not go in at all.

"That board is the only thing on that door joining those two stiles at the bottom," her sister says. "Plane it down and you have a door with two soft ends and a strip of scaffold board across it, and the first time somebody leans a barrow on that door — and somebody leans a barrow on that door every day — the bottom of it comes away in four pieces."

---

So she takes the board out.

It takes most of the morning and it is the whole of the job. The felt comes off the shed first and is stood against the wall of the house with its top corner up, the way it has lain since the summer. Then the screws come out and the scaffold board comes out of the two ends of itself and the soft bottoms of both stiles come out after it with a chisel and a bit of pulling, and they come out in three pieces each and the inside of them is black.

Then the bottom of the hinge stile goes.

Her sister has a length of oak off the face of the bound of the house and a length of redwood off an old gate of her own, and she works out which of them is going in where before she cuts anything, because a stile and a ledge are not the same job and a board that is a quarter too thick is the same mistake that costs you a window.

"Then what goes in it."

"The stile goes in first." She stands the oak up in the opening and finds its length against the old one. "It is a full length stile from the head of the door to the bottom, and it is the full thickness of the stile it is going beside, because the reason that door shuts is that its two stiles are the same thickness and the middle of it is thinner, and if you put a thinner one in there you have made a door that is a hinge with a board on it."

"And the bottom."

"The bottom is a ledge." She cuts the redwood to the width inside the two stiles and stands it in the bottom of the door between them. "A ledge is the thickness of the ledge it is going beside, which is an inch, and it is a quarter of an inch less than the scaffold board was, and the door will stand a quarter of an inch less proud of its own frame. And that is what a ledge is for. You have had a thing across the bottom of that door that is thicker than anything else in it, and every time that door shuts, the thickest thing in it shuts last."

---

Then it goes back together and the felt goes back over it.

The new stile goes in dry, wedged at the head, and the ledge is screwed into the stiles the way a ledge is screwed, from the inside face, and the new oak stile is screwed from the face of the door into the head and the bottom of the frame, and the screw heads are left proud and the face of the stile is planed over them until it is one surface again.

Then the felt goes back over the shed.

It goes back on the same way it has lain since the summer, which is over the top of the shed and down under the eaves at the front, with the top corner lifted clear of the boarding and nothing fastening it but its own weight and the fact that it is a wet felt and it is clinging to a wet board. Nothing is driven into it. It has never been nailed and it is not nailed now, and the corner stands up because it has always stood up.

---

The door shuts without being lifted.

She goes at it four times to be certain. She takes her foot off the ground and pulls it to and it comes over the ledge and goes down onto the frame and stays there, and there is nothing at the bottom of it to lift.

Her sister stands on the side of the shed and watches it shut.

"That is a door."

"A door that shuts," her sister says. "And it shuts because the stile in it is the thickness of the stile beside it and the ledge in it is a ledge, and you can put the water off that limb on it for as long as there is a limb on that tree."

"It is still going to let water in at the bottom."

"It is going to let water at the bottom of a door, which is what the bottom of a door is for, and it is going to run down the face of that ledge and off it, because that ledge is now lower than the board that was there and the water will find its own way down to the ground." She steps back off the grass. "It was not the board on the front of it that let water in. It was the board on the front of it going down the face and turning in at the ends."

---

Then the barrow goes back against that door.

Her sister has gone by then, and has gone out of the front gate without anybody walking her to it, and she goes out to the shed because there is nothing else to do in that yard.

It is a builder's barrow with a steel wheel and a wooden body and it has never been kept in the shed, because the shed has never been big enough and the door has never wanted it leaning. It goes against the face of that shed door when she has finished barrowing, and it stays there until she wants it again, and nobody has ever thought about it and nobody ever will.

It stands about as high as the new ledge and it leans its whole length on that door from the yard side, and the feet of whoever tips it out go through to the frame, and the wheel is about a hand from the stile.

And she does the thing she has done every day since the summer without once asking herself why.

She stands it against the face of the door and leans it there, and the feet go through to the frame, and the barrow is leaning its whole length on a door with a new oak stile in it, a full inch and a half at the thickest part and dry and hard and put in that morning, taking the weight of a barrow that has no business being leaned on anything.
