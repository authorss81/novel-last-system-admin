# Chapter 1025 — An Apron Over The Back Of A Chair

Undated in this draft.

**THE BACK BEDROOM AT NUMBER TWELVE HAS A SMALL TABLE UNDER THE WINDOW AND A CHAIR WITH AN APRON OVER IT.**

Up one flight at the back of that house upstairs at the back there is a room with a window in the roof slope and a lead pipe running across under it, and a bed stood along the wall that has no window in it, and a chair with an apron thrown over the back of it, and a small table under the window with nothing standing on that table at all. The boards have been painted round the edges of the floor by somebody who was in no hurry about it, and the paint has gone under the bed and not come out again. It is the only room in that house with a door on it that shuts, and she has been in it since the Tuesday morning she came down the river, most of that week with her coat still on, because she is not a woman who has settled anywhere and will not stand in a hall pretending that she has.

She went out of that front door on her own two hands at the end of the day before and she did not shut it behind her, and she has been back in this room since, and she has not told anybody where she went, and nobody has asked her, and neither of those two things is going to happen this morning either.

He came up that frontage at the middle of the morning with the thing he had been carrying in his mouth for a week and he got it out of him in the doorway, and she was at the window with the chair pulled out from the wall behind her.

"I am starting."

She did not turn round.

"Then the money can come down off the table."

"It is in the apron."

"I know where it is in the apron. I put it there myself."

He stood in that doorway and put into words the whole of what he had come up there to find out, which was that the job wanted two men under a range the length of that one and he had not got two men, and that the sister at number ten had not come back to him with the answer she owed him, and that a fortnight of a man's time had no morning on it that anybody could put a name to. He put all of it out in the flat voice he uses when he sets a length of wood down and takes his hands off it.

"And I am taking it anyway," he told her. "Because a man who has not taken the money cannot begin either, and it is the not beginning that has cost me the whole of the winter, and I have stood in my own yard for eleven weeks telling myself that a week is not the same thing as a fortnight."

The eleven weeks is what he has been given and no more. Not one penny of his own has ever gone into that kitchen, or into anything else that anybody has asked him for, and this is the first money that has ever come to him out of a front room on this frontage, and it is a sum a woman worked out for herself at her own table over two days and then went to a pantry and asked him into.

---

That is the argument, and he made it standing up, and she listened to the whole of it with her face at the window and her hands on the sill behind her. The window is a short one. You cannot see out of it in that house without putting your head over the sill and getting the run of the gutter on the back of your neck, and she stood with her head down and looked at the sill and let a man she has never taken a shilling off tell her in a cold room that he is going to take her money and cannot put in words what it buys.

She got the apron off the chair and she got the coins out of its pocket in front of him, and she did not turn her back on him while she did it, and she did not lay them out on the table one after another the way a woman does when she wants a man to see every piece of it go down. She put them into his hand.

he had it out flat under hers so that they came down onto it and not into it, and the whole of the difference between a man taking money and a man being handed it is that, and neither of them put a word on it. They went down one after another and he did not look at his own hand while she was doing it and he did not put a single one of them into his pocket until the last one was down there.

"You have not gone through them."

"No need for that. I went through them in your front room when you went over them on your own table, and neither of us has touched them since, and you have carried them in an apron for a week so that you would know at any moment of the day whether they were still in it."

She looked at him then, and it was the first time she had looked at him since he came into that room.

"Will you tell me what it is for."

"It is for a tray and a length of flue and a fortnight of two men who are not me, and I have got none of the three, and the state of things is that and it is not an excuse for any of it."

"That is not what I asked you."

"Then it is for the wall behind your range, and I have told you that a great many times now, and you know it better than I do."

"No." She put her hand flat on the back of the chair. "I am asking what you are going to be afterwards. You have never taken money off anybody in this borough as far as I know and I am the second woman to hand you some, and you are going into that scullery a different sort of man from the one who came up this frontage, and I have seen a good many men in this house get handed something and go out of that door and be a different sort of man by the end of the week, and not one of them ever came back the same."

"It will not be like that."

"I have not promised you that it will not. I only put to you that I have seen it, and I am telling you now so that if it is like that you cannot come up here afterwards and tell anybody they were not warned."

That was the price of it and she put it in a room with a bed in it, and it was not money and it was not work and it was the first thing anybody on that frontage had ever put a condition on him with.

He stood there and worked out that she was right and that being right would not stop it, and that a woman who has seen a thing happen twice in one house learns it from the inside of it and not from anybody's mouth. And he worked out that she was not asking him to be careful with the money. She was asking him to be careful with himself, and she had put her hand on the back of a chair to put it that way, and she had not asked him for a single promise about the wall.

---

He put the coins into his pocket and she put the apron back over the chair with the pocket lying flat against the boards.

Because a man who has been paid is a man somebody can put a question to, and nothing in this borough will carry what has gone out of that apron, she is going to have to carry it.
