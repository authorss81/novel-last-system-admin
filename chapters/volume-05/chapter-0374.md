# Chapter 0374 — What Came in on the Air

Thursday the seventh of December 2017, from about half past seven in the morning until about half past eight in the evening, a parade, a shop off it, a hall on a meter, and a building by the water, in Tideglass.

**FOURTEEN DAYS WENT THROUGH THAT BOROUGH AND THE SAME PEOPLE WALKED THE SAME ROUNDS AT THE SAME HOURS, AND A SWEET SMELL HAS BEEN COMING INTO THE MIDDLE OF THAT PARADE UNDER AN AWNING IN THE MORNINGS, AND THREE PEOPLE IN THAT BOROUGH HAVE NOTICED IT AND TWO OF THE THREE CAN PUT A DATE NEAR IT, AND THE ONE MAN WHO WALKS THAT PARADE FOUR OR FIVE TIMES A DAY CANNOT PUT ANY DATE ON IT AT ALL AND WILL NEVER BE ABLE TO, AND NOBODY WENT AND FOUND OUT.**

A fortnight is a fortnight. It is not a turn of anything and nothing in it came to an end and nothing in it was decided, and no term ran out in that borough, and no one in it was asked anything, and no one in it has been given a title or a grade or a chair or a seat or a way of being spoken to.

The man who puts shutters up was on that parade at about ten past seven with his ladder and did the same shops in the same order and left the one at the top until the end of the morning.

The woman who works in a shop had her counter from about ten to nine, and the man in his thirties came in at about half past ten for a coil of wire, and the man in his forties came in at about ten past four for a bag of plugs, and both of them went out again, and both of them came back later, and that is a Monday in most weeks and it was a Thursday and it was nothing.

The man who works nights went to that parade at about half past four and stood in it and went home with what he went home with. The man in his sixties was out on a low wall by a yard gate for about an hour with a cup and went in. The man in his forties was two days that Monday doing a kitchen at the bottom of the town, and was in a yard off the Cleeve road in the middle of the afternoon, and came home at about half past seven.

Nine people went through that fortnight with nothing on them at all, and every one of them had something the other eight had not got, and there was no afternoon in that fortnight on which one of them said a word about his own to another.

---

The smell is of hot sugar, or of toffee, or of something brown that has gone a stage too far, and it comes into the middle of that parade from under the awning of the shop that shuts early at about half past seven in the morning and it is strongest at about three foot off the ground, and it is gone by about ten and it has not been back in the afternoon at all.

The woman who runs the shop that shuts early says it is the flat above that shop and that somebody in it has been doing something at night. The man who does that hall says it is the pipe on that side and that it is nothing. The man in his forties says it is the weather, which it is not.

The woman who works in a shop first noticed it on the twenty-eighth of November, in the morning, and has smelled it every working morning since, and she can put a date on it to the day, because she knows the twenty-seventh.

The man in his thirties first noticed it on the first of December, coming down that parade at about a quarter to eight in the morning, and he can put a date on it to within two or three days.

The man in his forties walks that parade four or five times a day and has done since long before the hot weather, and he cannot put any date on it at all, and he has tried, and he has stood in the middle of that parade at about half past seven in the morning on purpose on two mornings since the start of December trying to work out whether it was there in October, and he cannot.

There is no way to do it. He has no date on it because he was in that parade on every morning between the twentieth of November and the thirtieth and there is nothing in him that separates one of those mornings from another one.

Nobody has gone and stood in that flat above the shop that shuts early and asked anybody anything, and the woman who runs that shop has not said who lives in it, and nobody has asked her, and there is no reason on earth for a man with a coil of wire or a bag of plugs to go and knock on the door of a flat because there is a smell in the street.

It does not matter. It is a smell in a street in the cold, it is gone by ten, and it is the smallest thing anybody in that parade has got a view about. Nobody has turned anything round over it and nobody is going to, and it is there.

The hall on a meter down that parade was open on the Sunday from about ten until about four with the urn going and the tables out on the pavement, and the man who does that hall was behind the counter at the end of it, and about fourteen people came and went in those four hours, and none of them asked him anything, and he has not been asked anything by the smell either.

He had that urn on from about half nine and it stands behind that counter with the lid off it and about three heaped spoons of sugar in a pot, and that is what that room smells of, and it has smelled of that on a Sunday for about six years, and nobody who came into it that morning had any reason to think of anything else.

He walks up that parade to that hall on a Wednesday as well as a Sunday and has done for about six years, and he has not noticed the smell anybody else has been noticing at all, and if anybody had put it to him in that hall that morning he would have told them about the urn, and nobody put it to him, and he was behind that counter from about ten until about four and asked nobody anything.

At about the middle of that Thursday morning the man in his forties was at the counter of the shop off the parade, and the man in his thirties was at the end of it with a coil of wire, and there was nothing else on that counter at all.

The man in his thirties had been in a queue of nothing beside a basket of nothing and he had said, out of nothing, that whoever writes anything down in this town is going to be the one everybody goes to in about ten years.

She said that was the first sensible thing anybody had said in that shop in a fortnight.

He said no, he did not think there would be anything written down. He said it was going to be the thing two people can both stand in front of and see, and that a thing like that does not need anything written about it, and that a man in his thirties and a man in his forties could say a thing happened and the borough would have to say it happened or say it did not.

The man in his forties said that a line in a book is what it would be, because a book does not argue, and because the day somebody opens a book and finds a date and a sentence on the same page is the day this town has one.

The woman who works in a shop said that neither of them had a book, and that she had been behind that counter for about six years and could not tell a man where a book was, and that everything in this town that anybody needed had been in somebody's head the whole time, and that the first thing that got proved in this town would be a small thing, and that by then everybody who could have said it was different would be dead.

And then the man in his forties said the other half of it, which was that the first one to be proved would be a thing somebody was told about before anybody had laid eyes on it, and that the telling would happen in about four mouths and none of the four would know where it had come from.

The man in his thirties said that was a load of nonsense and went and paid for his coil of wire.

There are four answers to that question in that shop on that morning, and they were all given by people who had a point, and there is no way on this earth to put one of them beside any of the other three, because a line in a book is not a thing two men can stand in front of and a thing two men can stand in front of is not a thing somebody was told about, and none of the four is about the same kind of thing as any of the other three.

That question is still a question. Nobody answered it in that shop and nobody answered it anywhere else in that fortnight.

---

He went up the outside stair of the building by the water at about a quarter to two that afternoon, because there was a woman of about fifty-five on that landing who wanted a cupboard taken off its brackets and put up higher, and he had the tools for it in his bag and it takes about an hour.

Coming back down, he stopped in that corridor, because the end of it was open to the air and it was the end of a warm stair. There is a recess off the end of that corridor with a door in it that has never caught properly since before the summer, and the door stands open, and there is a book on a shelf inside it with its back against the inside face of that door.

That book stands at nineteen entries and will not take a twentieth without a heading somebody has to invent. The nineteenth of them is a lamp outside a yard, put down as missing a couple of Novembers back against a heading kept open for that class of loss, and nothing at all has been entered under that heading since. The paper on that page runs on for a couple of fingers past it and then stops well short of where the foot of the volume is, and nobody has ever closed that gap.

A line under that last entry has been sitting there, with nothing on it, for one thousand one hundred and seven days.

He did not go into that bay. He has not been into that bay since the summer and he is not going to, and he stood in that corridor for the length of a cigarette he did not have a light for and then went down the concrete stair and drew the street door back to behind him at the bottom.

The woman of about fifty-five was on that landing twice more that week, going up and down it with a jug in each hand the way she has done since June, and that recess off the corridor was as she left it, and nobody has looked inside that door since the summer.

**THE SMELL WAS UNDER THAT AWNING AGAIN AT ABOUT HALF PAST SEVEN ON THAT THURSDAY MORNING AND IT WAS GONE BY TEN, AND ON THE SATURDAY IT CAME BACK, AND THE ONLY TWO PEOPLE WHO CAN PUT ANYTHING LIKE A DATE ON IT ARE THE TWO WHO CAME ALONG AFTER IT, AND THE MAN WHO HAS BEEN IN THAT PARADE EVERY DAY SINCE THE TWENTIETH OF NOVEMBER STANDS IN THE MIDDLE OF THAT STREET AT HALF PAST SEVEN IN THE MORNING AND SMELLS IT AND CANNOT SAY.**
