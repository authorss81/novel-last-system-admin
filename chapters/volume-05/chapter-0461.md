# Chapter 0461 — The Seventh Slab

The nineteenth of December 2018, and about fifty minutes of the middle of it. The ground between two blocks of flats, in Tideglass.

**TWO MEN PUT THE SAME BENT WIRE INTO THE SAME JOINT IN THAT PATH ON TWO DIFFERENT MORNINGS AND EVENINGS TWO DAYS APART AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS THE SLIGHTEST IDEA THE OTHER ONE IS A MAN IN THIS BOROUGH, AND NOBODY IS HOLDING ANYTHING IN THAT PLACE.**

Between two blocks of flats off that parade there is about sixty yard of ground that belongs to nobody and that everybody uses, and the way across it is a line of about nine paving slabs that came out of a path in about 2011 and were put down again by a man who is not known and who did it badly.

Six of the nine have sunk. The seventh stands about two inch proud of the other eight, at the point where the path meets the strip of grass, and in this weather it is the only thing in that sixty yard anybody looks at.

You go round it. Everybody goes round it. It is not an obstacle. It is a thing you step over.

---

On the Monday of the tenth of December a man in his forties came over that ground at about twenty past eight and stopped at the seventh slab and looked at it for about four seconds.

Then he went back about ten foot into a gateway, took a length of wire out of his bag, bent it in two, came back and put one end down in the joint at the side of that slab and the other end down in the joint at the side of the one next to it, and twisted them together, and stood up and looked at it, and went to work.

That is a thing you do to a slab that stands proud. It holds the near edge down and lets water run off the joint instead of standing in it, and it goes in in about ten seconds and stays in about as many weeks as this weather lasts.

He told nobody. He has not thought about it since the Monday except in the sense that he has not gone and looked.

The man who has been going across that ground for about nine years is a man in his thirties and he goes across it in the evening, most evenings, at about half past five, on his way to his own place, which is the second of those two blocks.

On the Wednesday of the twelfth of December he came across at about half past five and stopped at the seventh slab, because it had not been proud on the Monday evening or the Tuesday, and it was proud again.

He looked at it. He looked at the joint. He looked at the ground on either side of it for longer than a man looks at ground.

Then he got a short length of wire out of the bottom of his bag, where it has been for about two years because that is where a man keeps a length of wire, and he put one end in the joint and the other end in the joint next to it, and twisted them, and stood up, and went to his own door.

He told nobody either. What he has thought about since the Wednesday is not the wire.

The thing he has thought about is who was there first.

He worked it out in about two seconds standing on that grass, and it has been with him every day since, and it is not a puzzle and it is not a mystery and he has not been able to leave it alone for more than about an hour at a time.

Somebody came across that ground in daylight about two days before him, and stood at that slab, and did exactly what he did, and used exactly what he had, and then took the wire out again and left not one other mark in the whole of that sixty yard.

He cannot stand it. It is not the wire. It is that a thing he did on a Wednesday was already there, and that the man who did it first is not on that ground in the evening, and has never been on that ground in the evening, and he has gone across it about six days a week for nine years and has not laid eyes on him.

He has decided it is not somebody he knows. He has decided it is not anybody out of either of those two blocks, because if it were, then somebody out of one of them has been able to watch him go across that ground at half past five for nine years and has watched him step over that slab and has never once said anything, and he has decided he cannot stand that being true either, and that is where it stops.

On the Sunday of the sixteenth a car went over that grass at about ten in the morning, not over the path, and a wheel found the edge of the seventh slab and lifted about a foot and a half of it, and the wire came up out of the joint with a bit of mortar hanging off it, and the slab went over onto the grass and lay there.

It lay there until the Tuesday of the eighteenth.

Nobody came in between. That is the thing about that sixty yard. It is not a place anybody goes to. There is nothing at the far side of it that anybody is doing. The six people who use it use it four or five days a week, in the morning and in the evening, and not one of them had any reason in that whole week to be on that grass on any day but a working one.

A woman who has the first floor of the first of those two blocks found it on the Tuesday at about four in the afternoon.

She came out for a cigarette and it was under her feet before she had got a proper look at anything, and she stood on the grass and looked at a hole in the path with a lip round it and about six inches of old joint showing, and at a slab lying over on the grass a yard off with a bent length of wire hanging off it.

She looked at it for about a minute.

What she thought about was that nine years is a long time, and that she has stepped over that corner of that path about four times a week for the whole of it, and that it has been the wrong shape from the start, and that she will keep her mouth shut about it, and that somebody will do it, because that is what happens, and that whoever it is will not be her.

She went inside and shut the door and did not tell anybody in that building and is not going to.

He came across on the Wednesday of the nineteenth at about ten past two in the afternoon, in daylight, in the middle of the day, which is not the hour he ever uses that ground at.

He had his bag. He stopped. There was no seventh slab, and there was a hole, and there was the slab over on the grass with the wire still hanging off it.

He stood there about four seconds. Then he went and picked the slab up, which took him two hands and a moment, and brought it back and dropped it into the hole from above, and it came to rest about half an inch proud, which is what the other six do anyway.

He stood on it with his own weight once. It did not move.

Then he looked at the wire.

It was a length of wire, bent in two, twisted, and gone into the joint at the side of that slab and the joint at the side of the next one, and it had mortar on it, and it was his.

He stood there with it in his hand for a while.

He has a van and a store off a street on the far side of that parade, and there has been a length of wire at the bottom of that bag for two years, and on the Wednesday of the twelfth he bent a piece of it in two and twisted it into that joint because a slab was standing proud, and then he went to his own door. Nobody has ever asked him where he gets it from and he has never said.

And then he looked at the joint, and the joint had wire in it that was his own wire, put in by somebody else, on the Monday, in daylight, before he had been anywhere near that ground.

And he could not do it any other way. That is the part he has not said out loud to himself and has not said to anybody either. A man who has been going across that ground for nine years and then finds that somebody has been doing to it, in the morning, at an hour he is never there at, the exact thing he did, cannot afterwards put a slab back in a hole without doing that.

He put the slab down properly. He took the wire off the slab, bent it in two again, put it back into the same two joints and twisted it, and stood up and looked at it, and went across that ground to his own van.

He has not thought about the man in the forties. He does not know there is a man in the forties. He has not got a shape to put him in and has not been looking for one, and what he has instead is a Monday morning and a pair of hands and somebody who was not there, and he has been carrying that about for a week and it is not going anywhere.

---

There is no list of that sixty yard. There is no person keeping the seventh slab. Nobody owns that ground and nobody has ever rung anybody about it, and no two men in this borough have ever said one word to one another about a piece of wire in a joint.

And the man in his forties will cross that ground most weekday mornings and the man in his thirties will cross it most weekday evenings, and a piece of wire in a wet joint does not mend a path, and the frost is under that whole line.

One of them will look at it. One of them will not.

**HE WENT ACROSS THAT GROUND AT TEN PAST TWO ON THAT WEDNESDAY WITH THE SLAB BACK IN AND A PIECE OF WIRE TWISTED INTO THE JOINT AGAIN, AND HE TOLD NOBODY, AND THE WOMAN WHO STOOD OVER THAT HOLE FOR ABOUT A MINUTE ON THE TUESDAY AFTERNOON WENT INSIDE AND SHUT THE DOOR AND SAID NOTHING AND HAS SAID NOTHING SINCE.**
