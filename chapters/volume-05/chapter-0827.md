# Chapter 0827 — Short Ones In The Middle

Wednesday the twelfth of July 2023, along the far side of that yard at number ten all through the middle of the day, and a run of palings on a boundary that is coming down is the whole of that Wednesday.

**THERE IS A RUN OF PALINGS ALONG THE FAR SIDE OF THE YARD AT NUMBER TEN AND SHE MEANS TO TAKE THEM OFF THE POSTS AND PUT THEM IN A STACK, AND HER SISTER COMES THROUGH THE YARD GATE AFTER THE MIDDLE OF THE DAY WITH A BOTTLE AND GOES OUT THE SAME WAY.**

That boundary is posts and rails with palings nailed on the yard side, about ten feet long, grey with weather, with ivy at one end and a lean to the whole of it that has been getting worse since the spring.

The posts go down into the ground and the rails go on the posts and the palings go on the rails, and there is nothing else to it, and that is why a fence like this comes apart in pieces instead of all at once. A post wants to be anchored in the ground, a rail wants to be fixed to the post, and a paling wants to be fixed to the rail, and when the fence leans it is always one of those three that has let go first and the other two that are still doing their job with nothing to do it to.

The palings are the uprights, about four feet long, three inches wide with an inch of light between them, nailed top and bottom to the rails with two nails in each end. There are twenty-eight on that run. Some of them are sound and some split at the nails, and a few have gone soft at the bottom where the rain has sat on the rail.

The split ones are the interesting ones. A nail into the end of a paling and then a wet winter and a dry summer will split the grain from the head of the nail down about four inches, and the split opens a year and closes the next, and a paling that has opened and closed three times has a hole in it at the nail that a finger goes into. One of these has both ends gone and it is the soundest-looking timber in the run and it is the worst of it.

She starts at the far end with a claw hammer and a wrecking bar wrapped in sacking where it bears on the wood, and a pair of gloves, and she works along the run taking the nails out whole where she can.

A paling comes off best if the nails come out first. She drives the claw under the head and rolls it out slow, and the paling springs off the rail with a soft pop and stays straight. If the head breaks off she drives the shank through with a punch and pulls it from the back, so the face stays clean.

If the head breaks off and the shank stays in the rail, the paling has to come off the rail and not off the nails, and it takes the head of the nail with it and puts a hole in the paling at each end the size of the nail head. She has one like that and she keeps it because the holes go round the same mark and a hole like that can be a dowel hole one day.

By the middle of the day she has half the run off and laid in a row on the flags, faces up, with the nails out in a tin.

Here is what she has to look at before she puts them in a stack.

The palings are not one length. Whoever put that fence up cut them to follow the ground, so the ones at the low end are a good hand longer than the ones at the high end, and the middle ones are all over the place. A stack of mixed lengths falls over if it is put up wrong, because the long ones bear on the short ones and the whole lot rocks.

She measures nothing and uses her own hand. She takes four palings she is sure of, from the low end and the middle and the high end, and lays them on the flags side by side with their ends level and looks at where the ends do not agree, and then she goes along the row and sorts by that instead of by the eye alone. A hand is nine inches and it is the same hand on every one of them.

That is the other thing about a stack like this. Palings off a fence are not boards and they are not slates, and the mistake is to treat them like either. A board stack wants the boards flat and the bearers close so the boards do not cup. A slate stack wants the slates leaned and tied. A paling stack wants the palings stood on end, which is how they were on the fence, and the whole reason is that a leaning paling is a lever and a flat one is not.

She sorts them on the flags into three lots by eye, long, middle and short, and she counts them by laying them side by side and not by numbers in her head. The long ones go at the back, the middle ones in front of them, and the short ones in the middle of the stack where they cannot tip it.

A stack that stands on a going roof wants the short ones in the middle. She has seen a stack of slates go the same way on a roof with a fall to it, where the weight runs to the low side. Here the ground at the far end falls away from the posts toward the middle of the yard, which puts the slope under the back foot of the stack and leaves the front foot standing on nothing at all, and that is the same fault the slates had. A stack like that walks out into the yard in the first wind off the wall unless the middle of it is packed tight.

Eleven long at the back, ten in the middle and seven short, and she packs the seven down in the middle on end so that the two lots either side of them cannot lean into one another, and then she packs the ten on top of the seven at both ends so the top of the stack is one width and not two.

She builds it against the posts at the far end, off the ground on two rails laid flat, palings upright, leaning back a little to the posts, long ones outside and short ones packed in the middle, with string round the whole lot at the top rail height.

Her sister comes through the yard gate after the middle of the day with a bottle left on the flags at the near end, and goes out the same way. She is at the near end by the kitchen steps and the palings are at the far end by the boundary, and there is the whole length of the yard between them. She does not come down to the far end and she does not look at the work twice.

“I have put it by the steps for you,” her sister tells her.

“Leave it there till I am up,” she tells her, without coming up the yard.

There is a bottle and a woman at the near end and there are twenty-eight palings and a yard at the far end, and neither of them is on the same side of the yard and neither of them stays.

---

The rest of the day is nails and rails and the posts left standing in a row with their tops cut square where the palings were.

She hammers the bent ones straight on a stone and puts them in the tin with the rest, and she sweeps the flags where the sorting was, and she wedges the bottom of the stack with offcuts so it cannot walk.

The tin of nails comes to a hundred and four, which is not a number she has been keeping. She counts them because they go back in the box at the back of the shed with the rest of the ironmongery and because a tin left with two nails in it and a hundred and two back in the box is a thing she will not be able to stop thinking about. So she counts them twice and puts them in the box and shuts it.

The offcuts under the stack are three pieces of rail she broke out of the middle where it had gone soft, and they are cut wedge shape and not square, so that a wedge under the foot of a stack gets under it and a square piece does not. She drives them in with the back of the hammer and then leaves them, because a stack that wants to walk has to want it more than a hammer wants to stop it.

The line at ten stays bare where it always has been, and the kitchen drawer with the hammer and the screwdriver and the paper in it is shut from first thing to last, and the shed door and its felt and the sheet of ply leaning on it and the butt lying on its side are exactly as they were at the start of the day.

When the light goes thin she stands at the near end and looks down the yard to the far end where the work was, and the wind comes over the wall the way it does at that hour and the whole ten feet of boundary is not there any more.

What is there is a row of posts with their tops cut square and two rails, and at the far end the ivy that was on the fence is now on nothing at all, hanging off the top rail with the bottom of it six inches in the air, and it has not come down yet and it will not come down on its own.
