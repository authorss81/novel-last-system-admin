# Chapter 0537 — Two Nets

The Monday of the twenty-third of December 2019, and most of the morning, at the bottom of one street in this borough and at the top of the same street.

**SHE IS A WOMAN IN HER EARLY SEVENTIES AND EVERY DECEMBER SINCE SHE WAS A GIRL IN HER TEENS SHE HAS CARRIED A NET OF ONIONS DOWN TO THE SAME SHOP AND HANDED IT OVER AT THE FRONT OF IT, AND A WOMAN LEARNED IN THE SECOND WEEK OF NOVEMBER THAT A GIRL OF THIRTY-ONE HAS BEEN BUYING THE SAME THING OFF HER LATER IN THE YEAR, AND THAT WOMAN ASKED HER TO SAY NOTHING, AND SHE HAS SAID NOTHING, AND ON THIS MORNING SHE WALKED BACK UP THAT STREET WITH TWO OF THEM IN HER HANDS.**

The shop is at the bottom of that street on the south side and it has been in the same family since before she was born. It opens at seven and shuts at half past five and it sells vegetables, and it has sold a net of onions to that woman every December since the first one in 1961, and this December is the fifty-ninth of them.

Nobody has ever arranged it. That is the first thing to understand about it. The woman does not have a standing arrangement and has never said she had one, and in fifty-nine years the two of them have never once discussed it.

The woman comes in on the third Monday of December. She has come in on the third Monday of December for fifty-nine years. She comes in, she says good morning, and the woman behind the front of that shop says good morning back, and they have a conversation about something else, and then the net comes out.

The conversation is never about the net. It is about the year, and it is about what has happened to people they both know, and in fifty-nine years there has not been a December in which one of them did not say at least one thing about somebody's health. The net comes at the end of it and the woman pays for it and goes out.

She has never once been handed one for nothing. She has never been offered one and she would have said no.

---

How she found out is this.

The woman in the shop said it. Not in November of this year by accident. She had decided to say it and she waited until the woman was in and had said good morning and had been told about somebody's hip, and then she said, while she was putting a net of something else into a net, "Do not say anything to them about it, because I would rather they did not know I knew."

She said: "Your brother's girl has been coming in here three years for the same thing as you. Not in December. Later in the year, when they come down. Three years this October would be the third."

Then she put the net into a second net and tied it at the top the way she does, and said, "There is one of yours behind the scales, because she came in on Saturday and I put two of hers by, and you are not to take hers."

The woman who lives at the top of that street did not take hers. She took the one that had been put by, and she paid for it, and she said good morning, and she came home up the south side with a net of onions in each hand, a quarter of an hour before the end of the morning, and then did not open her mouth again that day, because there was nobody in that house to open it at.

She has not said a word about it. Not to her brother, not to her brother's wife, not to her brother's daughter, not to anybody at the two places where she goes where there are people to talk to.

She has also not done anything about it. She has not gone to the shop and asked for it to stop. She has not rung her brother and asked. She has not written to anybody.

What she has done is that she has worked the thing out, and the thing she has worked out is not the same one that anybody else in her family would work out.

The thing she has worked out is that a girl in her thirties with three children and a job she does four days has been going into a shop twice a year, on a Saturday, from about the middle of September, and paying for a net of onions, and then in December the woman at the top of that street has come in and been handed the same thing. And that neither of them has ever mentioned it. And that the woman in the shop has been standing in the middle of it for three years on the word of nobody, doing nothing at all except keep two nets instead of one.

---

What she has done with it since the second week of November is nothing, and she has been very good at the nothing.

She has been to the shop nine times since then, because she goes twice a week and has done for years. Nine times she has stood in there and bought what she came for and had a conversation about somebody's hip and gone out again, and nine times the woman behind the front of that shop has carried on exactly as she carries on, and on none of those nine occasions did the subject of a Saturday in October come up between them, or the subject of a girl in her thirties who comes down from the north end of the county with three children and a job she does four days.

The woman at the top of that street has also not done anything about the second part of it, which is that her brother's daughter does not know. That is the part she turns over most in the evening. The girl thinks she is buying onions. The girl has been buying onions twice a year on a Saturday for three years and has no idea that a woman in her early seventies is going to walk into that shop in about four weeks and be handed the same thing by somebody who has been keeping them back since October.

If she tells the girl, then the girl will feel watched. If she tells her brother, then her brother will feel that something has been kept from him, and he will want to know what, and there is not one version of this in which he finds out what without the shop being in it.

So she has not told anybody, and she has not stopped anything, and she is not going to say anything at all about any part of it to anybody in this borough.

On the Monday the twenty-third of December she got up at the usual time and she did not think about any of it once.

She went down at ten past ten. It was cold and dry and there were people in the street doing nothing outside the two shops that have a door they can stand in, and she went into the shop at the bottom and the woman was where she is on a Monday morning, and they said good morning, and then they said a thing about a hip.

The hip was somebody's hip and it was going to be somebody else's problem in the new year.

Then the net came out. It came out from behind the scales in the back, where it had been since Saturday, and the woman in the shop put it into a second net and tied it at the top and set it down in front of her, and the woman who lives at the top of that street stood there and looked at it.

"That is Tuesday's," the woman in the shop said, meaning the Saturday before, "and there is another one behind it. You are taking one of those."

And then she said, "And the rest are here until next December, so you may as well take one while you are standing there."

The woman at the top of that street said, "Do not be daft."

The woman in the shop said, "They will not keep."

And then the woman at the top of that street paid for the net she had come in for and for the second net, and the second one was in her hands inside about two minutes, and she went out and up the south side of that street past the place where the man has been walking down it at half past six every morning since the March before last and the place where the woman has been walking up it at the same hour since November, and got to her own door a quarter of an hour before the end of the morning with two nets of onions in her hands, one in each hand, the way she had come down with the first.

She put the first one in the cupboard by the door where it goes. She put the second one on the space by the cooker where things go that have no other place, and there is not space for it there, and she has moved three things to make some, and it is still there this evening.

She has not decided what to do with it. She has thought about it three times today and each time she has come back to the same place, which is that a net of onions at the end of December in a house where an old woman lives alone is a thing that will be found and eaten and that is the end of it, and that if she is not going to do that with it then she has bought a net of onions for no reason at all, and there is no version of this afternoon in which there is a better answer than the one she has.

Her brother rang at two in the afternoon about the heating at his mother's old house. He rang about it because he had been asked about it and not because he knows anything about it, and she listened to about two minutes of it, and he asked her whether she was all right for Christmas, and she said she was at home, and he said, "Right," and rang off.

She has not told him about the shop. She is not going to tell him about the shop. If she tells him then he will tell the girl, and the girl will know the shop had been let on, and the shop will be a thing that somebody knows about instead of a thing that two women in one street have agreed on without ever agreeing on it.

**SHE BOUGHT A SECOND NET OF ONIONS AT TEN PAST TEN ON THE MONDAY AND PAID FOR IT HERSELF AND CARRIED IT UP HER OWN STREET IN BOTH HANDS, AND IT IS ON THE SPACE BY THE COOKER, AND HER BROTHER RANG HER ABOUT THE HEATING AT TWO AND SHE SAID SHE WAS AT HOME, AND SHE IS NOT GOING TO TELL ANYBODY ABOUT ANY PART OF IT.**
