# Chapter 0979 — Along The Pantile Row

Undated in this draft.

**A STACK OF OLD CLAY PANTILES STANDS ON END AGAINST THE SHED WALL WITH ITS ROLLED HEADS FACING OUT.**

The stack stands against the shed wall at number ten, at the end where the wall is highest and the render is soundest, and it is about four courses deep and twenty tall and built of clay pantiles taken off the roof of a porch that came down years back. They are stacked on their sides with the roll of each one facing out into the yard, which is the way they come off a roof and the way they will go back on if anybody ever wants them to.

Each roll is a half-round channel about three inches across and four deep, running the whole length of the tile, and where twenty of them stand side by side with their rolls in a line there is a row of twenty half-round spaces about the width of two fingers between them. Nothing can be seen into that row from any angle. From the side it is a line of shadow, and from above it is a line of openings going down into the dark between the tiles.

The tiles are of two kinds. The front rank is unglazed pantile, red where the clay was fired hot and pale grey where it was fired soft, and the roll on those has been worn round to nothing by weather on the inside of it. The rank behind that carries a square channel worked into it rather than a roll, and those channels are square too and have nothing in them but dust.

The clay of the rolls is damp through a wet week and keeps what it takes in its pores, and where a roll has a chip out of its lower lip the wet stays in it for days afterwards. The render of the shed wall behind the stack is damp at the bottom for a foot and dry above it, and the stack stands against the wet part of it.

The shed stands at the side of the yard with its felt gone along the front edge over about three feet of it, and that gap is where the water that gets in behind the felt goes. It has not been mended and nothing is going to be put over it this year, so the four foot of wall at the shed end takes the rain on its face and stays wet at the bottom through the whole winter.

The wall down the far side of that yard is brick with a coping of engineering brick on it, and the alley between the two is about two feet wide at its narrowest and four at its widest, and it is where the rain off the coping falls. Nothing grows along the foot of it because nothing has been left lying there long enough, but the brick of the wall is green at the foot of it and the ground between the shed and the wall is mud by the end of a wet week.

The yard itself is flags and hard standing, and the ground at the shed end has gone down by about an inch where the fill beneath it has settled, so that the stack of tiles leans back against the shed to keep itself up. The bed the stack was stood in was earth taken from the top of the yard and spread over the flags without a course of sand under it, and that bed has washed out from the foot of the stack where the coping water comes down.

A brick was laid at the foot of the stack to stop it going, and it lies flat on the ground at the near end and one edge of the tile at that end stands an inch clear of the ground because of it. The rest of the tile above it stands a little further back into the stack, so that the open row is not quite true at the bottom of it.
---

One finger goes into the roll of the top tile at the end of the row and comes up over the lower lip into the half-round, and then is pushed along the row from that tile to the next, lifting a little at the roll and dropping back between each.

Going along the row in that way reads twenty rolls in a length of about eighteen inches, and no two of them are alike. The first four take the finger as readily as a finger takes a cup, and the fifth is a finger's width less, and from the sixth to the eleventh the rolls are worn so far round on their lower lips that the finger comes along the top of them and touches the tile opposite before it reaches the bottom.

From the twelfth to the seventeenth the rolls are back to a shape you could put two fingers into, and these are the same kind as the first four. The last three are the narrow ones from the channel tiles, and into those the finger goes easily and finds a square corner at the bottom of each and a right angle at both ends of it.

Above the first roll the row goes on out of sight, because the tiles stand on each other and the second course lies back over the first. So the finger that can be put into the bottom roll is the only one that can be put into anything at all, and everything above it is shut by the tile that lies across.

Under the row, the tops of the tiles are in their own shade and are dry, and where a roll has worn through to the tile behind it there is a hole of about the width of a pencil that goes straight through the tile into the dark of the stack. Nothing moves under the finger. The tiles do not shift against one another, the roll of the bottom tile does not alter its shape under the pad of it, and the stack stays leaning on the shed wall in the way it was leaning before.

A pantile is made by hand and the roll on it is rolled by hand too, over a length of something round, and no two of them come out the same. On the front rank the variation is small and the rolls are within an inch of each other from the top to the bottom of the stack. On the back rank, where the channel tiles have been used for something else before they came off, the channels are narrow and square and the wire that was in them is still there in some of them, green with rust where it has not gone.

Where two unglazed tiles stand together with a roll each, the rolls meet at their openings and make a space between them that is almost round and almost straight for its four inches of length. Where a channel tile stands between two unglazed ones, the space is made of a round part and two square corners, and the finger finds the corner first every time.

The chips in the lower lips of the rolls are of two kinds. Some are old, with the broken face in them gone the same colour as the rest, which means they have been there since the tiles came off the porch. Some are new, and the broken face in those is a paler and cleaner body of clay that has not been in the weather at all. The wall of the shed behind it is brick under the render, and the render is a thin coat that has come away in two places about a foot above the ground, so that the brick is showing there and the weather has got at it and the clay of the stack leans on the wall with its top inch against render and its bottom against bare brick.
---

The sister comes into the alley from the back step with the shed at her back and the wall at her shoulder, and she stops in the wide part of it where the ground is firm, with not a thing in either hand. The alley is two feet wide at that point and she does not come along it to the stack, and the stack stands against the shed with its row of rolls open to the yard and not to her.

Nothing is put about the tiles or about the space between them. She remarks that the felt has come off the shed at the front again, and that the ground in the alley will be mud by the end of the week, and neither of the two answers either of those things with any particular care.

Her hand goes out for the weight of the washing line, which is being moved in the wind at that moment, and turns out not to need it, and comes back to her side with nothing on it.

The row of rolls stands open along the bottom of the stack for the same length of eighteen inches it has stood open for, with the same two fingers of space in it and the same square corners at the end of it.

The tiles give nothing to the finger that goes along them, yet they keep the row open all winter, and the brick at the foot of the stack stands where it was laid.
