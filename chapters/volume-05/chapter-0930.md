# Chapter 0930 — Round The Inside Curve

Saturday the third of August 2024, at the curved edging of the front walk at number ten from the gate to the step. Round the inside of a turn.

**SHE HAS AN EDGING OF BRICKS LAID IN AN ARC WHERE THE FRONT WALK TURNS ITS CORNER AT NUMBER TEN AND SHE MEANS TO RUN THE PAD OF THE THUMB ROUND THE INSIDE OF THAT CURVE FROM ONE END TO THE OTHER WITH THE REST OF THE HAND LEFT OUTSIDE IT, TO FIND OUT WHERE THE CURVE IS TIGHTEST AND WHAT HAS COLLECTED IN IT.**

The walk was turned in a morning and the arc of edging went down with it, and the walk has been taken up and relaid twice since while the arc has kept its set. It is a quarter-circle of bricks on edge, six feet across the chord, with the bricks set close enough at the tight end that the joints there are a pencil wide. She wants the inside of it, where the curve doubles back on itself and the two faces stand nearest, and the two faces there stand about an inch apart at the tightest point of the whole curve.

The pad of the thumb goes round the concave side and the hand stays outside the bricks. Where the bricks are slick with six summers of a broom the pad knows them at once, and where the joint stands open the pad drops into it and comes out again.

The curve tightens as it goes and the joints narrow with it. Where they are narrowest, about a foot from the step, the thumb passes a joint that has not opened at all, and it is the only joint on the whole arc in that condition. The pad comes off it and goes on round to the wide end, and the two ends of the arc are as different as chalk and cheese under the same hand.

---

A curve set in bricks has no straight joint anywhere on it. Every joint in an arc is a radial joint, and a radial joint in a bed laid on edge is a wedge that either closes on itself or opens along its whole length.

Wedges of that shape cannot be packed with mortar, since a pointer will not go into them, so they are laid dry and bedded in the same lime that goes behind the whole edging. A dry joint in lime closes as the lime swells and opens as it goes, and it opens along the joint rather than across it, which is why a curved edging sheds its stones along the curve rather than out of it. A straight run of edging on the same ground loses the same stones in the same direction, and the difference is that on a curve the stones give no account of the direction they went in.

The arc has lost two stones along its outer face in eleven years and neither came from a joint that opened along the curve. Both came from a bed under them that had gone.

The outer face is the convex side, and the convex side of an arc is where the stones stand proudest, because a stone on the outside of a curve has nothing behind it to lean on and the wall behind takes no part in the work at all. Every stone on that outer face is doing its job by weight alone, and weight alone is enough for eleven years and not for twelve.

---

Six bricks make the tight end of that arc and they were set by eye against a line struck from the step and not against a centre, which is why the sixth one stands a quarter of an inch out of the curve and does not go back.

A brick out of curve on the concave side narrows the walk by a quarter of an inch at knee height, which is the height a person's hip finds and nothing else does. It also takes the corner off the broom on every pass, and the broom corner is a corner of lead and not a thing that can be argued with. The joints either side of that brick are pencil wide and the brick itself is a finger wide across its face, and the finger of extra is the reason the arc has never needed pointing.

The bedding under the arc goes down about a foot and then stops, and below the bedding is the ground of the front path, which is hard from the old metalling and soft at the edges where the roots get under. Roots under an edging lift it. They lift it the same amount every year and it comes back the same amount every year, and an edging that has been lifted and put back eleven times has joints that are wider in the middle than at either end. That arc's joints are widest about two thirds along, which is where the lime is thinnest under them, and it is the only place on the curve where the bricks move against one another in a dry month. The bricks of the arc were thrown up out of a gateway that came down, and they are a mixed lot: hard blue from one building, softer red from another, and two of a pale stock that is not brick at all and does not take mortar.

A pale stock laid on edge in lime goes first, because it drinks the lime instead of resisting it, and it goes soft along its bed first and then loses its arris and then leaves the joint. A pale stock is also the one that goes slick under a wet palm and slick again under a dry one, because it has nothing in its face to give up.

Both of the two pale bricks on that arc are on the outer face and both have lost a corner, and where they have lost a corner the joint behind them is the widest on the curve. The corner that has gone is no bigger than a thumbnail and it is the only part of either brick that shows anything at all on the whole arc. Water from the whole front walk arrives at the step and does a half-turn through that arc before it goes down the gully, and it runs on the concave side the whole way because the concave side is the low side. A run kept on one side of an edging for eleven years takes the face off that side and leaves the other side with a film of lime dust on it that has not been disturbed since the arc was laid.

The lime dust on the convex side is three years old and it rubs off in a season on a sleeve, and the film that puts back is not lime dust but the fine grit the broom carries off the concrete behind it. That grit is slick to the pad and the lime dust was not, and the difference between them is the whole of the shine on the inside of the arc.

Her sister is on the front step when she comes round from the garden, a folded jacket over her arm that she has not set down.

Jacket on her arm, she stood at the step and looked along the walk to the gate, then down at the arc, then along the walk again, and then she shifted the jacket to her other arm and went on round the corner of the house with it. She did not come back along the walk and she did not stop at the arc on her way.

What passed between them was the hens again and whether the run wanted a second shade over the water butt, and that it did not want one while the wall was in the sun till nine.

None of that touched the arc, and the quarter of an inch the sixth brick stands out went unnamed, and the pencil-wide joint at the tight end went unnamed the whole time she was standing there.

The sweep does not lift the stones and the stones do not move towards the middle, and the pale bricks on the outer face keep their two corners whichever way the arc is swept.

The inside of that curve keeps the shine worked out of the corner grit by a broom in six summers, and whenever the walk is swept along it the shine comes back on it inside a month.
