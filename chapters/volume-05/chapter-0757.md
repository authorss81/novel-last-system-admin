# Chapter 0757 — The Rear Step Of The Van

Monday the twelfth of September 2022, in this borough. She is at her own lock-up and yard from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a rear step re-bedded with a chip under one corner and nothing under the other.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES HAS THE BOARD ON HER VAN'S REAR STEP LOOSE AND WEATHERED, THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK RE-BEDS IT WITH A CHIP AT ONE END ONLY, AND THE STEP IS LEFT ROCKING AT THE NEAR CORNER.**

She is a woman in her late forties at the lock-up on Monday. The woman who books work comes to the rear of the van with her own glass paper, takes the second half of the job, and packs it her own way.

---

She is in the lock-up from early with the doors up and the van backed into the yard the way she backs it in. The rack of boards is shut along the far wall. The rag bag is tied on its hook. The blanket she set aside in November is on the top shelf where she put it and has not been on since. The bench is where the bench is, and behind it the box stands on its corner with the strip of gummed paper let go along one length and the lid not nailed, and she goes past it every morning without her hand going near it. The drawer under the bench with the leads and the screws in it has been shut since November and is shut now. The invoice pad is in the pocket of the coat on the nail and she does not take it out.

The van's rear step is a board on two rails with a moulded edge, and the board has worn into a hollow where a boot goes and there is a nail head standing proud of the wood at the near corner, silver with rust round it, high enough to catch the sole of a shoe.

She has a strip of coarse glass paper in the side of the door and a bar, and she means to draw the nail, take the board up, take the hollow out of the top, and set it back down on the same two rails.

The woman who books work comes up the yard at the middle of the morning with her handbag on her shoulder and a piece of paper under her arm.

---

"You are doing that on the tail lift."

"I am doing that on the tail lift."

"You have no packers." The woman looks at the step. "Have you got any packers?"

"I have got a bar and I have got glass paper."

"You have got a bar and you have got glass paper and you have not got packers." She sets her handbag down on the bumper. "Which means you are going to take that board off and put it back on the same two rails with nothing under it, and it will be back where it was in a month with a nail hole either side of the old one."

"You have not got anything under it."

"You have not got anything under it." She takes the bar off the tail lift. "I have not got packers and you have not got packers, and we both know what that is. Then I will find something to put under it."

"You will find something out of your own pocket in about ten minutes and it will be the wrong thing and it will be the right thickness." The woman takes her handbag off the bumper again. "Let me do the top of it. I did a step like this in the spring and I still have some of that paper."

She looks at the woman standing at the tail of her own van.

"You are going to do the top of it."

"I am going to do the top of it." The woman takes her coat off over the handbag. "Give me the step and go and stand at the doors."

---

It is quicker with two. The bar gets under the board and the board comes up off the rails and the underside of it is grey and dry and full of grit, and the near rail has a bright stripe down it where a nail has been pulled along it before.

She stands at the doors with the roller door's motor warm behind her while the woman works the board.

The woman goes across the hollow first with the coarse paper, then back along it the other way, and she keeps the board off the tail lift and works it in the air on her knee with the paper under her own knee, and every so often she puts a thumb into the hollow and feels for the low place.

"You are taking it out on the corner and not in the middle."

"I am taking out the corner because the corner is what your boot goes over first." The woman does not stop. "If I take the middle down to nothing you will have a ridge across the step and I will be the one who put it there."

"It is not a ridge. It is a hollow."

"It is a hollow with a ridge round it, which is the same thing to a person carrying a sideboard." She shifts her knee. "Turn round. That is my elbow."

The board comes back pale and flat on the top and grey underneath, and it is a good piece of work and she cannot fault any of it.

Then it goes back down on the same two rails, and the woman has a chip of ply in her hand the size of a playing card with a thickness taken off the top of the piece by her thumbnail, and she puts it under the near corner and nowhere else. The board comes down on the chip and stands up along that one corner while it lies flat on the bare rail the whole length of the other end.

"That is beautiful." She looks at the step from above. "Look at that. That is not rocking."

"That is not rocking at the near end."

---

"It is not rocking anywhere."

"It is rocking at this end." The woman puts her hand flat on the step and presses down at the near corner, and the board tips about the far end of the rail and the near side comes up off the chip with a click. "That chip is doing the whole board at this end and there is nothing at that end. It is a see-saw now. Somebody puts a sideboard on it in the wet and the whole weight comes on that corner and this corner lifts, and then the water gets in and stands in it and in the spring that board is grey to the nail again."

"Put another one under that end."

"I will put another one under that end." The woman takes the bar and knocks the board up at the far corner and looks at the gap under the rail and then into the pocket of her coat, and she has nothing in the pocket of her coat. "I have not got one on me and I am not cutting a piece off that rail at the end of your job."

"It is a quarter of the thickness of that."

"It is a quarter of the thickness of that and it will be right." She takes the board down off the chip and sets it back on the bare rail and knocks it home with the heel of her hand and stands back. "And you will find a chip. That is not hard and it is not anybody else's job. I have done the bed and I have done the edge and I have not done the packing, and I will tell the yard what I have done so they can see I have not taken all of it on."

"You are going to tell the yard."

"I always tell the yard." She picks up her coat. "It saves an argument at the end of the month and it is the only thing I have ever been accused of doing properly."

---

She stands at the tail of the van with her hand on the bumper and looks at the step, and then she watches it go out of the yard and down the lane at a speed that is not fast and not slow.

From above it is a good-looking step. Sanded pale, the moulded edge clean, the hollow gone out of the top so that the middle is flat and the two edges stand a little proud where the paper crossed them. There is a nail hole at each corner and a bright stripe down the near rail.

She puts her palm flat on the far corner and presses down and lets go. Nothing moves, and that is the half of it that is true. Then she puts her palm flat over the chip and presses down, and the far corner lifts off the bare rail with a knock she can hear in the yard with nothing else in it.

She goes in and stands a while by the shut drawer with her hand flat on the top of it, past the bench and the corner of the box with its lid not nailed, and out in the yard behind her the bay where the van stood is bare concrete with a bright stripe run down the rail by all the water that has come off that van in two years.
