# Chapter 0869 — Board Against Brick

Wednesday the thirteenth of December 2023, in the back passage at number ten from the time the door has gone light until the middle of the afternoon, and the depth of a path board against the depth of a brick is the whole of that Wednesday.

**THERE ARE THREE BOARDS OFF THE WALL OF THE BACK PASSAGE AT NUMBER TEN AND A DRY BRICK OFF THE HEAP BESIDE THEM AND SHE MEANS TO SET ONE THUMBNAIL INTO THE END OF A BOARD AND LAY THE OTHER THUMB ALONG ITS FACE, AND HER SISTER IS AT THE SHED WINDOW FROM OUTSIDE WITH A HAND FLAT ON THE OPENING.**

The boards are path boards. They are three of about six feet, they are softwood, they were cut off a pallet that was used once for shuttering and they have been against that wall since the summer with the sawed ends facing the passage.

The brick is a brick.

There is one in the heap at the end of the passage because there is one in the heap and nobody has ever thrown it out. It is a stock brick, which means an older brick than the ones in the wall, and it is the only one of the two dozen in that heap with a whole face on it.

A thumb and a finger are no use on a board.

That is the first thing to say, and it is the thing that wastes most of an afternoon if you do not know it. A thumb and first finger close on about an inch, and they close on three-quarters of an inch as readily, and they close on half an inch with no difficulty at all, because there is nothing about a board under an inch that stops the fingers meeting. A hand that can close on a thing to within a quarter of an inch is not a rule. It is a guess with a hand on the end of it.

What works on a board is the corner.

The corner of a board is the one place where two faces meet at a right angle and both of them are true, and the length of that corner is the thickness of the board, and it can be read off with a thumbnail the way you read the rim of a coin.

So she sets the nail of her left thumb into the end grain and lays the pad of the right thumb along the face and closes them onto the corner, and the corner comes up against the edge of the thumbnail and she knows how deep it is before her eyes have looked at it.

She does it at four places along the board and it is three-quarters of an inch for about five feet and then it is not.

At the last foot, where the board was cut for the shutter, it runs out to about half an inch. Somebody took that board to a saw and cut a bevel off the end of it and left it leaning against a wall, and the bevel has been there since the summer. The saw marks in it are still bright in the grooves because they are narrow enough to keep their sap in, and there is no wear on any of them at all, and there is no wear on the edges of these boards anywhere, because a board that has never been walked on is a board that has never been anything but a side wall in a passage.

The brick is a different job with your hands.

A brick is two and a quarter inches, which is thicker than a thumb is wide, so the corner will not take it and the hand has to go round two edges. She puts her hand across the face of it and closes on the thickness, and it is two and a quarter at the end nearest the passage and two and a half at the other.

Two and a half.

That is a stock brick and it has been out of the ground for a hundred and fifty years, and it has got what every brick that has been out of the ground that long has got: one soft end that came out of the bed first and one hard end that did not. The soft end has lost about a quarter of an inch over a hundred and fifty years of being carried about in barrows and stood in heaps and never having been cleaned once.

Which means the brick is two and a quarter at the hard end and two and a half at the soft one, and every brick in every heap in this yard has that same quarter of an inch in it somewhere, and there is not one course laid in this town with two bricks in it that are the same depth.

She puts her thumbnail on the soft end and drags it the length of the brick and it is soft there, not in the way of a soft brick, which crumbles, but soft in the way of a face that has been rubbed at by weather and by sacks and by the edges of other bricks for longer than anybody alive.

The board is three-quarters of an inch for five feet of its six and half an inch at the sawed end.

The brick is two and a quarter.

So the board is a third of the brick.

That is the whole of the arithmetic and it is not much, and it has taken the whole of a Wednesday.

What it comes to is that the three boards under the back path at number ten are one and a half inches of wood on a bed of sand, and a course laid on them wants two and a quarter and there is not two and a quarter there. So the flags on that path have been laid on boards three-quarters of an inch thick where a flag wants to sit on something solid, and the one at the door end has gone down about half an inch into the sand, and the door across the top of it has had to be lifted for nine years.

And she has been blaming the door.

It is worth being exact about that, because it is the whole of what an afternoon of hands is for. A door that will not close is a thing you can blame on the door, and the door is a thing that can be planed, and planing it costs nothing and does nothing. What is wrong with it is nine years of a sand bed with a board too thin in it, and the door is the last thing in the chain and the door is doing its job exactly.

What would put it right is not planing and it is not a new door. It is a fourth board, and a fourth board under that path would take the bed from one and a half inches up to two and a quarter, which is the depth of a brick, and the flag at the door end would come up out of the sand it is in and the door would come down onto its frame and shut.

She has the fourth board. There is a fourth board, it is the one with the sawed end, and it is half an inch at that end and three-quarters for the rest of it, and a half-inch end under the door is no use to a door that is trying to close.

Which is the answer to the question, and the question was about the door, and neither of them is going anywhere.

Her sister comes to the shed window in the middle of the afternoon and puts a hand flat on the opening from outside and stands there.

She was asked whether the small chisel was in there and it was not there. She would come back for it. The front step at her end had gone loose at the corner, and it would have. The whole path had gone, and it had. It wanted doing before the rain, and it did, and she did not ask after anything else, and she took her hand off the opening and went back down the side of the shed.

The shed window faces the side of the passage and the boards are at the end of it, and she stayed at the window for the length of what she had to say and no longer.

---

She puts the three boards back against the wall with their sawed ends facing the passage, because they were facing the passage when she came out, and she puts the brick back in the heap at the end of it with its soft end out.

The board is a third of the brick, and the brick is the one that was in the wall.
