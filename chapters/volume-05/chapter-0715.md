# Chapter 0715 — Two Pairs On One Plate

Monday the fourth of April 2022, in this borough, and not in this borough, because the house is on the far side of the ring road. She is in that house from first light until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a hall wall and a telephone taken off it.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES TAKES A TELEPHONE OFF A HALL WALL IN AN EMPTY HOUSE BECAUSE THE ROOM HAS TO BE EMPTY, FINDS TWO PAIRS OF WIRE ON THE BACK OF THE PLATE JOINED TO EACH OTHER, PUTS IT IN A BAG WITH HER OWN RAGS, AND TELLS THE MAN WITH THE KEYS AT THE LOCK-UP THAT THE ROOM IS CLEAR.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is in an empty house all day and at a lock-up twice. A man with keys is at the lock-up doors in the middle of the day. She speaks to him at the doors.

---

She starts with the hall because the hall is the room that has to be empty first.

The house is on a road off the ring road and it has been empty since before the spring and there are eight boxes in it that are not hers and a heap by the stairs that is not hers, and the woman who booked it told her on Friday that the brother had the back room taken back and had not been answering, and she had said yes to the go-back in a kitchen with a clamp standing up out of the middle of the table, and the reason she had said yes is that eight boxes are still on the invoice as a take-away and there is no line anywhere on the invoice that says a woman went in with a key at midday and put half of it back.

So she goes in. The front door is off the latch and it is off the latch in every one of these houses and she has never once reported that.

The hall is narrow with the stairs going up on the left and a front room door on the right and a strip of floorboard at the bottom of the stairs that has come up and gone back down so many times it has gone soft. There is a telephone on the wall by the front door at shoulder height, cream, round, and the lid is off it and lying on the sill beside it, and the bell is lying loose in the bottom of the case on a flex of its own about a foot long, with two bare wires out of the end of it, and somebody put it that way and never came back for it.

She stands in front of it with her hands behind her back for a while.

She has taken three of these off walls since the spring. She has put two of them in bags and one of them back on a wall because the woman who lived there wanted it, and she has never once said to anybody what was on the back of the plate.

---

She takes the cover off before she takes the telephone off, which is the wrong order and she knows it is the wrong order.

She puts the blade of a screwdriver into the slot and turns and the cover comes away in her hand with a corner broken, and she lays it on the strip of floorboard with the soft edge. Behind it on the plate there are four screws and two screws are in and two are hanging, and out of the plate come two pairs of wire.

One pair is old, the colours gone out of it, brown and green gone to grey, and the other pair is newer with the colours still on it, and the two pairs are not going to where the plate says they go.

They are joined to each other. Blue to brown. She can see where it was done, a twist in the sleeving and a bit of tape round the twist gone brown, and the two pairs leave the plate together and go up the wall into the plaster.

She stands with the screwdriver in her hand and looks at it.

And what comes into her head is not a fault and it is not a job. What comes into her head is a street she cleared in the winter before last, and a different telephone on a different wall, and the same tape round the same kind of twist.

She has never said that out loud to anybody, and she is not going to say it out loud to a man with keys in a lock-up in a car park.

The room has to be empty. That is the whole reason she is in this house.

---

She unscrews the plate off the plaster and pulls the two pairs out of the hole and lets them drop.

The wire is longer than she expected. It comes down about two feet below where it went in, because somebody on some other day has run it down the wall and across and it has been plastered over where it crossed, and she can feel the lump of it with her thumb as she works.

She wraps both pairs round the body of the telephone twice and ties them with a piece of string off the bag of rags, and the telephone goes into the bag with the rags on top so it cannot knock the tin things, and she ties the neck of the bag.

Then she stands in the empty hall and looks at the wall.

There is a clean rectangle behind the plate where the paper has not gone the same colour as the paper around it, and inside the rectangle there are two small holes for the screws and one longer smear where a strip of tape kept a label that is not there now.

She takes the cloth off the top of her trolley and wipes the wall round the rectangle and round the holes and round the smear, and she does not do anything about the rectangle, because a rectangle on a wall in an empty house is not a thing anybody pays for.

She puts the cover in her coat pocket, broken corner and all, because it is not hers and it is not rubbish, and because that pocket has four other people's covers in it already and one more makes no difference to anybody.

---

She eats in the cab with the doors locked and the bag on the passenger seat with the rags showing at the neck.

The van pulls a little to the left on the long straight, as it has since the spring. She writes the figure for the job on the pad on her knee and puts no line for anything else on it, and she writes the hall as a separate line because the hall is a separate line, and the telephone does not go on the invoice at all, because a telephone is not a thing anybody booked.

Her own mobile is in her pocket and it has not gone off. The card with the man's number on it is in the door pocket where the card lives, a mobile and a landline and a symbol at an angle, and the man who prints it does gates as well as kitchens, and she has had that card since before Christmas.

She has never rung it.

She has had the number of the man who built the stool in her phone under a unit off the industrial estate since the spring as well, and she has never rung that one either, and the reason is the same reason, and the reason is a bench that does not exist any more.

She turns the pad round and puts the lid on it and puts it away.

---

The man with the keys is at the lock-up doors at the middle of the day with a cup of tea going cold in his hand.

She has the bag at her feet. He looks down at it and then at the trolley and then at her face.

"You've had a good morning," he says.

"Room's clear," she says. "Hall's clear. Front room's clear. Upstairs the same as when you shut it."

"You'd have been quicker with a breaker."

"I know."

He looks at the bag for a bit longer than a man needs to look at a bag.

"What's that in it."

"Rags."

"That a telephone in with the rags."

"That a telephone off a wall with the rags," she says. "It's got no bell in it. It's not mine. It's not on the invoice."

"Right," he says, and drinks his tea, and does not ask which house it came out of, and she does not tell him.

He is a man who has been running a lock-up longer than she has been running a van and he has the thing down to a habit now of not asking a woman where a thing in a bag came from, and she has never once wondered whether that is kindness, and she is not going to start today.

---

She loads the bags into the van before the light goes and the telephone stays where it is in the bag.

Back in the house she takes the eight boxes that are not hers out of the front room and puts them by the door, which is what the woman who booked it wanted, and she puts the heap by the stairs straight because a heap by the stairs is not a heap, it is a shape somebody had.

She does not open the eight boxes. She has never opened one of them and she is not starting in a house where the brother has already taken one room back.

She stands in the front room with the curtains open for the first time since she came and looks at the carpet, where a wardrobe stood until the winter before last and left a rectangle of unfaded colour in a floor that has been walked on since.

Two rectangles in this house. One in the carpet and one on the hall wall.

She looks at both of them from the doorway and then she goes out and lets the front door be.

---

She is in the van at the lock-up before dark with the bag off the seat and on her knee.

She takes the telephone out of the bag and looks at it. The dial is dull with dust and the finger holes are all the same size, which is old, which is not the one thing she would have picked. She puts her thumb in the hole where the one goes and does not take it out again, because taking it out again is a thing to do in a van in a car park with a man watching, and she has never done a job in front of a man she was not booked by.

She puts it back in the bag and pushes it down under the rags, and ties the neck, and the knot is the one she has tied since October, and she does it without looking.

Then she puts her elbow on the bag so it does not move for the drive, because there is a place on that road where the road has been dug up and the van drops into it whatever speed she takes it at, and a telephone that came off a wall in a house on the far side of the ring road is not going to have the worse of that for nothing.

---

The man with the keys is doing the doors and she has to come round the side of the van to get at her bag, and the bench is between her and the back of it.

The drawer is under that bench. It has been shut since the middle of November and there are leads and screws in it and a coil of something she has never asked him about, and in ten months of Tuesdays and Saturdays it is the only drawer she has been in a house with and not opened.

To get into it she would have to go down on one knee on a concrete floor with her back to a man who is four foot away with a cup in his hand, and she would have to do it with her face turned away, and he would have to be looking at the ceiling.

She picks the bag up off the seat and puts it in the cab and shuts the door on it.

---

At dark she says it out loud to the empty cab, which is a thing she does perhaps twice a month and only when the engine has been off a while.

"Four screws and two in," she says. "Two pairs. Taped at the plate."

And then, because the cab is dark and the road is empty and there is nobody to hear a sentence that would cost her a day:

"Third one."

And she starts it, and the van pulls to the left on the long straight, and she does not turn the radio on, and the bag rides up and down on the seat with the wire inside it shifting and settling, and at the far end of the road she stops at the lights and there is a woman on the pavement with a bag of shopping waiting to cross and she waits for her.

The woman gets to the middle of the road on the green and carries the bag over and the other arm gets up to hold her hair off her face, and she is gone into the far pavement before the lights change back.

The bag goes up and down on the seat for another mile and then the road straightens and it settles, and the two pairs of wire inside it shift once and lie still, and she does not put her hand on it again for the rest of the way.
