# Chapter 0984 — Down Into The Plate Hollow

Undated in this draft.

**TWO STEEL ROAD PLATES LIE ONE ON THE OTHER IN THE BAY WITH THE LOWER EDGE TURNED UP.**

The plates lie in the bay at the lock-up, one on the other, and the lower one has its edge turned up along the length of it so that it makes a shallow trough about a foot wide. The upper plate sits down into that trough and closes it, and what is left between them is a hollow about three inches wide that runs the whole six feet of the plate and is shut at either end.

They came off a job and were carried in on a barrow and put down on the bay floor, which is old concrete about two inches thick over a base nobody has looked at. The lower plate was set down first on its turned edge and the upper one was put on top of it by a person who did not know which of the two was which, and both have been on the bay floor since the autumn.

The turned edge of the lower plate runs to a quarter of an inch thick and stands about four inches above its own plane, and the hollow between it and the plate above is closed along its bottom by nothing at all. It is deep at the middle of the plate and shallow at its two ends, because the lower plate is dished, having been used to spread soft ground and having taken the shape of it. Where the two plates touch at the turned edges the steel is bright on the edge and rust-coloured on the flat of it, and where they do not touch, because the upper plate has run a quarter of an inch out of true along its length, there is a line of daylight along one side of the hollow about the width of a coin. The steel of the lower plate is damp along the turned edge where the floor keeps it, and the plate above it is not, because it gets the air and the light off the bay floor and dries out first.

This is the middle bay of the three, and because its shutter is down the plates stand in open air all day and can be got at from the yard without anybody going into the office. The floor of the bay slopes about an inch in eight towards the gully at the front, which means that whatever is under the lower plate has had a wet winter. Rain that comes in under the shutter blows across the floor in sheets and goes out through the gap at the front of the bay, and it does so across the plates rather than under them, so the plates are wet all over every time it rains and dry all over within two hours of the rain stopping.

The rack stands along the far wall behind the plates and carries rolled sections, and a bank of shuttering boards leans against the wall between the rack and the plates with its ends off the floor. The oil film on the bay floor comes off the shutter and off the wheels and has been worked into the concrete until it is a dark patch that spreads across the middle of the bay and is about a foot across where it is darkest.

The plates lie on that patch. Nothing about the floor beneath them has moved since they were put down, and the plates have not slid a quarter of an inch in any direction, and the hollow between them has not filled with anything at all.

The floor under the oil is gritty with the fines that have come out of the concrete as the shutter has been dragged across it, and where a plate edge has pressed on that patch for a season the grit has been pressed into a skin about the thickness of a coin. The skin is damp under the plate and dries out at its edge within the hour, and no eye will ever go back to it twice.
---

One fingertip goes down into the hollow between the two plates and is pressed on to the bottom of it, first at the end nearest the shutter, and then the whole length of it is walked and it is pressed again at the far end.

At the near end the finger goes down about an inch and a half before it comes on the bottom of the trough, which is the floor of the plate standing on its edge. At the far end it goes down half that. The reason is the dish in the lower plate, and the hollow follows it exactly, being deepest where the plate is most out of its own plane.

Along the bottom of the hollow the fingertip finds that turned edge again, and the curve of it is nothing like smooth. It is rolled, in the way that a plate's edge is rolled to stiffen it, and the roll has a hollow along it about the width of a pencil where the steel has come back on itself.

In that hollow there is about a fortnight of oil and grit, and it is packed there rather than lying on the floor because the hollow is the one place on the whole of the two plates that a broom does not reach. The fingertip comes out of it black to the second joint and the black is not off the hand afterwards.

The steel of the two plates is the same throughout. It has the raised diamond of a chequer plate on its upper face, which is what it is sold for and what it is sold for whether anybody needs it or not, and it has the chequer on the top of the lower plate too, which nobody sees and which is still there underneath.

The rust on the plates is not one colour. There is a brown that comes off on a hand where the plates were wet for weeks, and there is a grey that does not come off at all, and there is a bright orange in the creases of the chequer at the ends where the water stood, and the three of them are in bands about four inches wide going the length of both plates. Along the line of daylight where the upper plate does not meet the turned edge, the edge of the upper plate has been rubbed by whatever came past it and is bright along a strip of about a foot, and the bright strip is in the middle of a grey band, which is where the bay door gets swung. The plates are the pair that goes under a track on soft ground and under nothing else, and they weigh about half a hundredweight between them, which is why they are in a bay and not in a rack and why nobody has ever tried to put them anywhere else.
---

At the head of the site steps stands the woman who books work, the steps behind her and the plates in front, and her hands without anything in them.

She stays at the head of the steps and does not come down into the bay. The handrail is at her left shoulder and she puts a hand on it there for the sake of having something to stand against, and it is used for nothing else, and then her hand comes off it again.

The plates are not spoken of. She stands on the steps and the other of them remarks on the shutter being down since before the weather turned, and that nobody has turned it up, and the answer given is that nobody wants it up and that the office is warmer with it down. The bay is quiet behind her, and the plates lie on the oil patch with the hollow between them the same depth at one end as at the other, and the rolled edge of the lower one keeps its pencil's width of packed oil and grit where nothing reaches it.

The hollow will not take a broom and it will not take a foot, and it will go on lying along the whole six feet of that plate with the daylight along one side of it and the darkness along the other, and there is nothing whatever in it that can be seen.
