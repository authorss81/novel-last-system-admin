# Chapter 0785 — The Rear Step Of The Van

Saturday the fourteenth of January 2023, in this borough. She is at her own lock-up and at the back of her own bay from opening up to the roller door going down again, and the whole of it is the rear step of a van and a hinge that has gone.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES HAS THE REAR STEP OF HER VAN PACKED ON A SINGLE CHIP AND ONE HINGE PULLED OPEN, AND MEANS TO PACK IT ON TWO AND FIT A NEW HINGE, THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK TAKES THE WHOLE STEP OFF THE FLOOR, PUTS THE BED OF IT DOWN ONTO THE CHASSIS RAIL AND PUTS IN A NEW HINGE WITH THE OLD PIN OUT AND A NEW PIN IN, AND THE STEP IS RIGHT, AND INSIDE A MONTH SHE IS PACKING IT AGAIN BY HAND WITH WHATEVER IS TO HAND, BECAUSE THE STEP IS NOT THE THING THAT IS WRONG WITH THE WAY SHE LOADS.**

She is a woman in her late forties at her own lock-up on Saturday. The woman who books work comes into the bay in the middle of the morning with the roller door up over her.

---

The van stands in the bay nose to the back wall. Along the outside wall the four pallets are on edge with a stack of blocks in the middle of every one of them, and the blocks have been wet through since June. The trolley is against the far wall with an empty deck and its two back legs hanging a yard short of the top of the ramp. The drawer in the bench is shut with the leads and the screws in it. The box behind the bench has not been opened since the summer and nobody has said a thing about its lid either way. There is grit trodden into the floor at the leg of the rack where it was swept to and never carried out.

The rear step is under the back doors of the van.

It is a folding steel tray on two brackets bolted up through the floor. The tray drops to about the height of the top of your boot and the whole thing goes up under the sill out of the way when the doors are shut. In the bed of that tray there should be two rubber chips, moulded blocks the size of the palm of your hand, one at each end, and the tray bedded level front to back on the two of them.

There is a chip at the back of it and there has never been one at the front.

So the tray is bedded on that chip at the back and the front of it has come down about the thickness of a finger onto the brackets, and the whole bed has twisted. It sits down on the left more than on the right. And where the left hinge is fixed to its bracket the leaves have opened up and the pin has been standing in the gap between them for a fortnight, bright at the shoulder, and the tray plays when you put a sack on it.

Her plan is two chips and a new hinge. She has a box of them in the office. There are two more rubber chips in it and there is a hinge with two new pins, and she means to bed the tray level on the two of them, bolt the new hinge over the old one, and be finished inside a week.

---

The woman who books work comes in under the roller door in the middle of the morning and stops a few feet inside the bay and looks down at the step without coming further.

"You are not fitting that hinge."

"I am fitting that hinge."

"You are bolting a new hinge over the top of an old one, and then in a month you will be bolting another one over that." She comes further in. "How is that tray bedded."

"It is bedded."

"Lift the front lip of it."

She crouches and puts her fingers under the front of the tray and lifts. The front comes up about the thickness of a finger and the back does not come up at all, and the pin in the loose hinge goes up with it, and the tray twists under her hand as it comes.

"That is bedded."

"At the back and hanging at the front. You have a chip under the back of it and nothing under the front of it, and the tray is sitting on the brackets at the front, and it is nowhere near where a tray ought to be bedded."

"Then I will put the second chip under the front of it."

"You will bed a twisted tray on two lumps. The front of it will be a lump and the back of it will be a lump and nothing in between them." She crouches the other way and looks along the bed of it from the low side. "And how heavy is the heaviest thing you put on that step."

"What do you mean."

"A roll of netting. A drum. A box of hinges."

"A roll of netting. A box of hinges."

"How do you get it up there."

She does not answer at once. She puts her hand on the back of the tray and looks at the back doors of the van.

"I get it up off the yard floor on my shoulder."

---

The woman who books work goes down on one knee in the bay beside the step and looks up the length of the underside of the tray.

"Do you know where that tray is fixed."

"It is bolted through the floor."

"It is bolted through the floor to two brackets, and those brackets are up under the floor about a foot, and there is nothing between the bed of that tray and the top of the chassis rail but a foot of air." She puts two fingers under the tray and lifts it again. "Everything you have ever put on that step has gone down through that tray, through those brackets, into that hinge, and down the front of the hinge into the bracket, and it has been doing it since the van came."

"So the bracket is bent."

"The bracket is not bent. It is doing what it was put there to do, and it will do it for years more. And you have been putting a new washer on that hinge every winter since it started to open, and the hinge has not opened because anything is wrong with the hinge."

---

Then they take it off.

The tray comes off the van rather than out from under it, four bolts, and it has to be dragged across the bay floor on its back with the doors open and the roller door up over both of them, which puts a long scrape along the underside of the sill that neither of them will ever mention.

It stands against the wall by the rack of boards when it is off, and the woman who books work looks at what is under where it was, which is the chassis rail running the length of the van about a foot down, and lays a length of batten along the top of it.

"The bed of that tray goes down here. Not on those two brackets."

"That is the chassis rail."

"That is the chassis rail and it is the only thing in that van a roll of netting was ever meant to be stood on." She takes the brackets off with a spanner, four of them, two a side. "Bring yours down and bolt them to the bed of the tray so the bed comes down onto the rail, and then there is nothing between the tray and the rail but the tray. Shim at the front until it is level. Then the chips go in, and the chips are what a tray is meant to ride on and not a thing to keep it off a bracket."

"The bed sits on the rail."

"On the rail, and the hinge."

---

The new hinge goes on last, with the old pin out.

She does not unbolt the old one. She puts a bar through the leaves, lifts the pin out, puts the new pin through the new hinge and drives it home, and the old hinge stays on the tray underneath where it can be seen and cannot be got at.

That is not tidiness. That is the pin.

"You can get that old hinge off now."

"I can and I am not going to." She turns the tray over on the brackets bolted to its bed. "That pin has been in that hinge for years with everything you have ever put on that step going through it, and it has opened a cup in the leaves and gone bright at the shoulder. Take the old hinge away and you take the cup with it, and the new pin stands in a leaf that has never been opened, and a hinge that has never been opened is a hinge that has not started yet."

---

They put it back in and bolt it down and bed it, and that takes the rest of the day.

She shims at the front with offcuts of the batten until the bed is level from side to side, and they try it with the spirit level off the shelf and again with the back of a chisel. The two chips go in last, at the front and at the back, and the tray beds on them and does not touch the brackets anywhere.

The doors are shut and the tray goes up under the sill and comes down again without a sound.

Then they put a roll of netting on it.

She gets it up on her shoulder the way she always has, from behind, in one movement, and this time the tray does not twist under it and the hinge does not move and the pin does not go up in the bracket. The roll goes forward against the front of the tray and stops, and the tray stays level, and she puts her hand on the roll and the whole of the weight of it goes down through the chips and the bed of the tray and the chassis rail and the two brackets that are bolted to the tray doing nothing at all.

"That is the step."

"Yes." She is packing the spanner away. "Do that every time from now on and that hinge will outlive the van."

---

Then a month comes and goes and she packs it again herself.

She is in the bay on a wet Thursday with a roll of netting to go and there is nothing in the bed of the tray to lay it on, because the chip at the back has gone flat and cracked through the middle and the one at the front has gone down to nothing, and the tray is bedded on two brackets again with a washer under the back of it and a length of green plastic conduit cut off square at both ends under the front of it.

She does not think about it. She gets the roll up on her shoulder in one movement out of the yard the way she always has, and the front of the tray comes down about a finger onto the conduit, and the hinge opens about as much as it ever did, and the pin stands up in the bracket bright at the shoulder with a fresh ring of wear round it.

She puts her hand on the roll to keep it from turning and looks down into the bed of it at the green conduit and the cracked chip, and she does not think about the chassis rail a foot below it, or the four brackets she has never taken off, or the two sound chips still in the box in the office where they have been since January.

She shuts the doors and the tray goes up under the sill and out of the way, and it is the best tray under that sill in the county, and she packs it herself, again, with whatever is to hand.
