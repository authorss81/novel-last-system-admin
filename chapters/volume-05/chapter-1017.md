# Chapter 1017 — The First Half Of A No

Undated in this draft.

**THE PANTRY OFF THE BACK OF NUMBER TWELVE HAS A STONE FLOOR AND ONE SHELF.**

That pantry at the back of that house is a cold little room about five feet by four with a floor of stone slabs about a foot across that are not level across the middle of it, and a door on the passage side that has been painted four times and is the same green as the last three, and no window of any kind and a bulb on a flex out of a rose in the ceiling. There is one shelf on the left-hand wall about five feet off the floor, screwed to two brackets that are into brickwork, and the wall that shelf is screwed to has gone out of level over about forty years of a house settling and the shelf has gone with it, so that anything set on the far end of it stays where it is put and anything set on the near end comes slowly to the wall.

It is the coldest room in that house and the only one a person can stand in for an hour without anybody talking to them. The mother has been in and out of it since she came down the river, and it is where she went when her daughter put the whole of the last seven weeks into words at that table with the six-house page lying in front of her, and it is where she went again after that, and she is in it now with the door standing open on the passage behind her and her outdoor coat still on.

He came up that frontage with nine words ready and he had been carrying them since the middle of the day before, and he got them out in the passage with his coat still on, and she took all nine of them from inside the pantry with the door standing open.

He put them out in the flat voice a man uses when he sets down a length of wood and takes his hands off it. He wanted to do the wall behind her range.

She let it stand a while. She put her hand flat on the shelf at the near end of it, which is the end where a thing slides towards the wall, and looked at the two inch gap between the shelf and the wall, and then she took her hand off.

"No."

---

"Then tell me why and I will not come back about it."

"You have been in this house nine times since the spring and you have never once come about that wall, and I am going to give you the whole of it and then you can go and be somewhere else." She kept her back to the passage. "I came down the river on the Tuesday morning and my daughter put the whole of it into words at that table with that page lying where it was, and what she told me was that a man came about my range in the spring and did not do it and came again at the back of the summer and did not do it then either. And a man has been in this house since I came back and has not come about that wall once, and he stood at my window that morning with the sash in his hand and would not tell me his name. I put those two things together the second night and I did not like it and I have not got over it."

"I have not come about it for my own sake. I have not been able to get to it four times and you have beaten me to it every time."

"I know why you could never get near it and that was not the reason you have just given me." She turned round then. "I was on that book at seventeen. My mother was on it before me and my mother is the woman whose name is on a piece of paper in a folded bit of paper in this apron that I did not ask for and cannot make anything of, and I have carried that in my apron since before you came up this frontage the first time. You do not get offered that. Nobody walks up a frontage and offers you that. And I am not having a thing done to my wall because a man has had a wet fortnight and a yard that is a quarter of what it was and nothing coming in the spring."

He stood in the opening of that doorway and worked out that she was not refusing him because she thought he was no good at it. What she was refusing him for was that she thought him good at it and he had come at the wrong moment of the year, and both of those are refusals and only one of them is a thing a man can argue with.

He did not go. That is a bigger thing than anything else that happened in that room, and he wants it in ink that he knew what it was while he was doing it. He put the flat of his hand against the door frame beside him and left it there, and he did not go past her, and he did not go forward, and he stood in the opening of a cold pantry in a house where he had never once stayed anywhere, in front of a woman who could have put him out of it by saying one word and did not.

She waited for him to go and he did not go, and about the time it takes to stand still twice she understood that he was never going to.

"Now you are a man standing in my pantry."

"Yes."

"And I have not asked you for anything and I am not going to ask you for anything, and you know why I am not, and I have told you why I am not."

"You have told me why you will not have it. You have not told me why you will not ask for it."

Nobody on that frontage had ever had that put to her, and he had not known until it was out that it was the true question. He had come up this frontage for four months with a sentence in his mouth, and the sentence had always been about himself, and he worked out standing in that pantry that the whole of it was a man waiting to be asked, and that a man who has spent ten years arranging not to be asked is not a man nobody wants him, and that this woman had worked all of that out in about four seconds and had put the right question into the room.

She came across and stood on the far side of that opening so that he had the passage behind him.

"I will not have that wall offered to me," she told him. "So I am going to ask you for it. Do the wall behind my range."

---

"Yes."

"You have not asked me what it would cost you."

"I am not a penny short for a flue and a tray and eight days of a man time, and I have never in my life asked a woman what a thing costs her, and I do not know what that wall wants and neither do you." He got it out anyway. "And I agreed to it before I had worked out whether I could do it, and I am telling you that I agreed, and you can put it down as a man who was glad to be asked and who should have thought for a minute first."

"I am not going to put anything down about any of this." She stayed in the doorway. "You have been asked for that wall by a woman who was entitled to turn you down and did.  That is the only part of it that belongs to anybody and the rest of it is yours to work out."

She went back into the pantry then and he stood in the passage and worked out that nothing in that house stands a quarter of an hour different from what it stood like before he came up that yard, and that everything in the rest of his life has gone over in twenty minutes, and that he has no idea at all what he is doing.

In the passage behind that pantry the door stands open where she left it when she came across and asked him, and he is going to have to walk past that opening every working morning for a fortnight to get to that wall.
