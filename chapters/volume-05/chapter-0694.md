# Chapter 0694 — A Ladder In A Back Room That Is Not Hers

Friday the seventh of January 2022, in this borough. She is in her van and in a house from first light until dark, and the whole of the day is a back room with a step ladder folded against the wall of it and three things in this borough she cannot account for.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES FINDS A STEP LADDER FOLDED AGAINST THE WALL OF A BACK ROOM IN A HOUSE SHE IS CLEARING AND DOES NOT TAKE IT, LOOKS AT A STRIP OF DARK TAPE ROUND ITS SECOND RAIL AND A CHALK MARK ON ITS THIRD TREAD AND CANNOT PLACE EITHER, HAS A TINNED FISH BEHIND THE PASSENGER BENCH OF HER VAN THAT HAS NOT MOVED SINCE OCTOBER AND A BLANKET IN A BAG OF HER OWN RAGS AND NO WAY OF FINDING OUT WHO ANY OF IT BELONGS TO, AND WRITES THE FIGURE FOR THE JOB ON HER PAD AND NOTHING ELSE.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is on her own all day except for a man in his sixties. She does not speak to anybody else.

---

The back room is the last thing and she always does the back room last.

The house is a semi and it is a man's and he has been in a nursing home since about October and his daughter has the keys and has been saying for a while that Friday is the last Friday, and today is Friday.

The horse van comes at the end and goes away with the lot, and the man in the doorway is the son and he is in his sixties and he lives in a different town and has come for the day and does not want to be standing in a house that is being emptied.

"You all right down there?"

"I'm all right down here."

"That's a lot in one go."

"It's a lot in one go."

---

The back room is down two steps off the hall and it has a window looking into a side passage and it has been used as a place to put things, which is what a back room in a semi is for.

There is a bed frame with the mattress off it and a cardboard box of photographs with the top torn off and a chest of drawers with all the drawers out and stacked on the top, and there is a step ladder.

It is folded and it is standing against the far wall under the window with its tray end up and its feet on the carpet, and it is leaning about two inches off the wall, the way a thing leans when it has been put down in a hurry by somebody in a hurry.

She sees it at the door.

---

She does not go and get it. She stands in the doorway for a moment and then she does the bed frame.

She takes the frame apart quickly because it is a bed frame and it comes apart, and she puts the pieces in a pile by the door for the horse van man, and she comes back for the box of photographs and puts a hand in the top of it and stops, because a box of photographs is not a job she does and she has never once in this work opened one and she is not starting in the last house of January.

Then she goes back for the drawers and the last thing left is the ladder.

She looks at it standing there.

The tray end is up and the feet are on the carpet and it is leaning two inches off the wall, and the second rail has a strip of dark tape round it about two inches long, gone hard at the edges, cut and stuck over something, and the third tread has a chalk mark on it, and the maker's name on the rail has gone down to about half of itself.

She has seen all three of those things and has not gone looking for the other end of any of them.

She does not stand there being surprised about it, because she has not thought about a step ladder in this borough since the start of December, and what she does instead is stand in the doorway of a back room in a semi in a street she has never worked in before and work out where she has seen a step ladder with a strip of tape on the second rail and a chalk mark on the third tread, and it takes a moment, and then it takes most of the walk down the garden.

The twenty-ninth of November. A street off the ring road. A man in a fleece with a tape round his neck.

The third of December. The back of a van. The same van. A folded one across the load.

---

And there is no name on it, and there is no address on it, and there is no paper on it, and there is no gummed strip over anything with a street on it and a number at the end of it.

There is a piece of aluminium with a bit of tape on it.

She has put three things in other people's houses without a word to anybody. A blanket off the arm of a chair in a front room. A box off a fly tip that somebody threw away, which is worse, because a box off a tip is a box that somebody decided to get rid of, and she took it anyway. And a step ladder out of her own van, which she put in a porch because there was no third place for it.

Three things, and not one of them has a mark on it that anybody could act on.

---

Her rule is that she does not take other people's things.

On a tip, everything is already somebody's, because the person who put it there put it there deliberately and threw it away. In a house, the same rule is easier and harder: easier because a house is somebody's and there is an owner and the owner can be asked; harder because a house has a man in a nursing home and a daughter with the keys and a son who has come for the day and does not want to be standing in it.

She could ask the son.

She could go up into the hall and say there's a step ladder in the back room, do you want it, and he would say aye or no, and if he said aye it would go on the van and be done with.

And she does not do that, and she stands in a doorway working out why, and the reason she works out is this: the first time she put a thing in a porch on the third of December it was because she could not think of a fourth thing to do, and she has thought of a fourth thing to do about four times since and has not done any of them.

The fourth thing is to go and get the man.

---

She could find him. He is a joiner and he has a unit on an estate and there is one about four in every ten joiners in this borough who are the man who made something in somebody's kitchen in the summer. She has his van's colour. She has the fact that he was in a street off the ring road on the Monday morning in the last week of November with a ladder out of his back.

That is not a name and it is not a number and it is not a unit, and the best she has got is that he bought something off the woman at the shop on the ring road about that time, and she does not know that either, and there is no version of this in which she walks into four units in a week.

She looks at the ladder for a good while.

And what she is looking at is a strip of dark tape about two inches long, gone hard at the edges, cut and stuck over something, and a chalk mark on a tread, and a maker's name gone down to about half of itself.

Those are the marks. Those are the three things she has been carrying round this borough in her head since the twenty-ninth of November without knowing she was carrying them. And they are on a ladder in a back room in a semi in a street she has never worked in, and she put a ladder in a porch on the third of December and a porch is a long way from here, and a fold of a woman's shoulder in a gateway is a long way from here, and a front room at the end of a road she has driven past is a great deal further.

She cannot place it. That is the honest position and she has had a good while to get to any other one.

Then she goes and gets her pad off the passenger seat. The band is round it and the band is tight and the pad is shut and on the page inside there is the house from the Tuesday and the semi from today and a figure against each, and there is a line for the back room that is blank.

She writes the figure.

She does not write a line for the step ladder. She has got a rule in her that she has never had to write down because it has never come up: the pad is for work that is paid for. A step ladder is not work that is paid for. A blanket is not work that is paid for. A box is not work that is paid for.

Three things, and not one of them has been on a page, and the band round that pad is the tidiest object she owns, and it is the tidiest object she owns because of a rule that has nothing to do with tidiness.

She puts the pen in the door pocket by the roll of tape, shuts the pad, puts the pad back on the passenger seat and turns the key.

---

"You finished down there?"

She looks up. He is on the two steps in the hall.

"I've finished down there."

"That's the lot?"

"That's the lot."

He comes down the two steps and looks into the back room. He does not go in. He looks at the drawers stacked on the chest and the photographs and the bed frame in pieces by the door and the ladder under the window.

"That's my dad's, that."

"Right."

"He used it for the guttering." He says it and then hears it. "He was on it about nine years, that's about right. I don't know why it's in there, that's a back room, that's not where he kept a ladder."

"Do you want it out?"

---

And she has got the pad in her hand and the pen is in the door pocket and the man is standing on the two steps of his own father's back room in a semi in a street she has never worked in, in the last house of the Friday.

"Do you want it out?" she says, again.

"No, leave it. The house is going. What's the use." He looks at her. "You're not taking it?"

"No."

"That's mine, is it."

"I've had a step ladder in my van," she says, and stops there, because the next three words are *it might be this one* and she has not got the right to say them and she has not got the face to unsay them afterwards either.

"I can't tell you whose that is," she says. "That's the honest answer and it's the whole of my trade in one sentence. I don't take a thing out of a back room that I can't give back to somebody."

"Right."

"It's not mine. I know it's not mine. That's the part I'm certain of."

---

He looks at her for a second and then he laughs, which she does not mind, and then he goes up the two steps and she hears him in the hall and then she hears the front door and then the van.

She stands in the back room doorway with the folded step ladder under the window leaning two inches off the wall, and it is not hers, and she has now had three things that are not hers, and she has not been in a position to give any of them back at any point since the twenty-ninth of November.

---

She shuts the house and the son has gone and the horse van has gone and the bin on the kerb is out and she goes and puts her gloves in the door pocket next to the tape and the pen.

Then she gets in the van and pulls the tailgate down.

The parcel is behind the passenger bench on the floor against the bulkhead with a bag of rags on top of it, and the label under the rags has gone soft along the bottom edge where the damp got into it, and the number on it is under that, and she has not moved the rags since the twenty-second of October and she could put a hand under them and read the number any morning she likes and has not done it on any morning since.

It is taped. It is a box the size of two loaves. It is a box of something in tins, and she worked that out on the twenty-second of October by putting her hand flat on the top of it and pushing, and she has not found out what is in it at any point since the twenty-second of October.

And she has read a number off it twice and written it nowhere, and there is a bag of her own rags in the back of that van with somebody else's blanket in it.

And the third thing she cannot place is a piece of aluminium. It is not in her van. It has not been in her van since the third of December. She put it in a porch, and a woman took it in out of the rain, and she carried it round to a house, and she carried it up a road, and then it stopped being a thing she was doing anything with, and she has not found out where it went and she is not going to, and that is the only one of the three where the not-finding-out is her own decision.

---

There is one thing in this borough with two addresses on it and it is a box under a cloth on the left of a kitchen table at the end of a road, and it has not moved since the twenty-second of November and she has no idea what is in it, and it is the only object in this borough that anybody could put in an envelope and post.

And the only thing that has gone round four houses and two streets is a step ladder, and a step ladder is not an object anybody sends anything in, and it has not come back to her, and she will not be told, and there is nobody in this borough who could tell her whose it is.

She shuts the tailgate.

The drawer under the passenger bench is shut and has been shut since the middle of November and her own bolster is on the leads and the tin of screws is under the bolster, and she has not opened it in this borough since the summer and there is no reason to open it now.

She gets in and starts it and goes out to the ring road.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES HAS SEEN A STRIP OF TAPE ON A RAIL AND A CHALK MARK ON A TREAD AND HAS SAID OUT LOUD THAT SHE CANNOT TELL WHOSE THE LADDER IS, HAS HAD A MAN STAND ON TWO STEPS OF HIS OWN FATHER'S HOUSE AND SAY *THAT'S MY DAD'S* AND HAS PUT NO LINE FOR IT ON A PAD, HAS LEFT THE DRAWER UNDER THE BENCH SHUT WITH HER OWN BOLSTER IN IT, AND THE TAPED BOX BEHIND THAT BENCH HAS NOT MOVED SINCE THE TWENTY-SECOND OF OCTOBER AND SHE HAS STILL NOT READ THE NUMBER.**
