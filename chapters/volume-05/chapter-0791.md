# Chapter 0791 — The Flue At The Hearth End

Monday the sixth of February 2023, in this borough. In her own kitchen at number twelve the hearth is on the floor in front of her and her sister is sitting on it, and one flue takes the whole of a day.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS A FLUE IN HER KITCHEN THAT HAS NOT BEEN SWEPT SINCE BEFORE SHE CAME AND MEANS TO GET A ROD UP IT, HER SISTER SWEEPS IT FROM THE HEARTH END ON THE FLOOR WITH A ROD AND A COIL OF ROPE AND A FLUE BRUSH AND BRINGS DOWN THREE TIMES THE SOOT THAT WAS STANDING ON THE LEDGE WHERE THAT FLUE COMES THROUGH THE WALL, AND THE OLD WOMAN CARRIES A PARAFFIN STOVE OUT INTO THE YARD AND LIGHTS IT THERE, AND THE SMOKE COMES BACK INTO THE ROOM.**

She has been seventy-eight for this length of time and she lives at number twelve, and her sister comes in at the back door out of the yard a bit before the middle of the day and finds her up on the hearth with the register plate in her hand.

---

The kitchen is the cold room and it is the only one she will sit in. There is a fire in the grate that she does not use and a lamp on the table and her parcel on the table under the lamp where it has been since the autumn. The sash is down in its frame where it has been since the summer and is not touched. The trace of the old wallpaper by the breast is green and there is a nail in it that goes nowhere. The range has been out of use since before her time and there is nothing on the range but a tea cloth and a tin.

Out in the yard the gate is propped back against the wall and she steps over the brush lying on the runner going out to it. The four rebuilt feet at the coal shed end are capped and have not moved, and the three feet beside them capped in the autumn have not moved either. At the other end of the wall there is a length of new board bedded in cement along the front of her bed, and the brick above it is dark the whole height. The coal shed is shut and nobody has been into the front room since before she came.

The flue is the one that comes out of the back of the kitchen hearth.

The kitchen is the oldest part of the house and the hearth is an opening in a chimney breast with a firebrick floor and a cast iron register plate in the front of it that came with the house and has never been let out. There is nothing in that opening now. There has been nothing in it since before she came, because there is no fire in that house in the winter and the range is out of use and has been out of use since before her time.

The flue behind it goes up inside the chimney breast and out of the stack on the roof.

She has never had it swept. Nobody has swept it. There has been nobody in that house for a long time and the woman who came to see the house before her did not sweep it, and she asked once about a man with rods and she was told that there was no man and there had not been one for years and that anybody could do it if they wanted to.

Her plan is a rod.

She has a length of cane about four feet long and a wad of sacking on the end of a bit of wire, and she means to reach up into the opening, push the rod up the flue as far as it will go, work it about, and pull the soot down. She is seventy-eight and the opening is above her head and she has a stove she could light and she is going to be sitting in this kitchen all winter.

Her sister comes in at the back door about half way through the morning. The old woman has the register plate out of its rebate and is standing on the hearth with a length of cane in her hand, waiting for somebody to stop her.

"You are not doing that with that."

"I am doing that with that."

"You can get four feet of cane up that flue and you cannot get the wad past the bend, and that flue is twenty feet from that plate to the top of the stack. And a wad of sacking on a wire comes down with what it can reach and no more than that, and everything above it stays exactly where it is." Her sister looks into the opening. "And you have been on that hearth for ten minutes already and it is your knee you are standing on and there is nothing in that house you can put a knee in the way of."

---

So she does it at the hearth end, on the floor, with a rod and a coil of rope and a brush.

That is the whole of the difference and it is the whole of the method.

Her sister has a flue brush. It is a handle about four feet long with a round head of blunt wire at the end, and it came with the rod and the coil of rope off the back of a garage in the town where she took a kitchen out in the autumn, and she has brought the three of them in the car for this.

The rod goes up the middle. It is a bundle of cane and wire about the thickness of a broom handle and it goes up inside the flue on a rope that goes over a pulley she has rigged on the lintel with a length of string and a hook.

She ties the rope on to the top of the rod with three turns and a half hitch, puts the rod up the flue, and pulls the rope down from the kitchen floor at the open end of the duct, and the rod goes up on its own. When it will go no further she shakes it — up hard, down hard, up hard — and comes it down four feet at a time, and then up again, all the way from the register plate to the top of the stack, twice.

The brush goes up on the same rope behind it.

And they work the whole length of it from the bottom, which is the only direction a flue can be worked from.

---

There is nothing on the hearth boards for about ten minutes.

Then she pulls the first rod up out of the opening and the brush behind it, and the brush is not grey. It is black to about the middle of the wire head and the rest of it is solid.

She tips it out on the boards and it makes a soft noise on the boards, and it goes down into the cracks and off the edge of them onto the hearth, and the boards come up warm under your hand.

Then she sends the rod up again and shakes it and it comes down heavier than the first.

And then she sends it up again, and again, and again, all the way up and all the way down each time, and every time she does, what comes down onto those boards is what the rod has just brought loose. They go at it from after the light comes up in the window until the light has gone off the yard.

There is a finger of soot standing on the ledge where that flue comes through the wall, and it has been standing there since before she came, and what came off that brush in one day would have stood on that same ledge three times over.

She goes up it once more with the rod, on her own, at the end.

"How is it up there."

"I will tell you what I know." She says it from the hearth boards with the brush lying on them. "The rope went slack twice and I put my hand on the rod and there was a lump in it both times, and both times it came down with soot. The rod went the whole length of that flue and it met no obstruction at all, not once, and it came back down each time with nothing on the wire head that was not soft. Whatever is in there is loose from this plate to the top of the stack, and all of it came down, and there was not one thing else on the boards."

"And the top of it."

"The top of it I have not seen and neither has anybody, and I am not going to stand in front of you and tell you what is on the top of that chimney." She lays the brush down. "But whatever is in it, it is not hanging anything, because a rod went past it on the way up."

"It is the best it has been in my lifetime."

---

Then, in the first of the cold, she carries the paraffin stove out into the yard and lights it there.

She tries it in the kitchen first, and that is fair.

She drags it out from where it has stood since the autumn and puts it against the hearth and gets a match to it and it lights, and it burns, and for about a minute it is the warmest thing that has been in that kitchen for years. Then the smoke begins to come back down out of it and find its way out of the top of the stove and along the hearth and up into her face, and in five minutes there is smoke in the kitchen standing in a layer about as deep as the top of the firebrick, and she opens the back door and it goes out into the yard and comes back in again through the gap under the sash, which has been there since the summer and is for good.

She moves the stove out into the yard.

That is where she lights it from then on, on a piece of board on the flags on the south side of the house out of the wind, with the back door propped so she can see it from the kitchen, and in the cold weeks it is the only warm thing in her day. It burns there all afternoon and she goes out and puts her hands on the top of it the way she has done since she was a girl, and the smoke goes up out of her yard and over the roof of that house and off across the lane.

It comes back into the kitchen anyway, on the still days, through the sash that is a hand's width down for good, and lies along the hearth in a grey line, and she says it is that kitchen and not her stove.

And nothing at all goes up that flue again. Not a match, not a rod, not an ember.

It stands in that chimney breast now, going up to a stack on that roof, with no fire ever sent to it and nothing coming, and the whole of the air in that house goes up and down through the gap under a sash instead. It is the best that flue has been in her lifetime. She has a stove that burns on the flags on the south side of that house in the cold weeks and takes the smoke up over the roof and away, and she says that is that kitchen and not her stove.
