# Chapter 0879 — Under The Barrow Tray

Wednesday the twenty-fourth of January 2024, in the corner of the back yard at number ten from the time the flags have dried until the middle of the afternoon. Under the tray of the barrow, and what is under that.

**THERE IS A BARROW STANDING IN THE FAR CORNER OF THAT BACK YARD WITH ITS WHEEL AGAINST THE WALL, AND SHE MEANS TO LAY A HAND FLAT ON THE FLAGS AND PUSH IT IN UNDER THE TRAY OF IT PALM DOWN AND WRIST FIRST, AND HER SISTER IS STANDING IN THE DOOR OF THE SHED.**

That barrow has been in that corner since the summer and it has not been moved since, because a barrow lives in a corner and a corner is where you put a barrow.

The wheel is against the wall and the legs are out towards the middle of the yard and the tray is level enough to put a sack in without anything coming out of it.

She has been pushing that barrow about that yard for years and it has been scraping on one corner the whole time.

Not all the way. In one corner, and only when it is turned one way and not the other, and only at the start of a push. That is the thing that has been in the back of her mind since the summer.

It started in about June and it has got worse since, and there is a thing about it she has not told anybody because there is nobody to tell.

A quarter of an inch standing proud at the front left of a wheelbarrow does not only make a noise. It makes the barrow veer. The handle comes round on its own as you set off and the whole thing wants to go left, and if you let it, the barrow takes its own line across the yard, and if you do not let it, you are carrying about as much of the weight of that barrow as a woman with one hand on a handle can carry, and the load stays where it is.

So for two seasons she has been steering that barrow against its own corner, at the start of every push, with nothing wrong visible from where she walks.

A scraping barrow is not a mystery.

A scraping barrow is a barrow on a flag that has lifted at one corner, or a barrow over a nail standing up out of a joint, or a barrow with a tray that has come off its frame at one corner, and everybody who owns a barrow has had a version of it and everybody has a guess about which of the three it is.

The guess costs nothing and takes about nine seconds and is wrong often enough to be worth a morning.

So she goes to the corner and gets down on the flags and puts her hand flat, palm down, at the front of the barrow under the lip of the tray, and pushes it in under.

Wrist first. The hand goes flat in and the wrist follows and then the forearm starts, because there is more room under that tray than she expected, and she gets a good way in before the arm will not go any further.

Under a barrow there is a great deal of room. That is the first thing. Everybody imagines a barrow as a close thing.

The flags under there are clean, and they give under the flat of a hand exactly the way flags give.

She works the hand about, palm down, over the whole footprint, in every direction she can reach, and it comes on flags the whole way, and the grit in the joints, and nothing standing proud anywhere, and no nail head and no lifted corner.

Whatever it is on, it is not the ground.

She puts her hand flat against the bottom of the tray itself and pushes up, and the tray comes up about a quarter of an inch off the ground under the hand and settles back, and the same happens at the far side.

But not at the same rate in the same place.

The tray is not sitting flat on its frame.

A wheelbarrow tray is a pressed pan. It sits on an angle frame and it is fixed to that frame along both long edges and at the ends, and it should be a pan sitting on a frame with a gap of nothing at all between them, and along most of the length of this one there is a gap of nothing at all.

A tray is dished on purpose. It is pressed down in the middle so that a load sits in it rather than rides on two edges, and the dish is why the middle of a tray is about a quarter of an inch nearer the frame than the ends of it. Every tray in this yard has that in it and every tray in this yard is therefore carrying itself on its two ends and hollow in the middle, which is a tray that has been right for forty years and has never been looked at.

At the left-hand corner, about four inches of it, there is a quarter of an inch.

She gets a thumbnail into it. It goes in to the first joint and stops, which is a quarter of an inch and about what a joint of anything gives a nail.

So that is what it is. It is the tray. It has lifted off the frame at one corner over about four inches, and a quarter of an inch of steel standing proud at the left-hand corner of a wheelbarrow is a thing that will find every stone and every flag edge in a yard, and it is the start of a push and not the rest of one, which is exactly where it is.

And it explains the corner. The scraping is at the corner because the corner is the corner and the corner is where you turn a barrow.

She finds the other thing on the way out.

Under the middle of that frame, right across it, under where the handles come down to the legs, there is a bar.

Not part of a barrow. A length of square section steel about half an inch by half an inch, run from one side of the frame to the other and welded or bolted across the underside, and it is not in the right place for anything, because the underside of a wheelbarrow frame has a bar exactly there, and this one has two.

That is what a barrow looks like when somebody has put a straight bar across it to stiffen a frame that has been going soft at the middle. It is an ordinary repair. Half the barrows in this borough have one.

But she can feel where the tray sits over it. The tray is sitting lower over that bar than it is anywhere else, by about a quarter of an inch, because the tray is dished and the bar is taking the dish up where it was not dished.

So the tray rides on that bar at the middle of the frame, and rides on the frame itself at the two ends, and lifts off the frame at the corner where it has nothing underneath it to keep it down.

A barrow comes apart with a bar and a spanner and a Saturday, and the tray comes off the frame with two spanners and somebody to take the weight of it.

And there is a spanner on the shelf in that shed and she does not go and get it, because the thing on the wheel of this barrow has been on its flats with a washer a quarter of a turn out of square on the face of the hub since the summer, and that is a separate job for a different day with a different spanner, and if she opens a spanner drawer in that shed this afternoon there will be two things half done by dinner.

So the barrow stands where it stands, in the corner, wheel to the wall.

---

Her sister comes and stands in the shed door about half eleven with her hands in her pockets.

She wanted a length of wire and it is not in there, and the spare roll of it is on the shelf behind the sacks and she did not want to go behind the sacks. She was asked what for and she said the pea sticks are done and the ones for next year want doing now while there is nothing else in the ground. She put her hands deeper in and looked at the yard, which is a yard with one thing in it.

Then she went back inside and shut the door behind her, and the corner of the yard was the corner of the yard.

Under the frame of that barrow there is a length of steel that is not part of a wheelbarrow, and there will be one under there until the tray comes off the frame.
