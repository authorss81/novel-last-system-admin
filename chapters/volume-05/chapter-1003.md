# Chapter 1003 — A Favour And Not A Request

Undated in this draft.

**THE FAR BAY AT THE LOCK-UP HAS ITS LIGHT COMING IN AT THE TOP AND NOTHING UNDER IT.**

The third bay at the lock-up is the one nothing happens in. The frontage runs past it to a gate at the end that is chained, and the bay is open to the weather on three sides, with the fourth side shared with the middle bay through a partition that stops two feet short of the floor. Its floor is a plate of hard blue brick on a screed that has gone into a shallow dish along the front, where the water comes off the roof and runs out under the rail. There is a stack of shuttering boards against the back wall under a cover of corrugated sheet weighted with a length of angle iron, and there is nothing else in there at all, and the roof over it is high enough that the glass at the top of the front is the only light there is, so that at this time of day the bay is lit along its top nine inches and dark everywhere below that.

It smells of corrugated sheet and of dust and of a floor that has been wet and dried four times since the summer, and under that it smells of the river off the frontage at this end of the yard. He has been standing in the dark end of it for about ten minutes when she comes along the frontage from the office. She does not come in out of the weather. She comes in off the frontage, which is the dry side, and she stops at the mouth of the bay about four feet off him.

"Whatever this is, I am not having it in the office."

"A thing like that cannot be refused in an office. That is what I have learned this week." He put his hand flat down on the brick. "Put your name against a wall and a doorway in the room behind the middle bay and let me have the four days of it. That is all I have come for."

"No."

"You have not asked me one word about what I am going to do with that answer."

"I know what you are going to do with it, because I set it out in that hut on Monday. The answer to that question belongs in a hut and not in here." She leaned on the partition with her arms across her. "The crew I asked last summer came back up the water at the end of their six weeks and the book had none of them in it again. That is my whole reason and it has not altered since the summer."

---

The reason has been in that office since before the summer and it has never been handed to him as a refusal before, and hearing it out at the wrong end of a wet frontage under a bay roof makes it sound like a fact about the river rather than a fact about him. He hears that, and he does not trust it.

"It altered in a front room, in front of a woman who has never been on your book." He took his hand off the brick. "I want you to ask me. I have been trying to get at this for two days and what I want is the opposite of what I am giving you."

"Then give me the opposite, because I have got all morning and this is the coldest place on this frontage and I am standing in it."

"I do not want you to ask me. I want the woman at number ten to ask me."

The bay went very quiet. The light along its top nine inches did not move, and the only thing that moved in the whole of it was a piece of corrugated sheet over the shuttering boards shifting about its own weight where the wind got under one edge.

"No," she told him.

"She has never been asked for one thing in her life, and she has been paying me in coins out of a folded paper because she cannot think of another shape to put asking in, and she told me in that front room that she does not know what a book is."

"Then she will not be able to give you what you are after, which is a woman asking a man for work in a room with the rest of us in it." She came off the partition. "What she can give you is a favour. You would get the favour. It would be the two of you in a shut front room and nothing on any page anywhere. And I could put down that a man asked me for work. I could not put down whether he meant it, and the entire difference between those two things comes to that, and that book has no column for the part I cannot put down."

"That is not fair and you know it is not fair."

"It is exactly fair. It is the fairest thing I have given anybody this year, and I am giving it to you in a bay where the rain comes in at the top, because a man made me give it twice."

---

He let it finish, and what it finished was him, because there is nobody else left to finish it. The gate at the end of the frontage was chained and the chain has a padlock on it that is open, because the lock on a gate at the end of a yard is the one lock nobody worries about, and he put his hand on the chain to get his coat off it and then left it where it was. He had not asked for anything of hers and she had given him two things for nothing inside ten minutes, which was a straight answer and the fact that she had walked out here to give it to him instead of making him stand in her office, and both of those had cost her something and he knew the price of at least one of them, and he stood there and let both of them stand.

Then he worked out the rest of it, which is that a man who asks a woman for nothing gets told the truth by women, and always has, and that this is the arrangement in its purest form and that he has been living inside it since he was twenty-one, and that it is the arrangement and not the book that is the reason not one page in this borough carries a name with his hand anywhere near it. Two people on this frontage could have been asked to do a thing for him and both of them are up a river. The one who keeps the book will not. The one who is left has paid him in coin off a folded square. That arithmetic is not complicated and he has done it four times on the walk up from the unit this morning. He laid his palm back down on the blue brick where the cold comes through from the front and kept it there until he could not stand it any more, and then he took it up and turned it over to look at it, which of course told him nothing.

"You know what you have just done."

"I know what I have just given away."

"You have just told one person in this borough that you mean to go and use a woman who has never been asked for anything, because she is the only one left you can go to." She waited. "Give me the other half of it or give me nothing."

"I worked that out in that hut on Monday and I did not like it then either."

"Right." She came out of the mouth of the bay onto the dry frontage. "Then go and do it your way, because it is your way, and I keep the book, and the book will do exactly what it always does." He went down the frontage to the gate at the end without hurrying and stood with his back to the chain, and the rain came off the bay roof behind him in a line off the front edge of it and did not reach him, and he let it go on the brick for a while because there was nothing else about that afternoon he wanted to be doing.

The brick along the front of that bay is even, the way old brick floors always are, and it is about as cold in the middle of the bay as it is at the two ends. He is not even with her on any of it.
