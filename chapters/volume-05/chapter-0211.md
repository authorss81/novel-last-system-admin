# Chapter 0211 — The Corridor

Tuesday the fifteenth of December 2015, from about half past three in the afternoon, a corridor on the first floor at the works in Ravensmere.

**A PERSON WITH STANDING IN THE FIRST OF THE THREE REGIONAL SYSTEMS SAID THE TRUE THING ABOUT THE BARGAIN INTO A CORRIDOR, TO ONE PERSON, COULD NOT FINISH THE SENTENCE, SAID IT ANYWAY, SAID AFTERWARDS THAT SHE SHOULD NOT HAVE SAID IT, AND IT WAS NOT TAKEN BACK, AND HE DID NOT REPEAT IT TO ANYBODY AND DID NOT WRITE IT DOWN.**

The afternoon item was the standpipe hours, and it took fifty minutes, and everybody in the room had a different idea about what a reasonable hour was and they got to a reasonable hour the way people get to one, which is by all of them giving a little. Jonas Mercer was in the room for that, at the back, on a chair that had been put against the wall because there were not enough chairs for the people who were entitled to one, which was the first thing that had happened all week that he had not expected and did not comment on.

The argument about the hours was the ordinary kind. The man who runs the clinic in the third town wanted it shut at eight because the last bus came at ten past eight and the people who use the standpipe after that are the people who walk to it. The man from the first town wanted it shut at six, because the first town is four miles further on and the walk back from the standpipe in the dark is not four miles, it is longer, and everybody in that room knew it and nobody was prepared to be the first to write the number down.

They compromised on seven, which was nobody's number, and which is what a compromise of that kind always is.

Outside, afterwards, Nell Ardent was at the far window with a cup of tea out of the machine at the bottom of the stairs, and she said the decision was correct and that she would have written it herself, and Jonas Mercer said nothing at all, and the two of them stood at opposite ends of a corridor and looked at a car park for about six minutes.

Iselin Krogh came out of the room at about twenty past three with a folder under her arm and turned left into a corridor, and Jonas Mercer was in the corridor, because there was a window at the end of it with a view of a car park and a fence and nothing else, and he had gone to it.

She stopped.

"I want to say a thing," she said, "and I am not going to finish it, and I have known I was not going to finish it since about the middle of November, and I have thought about it every day since and I have decided that not finishing it is worse than not saying it, which I did not think was possible in October."

"Say it."

She did not say it. She put the folder down on the window ledge and took her glasses off and cleaned them on her sleeve and put them back on again, and Jonas Mercer stood at the other end of the corridor and waited and did not help her, because there was nothing he could have done with help.

"In this room, that sentence is an accusation," she said. "That is the thing I have got to first. If I say it in there, then I have made an accusation, and everybody at that table will hear an accusation, and the three of them will hear an accusation, and I will be a clerk who has made an accusation in a room, and that will be the end of me in every room in this building. And the sentence is not an accusation. That is what is so bad about it. It is not an accusation and there is no way of putting it in that room that it does not come out as one, and I have been in this building nineteen years and I have never found a way round that and I have stopped looking for one."

"Then say it here," said Jonas Mercer.

"I know. That is why I have come down here." She turned round and looked at the door she had come out of and did not go near it. "All right. It is not a bargain between two things. It is an agreement between three of us and a great many people outside this building that the part we are all responsible for will always be the part that somebody owns. The obligations were written first. The ownership came afterwards, in pieces, over a generation, in rooms I was not in. And nobody has had to do anything wrong for any of that to happen, and nobody in this room has done anything wrong, and every one of the parts fits, and the water is good, and the standpipe runs, and the filters were done on time. And the result of it — the result, which nobody has ever put in a sentence in that room, is that the whole of the part of this that three towns are answerable for is the part that nobody owns. And the part nobody owns is the part that gets sold."

She stopped there, in the middle of a corridor, with a folder on a window ledge.

"And that," said Iselin Krogh, "is where I stop. Because the next part of that sentence is the part I cannot say, and I have got a fairly good idea what the next part of that sentence is, and I have also got a fairly good idea that if I say it in a corridor to a man who has come over on a boat then by Friday it will be in a corridor in a building three towns away, and then it will be in a newspaper, and then the trust that owns the mains in the second town will read it in a newspaper, and the company that owns the works here will read it in a newspaper, and neither of them has done anything they can be shouted at for, and both of them will be gone inside a year, and the three towns will still have the same water and the same standpipe and the same sentence, with a new set of names on the top of it."

Jonas Mercer said nothing at all. He has done that before, in a room over a shop in his own city, to a shop steward who was right, and it is the only thing he can do with a sentence like that, and it is not a technique.

"I should not have said that," said Iselin Krogh. She picked the folder up. "To you, in a corridor, on a Tuesday afternoon, three days before Christmas. I have been the clerk here for nineteen years and I have never said a sentence like that out loud to a living person and I have said it now to a man from a city three towns and an estuary away who has no standing here and no office and no department and no body behind him, which is the only reason I could not stop myself, and I am going to go back in there now and say nothing for another fifty minutes."

She went back in there and said nothing for another fifty minutes.

He did not write it down. He has a notebook in the inside pocket of his coat that he has carried since the fifteenth of August and it was in the inside pocket of his coat at about half past three on the fifteenth of December, and it stayed in the inside pocket of his coat.

On the Friday morning, in a room over a shop in Ravensmere, Nell Ardent said to him, "You've been in that building twice. What is wrong with it?"

"Nothing's wrong with it," said Jonas Mercer. "The water's good."

"That isn't an answer, it's a third of one."

"It's all I've got," he said, and she looked at him for slightly too long and then went back to her paper, and neither of them said anything else about it, and it stayed in the corridor where she had put it.

The standpipe hours were settled at seven in the morning to eight in the evening. That is what fifty minutes of a room full of people with a different idea of a reasonable hour came to, and it is in a minute, and the minute is in a filing room, and it is the only thing that was decided that week.
