# Chapter 0670 — The Bowl Under The Tap

Saturday the twenty-fifth of September 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from morning until night with a bowl under a tap, and the whole of the day is a ceiling mark she does not want to look at and looks at.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT EMPTIES A FOLDED CLOTH OUT OF A BOWL UNDER A BATH TAP ON A SATURDAY, LISTENS TWICE TO A MESSAGE ON HER OWN ANSWERPHONE ABOUT HER OWN CEILING, PUTS THE CLOTH BACK, AND THE FRONT ROOM AND THE TAP ARE ONE THING IN HER HEAD.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is on her own all day. Two voices happen to her, and one of them is in a machine and one is her own.

---

She starts at the kitchen table under the window on the green chair with the cushion on it, with toast and tea and the cloth on the table, and the gas envelope face up square with the edge, pencil side up.

She does not pick the envelope up. The six things are on the back of it and the second list is on the front, and the top corner is gone, taken by tape, and neither woman can write on that paper again.

Four of six are done. The bath tap is not done. The front room is not done.

She steps over the brush in the middle of the runner with the handle across the walking line on her way to the bathroom under the landing, and she does it without looking down, the way she has done since the first.

The passage light is on. The front door is not on the latch. The rush-seated chair is against the wall by the window in the front room and nobody sits on it.

She does not go into the front room. She has not sat in there since the sixteenth of July.

---

The answerphone gives one short ring in the middle of the morning and she stands in the kitchen doorway and lets it ring twice more and then answers it, because a thing that has rung in a house since before Christmas is either somebody at the door or a fault in a line, and there is no fault in the line, and she would like it very much to be a fault in the line.

It is not a fault in the line. It is a message from a woman with a bright voice, and it goes like this.

"Hello, this is a message for the occupier of number twelve, the water side, from a building survey. There's a small amount of water staining on your first floor ceiling, in the room above the front room, and we'd like to come and have a look at where it's coming from. I can give you a slot later this week — I'm in the area Tuesday and Wednesday morning. Would you ring me back on this number, it's on there now, and just say Tuesday or Wednesday, whichever's easier. It's a free call, it's about twenty minutes, there's no charge for the visit. If I don't hear from you I'll try you again. It's for the freeholder, not for you, and we need to be sure you're happy for us to look, so it's nothing to do with the freeholder."

It stops. The machine says the message is from a woman whose name she has never written down, on a date in August, and the line goes dead.

She puts the receiver down on the side table and stands there.

Then she picks it up and presses the button and listens to the whole of it again, and the woman gets to *there's no charge for the visit* a second time in the same bright voice, and both times she hears the sentence *we need to be sure you're happy for us to look* and understands, quite clearly, that a woman in an office is asking to be allowed into a room in her house.

---

She has had the number in her hand twice since August. She has not rung it, and she has not been able to give herself a reason, and now she has one and it is worse than not having one.

She stands at the sink and puts her hand flat on the cloth and says out loud, to the sink, "It is only twenty minutes," and hears how that sounds in her own kitchen, and knows exactly what it is: it is a woman of seventy-eight doing a sum about how long it would take to be let into her own front room by a stranger with a van, and finding that the sum comes out wrong, because the twenty minutes is not the twenty minutes.

The machine has a button on it for recording. She has never used it. She stands and looks at it for a while with the cloth in her hand, and then she presses it, because there is a bright voice in a house that wants twenty minutes of her time and the machine will take as long as she likes and will not interrupt.

She says, "It's me. You rang about the ceiling. The first floor, the front room. You could come Tuesday. I would want to be in the room myself. I have not asked anybody to look at it and I am not going to. That is all. You can come Tuesday."

Her own voice in her own front room, saying *I have not asked anybody to look at it and I am not going to*, in a flat bright tone off a machine in the passage.

She listens to it back. Twice.

Then she keeps her finger on the button until it says the message has been deleted, and it says it, and she puts the machine back the way it was with the side toward the wall, and the tea goes cold while she is doing it, and she does not make more.

The ceiling is white. There is a patch on it now that was not there before the winter, and if somebody stood in that room with a torch and a camera and a good eye, they would be in and out and gone and she would know at the end of it where the water was coming from, and she would still not be able to do one thing about it, because she cannot get to the cistern and she cannot ask anybody to get to it and has not since before Christmas.

So the call is not made. She does not feel guilty about it, which is the thing she expected to feel, and she puts the answerphone on automatic and lets it ring in the middle of the house where it lives.

---

In the bathroom she lifts the folded cloth out of the bowl under the bath tap, and the bowl has water in it from the night, and she wrings the cloth into the bowl and tips the bowl into the bath.

The tap drips while she watches, a steady drip that has been going since before Christmas, and the next drop gathers and falls while she puts the bowl back.

She wants to get the bowl empty without looking at the ceiling, because looking at it makes it a thing she has to do something about, and there is nothing she can do.

She looks anyway. The brown mark is still there, about a foot across with a hard edge, and the edge is harder than it was on the fourth, and the middle is softer.

She puts her thumb to the edge about a quarter of an inch in, the way she did in August, and it comes back with grit on it, and she wipes the grit on the cloth in the bowl.

She stands with her thumb in the air and looks at the grit in the weave, and she knows the water is still coming from above, from a cistern weeping at the far side of a joist over the landing.

---

She washes her hands at the basin and dries them, and she goes up the stairs of her own house in her slippers to the half landing, because the landing is over the front room and she wants to see the underside of the landing ceiling from below.

The hatch with the fixed ladder is shut. The paint round it shows a faint line where damp has got to the edge nearest the front of the house.

She does not touch the hatch. Touching it would make it a thing she had opened.

She stands under it for a while with her hands behind her back, which is how she stood in August on a stool in the front room, and she looks up at a line about the length of her forearm, and the line is not a crack, because cracks go with the plaster and this goes across it.

---

She comes down and sits at the kitchen table, and she works out what she worked out in August and has not put words to since, that the landing being over the front room makes the second thing on her list the room the water is going to come into.

The front room is not painted since before Christmas, and it is the one line she cannot explain, and it is now the room she will not sit in and the room the water from the roof space is going to come through.

The fifth thing and the second thing are not two jobs but one thing.

She has been keeping them apart for a long time because they were written down apart, and there is one paper in the world with both of them on it, six lines in a pencil that does not exist, and they were never two lines. They are one line with a tap at the end of it.

---

She eats at midday without tasting much, and she empties the bowl again in the afternoon, morning and night being the rhythm since before Christmas, and the mark is there when she does not look and there when she does.

The back room window is done, a tape sheet with a blanket folded on a mattress, top corner re-taped with somebody else's roll. The hotplate on the left is done, the seventh of July. The passage light is done, four minutes on a Wednesday in June.

The gate is done, four hours on the seventh of August, drops out and back in, swinging and not coming in on the path, standing open against the wall of a coal shed since the first with both bolts in an apron pocket.

---

She goes round the side in the afternoon on the grass past the coal shed on the walked-flat line, with her coat on over her apron, to look at the gate the way she looked on the fourth.

It is still back against the wall out of the way. The path is clear. The road has nobody on it.

She puts her hand in the apron pocket and feels the bolts, and she leaves them there, and she stands for a while on the inside of her own boundary with the whole of the other side of the road in front of her and nothing on it, and what she is aware of is that the answerphone is in the front room, because the front room is the room the line and the machine are in, and the front room is the room the water is coming into, and it is the room she will not sit in.

That is three things about one room and she has put them all in it herself and asked nobody for a single one of them.

She goes back in and shuts the front door behind her and steps over the brush.

---

In the evening she sits at the kitchen table with the light on, and the envelope still face up, and the table dead flat on a shim planed and sawn flush under the window-side foot, leg back in dry with a card on the joint, no glue.

A tradesman can see that from six feet away because the table stands dead flat. She is not a tradesman and she sees it every day without seeing it.

A tradesman came into her kitchen in the summer, and put a card joint in the leg of that table, and planed a piece of ash to a quarter of an inch and sawn it flush, and did not ask her what it cost and did not tell her what it was going to cost, and she did not ask him, and there is a hole in a jump-up at the top of a coil in the roof space of this house that has never been looked at since.

She has had two men in this house since the spring and she has not asked either of them one question about either of the rooms, and both of them left with a thing they had made sitting where she can see it every day, and she has never once asked how it was done.

---

She empties the bowl once more before bed, and the drip goes on while she sets the folded cloth back in the empty bowl, and she turns the bathroom light off and goes to the kitchen.

She puts the answerphone on loud for the first time since she got it, on the offchance, and turns the volume up so the machine is at the far end of the passage in the front room, and then she stands in her own front room doorway for a moment listening to it ring in the middle of the floor, and it does not ring.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS HEARD A WOMAN IN AN OFFICE ASK TO BE LET INTO HER OWN HOUSE AND HAS NOT RUNG HER BACK, AND HAS LOOKED AT WATER SHE CANNOT REACH AND HAS PUT THE CLOTH BACK UNDER A TAP SHE CANNOT MEND, AND HAS LEFT A GATE OPEN AND A BRUSH ON A FLOOR, AND FOUR OF SIX IS DONE AND NOTHING HAS BEEN CROSSED OFF BECAUSE THERE IS NO PENCIL IN THAT HOUSE.**
