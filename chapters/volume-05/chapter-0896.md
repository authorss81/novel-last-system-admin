# Chapter 0896 — Three Holes In A Post

Monday the twenty-fifth of March 2024, at the gate post at the front of number nine from first light until the dew has gone off the timber. Three blind holes in a post.

**SHE HAS A GATE POST OF TIMBER AT THE FRONT OF NUMBER NINE WITH THREE BORED HOLES IN THE FACE OF IT AND SHE MEANS TO PUT THE TIPS OF TWO FINGERS IN TOGETHER AND TURN THEM ROUND INSIDE EACH HOLE AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING IS AT THE FRONT STEP STANDING A FOOT SHORT OF IT WITH A BAG IN BOTH HANDS.**

That post has stood at the front of that house since the gate went in, and the gate has been mended round it twice while the post has stayed where it was put.

It is a post of oak about four inches square, with the top cut to a slope so the rain runs off, and the face of it to the path carries three holes in a line down the middle.

She has passed them every day for years without ever putting a finger in one, the way a person passes a row of nail heads in a fence without ever asking what they keep up.

A bored post keeps its counsel from the street, because oak faces the path while its middle stays shut to all but fingertips.

So the tips of two fingers go in together into the top hole and turn round inside the timber.

The top hole is about the width of a fingertip and about an inch deep, with the sides smooth from the auger and the bottom rough where the point went through last.

What is at the bottom is grit gone firm, grey and dry, with a beetle wing in it and a flake of paint off the gate.

She turns the two tips together and the grit moves half a turn and then locks firm again, because grit in a blind hole packs down the more it is turned.

The middle hole is a little wider and a little deeper, about a finger joint and a half down, with the sides dark where water has got in and dried over many winters.

What is at the bottom is soil washed in off the post top, firm and dark, with small stones in it the size of pins.

The bottom hole is the deepest of the three, about two joints down, with the sides pale and fresh where the auger went in last and never weathered.

What is at the bottom is a plug of old grout already gone hard, put in when the post was bored for bolts that were never fitted, and left to set in the timber.

The woman who does her shopping comes up the path with a bag in both hands and stops a foot short of the front step and does not knock.

She stood there with the bags on her arms and looked at the post and then at the door and back again, and she set one bag down and picked it up again, and then she went round to the side gate with both bags and left the front step empty.

---

A post bored for bolts that were never fitted is a common thing on a front, because a gate is hung and then re-hung, and each hanging leaves its own marks.

The three holes were bored on the same day with the same auger, and the auger was sharp for the first and dull for the last, which is why the top is smooth and the bottom is torn.

Oak takes an auger clean when the timber is firm and green, and tears it when the timber has dried and gone hard with age, and the post was green at the top and dry at the foot on the day it was bored.

That day was years back, when the gate was to have a second rail and the rail was to be bolted through the post with three bolts.

The rail was never made, because the timber for it was found to be short in the yard and used for another job, and the holes were left open to the weather.

An open hole in oak does not rot the post. Water runs in and runs out again, and the sides go dark and then go firm, and the hole becomes part of the post the way a knot becomes part of it.

What fills it fills from the top down. Grit off the path in the top hole, soil off the post in the middle one, and grout put in by hand in the bottom one to stop the draught through it.

The grout in the bottom hole is old and already gone hard, with the top of it domed up a little where it was pressed in with a thumb.

It has shrunk back from the sides a hair all round, because grout shrinks as it sets and oak moves with the seasons, and the hair of air between is what lets the hole breathe in wet weather.

She puts the two tips into the top hole again and feels how the grit sits. Loose on top and firm underneath, with the firm part as hard as the timber round it.

A blind hole with grit firm in the bottom will take a screw. The screw cuts into the grit and the grit locks round it, and the fixing lasts a summer and then works loose when the timber moves.

A blind hole with soil firm in it will not. Soil swells with rain and shrinks with sun, and a screw in soil moves every week until it falls out.

A blind hole with old grout already gone hard in it will take a bolt. The bolt goes through the grout and the grout spreads the load into the timber, and the post carries it the way it would carry a knot.

That is why the bottom hole was plugged and the other two were not. The man who bored them knew which one would ever be used, and plugged the one that mattered, and left the other two to the rain.

The timber of the post is firm from top to foot, with no soft place in it and no shake along the grain.

Oak goes dark where water sits in it because the iron in the rain meets the sour stuff in the oak and stains it, and the stain goes in a little way and then stops where the timber is firm. Sour oak smells faintly of vinegar after rain, and the scent leaves by noon as the face dries pale again. The smell clings longest inside the middle bore, where sun never reaches to bake it out.

That is why the middle hole is dark all round and the top one only at the rim. The middle one takes the drip off the top of the post and keeps it, while the top one throws it off down the face.

An auger goes dull in oak faster than in pine because oak carries grit in its pores from the years it stood in open ground, and each pore takes the edge off the iron a little.

On the third hole the edge was gone, so the timber tore instead of cutting, and the torn fibres have stood up ever since and caught the dust off the path.

---

The hinge iron stays firm, and the step stone keeps firm where tread touches least.

She takes the fingers out of the post and rubs the grit off them on the side of the post, and studies the three holes from the path for what the face gives away.

From the front the three holes look like a row of dark spots down the middle of the post, evenly spaced and evenly bored.

From the side the top two show a lip of weathered timber round each, where the water has run out and stained the face, while the bottom one shows clean where the grout keeps the water out.

Each aspect speaks straight, and the fingers tell which bore would bear iron while two would bear weather alone, namely the lowest with grout firm inside, because it was stopped the day it was cut and left to set in oak.
