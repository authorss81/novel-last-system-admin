# Chapter 0973 — Through The Piled Washers

Undated in this draft.

**A PILE OF WASHERS ON A POST KEEPS A SMALL ROUND HOLE STRAIGHT THROUGH THE MIDDLE.**

The yard at the lock-up carries a row of timber posts along the side of the site steps, each about four feet high and set in earth with stones packed round the foot.

One post near the steps carries a pile of iron washers dropped over its top, some twenty in number, each about two inches across with a round hole in the middle about half an inch wide. The holes line up one above the next to form a small round shaft straight through the pile from the top washer down to the wood of the post.

The washers are of different ages and makes, some bright with recent galvanising and some dull with rust, some thick and some thin. The pile stands about three inches high and leans a hair to one side where a thin washer warped. Dust has settled between the washers in pale lines that show at the rims.

Washer iron bites the palm cold, keen and flashing. Corroded rings take slighter warmth, roughening rims to touch. The timber below sips meagre warmth, breathing tarry preservative.

The post itself is of softwood, squared and pointed at the foot before it was driven, with the grain running straight along its length. The top of the post where the washers sit has been rounded by weather and shows rings where each washer rested through rain. The rings are dark with rust except where a bright washer kept the wood pale beneath it.

The stones round the foot of the post are of field stone mixed with broken brick, packed tight when the post went in and settled since. One stone stands proud and rocks underfoot, while the rest sit fast. Grass grows between the stones in tufts, and a snail shell lies empty beside them.

The site steps behind the post run up to the office door in concrete with iron nosings on each tread, and the nosings have worn bright along their crowns where feet strike. The concrete between is rough and pale with dust, and grit gathers in the corners where the broom cannot reach. The grit there is of sand and paint flakes mixed, pale against the grey concrete, with each flake curled and thin. Tread nosings cast travelling umbrae matutinal, gaining the nether step by noon.

The handrail beside the steps is of pipe with collars at each turn, and the collars there are smooth and dark where hands have taken them. The pipe runs in three lengths joined at the turns, and each length rings faintly when struck. The brackets that fix it to the wall stand off on lugs with old paint gone hard round the screws.

Rain off the office roof falls onto the steps and runs down them in sheets, washing the grit from the middles and leaving it in the corners. Dew settles on the washers overnight and rusts them a little more with every week, while the post beneath stays dry under the pile. Roof water from the bay runs across the yard in a channel that passes the post by a foot and carries sand clear of the stones.

A second post further along carries no washers but a coil of twine hung on a nail, and the twine there has frayed at its end into pale fibres. The nail leans down a little under the weight and the twine sits in the angle where nail meets post. The fibres at the end catch the light and drift in wind, while the coil above stays wound tight. A spider has drawn a line from the twine to the post, thin and bright in sun. A third post carries a hook with nothing on it, bright at its tip where it was forged. The hooks and nails draw little heat, being small and rusted, while the washers draw most where fingers meet them together.

The washers were gathered through years, one here and one there from jobs about the yard, and dropped over the post for keeping. A washer spreads the load of a bolt head across timber that would crush beneath iron alone, and each size suits a different bolt. The pile here mixes sizes because it was gathered without sorting, with large washers below and small above as they came to hand. The small ones rock a little on the large below, while the large sit full and still.

No two match precisely, hence the lean and the bore skewing faintly off-plumb. The lean is slight and even, no more than the thickness of a thin washer across the height of the pile. One finger drops upright through the piled washers from the top to the post, kept straight so as not to catch on the rims.

It goes down slowly because the shaft runs a little out of true and the finger must ease past each rim. It decodes apertures tactually, disc by disc, hoisting none.

Deepness of pile tells first. The finger goes through to the second joint before it meets the wood, which is about three inches of iron and air. At the warped washer it wedges for a moment and then passes with a little pressure, turning to follow the lean.

Feel of the wood tells next. The wood there is smooth and dished where the lowest washer rested, with a dark ring of rust pressed into the grain. In the middle of the ring the wood stands a hair higher where the hole in the washer kept rain off it, pale and dry among the dark.

Blanched timber ingests scant warmth, pillowy to the point. The softwood post below aches cold upward, slow timber fashion. Inter-iron powder gifts naught, flouring to nails.

The finger ascends from the stack leaded on one flank, rust-ringed at the tip, dust beneath the nail, else bare. The stack lists exactly as listed, the shaft open from crown washer to timber.

No washer rises to touch, each clamped by overhead iron and faint rust solder. Thumb nudge sidles the crown washer a whisker; iron kin reclaim it with a dying murmur.

Rain that falls on the pile runs through the shaft and onto the post, which is why the ring below is dark and the wood within it pale. In heavy weather water stands between the washers in thin sheets that mirror the sky, and then drains through as the rain eases. The draining leaves rust lines down the post that darken through the year. The channel across the yard carries the spill clear of the stones, and the foot of the post stays firm except after heavy weather.


Post-top iron weathers in rings, each winter adding a darker band. The washers here display those bands distinctly, the lower discs darker than the upper, for damp rises from timber. One washer near the middle carries a stamped mark from its maker, a tiny anchor readable only in raking light. The anchor denotes a batch from the coastal forge, shipped inland decades past. No other washer in the pile bears that stamp, making it the odd one out.

Site-step nosings wear brightest at their centre where soles strike, dulling toward the ends where few tread. The brightest point on the second tread shows bare metal through paint, silver against grey. That silver spot grows a fraction each year, recording footfall more faithfully than any tally. Rain beads on that silver differently, flattening where oil from shoes lingers.
---

The ledger-keeper waits stair-foot yardward, treads dorsal, washer-post frontal, fingers unburdened.

The finger rises from the iron shaft and flicks rust dust to the earth as the clerk steps nearer the post.

"Potatoes are cheap at the market this week."

"They were dear last month."

"Someone lent a barrow and wants it back."

"It will be back by Friday."

Afterwards the talk runs to the market and the barrow lent out and not returned and the price asked for each; the post and the pile upon it go unremarked. The ledger-keeper keeps the stair foot, nearing the stack never, treads behind, yard agape ahead. The pile stays where it sits with its shaft running straight and its rims showing dust in the sun.

Nobody moves the pile from its post for the sake of tallying, though the washers sit there for any who pass to tally.
