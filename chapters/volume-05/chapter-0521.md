# Chapter 0521 — Two Descriptions

The Sunday of the twenty-ninth of September 2019, between about ten in the morning and about one. Two streets and a place in between, in this borough.

**SINCE ABOUT 2011 TWO MEN WHO HAVE NEVER MET THE THIRD MAN HAVE EACH TOLD THE OTHER WHAT HE IS LIKE, AND THE TWO PICTURES DO NOT FIT INSIDE EACH OTHER, AND IN AUGUST ONE OF THEM WAS HANDED A PHOTOGRAPH BY SOMEBODY ELSE AND IT WAS NOT A BAD MATCH, AND IT IS EXACTLY SIX WEEKS NOW AND HE HAS NOT MENTIONED IT TO THE OTHER.**

The two of them have known each other since 1998 and they have done almost nothing together except this.

They meet on a Sunday. That is the whole of it, and neither of them has ever put the Sunday into words. One of them goes to a place where he has been going for a long time and the other one comes, and they stand or sit, and they talk, and the third man comes up, and they have been talking about the third man for about eight years.

---

The third man is called nothing to either of them by anybody, and neither has ever laid eyes on him, and neither has ever put the question of how the other one knows him.

Here is what they do instead.

The first of them, a man of about fifty-two, knows the third man from a job he did in about 2004. He has not seen him since 2011. He has described him to the other man at least once a month for eight years and he has always begun the same way.

"Tall," he says. "Tall for his build. He had a beard, and it was not a groomed one. He spoke about two words in ten and the two were worth it."

The second of them, a man in his late forties, knew the third man at school. He has not seen him since about 2009. He has described him to the other man in the same way, at the same rate, for about the same length of time, and he has always described a different man.

"Shorter than me," he says. "Clean-shaven. Grey at the temples and he's not sixty. He talked all the time and he was funny, and you could tell he was funny before he said anything."

Not once in all that time has either of them set those two sentences next to each other.

They could have done it on any Sunday in eight years. Neither has ever asked the other for a fuller account of him. The arrangement is that each of them gives his own and the other one receives it, and neither of them is a man who has ever in his life said, and what have you actually got.

Both of them know they do not agree. Both of them have known it for years. Neither of them has ever once said the word tall or the word shorter to the other one as a question, and neither of them has ever said, that's not him, and neither of them has ever said, well, I may be wrong.

The third man has never come along on a Sunday. He has no idea that two men in this borough talk about him.

Here is what happened in mid-August, and it is the only part of this that ever took place face to face.

The man who goes to the place was at a funeral in mid-August in a town some way off, and afterwards four people stood about in a yard and one of them was a man he had worked beside in 2004, and the two of them talked for about ten minutes.

The man he had worked beside said, "Have you got a picture of Danny anywhere?"

And the first man said, "Why."

And the man he had worked beside said, "I've got a job on and he's the fitter. It's the old Danny. He wants two days off and he's not having them, and I've had to say I don't know him."

And the first man said, "You want to see whether it's him."

And the man he had worked beside said, "I want to see whether it's him."

And the first man said, "I haven't got one."

And the man he had worked beside got a telephone out and found a picture on it, and held it up, and it was a photograph of a man of about fifty standing outside a shop somewhere, taken by somebody else, badly, with the sun behind him.

And the first man looked at it for about six seconds and said, "That's him."

And the man he had worked beside said, "You're sure?"

And the first man said, "That's him."

And here is the whole of the eighteen hundred that have followed, and he has kept it from the only man who could have told him he was wrong, and that man does not know that anything happened.

He has not said it because of the beard.

The man in the picture is clean-shaven. There is a dark line along the jaw where it has been and not long ago, and the rest of the face is a face with nothing on it.

The man who has been saying, for eight years, in a place where the other man can hear him, that the third man had a beard and that it was not a groomed one, is looking at a clean-shaven man and saying, in his own head, in a yard in a town some way off, that is him.

And it is him. He has known it since about six seconds after the man held the telephone up and he has not been able to get the rest of it to agree with the rest of it and there is nobody in this world he can put that in front of.

If he tells the other man, then the other man will say, right, well, that's what I said, and the eight years of the beard go, and he will never know why they were wrong, and the other man will be right about the beard and wrong about the talking and neither of them will ever be able to sort out which of the two of them has been carrying a man who was not there.

If he does not tell the other man, then on some Sunday in about three years the other man will say, shorter than me, clean-shaven, grey at the temples, and he will have to sit there with it.

That Sunday came six weeks to the day after the eighteenth of August, and both of those men have counted it, and only one of them knows that he has counted it.

They met at about ten. They were in the same places they are always in and the other man had got there first and had two coffees and had given the first one away by the time the first man came in, which he always does, and which neither of them has ever mentioned.

And at about half past ten the first man said, "I was talking to a fitter."

And the other man said, "Which fitter."

And the first man said, "The one I used to do the heating work with. The tall one. Have you heard of him."

And the other man said, "Danny?"

And the first man said, "Who told you his name."

And the other man said, "You did, in about 2012."

And the first man said, "Did I."

And the other man said, "You said, the man I am telling you about is called Danny, and I said, right, and I have never once in eight years thought of him as anything else."

And the first man laughed, and the other man laughed, and neither of them said anything for about a minute.

And the first man said, "He's alive, anyway. That's what I was going to say. He's alive and he's a fitter and he's got a motor with his name on the side of it."

And the other man said, "That's good."

And the first man said, "He wanted two days off in the middle of September and his fitter-manager wouldn't give them."

And the other man said, "That's about right for Danny."

And then the first man did the thing, and he has not been able to account for it since, and he did it out of habit and not out of anything else.

He said, "He'll be fifty this year. He's a good fitter. He was a tall lad and he's got a beard, or he had one, and it was never a groomed one, and he still doesn't say much."

It came out of him the way it has come out of him for eight years, in the same order, in the same clauses, in the same voice.

And the other man did not answer.

And the first man looked at him.

And the other man was looking at the middle distance over the first man's left shoulder, with his cup in both hands, and he had stopped, and his face had gone quiet in a way the first man had not seen before and had no word for.

And the first man said, "What."

And the other man said, "Nothing."

And the first man said, "You've gone quiet."

And the other man said, "I've got a thing on with my jaw. It's the dentist."

And the first man said, "Since when."

And the other man said, "Since about the middle of the month."

And the first man said, "You'd have said."

And the other man said, "Would I."

And the first man said, "You'd have said, I've a thing on with my jaw."

And the other man said, "Would I," and drank some of the coffee, and the first man sat there for another ten minutes and told him about the fitter's manager, who is not a good man, and the other man said the right things at the right places and asked no questions at all.

They left at about one.

They went out and they stood on the pavement and the other man said, "I'll get the 12:40."

And the first man said, "Right."

And the other man said, "You coming on the 12:40 as well?"

And the first man said, "I'll walk."

And the other man said, "Suit yourself."

And the other man went off toward the stop and the first man stood on the pavement and watched him go, and it was the first time in about ten years that he had watched the other man walk away from him, and he stood there for two minutes.

And then he thought: he has not asked me once in six weeks where I have been or who I have been with.

And then he thought: no, he has not.

And then he thought, and this is the part he will not say out loud: he has asked me every single Sunday for six weeks about nothing, in a manner of speaking, and he has not asked me a single question about any of it, and a man who has heard something and has decided to sit on it is a man who has heard something.

The bus went at ten past one and the first man walked the long way home.

**ON THE TWENTY-NINTH OF SEPTEMBER HE SAID THE SENTENCE HE HAS SAID EVERY SUNDAY FOR EIGHT YEARS, THE ONE WITH THE BEARD IN IT, IN FRONT OF THE ONLY PERSON LIVING WHO COULD HAVE TOLD HIM HE HAD BEEN WRONG, AND THE OTHER MAN WENT QUIET AND SAID HE HAD SOMETHING ON WITH HIS JAW, AND HE HAS HAD SOMETHING ON WITH HIS JAW SINCE THE MIDDLE OF THE MONTH.**
