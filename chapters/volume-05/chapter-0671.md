# Chapter 0671 — Paper Of Her Own

Saturday the second of October 2021, in this borough. She is in her van and in a house with eight boxes to be put back, from morning until dark, and the whole of the day is paper she does not borrow.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES PRICES A CLEARANCE ON HER OWN PAD ON HER KNEE IN HER VAN ON A SATURDAY, PUTS A TELEPHONE NUMBER OF A MAN WHO BUILT A STOOL IN HER POCKET AND DOES NOT RING IT, SENDS A FIGURE, AND THE PAD STAYS IN THE VAN.**

She is a woman in her late forties. She is on her own all day, apart from the woman who booked the house and a man next door. She does not go up that road.

---

She drives past the end of that road on her way to the job without turning in, and the turning goes by on her left with the houses quiet behind it, and she keeps her eyes on the road ahead.

She wants to price a clearance in a house on the far side of the ring road without using paper that is not hers, because using what is not hers once cost her a corner taken by tape.

The drawer under the bench on the passenger side is shut, with a bolster off her own board on top of two extension leads and a tin of screws, and it does not rattle.

---

The house has eight boxes to be put back and a hall to be cleared to the loft, and she walks through it with the woman who booked it, and writes nothing down until she is back in the van.

She has learned since June, when she wrote a room-by-room job list on the back of somebody's handwriting without turning it over and taped it inside a cupboard door that is going in a skip, that a woman who does this for a living uses the client's paper or her own, and never the back of another's.

She stands in each room and looks and remembers, back room and hall and box room and bathroom and landing, and she nods and says she will send a figure.

"You'll want to see the loft."

"I've seen the loft."

"You've not seen my loft."

"I've seen the size of it off the landing and the hatch is the same as every other house on the row, and I can see the joists are doubled either side of it." She looks at the woman. "You'll have had a fire in this room."

"I had a fire in that room in nineteen eighty-one."

"Then it's not going to be a skip and half."

"That's what they said at the council. Skip and half."

"You want it all."

"I want it all out of my house and I don't want to pay for the whole thing twice." The woman folds her arms. "That's my mother in the front room and that's not going in a skip in eleven pieces."

"Right."

"It's not a skip and half if she's in it."

"No," she says. "It isn't." And she looks at the woman properly for the first time since the door, and decides that she will not put a number on the telephone today for a house with a mother in the front room, and that she will put a number on it, and that both of those are true.

---

In the van she sits sideways on the bench with her pad on her knee and writes the rooms in her own order, which is the order she would do them, and she puts figures against each in pencil.

The pad is hers and stays in the van. The pencil is hers and goes back in her coat.

The cuts inside both palms are pink where the skin pulls, with a plaster on one and not the other, and writing presses the crease, and she eases her grip to spare it and gets the numbers large and slow instead of small and quick.

Against that is the memory of the gas envelope, six things on the back in pencil with nothing crossed off, face up square with the edge on a kitchen table under a window at number twelve, and the front of it with her own writing and the top corner gone.

Neither woman can write on that paper again. She knows that without being told, because tape took the corner on the twenty-second of June and there is no surface left to take ink.

She closes her own pad and puts a band round it, and the band is tight and the pad is shut.

---

At midday she eats bread in the van with the side door open to the yard, and a man from the house next door comes out with two mugs and puts one down on the tailgate for her.

"You want a tea?"

"No thank you. I have my own."

He looks at the flask in her hand and does not argue, and he drinks his, and leans on the wall by the door, and neither of them says anything for a bit, and the man says:

"You doing the Hesketh place?"

"Who booked it."

"Word travels on a Sunday."

"It does when it's a skip and a half." She wipes her hands on her cloth. "How do you know me."

"I don't know you. I've seen the van."

"I've been on this road about four years."

"I've seen the van about four years," he says, and finishes his tea, and goes in, and she is left with the whole of it, which is that a man has known her van for four years and did not know her name and did not need to, and that is the arrangement and it is not a bad one.

She is glad to be a person with her own tea and her own paper and her own drawer shut under a bench.

Four hours of gate work in August is still unpaid with no invoice, and she still thinks it was the right thing, and saying nothing in a kitchen on the sixteenth of July is right in her head, though it was his sentence and not hers.

---

In the afternoon she puts the eight boxes back herself without a word of the tone in her voice, and she does the back room last and takes her time over it, and she sweeps the hall and stacks the loft ladders by the door for the next visit.

The boxes are the job. She opens each one and looks at it, and they are a life put into eight cardboard shapes and tied with the handles torn off, and she has done this four years and she has never got better at it, and she thinks that anybody who says they have got better at it is either lying or has a van she would like.

Two of them are clothes and she bags them for the charity shop and writes the bag number on her own pad and not on a box. One is china she wraps in a blanket and does not touch again. One is photographs, and she does not lift the lid past an inch, because there is a rule about photographs that she has never worked out from anybody and has never been wrong about.

The fifth one is the front room, and the front room is the problem, and it has been the problem since she walked in.

The woman with the paperwork said the mother is in that room, and meant it, and meant it in the way a person means a thing that has been going on so long it has stopped being information. There is a chair against the wall, and a small table, and a blanket over the arm of the chair, and a fire surround with an electric thing in it that has been there since the eighties, and a corner of the carpet with a wear mark in it in the shape of a chair that used to be on the other side.

"You can clear round it," the woman with the paperwork said in the morning. "That's all I want. Clear round it."

"Right."

"It'll have to go eventually."

"Right."

"That's not me saying that. That's me saying what they say at the council."

"Right," she said again, and went out to the van, and stood at the tailgate for a bit, and came back in and did the back room instead.

So in the afternoon she clears round it, and she does it properly, and it takes her an hour and a half, and at the end of it the front room of that house is the cleanest room she has been in for two years, and the woman with the paperwork comes in, looks round, and says, "That's better. That's much better," in a voice she is pleased with, and the woman standing in the doorway with a bolster in her hand says nothing at all, because there is no figure in the world for the other half of that job and there is no way of writing it on a pad.

She does not ring the man who built the stool about any price, because asking him which side he is on is a bench thing, and the bench that agreement was made on no longer exists.

She has his number. It is in her phone under a unit off the industrial estate, where she put it in the spring when she had a door frame to drop off, and she has had it there since, and the reason it is there is not the door frame.

She gets it up on the screen with her thumb and looks at it for the length of time it takes her to write a figure for eight boxes and a hall, and what goes through her is not that he would be pleased. It is that he would ask her a question, the way he asked her how much a gate was in a kitchen in July, and she does not want a question, and he is the only person in that county who would ask her one.

So she puts the number down and rings the woman who booked it instead.

"That's me. Two hundred and forty for the eight boxes and the hall, and that's with the loft ladders stacked, and the front room is not in it."

"That's more than the other fella said."

"The other fella was talking about the boxes."

"He said a hundred and sixty."

"Then ring the other fella."

A pause. "I'm not saying I won't have you. I'm saying he was cheaper."

"He was cheaper because he wasn't doing the front room and he wasn't doing the mother."

"I never said there was a mother."

"You said you had a fire in that room in nineteen eighty-one," she says, and the woman on the other end of the line is quiet for a second, and then says, "Go on, then," and agrees to the figure in a voice that has changed.

---

She closes her own pad and puts the band round it again, and the band is tight and the pad is shut, and she takes it in and drops it in the box under the bench that is not the drawer, and the drawer above it stays shut all the way home with the bolster lying flat on the leads.

She drives home the long way round so she does not pass the end of that road twice, and the light goes early now in October and the lamps are on along the ring road.

The shop with the galvanised bucket by the door goes by on her right, the shop with a rack by the window where a man bought nine pounds' worth of brush with his own money, and she does not stop.

She parks and carries her bag in and washes at the sink, and the plaster on one palm is soft at the edges and she leaves it, and the other palm is open to the air.

She eats with her hands because both of them are a problem for a knife, and she watches a woman on the television put a skip on a front drive and says, to nobody, "Two hundred and forty," which is a figure she is still pleased with, and which is the reason she does not have an invoice for a gate in August and has never once thought of asking for one.

She puts the pad on her own table with the band round it, and she will take it back to the van in the morning.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES HAS GOT THROUGH A SATURDAY WITHOUT WRITING ON ANYTHING THAT IS NOT HERS, AND WITHOUT RINGING A MAN WHO WOULD HAVE ASKED HER A QUESTION, AND WITHOUT DRIVING UP A ROAD SHE HAS NO WORK ON, AND A SHUT DRAWER UNDER A BENCH IN HER VAN IS STILL SHUT.**
