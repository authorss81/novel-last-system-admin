# Chapter 0450 — Ten Past Six and Ten Past Seven

Monday the twenty-ninth of October 2018, and six weekday mornings of it. A forecourt off a parade and the street behind it, in Tideglass.

**HE HAD BEEN DOING HIS HALF OF THAT WINDOW AT TEN PAST SIX FOR SIX YEARS AND SHE STARTED ON HERS AT TEN PAST SEVEN, AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS BROUGHT IT UP WITH THE OTHER, AND NEITHER WOULD IF YOU ASKED.**

The premises he wipes is not his. He has been going into that forecourt most weekday mornings for six years, on the way to a van, about ten past six, and he takes a cloth out of the side of that door and he wipes the left-hand pane of that glass from the top down.

Not the whole pane. A rectangle, about two foot by three, from the top left corner down as far as the middle bar across it meets the frame, because that is the part of the glass you can see through when you are standing at that window waiting for somebody who is late, and the rest of that pane has not been wiped by anybody in a very long time and has a bloom on the outside of it that is the same on every window on that street.

The cloth is a piece of an old shirt and he has had it since before he came here. It lives in a recess by the side of that door where the render has come away in a strip about a foot long, and it is dry in there, and it is the only thing in that recess, and there is a ring in the brick about where it sits if you have been putting it in the same place for six years.

Two passes, and then a third one to take the corners off where the cloth leaves its lines, and then the flat of his hand down the whole of it once, which is what makes it. It takes about a minute and a half. The glass at the end of it is not clean. It is clear in a rectangle in a bloom, and the bloom starts coming back about the Wednesday, and by the Friday you can put a fingernail into it, and nobody in that premises has ever wondered why the left-hand side of that window looks the way it does on a Monday.

He started it in the November of the year he came here, on a morning when he was in that forecourt for something else and put his sleeve on that window because his hands were in his pockets, and he noticed afterwards that he could see the shape of the bus shelter across the road in the bottom of it.

He has never told anybody he does it. Nobody in that premises knows he does it and nobody has ever asked him to and there is no record of it anywhere on earth.

---

She saw him do it the first time from the bottom of her own stair, in about the February of the year before last, and did not go down.

On the twenty-ninth of October, at about ten past seven, with that shop open and the first hour quiet and nobody in at the front, she went out to the front of her own premises with a cloth and wiped the right-hand pane of that same window from the bottom up, in one sweep, along the frame and across, and she did about two foot of it and stopped.

From the middle of that road the two of them are one gesture. There is a man walking the width of a window and then a woman finishing it, and it looks like somebody wiping a window who is in a hurry, and it is not.

She has done it about nine times. He has seen her do it four times, and on the first of those mornings he stopped in the middle of that street for the best part of two seconds, and then carried on with his own half and went to his van.

He has not thought about it since except to know, which is a different thing.

The two of them are not a thing. That is what makes it one. There is no job in it and no rota and nobody keeping it, and no arrangement of any kind exists between them about that window, and if either of them were asked to describe what the other one does they would both have to stop and think about it, and the woman would end up saying that somebody comes in early.

From a bus going past at about ten past seven you cannot see either of them.

---

The man in his sixties was out front of that shop on the Thursday of the first of November and said, without any preamble at all, "Who is doing the window?"

The man in his forties said, "I do a bit of it."

The man in his sixties said, "Aye," and looked at the window, and said, "It's good for it," and went back inside.

Neither of them said the other half. Not one word about the other half of that window was said on that pavement, then or at any time since, and neither of the two of them has ever mentioned to the man in his forties that the other half exists, and the man in his forties has never said a word to the woman who works in a shop about any part of it, and the two of them have stood on that forecourt a good many times since and discussed the price of everything except that window.

On the Friday of the second of November it rained from about half past six and neither of them did it, and the window was not wiped, and the man in his forties saw it at about ten past six on his way to the van and stood there looking at it, thought about it, and went to his van.

He thought about it on the way across that forecourt and what he thought about it was that it was not a disappointment. That is the part that took him by surprise, standing in the middle of that street with his hand on the strap of his bag. He had come round that corner ready to be annoyed at somebody and there was nobody to be annoyed at, and the glass was exactly as dirty as it had been on the Wednesday, and he was not annoyed, and he got in the van and shut the door and went to work.

He has not been able to account for that either, and he has stopped trying, and the two of them are not a thing, and there is nobody on it.

There is no rule and there is nothing written and there is nobody keeping it and neither of them would say to anybody that they did it or that they know the other does, and if you asked either of them about the window tomorrow you would get nothing out of either.

**A MAN STOPPED IN THE MIDDLE OF THAT STREET FOR THE BEST PART OF TWO SECONDS ON THE TWENTY-NINTH OF OCTOBER, AND THEN WENT ON WITH HIS OWN HALF AND OFF TO HIS VAN, AND A WOMAN DID THE OTHER HALF HALF AN HOUR LATER.**
