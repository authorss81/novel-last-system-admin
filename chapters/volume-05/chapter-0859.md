# Chapter 0859 — The Lump Of The Broken Base

Tuesday the seventh of November 2023, in the yard at the lock-up from first light until the office glass has gone dark, and the lump of the broken base read edge-on is the whole of that Tuesday.

**A LUMP OF THE BROKEN APRON BASE IS OFF THE HEAP AT HER FEET AND SHE MEANS TO HOLD IT OUT AT ARM'S LENGTH AND LOOK AT IT EDGE-ON TO SEE WHAT IS STUCK TO IT AND PUT IT BACK WITHOUT PICKING ANYTHING OFF IT, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK GOES BY THE OFFICE WINDOW AND THE GLASS GOES UP.**

The heap is on a sheet against the wall of the bay, and it is what came out of the foot of that wall when the strip of base along it was taken up. There is about half a barrow of it in the heap and it has been standing there since the summer under a piece of the polythene the bays are given.

The square hole is in the apron a foot in front of it, cut clean at the four corners and a spade deep, and it is where the base came out of. The apron itself goes from the hole to the kerbstone and out to the fence and it is a good apron and it is not going anywhere.

It is the heap she wants to look at, and she has wanted to look at it since the day it was barrowed in, and there has never been a day that was the day for it until this one.

She is not breaking anything up. It is broken up. Every lump in that heap is already a lump and has been for months, and there is nothing in it that wants a bar and a morning.

---

What she wants to know is what is on them.

Because a heap of broken concrete out of the foot of a wall is not a heap of concrete. Half of what comes out of a footing is the footing. The other half is whatever the footing was carrying, and the two look the same once they are in a heap and they are not the same to go back in a hole.

She gets the biggest lump she can lift without putting her back out, off the top of the heap with two hands, and then she takes it out to arm's length.

Arm's length is the whole of it. Two feet away a lump is a lump. Four feet away a lump is a lump. Out at the length of your arm with the light behind you, a lump turns edge-on to your eye, and what is on the face of it stops being a colour and becomes a layer.

She turns her wrist a quarter at a time and looks at the edge, and there it is.

Grey. And then, along the one face, a strip about half an inch thick of something quite different that is not grey.

---

The strip is mortar, and it is lime mortar, and it is only on that one face.

What makes lime mortar read as mortar and not as a second kind of concrete is the grain in it. Concrete is crushed stone and sand crushed small, and it is the same all the way through at the scale you can see it. Lime mortar is lime and sand, and the sand in it has not been crushed, so the face of it has a grain running one way through it, and where it has been against a stone for fifty years that grain has taken the print of the stone and gone smooth in the hollows.

That is the reading and it took about four minutes. She turns the lump through three-quarters of a way round and the mortar goes round with it and stays on the same face, and the other three faces have no mortar on them at all, and the mortar is not mixed into the concrete, it is lying on the concrete like a skin.

A skin of mortar on the face of a lump of concrete means the lump had mortar against it and nothing else did. Concrete does not carry mortar on it. Concrete is stone and sand and water and it goes off as one solid and it comes apart grey all the way through, and where a lump of it breaks you get grey through the middle.

Mortar on one face means a joint. That face was against something laid in mortar, and the mortar has been squeezed between two things and stayed where it was squeezed, and everything on the other side of it has gone. That face was the face of the base that the wall stood on.

So this wall was built on this concrete and not cast into it, which anyone standing in that yard could have told her for nothing. What she could not have told her for nothing was which half of that heap was footing and which half was the bottom course of the wall, and now she knows that every lump in that heap that has a strip of mortar along one face is a piece of the footing and every lump without one is the wall.

And there is no way to tell them apart by looking once they are mixed, and no way at all once the sheet of polythene has been off and on and off again in the rain.

She puts the lump back on the heap where it came off and does not pick anything off it.

Not one piece. She stands over the heap with her hands at her sides and lets that be the answer for a minute.

The mortar is fifty years old or thereabouts and it has been on that concrete since before she took the wall down. It is lime and sand and it is softer than the concrete under it and it has gone hard and grey and it is a skin about half an inch thick. It could be picked off a lump of that size in about ten minutes with the point of a cold chisel, and there are perhaps a hundred lumps in that heap, so it is a day and a half of standing in a wet yard in November prising at old mortar, and the result of it is nil.

It amounts to nothing, because that heap goes back into a hole. All of it goes back into a hole, mortar skin and no mortar skin, and it goes in broken and it gets tamped down and a building stands on it exactly as it stood before. The only reason it would come off is if the heap was going to be sieved, and it is not, because there is no reason to sieve a heap of footing that is going into a hole that already has a footing in it.

There is a great deal in this book that gets done for no reason, and this is the other kind.

The woman who books work goes past the office in the afternoon and the glass of the office window goes up while she is standing there, and she is inside the office and the band person is outside by the wall and neither of them comes in or goes out.

She asks through the glass whether anybody has been in the office today, and the woman tells her nobody has.

She asks her whether the light over the desk has been on since Friday, and the woman tells her it has.

She does not say anything about that. She asks her what time the office shuts, and the woman tells her four o'clock, and she says that is early for the time of year, and the woman agrees that it is, and the glass comes down about ten minutes later and the office door stands open on the latch where it stands.

The rest of the afternoon she stands at the sheet and looks at the heap, and that is all.

She does not sort it. She does not put the footings on one side and the wall on the other, because a heap split into two is two heaps and two heaps is two things to move on a wet day, and one heap is one thing and it can be barrowed in a morning by two people who know which end of the barrow to stand at. That is what a heap on a sheet is for. A heap on a sheet has no opinion about what is in it, and a sorted heap does, and it is the heap that will get up again.

She leaves the polythene where it is, folded back so the rain is off the heap and the wind is not getting under it, which is where it was.

By the time the glass in the office has gone dark there is mortar on the faces of the lumps in that heap and there is none of it on the sheet, and none of it is coming off.
