# Chapter 0470 — Half Past Six at the Car

Wednesday the thirtieth of January 2019, from about half past six in the morning until about twenty to seven. One window, one door and about thirty yard of street, in Tideglass.

**HE HAS STOOD AT HIS OWN MOTOR FOR ABOUT TWO MINUTES EVERY WEEKDAY MORNING FOR SIX YEARS, AND SHE HAS BEEN AT A WINDOW FOUR HOUSES UP FOR MOST OF THAT TIME, AND SINCE THE SEVENTEENTH OF JANUARY EACH OF THEM HAS KNOWN WHAT THE OTHER ONE KNOWS, AND NEITHER IS GOING TO PUT IT INTO WORDS.**

He is in his forties. He has a car outside his own house and he has a job that starts at about a quarter to eight and about two miles off, and the road is not there yet at half past six, so he comes out at ten past six.

What he does at the car takes about two minutes and he has never given anybody an account of it and would not give one now.

He puts his hand flat on the driver's door about even with the handle. He does not open it. He stands there with his key in his other hand and does not use it, and he does not do anything else.

On about two days in seven he puts the key in and turns it and the engine goes over and he turns it off again, and on those two days it takes about two and a half minutes.

Then he goes back in and shuts the door, and he gets out again at about twenty to seven, and goes.

That is six years. There are about six hundred of those two minutes in it.

She is four houses up that street and her front room faces it, and her window is the second from the top of a house that is otherwise a house like the others, and from where she stands at that window the whole of what he does is visible and none of the inside of it is.

She has watched that man at that car for a long time. Long enough that she could not tell you when it started and never has tried to, and long enough that she knows things about it she would not admit to knowing.

She knows he does not go in. She knows that from outside, from the fact that the car is there at twenty to seven every morning, and from the fact that he is in his kitchen at that time.

She knows the two days in the week when it takes longer, because the sound of an engine going over carries thirty yard down that street at half past six on a still morning, and she has not counted them and has not once been awake early enough to find out whether it is always the same two days.

And she knows the hand goes flat on the door, and that he does not open it, because from where she is, at that angle, she can see the elbow come out and stop.

She has never spoken to him. She does not know his name. If she went out at twenty past six and said good morning he would not have a clue who she was and would probably say good morning back and that would be that.

What she has been doing for six years is being at that window.

And here is the thing she has never had to look at, because there was never anything to look at: she does not have to be there.

Her room is on the front of that house and it is a warm room in January and the window is a good one and she takes her cup to it because it is a good one. She has never once told herself that she takes her cup to that window.

And on about two mornings in seven she does something about it. She goes and gets dressed first and then comes back, or she puts the room straight, or she stands up and then sits down again. Those are the two mornings in seven. There is no reason on earth for that and she has never examined it and she is not going to.

---

What happened on Wednesday the seventeenth of January was that he looked up.

Not for long. He had his hand flat on that door at about ten past six and he lifted his head and looked up at that window the way a man looks up when a sound reaches him from above, and she was at it, and it was not dark, and there was no blind and there was no reason on the face of the earth why anybody standing at a window at ten past six in the morning in the last week of January would not be looking out of it.

He looked for about a second and a half.

Then he went on standing there with his hand on that door until about twenty to seven, and he did not look up again, and he went in.

She did not move either. She was at that window with a cup in her hand for the whole of the rest of that two minutes and she did not go back from it, and when he went in she stood there a while and then she did the thing she has done about a thousand times, which is she made another cup.

Since the seventeenth there has been a rule and neither of them made it.

He comes out at ten past six. He puts his hand on that door. He does not look up. He stands there for his two minutes and goes in.

She is at that window. She is at that window from about ten past six and she does not go back from it until he has gone in. And he does not look up, and she knows he is not going to, and she is aware that he is aware of her, and the two of them have not exchanged one syllable about any of it, and there is no arrangement here, because an arrangement is two people agreeing to do something and these two have not exchanged a single syllable about it.

Thirteen days of that.

He has stood at that car for about six years and put a hand on a door for no reason anybody could put in a sentence, and for the first thirteen days of this year he has done it with a woman in the window and it has not changed the two minutes by a second.

---

What that two minutes is for is never going to be explained to anybody, by him, and it is not a sad thing, and it is a thing.

He got in that car in the June of 2013 and drove to a hospital in a town eight miles off and sat in a car park there for about two hours, and he has never been able to say what the two hours were, and he has never told anybody there were two hours, and the driving of that car since has been the only part of that week he is still doing.

It is not penance and he would be annoyed at anybody who said it was. He is not going to be a person who carries it. He is a man with a car and a job and a key in his hand, and about six hundred times he has put a hand flat on a door at ten past six in the morning and stood there while he put something away, and then he has gone to work.

And since the seventeenth of January there is a woman at a window thirty yard up that street who is at that window for the whole of it, and he has not looked up once, and he has not shortened it, and he has not lengthened it, and he has not gone in early.

She has been through her own half of it from about the third time.

The version she has settled on is that she is a woman who looks out of a window in the morning and that a man is in the street below her doing something that lasts two minutes, and that she has been doing this for six years, and that from about the middle of January she has been doing it knowing that he knows, and that she is not going to do anything about it, and that she is not going to stop either.

Because if she stops, then the next morning he comes out at ten past six and there is nobody at that window, and he will know that she has stopped, and he will know exactly what it means, and she will not be doing the sort of thing where a woman announces to a man that she has stopped.

And if he looked up, then she would have to do something, and there is nothing. There is nothing on that side of that window. There is a room and a cup and six years of getting up at ten past six without ever once admitting to herself why, and there is nothing in it that can be said to a man standing at a car thirty yard away with his hand flat on a door.

**AT ABOUT TWENTY TO SEVEN ON THE THIRTIETH OF JANUARY HE TURNED THE KEY, WHICH HE DOES NOT USUALLY DO, AND WENT BACK INSIDE, AND FOUR HOUSES UP THAT STREET A WOMAN STOOD AT A WINDOW AND WATCHED A DOOR CLOSE, AND NEITHER OF THEM LOOKED AT THE OTHER.**
