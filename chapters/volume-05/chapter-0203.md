# Chapter 0203 — The Square

Saturday the seventh of November 2015, from about half past ten in the morning, a hall off a parade of shops in Tideglass.

**A WOMAN WHO HAS THE KEY TO A ROOM ASKED HIM IN FRONT OF A ROOMFUL OF PEOPLE WHICH SQUARE ON THE DRAWING IS THE EMPTY ONE AND WHAT IS SUPPOSED TO GO IN IT, AND HE TOLD HER THE TRUTH, AND IT IS THE FIRST TIME HE HAS SAID ANYTHING ABOUT IT TO ANYBODY.**

The woman is about forty-two. She has had the key to that hall for about nine years and she lets it out on Saturday mornings for a class and on a Wednesday for a mothers' group, and she is not on a committee and there is no committee, and letting that hall out is done by a key and by a notebook she keeps in a drawer and by whoever turns up.

She had gummed a copy of the drawing to the wall by the door on the Friday, at about the height of a person of average height, with the squares and the arrows and the lines between them, because on the Friday before that a man of about twenty-seven had come round with a flatbed and a roll of tape and had done four of them without being asked, and because the ones on shutters are outside and this one is inside where the people are.

"That came off a shutter," she said. "Same paper. There is one on the wall at the top of this parade with the corner lifting, and the man that did that one says there is no end of them and no list, and I have had three people come in at this door in a fortnight and ask me what it is."

There were about nine people in the hall, which is a number that means nothing, and two of them were on the class and the rest were waiting, and a man was by the radiator and a woman of about thirty-two was putting chairs out.

Jonas Mercer was at the back with a cup of tea he had brought with him. He comes to Saturdays.

**AND SHE ASKED HIM IN THE MIDDLE OF THE ROOM, IN DAYLIGHT, WITH EVERYBODY LOOKING.**

"You were in the parade on the Wednesday in September, weren't you. The man on the shutter said you were the one that stopped a clause."

"That is right."

"Right." She took her hands off the back of a chair. "Then I am going to ask you in front of everybody, because I have been asked four times this week and I am the one who has to stand in this room, and I am not putting my name to an answer I have not got. There is a square on that sheet with nothing in it. Not the corners, the middle. What is it for and what is supposed to go in it?"

There are eight districts on that sheet and every one of them has a name above it and a term in it, and there is one more square, and it is in the middle-lower part of the drawing, and it is the same square that was on a different copy in a folder in a woman's hands at about twenty past nine on the morning of Friday the eighteenth of September while she was still talking about a road with a hole in it.

Jonas Mercer had read that square on the eighteenth of September. He had read it twice. He had stood in front of a shutter on the twenty-third of September and been asked what it was for by a man with a bicycle, and had said *I don't know*, which was true, and had not said where the reading had come from, and has not said since.

He did not say where it came from now either. What he said was the next part.

"I do not know what goes in it."

The hall was not quiet. Nine people in a hall on a Saturday morning are never quiet. But the woman putting chairs out stopped putting them out.

"That is the honest answer," he said, "and I am going to say two things on top of it, because you have asked me in front of everybody and you are the one who has to stand in this room. I read that sheet on the eighteenth of September, before it went on any wall, and it has been empty on every copy of it that has been put up since, and I have not said that to anybody until this morning, and I am not going to guess. If I told you what went in that square I would be making it up, and I have not made anything up in this borough for nine months and I am not going to start on a Saturday."

"That is the first thing anybody has said in this room that has not been somebody else's opinion," said the man by the radiator.

"It is a fact," said the woman of about forty-two. "It is not a better answer."

"No," he said. "It is a worse one, and you should be annoyed with me, and I would be, if it were my hall."

A man at the back said the square was obviously for a district nobody had made yet, and the woman of about forty-two said no it was not, it had no term in it and a district with no term in it is not a district, which is correct, and then a woman of about twenty-four said it was for a person, and about four people said that was not what a square is on a drawing, and a man of about seventy-nine said the whole thing was a poster and had been since the eighteenth of September, and a man of about eighty-two said his mate had said it was where they put the thing they had not decided yet, and nobody laughed.

"Which is not the same as nothing," said Jonas Mercer. "If somebody drew a square and left it, that is somebody drawing a square and leaving it. It is not a district, because it has no term in it. It is not a seat either, because a seat on that sheet is a shape with a name over it and there is no name over this one, and there is no person on any of that paper anywhere, and that is not an oversight. And the line that comes into it from the district above goes nowhere, and it goes nowhere because there is nothing on the other end of it yet."

"So somebody left a room."

"Somebody left a room," he said, "or somebody is going to walk into it. I know which of those two I would rather it was, and I have not got a reason, and I am not going to give you a reason I made up at half past ten on a Saturday in a hall with the heating on."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then the woman putting chairs out said, "Are you the one who's on all of them?" meaning the drawing, and he said no, and she said sorry, and it was over.

What has happened is that there is a fact about a drawing in a room in Tideglass, said out loud in front of a roomful of people by a man of about thirty-two who read it on a Friday in September and had said nothing for seven weeks. There is no box on a room. A room can hold a sentence and a room cannot hold a person, and in about a week some of those nine will have told about nine other people and two of those will have got it slightly wrong, which is what a sentence does. The sentence is in the room now and it cannot be got back out of the room, and there is no way of knowing on any evening in December which room it is in or who is in it.

Nothing was given to him for it. He did not get a department out of it and he did not get a form, and nobody stood up and gave him anything, because it was half past ten on a Saturday in a hall and the nine people in it had come about a class and about a warm room.

The class started at eleven. The woman of about forty-two took the classes' register off the table by the door and put it in the drawer with the notebook, and the drawing stayed on the wall, and it is on more walls than it was in September and nobody in this borough could say how many, and there is a square in the middle of every one of them with nothing in it, and the woman who has the key to this hall is going to be the one asked about it, and she has now got a sentence to give and it is worse than nothing and it is true.
