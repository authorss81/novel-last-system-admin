# Chapter 0285 — The Catch

Saturday the thirty-first of December 2016, from about ten past eight in the morning, a hall off a parade in Tideglass.

**THE HALL DOOR CAME IN OFF ITS CATCH ALL WINTER AND A MAN IN A HI-VIS TOOK IT OFF ITS HINGES AT HALF PAST SEVEN TO SEE WHY, AND HE PUT IT BACK WITH A WASHER UNDER EACH SCREW, AND HE SAID WHAT IT WOULD COST TO DO PROPERLY, AND HE DID NOT LEAVE A CARD, AND NOTHING WAS ASKED FOR AND NOTHING WAS SAID ABOUT IT AFTERWARDS.**

The parade was shut by ten. The shop that shuts early had put its shutter down at one on the Friday and the boards had gone up on the Saturday morning over three windows, and the woman who runs that shop stood on the pavement through all of it with her key in the shutter and watched a man put one of them over her own window, and she did not go in and did not help and did not go away.

The man in the hi-vis was at the door of that hall at about ten to eight with a caulk gun in a bucket and his own time, and the door of that hall has swung both ways since before the summer on a catch that has never once held, and it had been coming in off the box it stands on about four times an evening, most weeks, and the woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall had been holding it with her back every time.

She was in the passage at ten past eight with the key in her hand and a pound in her coat pocket, waiting for him to come back because that was the arrangement and had been since September, and he came back down the parade with the bucket in one hand and stopped at the door of that hall and said, "Hold that a minute."

"You haven't got time," said the woman of about forty-two.

"I haven't," said the man in the hi-vis. "That's why I'm doing it now."

He had the door off its hinges before the light was properly up. The screws at the top hinge had rusted down to a stub and the heads were polished round where the door had been coming in on them for a year, and he put the blade of a screwdriver under the head of each of the four in turn and lifted it and turned it, and the top two came straight out and the bottom one came out about halfway and he worked it back and forth and got it.

"Hinges," he said. "That's all that is. The top ones are chewed. You could put a washer under each one and it'd shut for a while, and while's a word, and I'd not write the word down."

He put a washer under each of the four, and a washer under the strike plate on the frame for the sake of it, and he got the door back onto its hinges and pulled it to and it shut, and he opened it and it shut again, and he shut it for the last time and stood with his hand on it.

"There," he said.

The woman of about forty-two had not said anything through any of that. She had the key in her hand and the pound in her pocket and she was looking at the door.

"That's not a repair," she said.

"It's not," said the man in the hi-vis. "That catch is bent and it will go again in about six weeks and I'd not want to be quoted on it. If you were having it done, it'd be a new catch and a pack of screws and half an hour, and there's a figure for that, and I'll tell you the figure if you want it, but I'm not writing it down and I'm not leaving a card, because you'd ring me in March when it's shut and gone again and I'd not come, and then you'd say I'd said I would."

"What's the figure."

"Eighty-two," said the man in the hi-vis, "for the catch, and I'd not charge you for the hour."

She thought about that for a while with her thumb on her key.

"Leave it," she said.

"Aye," said the man in the hi-vis. He put his bucket on the step and got the screwdriver off the flagstones where he had put it, and he said, "You'll want to shut that box away now. That box has been doing that door's job for a month."

And then he was gone up the parade at twenty to nine with the bucket, and he stopped at the shutter of the shop that shuts early, where the woman who runs it was still standing with her key in the lock.

"You're the one that does the hall," said the woman who runs that shop.

"Aye."

"What's wrong with it."

"Nothing," said the man in the hi-vis. "The catch is bent and I put a washer under each of the hinge screws and it shuts. That's all. It'll go again in the spring and I'll not be stood there when it does, and don't ring me about it, because I'd not come."

"I wasn't going to ring you," said the woman who runs that shop. "I've been standing in this parade since about half past seven watching that man put a board over my window and I haven't said a word to him either, so I know what it's like."

"Aye," said the man in the hi-vis, and went on up the parade with the bucket.

He stopped there for about ninety seconds and he told the woman who runs that shop what he had done to that door, and the woman of about forty-two with the key was standing about four foot inside the passage of that hall at the time and did not go out, and she did not go after him, and she has not said a word about it to anybody since.

The woman of about forty-two took the flattened cardboard box out from under the door and carried it out to the bin store at the back and put it in with the card one and came back in and shut the hall door behind her, and it shut, and she stood in the dark for a moment with her hand flat on it before she found the switch.

---

The hall was not open that day. Nobody was going to open it on a Saturday, and the woman of about fifty-five came in at about one o'clock with her bag over her shoulder because she had been at the parade and her feet were wet, and she came up the passage and put her hand on the door and pushed it and it went about four inches and stopped.

She stood there.

Then she put her shoulder to it and it came in off the catch, hard enough to shift it, and she got through and let it go and it shut behind her and the sound of it went the length of that hall and the meter on the wall made a noise when it came off its bracket.

"Well," she said.

Nobody was in there. There were nine chairs stacked against the stage from the Friday and the jug was on the table where somebody had put it, and the light over the stage has been the same for years and comes on about half as long as it should.

She had about an hour. She did the things she does in that hall, which are not many, and at about ten past two she got the top chair down off the stack and set it where she always sets it, which is the fourth one along the table, and sat in it, and the door was shut.

"Somebody's had that door," she said, at about ten past two, to about nine stacked chairs.

Nobody came in that day. The man in his forties did not come, the man from the parade did not come, the man who works nights was somewhere with his coat on, and the woman who has been in that hall about two years was at a counter in a shop in a place off Corrance Lane. The gas stayed on from the morning until about ten past four, when the woman of about forty-two came in with a key and put it into a meter on the wall and shut it and locked it, and then went out again, and the door shut behind her too.

**AND ON THE TUESDAY NIGHT A MAN CAME OUT OF THAT HALL AND WENT UP THE PARADE AND A GIRL WENT THE OTHER WAY AND THE DOOR SHUT ON ITS OWN BEHIND BOTH OF THEM, AND ON THE THIRD FRIDAY IN JANUARY IT STUCK FAST AT THE TOP AND HAD TO BE LIFTED, AND IN THE MIDDLE OF FEBRUARY THE MAN IN THE HI-VIS CAME BACK UP THAT PARADE AND LOOKED AT IT AND WALKED ON.**
