# Chapter 0971 — Along The Frame Groove

Undated in this draft.

**A TIMBER FRAME ON THE WALL OF THE STORE CARRIES A GROOVE ALONG ITS INNER EDGE.**

The store wall at the unit carries a timber frame hung at shoulder height, made of oak with a groove run along its inner edge to take a board long since gone.

The frame is about three feet square with joints at its corners, each joint pegged with two oak pegs that stand proud by a hair. The groove runs along all four inner edges in one continuous cut, about half an inch wide and half an inch deep, with a flat bottom where the plough left its mark.

Dust has gathered in the groove through years, soft and grey, deepest in the lower length where it settles and thinner along the uprights where it clings. A dead fly lies in the lower groove near the right corner, folded and dry, with dust drifted over it.

The oak of the frame is dark with age and hard under the nail, with grain that runs straight along three sides and curls round a knot on the fourth. The knot is sound and draws no more than the rest, and the groove beside it runs true without wavering.

Aged oak presses cold up the finger flank, hardwood-deep and solemn. Iron hooks beneath answer cold shriller, forged metal impatient. Channel powder grants fingertips zero warmth.

The frame hangs on two iron hooks driven into the store wall, and the hooks have rusted into the timber where they bear. Rearward rises rippled sheeting on sawn ledges, one ledge tracking the frame crown, coarse-grained and knotted. The sheet there is dull with weather except where the frame has kept a pale shadow across it.

The hooks were driven true and the frame sits level upon them, though the left hook sits a hair higher so the frame leans almost unseen to the right. Iron hooks of that size are forged with a barb that bites where driven, and neither has moved since the day it went in. The rust round each runs down the sheet in a thin streak that stops where the frame shadows the wall. The rust trickle runs arid, rasping; a fingertip crossing returns umber. The lean is enough to throw dust toward the right corner, which is why the drift lies deepest there by half an inch.

Below the frame a stack of pallets stands against the wall, three high, with slats that have warped in rain and straightened again in sun. The top pallet carries a folded tarpaulin, stiff with age and pale with dust, bound with cord sunk into the crease. The twine there has left a bright line where it pressed, though the tarp itself gives under the hand.

The flags under the pallets were seated on grit, since eroded at one quarter to void a pocket below a lip. That pocket bides parched and pebbly; a dipped finger meets nether flag satin-slick from casting. The overhead flag vaults its cavity unwavering.

Rain off the store roof falls clear of the frame and runs down the sheet behind it, shadowing the ledge a full day after rain as the square itself bides arid. Eaves shadow parades the square through forenoon, attaining the sill at noon ere departing. Dew does not reach the wall under the overhang, and the dust in the groove stays dry through the year.

A nail in the left upright of the frame shows where a label once hung, with a pale shadow round it where the label kept the weather off. The shadow is square and of a size to cover a hand, with its edges sharp where the card screened the oak. The nail itself was cut by a smith and not drawn, square in section and tapering, and its head stands proud with hammer marks still showing. The nail stands proud by a quarter inch and bright at its tip where hands brushed it. The shadow beside it is square and sharp, marking a card no larger than a hand.

The pallets below were stacked by one person working alone, which is why the slats run true at one end and step by half an inch at the other. Each pallet will carry a person without bending, and each spans its bearers fully. The warping in the slats runs along the grain and does not split it, opening only in dry weather and closing again with rain. Each slat carries two nails at either end, and the nails have rusted into the bearers so the heads turn with difficulty. The bearers beneath are of harder timber and sound dull when tapped, while the slats ring a little where they span clear.


Tarpaulin weave runs in a simple interlock, warp over weft alternately, proof against drizzle but not against standing water. The tarp atop the pallets carries such weave under a dressing of wax long since worn to patches. Where wax survives, rain beads and runs; where gone, the weave darkens and drinks. The drinking patches map the folds exactly, for folds abrade wax first. A mouse has nibbled one corner of the tarp into a scallop, the scallop edge frayed to white threads.

Pallet feet run three to a deck, notched to take forks. The notches here show bruising where forks missed, pale dents in softwood. One foot carries a stencil mark from the yard that owned it first, black on grey, half worn away. The mark reads as a lone initial, all else weathered off. Fork handlers sight that initial and aim without conscious thought, tines finding notches blind through long practice in tight yards.
---

The side of one finger runs along the groove from one end to the other, beginning at the top left corner and ending at the top right.

It runs on its side because the groove will not take the tip flat, and it reads the bottom by pressure rather than by depth. Travel proceeds by nudges, checking at dowels and skirting the knurl.

Length speaks first. It runs the full square without break, three feet to a side and twelve feet in all, with the corners cut square where the plough stopped and started. The bottom is flat throughout except at the knot, where it rises by a hair over the grain.

Lodgers speak second. Through the greater square dust alone drifts, fluffy above and rammed below, ponding deep enough in the sill to hide the finger flank. Nigh the dexter corner crouches an oak spall flaked from channel lip, straddling, leaf-thin, wan. Overhead touch finds it dead; shove sends it travelling till the dead fly blocks it.

The spall sips scant warmth, keen-edged to touch. The flagstone below the pallets breathes cold upward through the soles, deep quarry manner. Flanking powder bestows naught, breathing sawn oak.

The digit exits the channel crown-right, flank dusted, else unstained. The channel keeps its gauge, the oak chip parked where the push parked it, a fingertip sideways of origin.

The square hangs unshifted on iron, gravity seating it and barbs biting timber. Palm pressure sidles it a whisker; twin barbs reclaim it jointly.

Rain that runs down the sheet behind does not reach the groove, which is why the dust lies dry and the chip stays pale. The tarpaulin below throws drips clear of the flags, and the hollow beneath stays dry except in driving rain.

---

Two-doors-down stands abreast the pallet stack yardward, timber piles screening her from the square, never circling, fingers unladen.

The finger edge quits the oak channel and taps twice on the frame corner before the caller lifts her eyes from the pallets.

"There is a board up by the road about the fete."

"It has been up there since Tuesday."

"Then someone lost a scarf by the gate."

"It will be on the board by nightfall."

Later talk covers the road and the lost scarf and the fete fund, and nothing that hangs on the oak or stands below it. The woman stays by the pallets and does not come nearer the frame, and looks once at the yard gate before turning away. The frame stays where it hangs with its groove full of dust.

Squared oak grips its powder hard against brickwork always, once the fingertip tour ends and pallet feet shuffle below in the yard.
