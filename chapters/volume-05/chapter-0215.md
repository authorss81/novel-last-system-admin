# Chapter 0215 — Five Things

Wednesday the sixth of January 2016, from about eleven in the morning, a corridor in Marrowgate, and then a room off it.

**THE SECOND OF THE THREE REGIONAL SYSTEMS IS CHEAPER THAN THE FIRST ONE AND BETTER, AND NOBODY IN IT IS HIDING ANYTHING, AND THE WHOLE OF WHAT THAT TOWN AGREES ABOUT IS THAT ONE NAMED MAN IS RESPONSIBLE FOR FIVE THINGS, AND FOUR PEOPLE IN THAT ROOM SAID SO COMFORTABLY IN ONE MORNING AND NOBODY HAD ASKED HIM.**

Marrowgate is a town of about three thousand, up the estuary, eight miles past the works, and it is the other kind of place. In the first kind the works belongs to a company and the mains belong to a trust and the part everybody is answerable for is the part nobody owns. In Marrowgate the whole thing belongs to the town.

The town bought it. There was a company and then there was not a company, and in the year the company went the town had three choices and it took the one where the bill goes to everybody, which is the reason a house in Marrowgate has a water bill of about ninety pence a quarter and the reason nobody who lives there has ever been asked for a card.

"There is no charge for anything," said the woman who chaired the room, and she said it the way a person says a thing that has been true for a long time and that they are slightly tired of saying. "Nothing. The works, the clinic, the shelter, the gate, the yard. The gate is open from seven to six and it is shut at six because a yard is a yard. You can take water out of the standpipe at the top of Church Street at any hour it is on and nobody has ever asked anybody for anything at it, and if you do not want to use it you do not have to use it, and the person who set that up is in this room and it is not a favour, it is a rate."

Her name was on a card on the table. Mavis Bray, sixty-six, and she had been the clerk of that town for twenty years, and everything she said in the first half hour was correct.

Jonas Mercer had put his coat over the back of a chair in the corridor outside and had not gone in, and Nell Ardent was standing up the corridor with her bag between her feet, looking at a window at the end of it.

**AND THE ONLY LATTICE NOTICE IN THAT CORRIDOR THAT MORNING, printed and fixed to the wall at about the height of a person of average height, and it defines nothing, and nothing on it defines *holder*:**

> **A THING THAT IS HELD IN COMMON CAN BE HELD BY ONE PERSON.**
> **A PERSON IS NOT HELD IN COMMON.**
> **NEITHER OF THOSE IS A COMPLAINT.**

He had come across on the Sunday with the others and up here on the Monday, and had written to the town himself on the Friday before Christmas, on his own paper, and Mavis Bray had written back and had put him in the room and had told him so, and had not made a thing of it. He was not on the agenda. He was not a party to anything. There were about nine people in that room and every one of them belonged to that town in the way that a person belongs to a place they were born.

"So who runs the works," said Jonas Mercer, from the doorway, and the clerk turned round, and everybody in the room found it helpful that he had asked.

"Walter," somebody said, before she had got to it.

"Walter Sallow," said the clerk. "Since before the town had it, as a matter of fact. He was the fitter at the works for about fifty years, and then he retired, and then the works had no fitter, and now he does the works, and he has done since the year the town bought it."

"And he is not paid for it," said Jonas Mercer.

"He is paid for it," said the clerk, and she was not offended and she was not defensive, and she said it the way a person corrects a thing that has been corrected before. "He is on the town's books, he is paid the going rate, he has a house at a rent the town sets and the rent is small, and he has a pension besides it and that is not our business. It is all in writing. It has been in writing for the whole time."

"In writing, and named," said Jonas Mercer.

"Named," she agreed. "Walter Sallow, named on the arrangement, the same as the gate and the shelter and the clinic and the yard. It is on the sheet on that wall and it has been on the sheet since before you were born and it will be on it long after."

Somebody at the back said, "He'll be at the intake at six tomorrow," and somebody else said, "He'll be in the shed with the mower on Saturday," and both of those were said the way people say a thing that is a comfort to them, and neither of them was said to Jonas Mercer.

**AND THE ARGUMENT OF THAT ROOM, WHICH IS ALSO THE ARGUMENT OF THE TOWN, AND WHICH NOBODY IN IT THINKS OF AS AN ARGUMENT AT ALL:**

"A duty is an act and not a place," said Mavis Bray, "and that is the only thing anybody has to remember about all this. You cannot give a job to a man and you cannot give a man to a job. If you could, what would you have written? Walter Sallow is the works? He is not the works. He is a man, and he turns up, and a man is not a place you can put down and come back to. That is not a fault in the arrangement. That is what an arrangement is. And the reason he cannot be replaced is not that we would rather he stayed. It is that there is nobody who can."

Nobody in the room disagreed with any part of that. Nobody in the room disagreed with any part of it at all.

"You have got a gate, a works, a clinic, a shelter and a yard," said Jonas Mercer, "and about three thousand people, and every one of those five is in that man's hands on some day in the week. What happens to the clinic if he is ill for a week?"

"He has never been ill for a week," said somebody.

"He is eighty-six."

"He is eighty-six," Mavis Bray agreed, "and he had the pump house at that works going by a quarter to six this morning without being asked to, and if he is ever ill for a week, then the works is shut until he is not ill, and the gate is shut until he is not ill, and the shelter's boiler is somebody's problem for that week, and that is what it is, and it is not anybody's fault and it is not anybody being let down, because nobody has been promised anything. There is nothing here that anybody has to do."

That was the sentence. He held it in his head, which is a thing he is good at, and he did not write it anywhere, because there is nowhere in this borough to write a sentence like that.

Nobody in that room had lied to him. Nobody in that room had hidden a thing. And what the room had said out loud, in the plainest order, was that the whole of a town is held together by an act somebody performs, and that a performance is not a place, and that therefore it cannot be held, and that therefore the person performing it cannot be replaced, and that therefore nobody has ever thought to ask him whether he would like to stop.

**AND HE SAID THE OTHER HALF OF IT IN A CORRIDOR, TO HIMSELF, IN A VOICE THAT DID NOT GO ABOVE A NORMAL ONE, AND TO NOBODY.**

*A place cannot be handed over, so when the person goes, the thing goes, and everybody in that room has said yes to that, and none of them has thought about the day.*

He stood at the window at the end of the corridor for a while and looked at a yard with a lorry in it and a tractor at the far end of it.

At about one o'clock Nell Ardent said, "He is not in the room."

"No."

"He is the whole of the room," she said, "and he is out there somewhere doing one of the five, and they have been in that room an hour over the arrangement without asking whether he would swap two of them for a day off."

"He will not swap two of them for a day off," said Jonas Mercer. "He will not be asked, either, and if he were asked he would say that the gate has to be open at seven and the shelter boiler is not a thing you leave, and he would be right, and then everybody would feel bad, and it would be the end of that."

Nell Ardent did not answer that and wrote nothing down, and she had a handbag with four sheets in it that were in her own handwriting, and she has never let anybody else hold one of them and is not going to.

At half past one Jonas Mercer walked out of the yard gate, which was up, and up the bank to the intake, because somebody in a room had said *he'll be on the intake at six tomorrow* and it was half past one on the Wednesday and it was worth the eight minutes to find out what sort of place this was.

The works was shut and the intake was not. There was a man of about eighty-six sitting on an upturned crate at the end of the steel platform with his back to the water and his legs over the side, doing nothing at all, in the cold, with a flask beside him and his hat over his face, which is what a man of eighty-six does at half past one on a Wednesday in January when the hard part of his day was over at seven that morning.

Jonas Mercer did not go down and did not speak to him and did not ask him anything, and the man did not lift the hat, and the eight minutes were the last eight minutes of that day that had nothing to do with the room.

**AND THE TOWN IS CHEAPER THAN THE OTHER ONE AND BETTER, AND JONAS MERCER HAS NOT BEEN IN THE ROOM, AND NOBODY HAS ASKED THAT MAN ANYTHING, AND THE ROOM WILL DO THE SAME THING ON THE SAME DAY NEXT MONTH AND THE MONTH AFTER THAT, AND NOTHING IN IT IS A SECRET, AND THAT IS THE FINDING AND IT IS NOT A RELIEF.**
