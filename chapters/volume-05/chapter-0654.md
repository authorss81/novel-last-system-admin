# Chapter 0654 — Read Out Loud

Friday the sixteenth of July 2021, in this borough. He is in a kitchen off the parade from about ten in the morning until about half past twelve, and there are three of them in that room for the first time since the Wednesday of the nineteenth of May, and the whole of it is six lines of pencil in a hand that is not his, read out loud in front of the person who wrote them.

**THE WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES OPENS THE DOOR ON A FRIDAY TO A MAN SHE HAS AGREED WITH ON A BENCH NEVER TO PUT THEIR TWO HEADS TOGETHER IN THAT HOUSE, AND SHE STANDS ASIDE AND HE GOES THROUGH TO A KITCHEN AND READS A LIST OUT LOUD IN IT, AND HE DOES NOT ASK HER WHY IT IS IN THAT ORDER, HE ONLY SAYS THAT IT IS NOT AN ORDER AND WAITS, AND SHE ANSWERS HIM, AND THE ANSWER IS THAT THE FIRST THING ON IT IS THE ONE SHE WANTS AND IS NOT GOING TO ASK FOR.**

He is a man in his early forties. She is a woman in her late forties. She is a woman in her late seventies. There is no fourth person in this house.

---

He has been sitting in a van on the parade since about half past eight with a gas envelope on the passenger seat, and here is what he did in that van, which is the thing that makes the morning happen, and he will not enjoy knowing that.

At about twenty to ten, a van came off the ring road and turned up the parade.

He watched it go past.

That is all. He has driven up that road a handful of times since the twenty-eighth of June and he has looked for that van every single time without admitting it to himself, and on the Friday morning of the sixteenth of July he watched it come and go past him and did not follow it, and then he sat there until ten o'clock, and then he drove up.

And the thing he is not going to tell anybody, and is not going to be able to explain if anybody asks him, is that if he had followed it he would not have had anything to say when he got there.

---

He knocks at about ten. Two and a slow one, which is his own, which he gave on a Wednesday in June and which was answered in about three seconds, and which is the only knock anybody has ever made on that door that the woman inside has recognised on the sound alone.

The door opens.

It is the woman in her late forties, and she has a bag of shopping in her hand, and she opens that door at about ten past ten on a Friday morning on a road off the parade.

---

And here is what happens on that doorstep, and it is about four seconds long, and neither of them will ever refer to it.

He says, "Is this a bad time."

And she says, "What's the day."

And he says, "Friday."

And there are about two seconds.

And she says, "It's a Friday."

And he says, "I know it's a Friday."

---

And she stands aside.

Not because she has decided anything. Because a woman who opens a door to a man with nothing in his hands steps aside, and he has a shoebox of experience at stepping through doors, and both of them do it at the same time in about a second.

And the kitchen light is on behind her and the woman in her late seventies is standing at the sink with a cloth in her hand.

---

And here is what the two of them do in a passage at about ten past ten on a Friday morning, which they agreed on a bench on the parade in May would never happen.

Nothing.

He does not ask. She does not explain. Neither of them looks at the other one for more than about a second and a half, and what they are both doing is working out the same sum, which is that they are in a kitchen with a woman in it, and that neither of them can get out of the room without making a thing of it, and that the only possible version of this is to be normal at each other in a kitchen for an hour.

---

And then the woman in her late forties says the thing that the whole morning turns on, and she says it to him at the door and not quietly, and she does not apologise for it afterwards.

And she says, "The fourth one."

And he says, "Yes."

And she says, "In the kitchen with me."

And he says, "If you don't want to."

And she says, "I'm not saying I don't want to. I'm saying I want it said out loud that I know."

---

And he goes into that kitchen and the table is under the window with a cloth on it, and there is a piece of ash about the thickness of a coin planed to a taper and driven under the foot on the window side, and the passage light is on, and there is a stove with a new element in it.

And the gas envelope is on the table in the middle.

It is face up. It is not square with the edge of the table. It is slightly crooked, about a half inch out, the way a piece of paper lands when somebody drops it rather than puts it down.

---

He puts his hand on the table and does not pick it up.

He says, "Is that the one."

And the woman in her late forties says, "That's the one."

And he says, "Right."

---

And then the woman in her late seventies says, from the sink, without turning round:

"You'll want to read it, then."

---

And he reads it.

He picks it up and it is face up and the pencil is facing him, and he reads it out loud in a kitchen off the parade at about ten past ten on the sixteenth of July 2021, in front of two people, one of whom is standing at a sink with a cloth in her hands.

He reads it slowly, because he is reading a stranger's handwriting, and because there are six lines of it, and because this is the only time in his life he is going to be in a room while somebody else hears them.

*the gate*

*the front room*

*the window in the back room*

*the hotplate on the left*

*the bath tap*

*the passage light*

---

And at the third line he stops for half a second, because a woman in her early thirties from two doors down took that glass out and taped a sheet across the opening, and he was not here for it and he knows nothing whatever about it, and she can see him knowing nothing, and neither of them says a word.

And he reads the gate twice.

He reads it the first time and then he reads it again, and the second time he is not reading it out loud so much as checking that he has it right, and that is the second time in this borough that a man has read that first line twice.

---

And then he puts it down on the table and says the two things a tradesman says.

And he says, "That's not an order."

---

And then he stops.

And he does not ask her why it is in that order.

And here is the thing, and it takes him about four seconds to manage, and four fifths of it is doing nothing at all.

He says, "That's not an order." And then he waits.

That is all he does. He puts a piece of paper down on a table and says a sentence that is not a question, and then he stands there in a kitchen and does not fill the space.

---

And the woman in her late forties does not fill it either.

She stands by the door with her shopping bag on the table and she looks at the floor.

And the woman in her late seventies stands at the sink with a cloth in her hands.

And there are about eight seconds of that kitchen in which nobody says anything, and they are the first eight seconds in this business in which nothing is being managed.

---

And then the woman in her late seventies says it.

And what she says is this, and she says it to the wall above the sink, and she does not turn round for it, and she takes about four seconds over it.

And she says, "I wrote it in the order I can't be doing with them."

And the woman in her late forties says, "Which is which."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "The window's third and not first, and I'll tell you why it's third. Because I did write it third and I could not have written it first. If it had been first I would have asked somebody about it inside a fortnight, and I would have had a stranger in this room before I knew what his name was."

And the man says, "The tap?"
And the woman in her late seventies says, "The tap's fifth and it has been dripping since before Christmas and I have a folded cloth in the bowl under it, and it does that all week, and I have never once said that out loud to anybody, and I am saying it to two of you in a kitchen on a Friday."

---

And then she says the thing.

And this is the sentence she has been refusing to say to anybody in this borough since before Christmas, and it takes her about three seconds to get to and she gets there by herself.

And she says, "And the gate's at the top because it's the only one on there I actually want."

---

And there is about a second and a half of that kitchen.

And the man does not say *why*.

And the woman in her late forties does not say *why*.

---

And the woman in her late seventies says it, because they are not going to, and because there are three of them in a room.

And she says, "Because a gate is for letting somebody in. Everybody that has come to that door since before Christmas has knocked on it. Two of them knocked because they had knocked before. One of them knocked with a screwdriver in her pocket because she had seen a blanket. And I have not asked one single one of them to. I wrote it down and I rubbed it out and I wrote it again and I put it at the top."

---

And the woman in her late forties puts her hand over her mouth.

And it is about two seconds and then she takes it away again, because she is in her late forties and she is not seventeen.

---

And then the woman in her late forties says the thing that has to be said, and she says it standing up, to the woman and not to the man, and it is the only sentence anybody in this borough has said in this kitchen that is not a story about anything.

And she says, "I'll do your gate."

---

And here is what that costs, and it is the whole of what it costs, and it is not about a gate.

She has said it in a kitchen where a man is standing.

She said *your gate* to a woman of seventy-eight with a man of forty in the room, and there is now no version of that Friday on which the two of them have not both been in that kitchen at once, and the bench on the parade is not a thing that has been broken. It is a thing that does not exist any more.

And the woman at number twelve now has two people in her kitchen who have both been to it on the same morning and have both stayed.

And the woman in her late forties has just committed herself to knocking on a door she has never knocked on, to be thanked by a woman she has never been thanked by, and she does not know what a woman of seventy-eight says when she is thanked.

---

And the man does the only thing available, which is be a tradesman about it.

And he says, "Take the weight off it before you shut it. Don't shut it on anything you've just fitted, and if you do shut it, ease it again in the cold."

And the woman in her late forties says, "Right."

And the man says, "And the drops on the back of the frame are painted in. They go across the floor so the gate can't be blown open, and right now they're one solid lump with old gloss over them. That's a chisel and a wire brush and about ten minutes, and I want it said now that I haven't done it."

And the woman in her late forties says, "Do it."

And he says, "Not today. I haven't got a grinder on the van on a Friday afternoon."

And the woman in her late forties says, "Right."

And the woman in her late seventies says, from the sink, "How much is that."

And he says, "Nothing."

---

And the woman in her late forties says, "It's not nothing. That's two hours for you on a Friday."

And the man says, "It's not nothing if you do it in your own time and I don't want to come back for a fight about it, so I want it said here, in front of her, that it's nothing."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Thank you."

And the man says, "Don't."

And the woman in her late forties says, "I don't know what to do with that."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Put it on the list."

---

And then there is about an hour of that kitchen in which none of the three of them says one word about a gate.

They make tea. The woman in her late forties makes it, in a kitchen she has been in twice, at a table she fixed a fortnight ago, and she knows where the cups are because the cups are where the cups are in every house in this borough, and nobody has to be told anything.

And for about half an hour the three of them sit in a front room and do not talk about it.

And here is the last part of it, and it is about a quarter past twelve, and it is about five minutes long, and it is the only part of that morning that is funny.

And the man says, "Which line am I on."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "The fourth one's done."

And the man says, "What's the second one."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "The front room."

And he says, "What's wrong with the front room."

And there is about a second and a half of that kitchen.

And the woman in her late seventies says, "It's the only room anybody sits in."

And the man says, "Ah."

And the woman in her late forties says, "That's a job all right, then."

---

And the woman in her late forties puts her hand over her mouth again.

And the man says, "Right."

And the woman in her late forties says, "Don't put that in your diary."

---

And he goes at about half past twelve.

And the woman in her late forties is still there, and he does not wait for her and she does not come out at the same time as him, and there is about a minute in which she is in that kitchen on her own with a woman of seventy-eight, and neither of them says anything about the bench, and neither of them is going to.

And at the gate he does not put his boot on it.

He stands on the top step with his hand on it and gives it a push and lets it swing, the way you do to a gate you are about to work on, and it swings about three inches and stops against something on the hinge side, and he makes a noise with his mouth that he would not make in front of anybody.

---

And she is standing at the sink at about twenty to one with six lines of pencil on a gas envelope in front of her, and the fourth one is done, and there is a woman in her late forties in her front room who has just said she will do the first one.

And she is going to have to open that door to somebody she gave a stool to, and somebody she has never thanked, and she has no idea in the world what she is going to say when that woman does it, and there is nobody she can ask what to say.

And she has never once in this borough managed to say a true thing to anybody first go.

**HE READ IT OUT LOUD IN FRONT OF TWO PEOPLE AND NEVER ASKED HER WHY IT WAS IN THAT ORDER, AND SHE TOLD THEM ANYWAY, AND THE ORDER IS THE GATE FIRST BECAUSE A GATE IS FOR LETTING SOMEBODY IN AND SHE HAS NOT ASKED ONE SINGLE PERSON TO THAT DOOR SINCE BEFORE CHRISTMAS, AND THE SECOND ONE ON THE LIST IS THE ONLY ROOM ANYBODY SITS IN AND NOBODY HAD ASKED HER THAT BEFORE HE DID.**

(End of chapter 0654)
