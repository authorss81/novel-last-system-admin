# Chapter 0590 — Through the Wall

The Monday of the thirty-first of August 2020, from about half past five in the evening until about half past ten at night, in a house on a road in this borough.

**A SENTENCE WAS SAID IN THE FRONT ROOM OF THAT HOUSE THE NIGHT BEFORE SOME FURNITURE WENT OUT OF IT, AND IT WAS THE FIRST SENTENCE ANYBODY HAD SAID ABOUT THAT HOUSE THAT WAS WORTH SAYING, AND IT WAS SAID TO THE ONE WHO IS GOING, AND THE ONE WHO STAYS HEARD EVERY WORD OF IT FROM THE BACK OF THAT HOUSE AND SAID NOTHING AT ALL.**

The house is a two-up two-down on a road of about thirty of them and it is number sixteen. It has been in the family since her grandfather and it is going to a man in his late forties who is called by nobody in this chapter anything but the estate agent, and he has done nothing wrong and is not in this.

She is in her mid forties and she is the one who is going. The man is in his mid forties and he is the one who stays. Thirteen years married, and a van in the morning.

The removal men are booked for eight and that date is not hers to move. She has tried twice to move it. It is coming to a house a long way off where there is a woman in her early fifties who used to be her aunt and has offered the whole of the ground floor for as long as she wants it.

---

She is in the front room from about half past five because there is nothing left in the front room to sit on except the carpet and two chairs she is not taking.

He is in the kitchen at the back with the last of the boxes and the tea things and the things she has not decided about, and there is a wall between the front room and that kitchen, and it is an ordinary house and the wall is an ordinary wall, and you can hear a person speaking plainly through it if the house is quiet.

The man in his mid forties is in the front room with her because he has come to say he is not coming and she has already got used to that and he is saying it anyway.

She said, "You don't have to do this bit."

"I'm doing this bit," the man in his mid forties said. "I said I'd do it."

That is the arrangement they have had for a year, and both of them are entirely aware that he is doing a thing nobody asked him to do, and neither of them has ever mentioned that.

---

Then she said the sentence.

She said it at about twenty to seven, standing by the front window with a roll of tape in her hand, and the man in his mid forties was at the far end of that room with his coat already on.

She said: "It was a good house and we were no good in it, and I'm only sorry we took this long to work that out."

And the man in his mid forties stopped with his hand on the latch and turned round and said, "That's about the size of it," and neither of them said anything else at all, and he went out and got into his car and drove the length of the county to his brother's.

Here is the thing about that sentence that makes it the first one worth saying about that house. It is not a memory. It is not a complaint about the roof or the neighbour or the hot water or the road. It is a sentence in which a person says something about themselves and about somebody else in the same breath, in a house, and means it, and it had never been said in that house in thirty-one years, and there was nobody else in England who could have heard it.

At the back of that house the man in his mid forties was standing at the kitchen with a box open in front of him and he heard every word of it.

He heard the whole sentence. The wall is not that thick and she was not talking quietly and the man in his mid forties is not a man who talks quietly.

He stood there with a box of his own plates in both hands.

He did not go out. He had a rule in that house about not going into the front room when the two of them were in it, and the rule was hers and he had kept it, and it meant on that evening that he stood in that kitchen and heard his wife say the truest thing anybody had ever said about the house they had both spent a decade in, said to a man in a coat with his hand on the latch who had never once said a truer thing about anything.

And then the man in his mid forties said, "That's about the size of it," which is not a truer thing, and then a door, and then a car going away.

He carried on with the plates.

That is the whole of the evening, and here is all of it, because it does not go anywhere and it is not going to.

He carried on with the plates and then he did the last of the kitchen and he went up and checked the bedroom and came down and sat in the front room on the floor at about half past nine for a bit, and then he went to bed, and he did not go and say anything, and he slept.

It is a Monday night and a van is coming in the morning and he is not going to ask her about it before she has had some sleep, and he has not worked out how long he is going to be able to leave it, and he knows that it is going to be longer than he would like and shorter than she would like, and he has not asked her for a number because he does not want one.

Here is exactly why he is not going to raise it, and it is not a plan and it is not kindness.

If he raises it, she will find out that he heard it through a wall. And then she will find out that he stood in that kitchen and held a box of plates while a man agreed with her about the house they were going out of, and there is not one version of that conversation where he comes out of it with anything he wants.

So he will hold it. She has said the truest thing about that house, and it was said to the man in the coat, and the man in his mid forties has it and has nothing he can do with it and will not be able to do anything with it for some time.

**SHE SAID IT TO THE MAN WHO IS GOING AND THE ONE WHO STAYS HEARD IT THROUGH A WALL AND CARRIED ON WITH A BOX OF PLATES, AND IT IS STILL TRUE THAT HE HEARD IT, AND IT IS ALSO STILL TRUE THAT HE IS NOT GOING TO SAY ANYTHING, AND NEITHER OF THOSE IS GOING TO CHANGE.**
