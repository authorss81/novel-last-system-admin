# Chapter 0272 — Nobody Owns It

Wednesday the second of November 2016, from about half past seven in the evening, a hall off a parade in Tideglass.

**A SUM ARRIVED ON A PIECE OF PAPER WITH NOBODY BEHIND IT, AND A BOILER CAME OUT OF A CUPBOARD UNDER THE STAIRS OF A HALL, AND THE ROOM FOUND OUT THAT NOBODY OWNS EITHER.**

The offer has been on the door of the Methodist hall on Carrow Lane since about February. Any person who can show that hall does not have a working boiler may have one fitted at no charge, and that hall does not have a working boiler and has not had one since about 2004, and nobody has taken it, and the man in his sixties has said in a room that it will be there in March.

The same offer is on the door of the hall off this parade and has been since the summer, and the woman of about fifty-five was asked about it in the room on the Thursday by a man who opens doors for a living, who said he had come in good faith and that he was not going to come back.

"That is not a hall," she said. "That is a room with the gas on a meter and a key that belongs to a woman who pays for it out of her own money on a Monday or a Thursday depending what day she has remembered, and there is no body behind the door, and you may as well ask the radiator."

"I'd like to say yes to somebody."

"There is nobody to say yes to. That is what I am telling you and it is not me being difficult. There's a room, and a meter, and a key, and the meter is not the building's and neither is the key. If I had a say in that door it would have been shut in the summer and it would not have had your card on it."

---

The sheet came in the post on the Monday in an envelope with a window in it and it went to the woman with the key, because the address on the envelope was the hall and the hall is her post.

It was from the firm of heating engineers with the laminated card in the window two doors off the parade. It had a heading, a reference, a date in October and a figure of a hundred and eighty pounds. It said the figure was for the removal of a boiler from a cupboard under the stairs. It said the survey had been carried out on the seventh. And there was a space at the foot of it for a name and an account and nothing whatever written in it.

Nobody had asked for a survey. There was no such thing as anybody to ask.

"That's for taking a thing out," said the man from the parade. "That is not for mending anything. That is a van and two men and a skip, and they have put a figure on the back of it and it is the first figure that has ever come to this address off anybody."

"They've charged me for looking at it."

"They've charged nobody," said the man in his sixties. "They've put a figure on a piece of paper and there is nobody on it, and that is all that has happened. It will sit somewhere. That is what a piece of paper does when there is no body behind it. It sits."

The room worked out who owned the boiler, in about the time it takes to make a cup of tea, and it went round the table and it did not take long.

The freeholder of that building is a company in a city over the water that owns four shops on that parade and does not have a telephone number on its letterhead that anybody in this borough has ever rung. The hall itself was left in about 1981 to a trust, and the trust was wound up in about 1991, and the man in his forties found that out of a book in an office in about 2006 and told nobody at the time and has it in his phone. The gas is on a meter. The new boiler came from that same firm in 2004 and is theirs and works. The old one was boxed and wrapped in a sheet of building paper in 2004 by the man who came to put the new one in, who was being careful, and was put in that cupboard, and he was never asked to come back for it.

"That means nobody owns it," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"That means nobody owns it," said the man in his forties. "I am not claiming it and I want that on the table, and I am not refusing it either, and I would like the room to hear both of those, because in about an hour one of you is going to ask me which one I meant."

"Are you claiming it?"

"No," said the man in his forties.

"Are you refusing it?"

"No," said the man in his forties.

"Then put it back in the cupboard."

"It lives under that stair till the spring," said the man in his sixties, "and if it doesn't, then somebody has paid a hundred and eighty pound, and nobody at this table is going to be the one who paid it, so it goes back."

The woman of about fifty-five put the sheet on the table face up, and nobody turned it over, and nobody wrote anything on it, and it stayed on that table until the chairs went up and the woman with the key put her coat over it to stop it going under the table edge with the draught.

---

The cupboard under the stairs in that hall is a meter-high door in the angle where the stairs turn, and it has been sticking since about 2011, because the frame has been kicked a thousand times by people in a hurry going up a dark stair to a boiler.

The woman of about fifty-five stood up at about ten to nine to get her bag off the chair behind that door and put her hand flat on the cupboard to lean past it, and the door came off its hinge on the side away from her and came down on the boards with a noise like a coin dropped on a table.

Everything in the room stopped.

The boiler was in there, boxed, and it is a small one and it weighs what a small boiler weighs, and whoever boxed it in 2004 had taken the paper off it before he wrapped it, and there was a pencil mark on one of the boards of the box with a date and a name on it that nobody in that room could read at that light.

"That's off its hinge," said the woman of about fifty-five, from the floor, on her hands and knees.

"That's off its hinge," said the man in his forties.

"It is coming out or it is staying in," said the man in his sixties. "It is not going back in a cupboard with a door that will not shut, not after a hundred and eighty pound of paper about it."

"Then it comes out," said a man in his thirties, and nobody agreed with him out loud because there was nothing to agree to.

Nobody arranged any of it. The man in his sixties had a folding platform trolley in the boot of his car, because he has always had one and has never once used it for anything he was paid for, and the man in his thirties knew how much they were and where the third bracket was, and the other three in that room found out afterwards what had been in the boot of a car.

They got the paper off it and got it onto the trolley, and it went down the hall and out and down the step on the side where there is a handrail, and the man in his sixties took the front of the trolley on the pavement and the man in his thirties took the front on the step, and then the man in his sixties said he was going that way and would take it, and nobody had asked him to, and he had not said he would.

The meter by the hall door ticked and the gas was still on and nobody had asked for it to be on and nobody turned it off for about an hour.

**AND THE CUPBOARD UNDER THE STAIRS STOOD OPEN AND EMPTY WITH THE DOOR LYING FACE DOWN ON THE BOARDS BY THE RADIATOR THAT HAS BEEN OUT SINCE ABOUT 1998, AND THE CUP CAME OFF THE SHEET AT HALF PAST TEN AND WENT INTO A SINK, AND THE SHEET WENT BACK ON THAT TABLE FACE UP, AND THE TROLLEY WENT DOWN THE BACK LANE IN THE RAIN WITH A BOXED BOILER ON IT.**
