# Chapter 0720 — The Paper Out Of The Drawer

Tuesday the twenty-sixth of April 2022, in this borough. She is in her own kitchen and her own yard from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a piece of paper that has been in a drawer since December and is not in the drawer tonight.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES TAKES THE FOLDED PAPER WITH THREE LINES ON IT OUT OF THE DRAWER UNDER HER WORKTOP AND PUTS IT ON HER OWN TABLE AND LEAVES IT THERE, SHUTS HER OWN BACK DOOR ON A STRANGER WHO HAS BEEN TOLD HER SISTER WILL BE WAITING AT NUMBER TEN, AND DOES NOT GO NINETY YARDS ROUND TO NUMBER NINE TO ASK HER WHY.**

She is a woman in her early thirties. She is in her own house and her own yard all day. A woman looking for her sister is at her own back door in the morning. She speaks to her across her own yard and shuts the door on her.

---

The drawer is under the worktop and she opens it before there is any light in the kitchen.

Oak front, cup dark, shut since the twenty-eighth of December. She has opened it twice since then and sorted it both times without taking the paper out, hands either side of the open front, eyes going over each thing in turn.

She takes it out this morning.

It comes up with the ply on top of it, and the ply comes off easily because ply always comes off easily, and the paper is folded in three and it is soft along the folds from being folded since before Christmas and warm from the drawer.

She opens it out on the table under the window with her hand flat on the two bottom corners so it does not curl back.

Three lines. A roll. A tin. A lip of timber under a new edge.

And nothing else. Not a date, not a price, not a name of a person, not a day of the week. Three lines that say what a thing would be made of.

She reads them. She knows them by heart. She has not read them out loud to anybody since the twenty-eighth of December and she is not going to today, and she is nearly gone through them twice and can see the last line without looking at it.

She squares the paper on the table with the ply beside it and the tape roll on top of the ply with about three and a half inches left on it, and the hammer and the screwdriver side by side under both, and the pencil on the ply.

Then she shuts the drawer, empty, and it makes a noise it has not made all winter.

---

The knock is at her own back door at about half past the morning and it is the flat knock of somebody who has knocked twice already.

She wipes her hands and goes down the yard and the mat at the door is empty and the door shuts true behind her when she opens it.

"Yes."

"I'm looking for a woman," the woman says. "She told me to wait here. Number ten."

"Who told you that."

"She did. On the telephone. This morning."

That is it. She stands in the yard with the door open and a stranger on the step who has been told to wait at her gate by her own sister, and she does not ask her what the woman is called, and she does not ask her to describe her, and she does not say that no such thing has ever happened in this house.

She says, "There's nobody here for that."

"Are you sure? She was quite definite."

"There's nobody here for that," she says again, and closes the door on her.

---

She does not go round after.

That is the whole of the day and she has known it is going to be the whole of the day since she shut the door.

Number nine is ninety yards. It is her sister's. She has walked it in the dark more times than she can lay down and she could walk it now with her eyes shut, and there is a woman at the other end of it who has just told a stranger to wait at a gate she does not own, and the reason that woman did it is a jug lid and a cloth and four words in a hall, and the only person on earth who knows that is the woman who was standing at the sink.

She stands in her own yard with the door shut and thinks about walking round, and what stops her is not politeness.

What stops her is that if she goes round and asks her sister about it, then her sister will have to say something out loud about having said it, and whatever she says next will be a sentence she cannot take back, and they will both be standing in a kitchen with that sentence in it for the rest of the spring.

And if her sister says *I said it before I looked at the door*, then she will say *why didn't you look*, and then there is no answer to that which is not a thing about how they both live.

So she goes back up the yard and does not go round, and that is the whole cost of it, and she pays it by doing the washing.

---

The shed is at the end of the yard and it is doing its roof in, the same as it has done since the autumn, the felt off the whole of the front edge and a pale line where it lifts and a dark underneath.

She pegs the small washing out because it is a day when it is dry enough to do it, and the line goes where it has gone since May, which is straight across, and the draught comes off the top of the shed and goes across the yard and through the line, and every pegged thing on that line flutters.

She does not move the line. Moving the line round the shed is a morning's work with two people and a step under the corner, and it is on the list of things she has meant to do since May and has never named a date for.

A bit of ply will not do it. The felt comes on in strips three foot wide and the front edge of that shed is longer than any strip, and she has had this worked out since the autumn and has never told anybody she has worked it out.

She smooths each thing on the line with her palm the way she does, and the tea towels slap against the line and go quiet and slap again.

---

The telephone rings in the kitchen at about the middle of the afternoon.

She is standing at the line with a peg in her hand when she hears it, and it is a telephone she never uses, and there is nobody in this house who is expecting anybody.

She goes in and stands in the kitchen doorway and lets it ring.

It goes nine times.

On the table in the middle of the kitchen, under the window, in the flat morning light, is a piece of paper with three lines on it and a bit of ply and a tape roll on the ply.

She looks at the two of them. A piece of paper with three lines on it is a job. A job is a thing that can be given to a person, and there is a man with a card in a van in this borough and a number in a telephone and a sheet of scribing card in somebody's shirt pocket, and any one of the three could be given this paper on this afternoon by a woman standing in this doorway.

And if she picks the telephone up and it is a stranger, then she is a person who answers a telephone, and that is a person who asks.

So she stands there until it stops.

---

At dark the paper is still on the table.

She takes the washing in and folds it in squares on the kitchen table beside it, and the two things share the table, which is the first time anything has shared that table in a month.

She does not put the paper back. She puts it under the washing.

She does not put it under the washing to hide it. She puts it under the washing because the washing has to go somewhere and the table is the only flat thing in this kitchen, and she is not going to stand and decide where a piece of paper lives.

Then she blacks the stove, working the rag into the iron round and round until it comes up a flat grey, and she wipes her hands on the rag, and she sets the kettle back where it lives.

---

Her sister does not come.

That is the other thing about this day and she only sees it when the light has gone. Her sister has been at this gate three times since before Christmas, twice with soup and once with bread, and today there has been a stranger on the step at half past the morning asking for her and her sister has not come up the front path at any point in the whole of the day.

She stands in the kitchen and does not think about what that means, and she does not go to the window to look down the road.

The lamp at the end of the road burns where it burns, amber over a gate, steady through the cold. She cannot see it from her kitchen and she knows it is on, because it was on in December and in January and in February, and lamps left on stay on.

The paper is under the washing. The drawer is shut and it is empty for the first time since December. The shed is still letting water in where it has let it in all winter, and nobody has been under it, and nobody is going to be.

---

She puts her coat on over the apron and goes out to the gate at dark, which is not a thing she does.

The mat at the back door is still empty. The door she shut on a stranger at half past the morning is still shut true, and the shed is still taking the weather at its front edge, and there is nothing in her own yard that any of this is about.

She stands at the gate and looks down the road.

Ninety yards, and she could walk it with her eyes shut, and there is a woman at the other end of it who told a stranger to wait at a gate she does not own, and there is a stranger who has been at this gate once already today and gone away with nothing.

In number nine there is a light on in the front room with the curtains open, which is the room with the stool in it and the sixty-watt bulb in the ceiling that has been in since December.

She looks at that light and she does not go.

It would take about four minutes. And then her sister would have to say something out loud about having said it, and whatever she said next would be a sentence neither of them could take back, and they would both be standing in a kitchen with it in for the rest of the spring.

So she goes back up her own path and in by the back door, and it shuts true behind her, and the light at number nine is still on while she does the last of the washing.
