# Chapter 0652 — The Fourth Thing

Wednesday the seventh of July 2021, in this borough. He is in a kitchen off the parade from about half past ten until about one, and there is a stove out of the working order on the tiles and a table under the window with somebody else's shim under one of its feet, and the whole of it is a hotplate that was fourth of six.

**A MAN CARRIES A SHOBOX UP THAT PATH ON A WEDNESDAY AND DOES THE FOURTH THING ON A LIST AND GETS IT RIGHT, AND THE TWO OF THEM SPEND THE WHOLE MORNING IN THAT KITCHEN SAYING A GREAT DEAL TO EACH OTHER AND NOT ONE SINGLE QUESTION ABOUT ANY OF THE SIX, AND THE THING HE CAN SEE FROM THE DOOR AND CANNOT ASK ABOUT IS AN ASH SHIM UNDER A TABLE FOOT THAT HE DID NOT PUT THERE.**

He is a man in his early forties. She is a woman in her late seventies. There is no third person in that house.

---

He has been driving up that road for about an hour and has not stopped at the end of it once.

He goes up and round the parade and down and round the parade and up, and he does it twice, and the second time he puts the handbrake on outside a shop on the parade and sits there for about two minutes with the engine off, and there is a shoebox on the passenger seat that has been on the top of a bench since Monday morning and has not been out of his sight.

---

And here is what he has to solve before he can knock, and it is not a technical problem.

He cannot arrive with nothing in his hands. That was the rule he set himself on the Monday and it has not changed and it is going to stop him at that door.

But he cannot arrive with the shoebox in his hands either, because the second the box comes through that door he is a man who picked up a piece of paper off a woman's kitchen table on the tenth of June, read it, and said nothing about it for a month, and there is no version of that conversation which begins well.

So he sits outside a shop for two minutes with a hotplate element in a shoebox trying to work out how to do a favour without appearing to have read anything.

---

He gets it, in the end, and it is not clever.

He goes into the shop and he buys a bulb. A five watt one. And he pays for it with a note and he comes out with a bulb in a paper bag in his hand, and the bulb is the reason he is on that road.

And the bulb is a real bulb and he does not need a bulb, and he has paid a couple of pounds into a shop on the parade to be allowed to arrive somewhere with a bag.

---

He is up that path at about half past ten with a shoebox in a canvas bag and a bulb in a paper bag.

He knocks. Two and a slow one, which is his own.

And she opens the door in about three seconds with her hand on it, because the latch has never been on, and she looks at the bags and then at him.

And she says, "You've come about the hotplate."

---

And he says, "Yes."

And she says, "Right."

And then she stands aside and he goes in and puts the canvas bag in the passage and the paper bag on the table, and the table is under the window with a cloth on it and the cloth is crooked and has been laid down twice this morning by somebody who has nothing else to do with her hands.

---

And here is the first thing he sees in that kitchen that he did not expect, and it is in the first four seconds and it is what makes the morning go the way it goes.

The table is not crooked.

He can see it from the doorway. He has been in about fifteen hundred kitchens in this borough and he can tell a shimmed table from six feet away, because a shimmed table stands flat, dead flat, and never rocks, and that is not what a table looks like in a house like this.

And it is ash. It is pale ash, planed off the end of a length to a taper and sawn flush, which takes an experienced hand and about ten minutes, and it is not a card and it is not a wedge you buy.

Somebody who knows what they are doing has been to this house, about a fortnight ago, and has done the thing he would have done.

---

And he stands in a doorway and looks at that for about three seconds, and he has a shoebox in a bag and a bulb in a paper bag and he now cannot arrive with nothing in his hands, because somebody else has already arrived with something in theirs and it has been done and paid for and not mentioned.

---

And what he does is the only thing available, which is he says nothing about the table, and he starts on the stove.

---

Here is what the stove is, and it is worth setting down because it is a real thing and he does it properly and it takes him most of the morning.

It is a cooker with four hotplates on top and an oven underneath and a grill, and it has a back panel and two sides that come off with a coin, and the sides come off because that is how these things are built and it is not because anybody has been inside it.

The left-hand front hotplate is the one that is going. It has been going since before Christmas.

And what going means on that plate is not that it does not get hot. What it means is that it gets hot, and then it stops getting hot, and then it gets hot again, and nobody in the world can say when. It drops out. It has been dropping out since before Christmas, and a woman who has been cooking on it has been cooking on it with a mind that is only half on the job.

---

He takes the side off. He takes the back off. He pulls the element out about four inches and puts a cloth round it because the ceramic on those things is like a hot plate and it does not last if you knock it.

And the old element is not burnt through. He looks at it for about a minute and it is a perfectly good element and it has a burnt patch about the size of a thumbnail on the underside where the terminal is, and the terminal has gone.

And what he says out loud in that kitchen, to a woman of seventy-eight who is sitting at a table with a cloth on it, is the most useful sentence anybody has said in that house since before Christmas.

And he says, "That's the terminal."

And she says, "The what."

And he says, "That's the bit where it joins up. It's arcing across there instead of through it, and when that happens the plate drops out. It doesn't get hotter. It goes off in the middle of cooking it. You put a pan on that at six and it comes up and goes down and comes up, and you have to stand and watch it like a kettle."

And that is the moment, and it is about mid-morning on the seventh of July, and it changes something.

She says, "So I could have been standing at that stove with a pan on it and not known."

And he says, "You could."

And she says, "Right."

And she puts her hands flat on the cloth on the table.

And she says, "That's all I wanted to know."

---

He is about halfway through putting the new one in when she says the thing.

She says it to the floor, not to him, and she has been working up to it since about half past ten.

And she says, "There's a thing I keep not asking."

And he says, "Go on."

And there are about four seconds in which a woman of seventy-eight is at a table in her own kitchen with a man on his knees in front of her stove.

And she says, "No. It's daft."

And he says, "I read a thing off your table."

And she says, "You did."

And he says, "Yes."

---

And she does not go on.

And he does not push, and he goes back to the element, and the new one goes in with four terminals and a spring clip and a bolt through a bracket, and it takes about a quarter of an hour, and he does it without a book, which he has never done with a new element in his life and does not tell her about.

---

And the last hour and a bit is the work of putting it back together.

He bolts the back panel on. He clips the sides. He checks the earth strap. He does not need to do the earth strap.

And he turns everything on, all four plates and the oven, and the fan comes on and the light comes on and the thermostat comes on, and then he takes an oven thermometer off the shelf and stands it on the middle plate and leaves it there about ten minutes, and that is the only test that means anything.

And then he puts a pan of water on the new hotplate at three and it comes up in about ten minutes, and he watches it, and she watches him watch it, and neither of them says one word for the whole of those ten minutes.

---

And in that ten minutes she sees three things and he sees one of them.

She sees that he has not mentioned the passage light.

He has been under the worktop and in the doorway and he has looked straight at that light four times, and it is a five watt bulb he fitted himself on a Wednesday in June, and he has not said one word about it, and she put it on her own list at the bottom of six things and did not ask him for it and did not ask the young woman of number ten either.

She sees that he has not looked at the envelope, because there is no envelope in that kitchen.

And she sees the table, and the shim, and that he saw the shim at four seconds and has not mentioned it once in the whole morning.

---

And at about a quarter to one she says the thing that is going to be in her head for the rest of the afternoon, and she says it standing up, and it is not the question she has been building.

And she says, "Somebody's been under my table."

And he says, "Somebody has."

And she says, "Right."

And he says, "It's good work. That's ash, planed, tapered and flush, and no glue anywhere in it, and whoever did that did it the way I'd do it."

And she says, "It was the woman."

And he does not ask which woman.

---

He packs up at about one. It goes in the van in about four minutes, which is the part of this he will think about most, because a stove takes two hours and takes four minutes to put away and that is the trade and he has never once liked it.

And he is in the passage and she is in the kitchen and the light in the passage is on.

---

And here is the last of it, and it takes about two seconds and neither of them refers to it afterwards.

He goes out through the front door and down the step and the gate comes in about four inches on its own, the way it does, and bang.

And he stops with his boot on it.

He does not lift it. He does not put his hand on it. He stands there with the weight of his boot on the gate and the gate does not move, and he looks at it, and the woman of seventy-eight is standing in her own front doorway about four feet away looking at it too.

And they look at it together for about two seconds.

And he lifts his boot off and goes down the path, and she shuts the door, and neither of them says one word about a gate.

---

And here is what she has at about ten past one on the seventh of July, standing in her own kitchen with a stove that works.

Three things off a list of six, in the wrong order, by three people who did not tell each other and none of whom has been asked a single question about any of it.

And she has a woman in her early thirties who put a sheet in her window and did not ask why.

And she has a man in a jumper who fitted her passage light in four minutes because it was the sixth thing on a list and it was the easiest thing to do.

And she has a woman in her late forties who took that leg out of the top of that table with a chisel and a mallet and put it back with a card and an ash shim and did not say so.

And not one of the three has asked her a single question, and she has not asked one of them one.

---

And she looks at the stove.

And she puts the cloth on the table straight, because it was crooked, and then she cannot leave it like that, and she puts it crooked again, and she goes and stands at the sink.

**A MAN DID THE FOURTH THING ON A LIST AND TOLD HER WHAT IT ACTUALLY WAS, WHICH WAS THAT THE PLATE DROPPED OUT IN THE MIDDLE OF COOKING IT, AND THAT SHE COULD HAVE STOOD AT THAT STOVE WITH A PAN ON IT AND NOT KNOWN, WHICH WAS THE ONLY THING SHE HAD ACTUALLY WANTED TO KNOW, AND STOOD WITH A BOOT ON A GATE FOR TWO SECONDS WITHOUT SAYING A WORD ABOUT IT, AND SHE IS STANDING IN A KITCHEN WITH THREE THINGS OFF A LIST AND FOUR PEOPLE WHO HAVE BEEN IN THAT ROOM AND NOT ONE OF THEM HAS ASKED HER A QUESTION, AND THE ONE AT THE TOP OF THE LIST IS STILL THE ONE SHE WANTS.**

(End of chapter 0652)
