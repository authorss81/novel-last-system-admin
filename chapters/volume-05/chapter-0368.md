# Chapter 0368 — What It Was For

Sunday the twelfth of November 2017, from about nine in the morning until about half past five in the afternoon, a block of flats at the top of a street, and a shop off a parade, in Tideglass.

**A MAN LAID ABOUT A HUNDRED AND THIRTY SQUARE YARD OF HARD STANDING ALONG ONE SIDE OF THAT BLOCK BETWEEN THE AUGUST AND THE NOVEMBER ON HIS OWN MONEY AND HIS OWN BARROW, AND NOBODY STOPPED HIM AND NOBODY ASKED HIM WHY, AND IT IS FINISHED AND EVERY PERSON IN THAT BUILDING CAN SEE IT AND NOT ONE OF THE SIX OF THEM CAN SAY WHAT IT WAS SUPPOSED TO BE.**

The block is eight flats in a building that was a school annexe, and it sits high up a road with a yard down one side of it about twenty foot wide, and at the top of that yard there is a back gate into a lane and the lane is where everybody in that building puts their rubbish and where everybody in that building comes in by.

About a hundred and thirty square yard of that yard was grass and a raised bed of heather along the side of the building, and the heather had been there since the building was done and nobody in that block has ever looked after it.

The man in his thirties has a yard about two streets away and a pickup and a barrow and a roller he borrows from a man about four miles off, and he did that work on the twelve Saturdays and Sundays between the sixth of August and the fourth of November.

He did it in three goes. A layer of hardcore about three inch deep, brought in nine barrow-loads at a time on the back of the pickup, and then sand and cement mixed by hand at the ratio he mixes it, and then rolled while it was still soft, and the whole of it is about three inch thick and it is now the surface of that yard from the gate right along the side of the building and out to the bottom of the two steps at each end.

It cost him about a hundred and ninety pounds in sand and stone and cement and he has never asked anybody for any of it and nobody has ever offered him any of it.

---

He did it on the Saturdays because it is the only time he has, and he started at about nine and stopped at about four, and nobody in that block knew he was doing it.

The man in the thirties who was in that block in the fourth week of August saw him in the yard with a barrow and did not go out. The woman who lives in the first of those eight flats, on the ground floor at the gate end, came out of her back door with a plate in her hand on the Sunday in September and stood in the doorway and watched a man lay a barrow-load of stone about nine foot from her step, and she went back in and shut the door, and she did not say anything to him.

On the Saturday after that a man on the top floor came down into the yard in his slippers to get his bike and found the whole length of it taken up with a barrow and a shovel, and stood in the doorway at the other end of it, and the man in his thirties said, "You can get through there," and the man went back in.

Nobody has ever stopped him. Nobody in that block has ever asked him what he was doing or who had told him to do it, and he has never said, and the two men in that block who have spoken to him about it spoke to him in the wrong week.

What it did, in the end, was this.

It came out level with the bottom of both sets of steps where before the ground fell about four inch away from them, and now the water that comes off the side of that building goes along that surface and off the far end of it and out through a gap in a brick at the bottom, and on the two days a week when the water is coming down hard it comes off the end of it and along the side of the building and under the step of the first flat, which is the flat of the woman with the plate, and puts about a hand's breadth of water on the tiles of her back step.

She has seen it happen on the Sunday and on the Wednesday and she has wiped it with a cloth both times, and she has never gone out and stood in that yard about it, and she has never said anything to any of the other seven people in that building about it either, and one of them has noticed the cloth.

What it also did was take the raised bed away. He took the heather out on the second Saturday in September in four barrow-loads and put it in the corner of the yard against the fence, and the woman who lives in the fourth flat asked him, on the Sunday, not to put it in the corner because it was in front of her window at about nine foot and she looked at it every morning, and he put it in the other corner, and it is in the other corner.

He came in there on a Sunday in the middle of that month, before the light had properly got on that yard, and rolled the far end of it a second time because the middle had gone soft, and packed the edges in with the back of a spade and went out through the gate at about four in the afternoon.

The man in his forties was in that yard on the Monday morning of the third week of November about a foot away from the top of it, on his hands and knees with a trowel, doing the base course of a path of his own that comes off that surface and goes out through the gate to the lane, and the two of them were in that yard for about an hour and a half and worked in the same yard without a word about the yard.

That is his own path. It is about five yard long and it is his and he is being paid for it and the woman in the fourth flat wanted the step outside her own back door done, which is the next one along and is a different job, and that one has not started.

The woman who lives in the first of those eight flats had the man in his forties in her front room on the Wednesday of the last week of November with a cup of tea, and she told him about the water at the back of her step, and she said she had not said anything to anybody because she did not know who to say it to.

He was the man she told, and he knew who had done it, and he had been in that yard for an hour and a half the previous Monday and had watched a man roll a surface and had held a trowel for him.

He did not say. There was a point at about the fourth minute of that conversation where he could have said a name, and he did not say it, and he is never going to, and what stops him is not loyalty to the man. A complaint is a thing that is put on a person, and a man in his thirties who has paid for a hundred and thirty square yard of his own work out of his own money is not on anything and cannot be put on anything, and a man who brings a name back to a woman with a wet step has made that man into something for the first time in his life, and he is not the man who does that to people.

So he said that the water was the water and that the fall was the fall and that the step could be laid higher, and she said that anybody could lay a step higher, and he said that anybody could, and drank his tea and changed the subject to the light in her kitchen.

---

There are eight people in that building and six of them have an account of that surface and no two of the six accounts are the same.

One says it is a path. One says it is for the bin men. One says it is for the bikes. One says it is a waste of good stone and that it should never have been laid in October. One says it stops the mud coming in off the lane, and that is the only one of the six that anybody can put a hand on, and the man in his forties cannot say whether it is true because he does not know what the man was thinking.

And the woman who lives in the first of those eight flats has a wet step, and the woman who lives in the fourth flat has had to look at somebody else's heather out of her window since September, and the man in his thirties has done about fifty hours on it and has told nobody what it was for, and not one person in that building has ever asked him.

**A MAN IN HIS THIRTIES PUT A BARROW DOWN IN THAT YARD ON THE FIRST SATURDAY IN AUGUST AT NINE IN THE MORNING AND PULLED A TROWEL OUT OF HIS OWN POCKET AND LEFT IT ON THE CONCRETE, AND ON THE SUNDAY IN THE MIDDLE OF NOVEMBER HE ROLLED IT AND PACKED THE EDGES AND WENT OUT THROUGH THE BACK GATE, AND HE WAS ASKED ONE QUESTION ABOUT IT BY ANYBODY IN THAT BUILDING IN THREE MONTHS AND THE QUESTION WAS WHETHER THE MIX WAS STRONG ENOUGH.**
