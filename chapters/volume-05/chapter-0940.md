# Chapter 0940 — Under The Worn Sill

Thursday the nineteenth of September 2024, at the sill of the back door at number ten with the flags dry since the morning. Under a worn sill.

**SHE HAS A WOODEN SILL LAID OVER THE FLAGS AT THE BACK DOOR OF NUMBER TEN, AND SHE MEANS TO LAY THE HAND FLAT AND PUSH IT UNDER THAT SILL WITH THE PALM DOWN AND THE FINGERTIPS LEADING, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE SILL STANDS OFF THE FLAGS AND WHAT IS LYING UNDER ITS NOSE.**

The back door was hung on the frame that was in the wall when the house went up, and the sill was put in at the same time as the frame and has been under that door every day since. The door has been taken off and hung twice and the sill has not been moved since the first week.

It is a sill of elm about six inches wide and two and a half thick, laid flat across the flags and bedded on them in a bed of lime and sand gone hard, with the door frame standing on the top of it and the flags going under the bottom of it, and the nose of it about a foot and a half from the wall on the inside and two feet out on the path. Under that sill is the space the door has been shutting over for ninety years, and nobody has ever been under it.

---

Between the underside of that sill and the flags there is an inch of nothing whatever, and it is a bridge rather than a joint. The hand goes down flat, palm to the stone, fingertips leading, thumb along the side of it. The under face of the sill is rough where it was sawn and blunt along both of its arrises, and it is about as much warmer than the flags under it as anything in a doorway ever is.

Under the nose of the sill there is a space as deep as the heel of a hand at the middle of it and about half that at either end, where the bed of lime has been drawn up under the wood by the drying. It is a space and not a hole, and nothing whatsoever goes into it. It is two knuckles deep at the middle and as long as the door is wide.

Under the nose, along the whole of it, lies a skin of stuff about the thickness of a leaf: sawdust and lime dust and a fibre or two of old rope, packed down into the low places of the sawn grain. It is dry, it is grey, and no person ever set it there.

An elm sill is sawn, not planed, because it is going to be on the flags and nobody is going to look at it, and a sawn surface is a set of little valleys running the length of the timber with the saw's travel in them. Anything that gets under a sill collects in those valleys and nothing sweeps them out, because the broom stops at the sill and the vacuum does not reach under it.

That skin is ninety years of the house's own dust. Every piece of lime that has ever been pointed into a joint of that back wall has come off it as a fine powder, and every winter's worth of that powder has been blown or swept under the door and has stopped when it came to the sill's nose. What is under there is not anything anybody carried in. It is the fall-out of the wall above it, gathered in the saw's travel in the underside of a piece of elm.

Under the head of that sill, where the frame is carried on it and the frame is nearly a hundred years older than the sill, the space closes to nothing. The frame was built first and the sill was scribed round it, and a scribed joint of that kind is tight by the man who cut it and gets tighter as the timber shrinks.

What closes the space at the two ends is the bed. The bed of lime and sand under the sill is about two inches thick at the middle of the door, where it is blunt along its top where the mason's trowel left it, and it runs out to nothing at either end, because that is how a mason lays a bed: fat in the middle and drawn off at the ends so that the stone above can be lifted off it again if it ever has to be.
Two inches of lime bed sets like a cake and never lifts, so the space at the middle of that sill is as it was laid and will be as it is in a hundred years.

At the two ends the flags themselves carry the sill. The flags under it are laid on the earth and have sunk a little, and the sill has gone down with them, which is the only reason the space exists at all: a sill laid on a dead-flat bed in a dead-flat wall would have no space under it anywhere at all, and this one has two inches' worth along most of its length.

What is under a sill is the only part of a doorway that nobody in the history of doorways has ever had a reason to be interested in, which is the whole reason it is worth putting a hand under it once.
Every other part of a door can be seen from the yard: the frame can be seen, the step can be seen, the door itself can be seen. The underside of a sill is a face of wood about six inches by the width of a door, lying flat on the ground in its own dust, and it goes on lying there whether the door works or not.

A sill is also the one piece of a doorway that is under a load. The frame stands on it, and the frame carries the door, and the door is hung on three hinges and swings four pounds of pine about four feet.
All of that comes down through two little pieces of timber at the two ends of the frame and into a piece of elm six inches wide, and the elm takes it without any sign of it at all, because wood is far harder along a cross-grain than anybody expects and far weaker down its own length than anybody expects.

There are three nails in that sill that have not been there since it went in. They came out of the wall with the flag stones on either side of them in a frost in the eighties, and the nails stayed in the stone, and the flags were put back without them. Three nail holes in a flag under a door sill is a complete record of that winter, and nowhere else on the back of that house is there a single thing that puts a date to anything.

The whole of that is what is under the back door at number ten, and it is as much as will ever be found under it. There is nothing in that space and nothing has been in that space except the dust of the wall above it, and the door shuts over the top of it and will go on doing so.

Her sister comes out of the side door into the yard with her hands behind her back and goes down the yard towards the gate. The sill gets neither her feet nor her eyes, and she does not stop.

A dog next door was the whole of it, first as itself and then as a question about whether it has given up coming through the hedge, and not one word of either end of it came near a piece of elm laid over a hundred flags.

The sill keeps its space and its skin of dust and its two inches of bed at the middle, and nothing that has happened under it in ninety years was put there by a person who meant to, and it will keep all three of those things whether or not anybody ever looks under it again, whether or not the frame stands where it stands, and whether or not that door is ever taken off its hinges a third time.
