# Chapter 0969 — Round The Pipe Bend

Undated in this draft.

**A BENT PIPE END ON THE RACK KEEPS A SMOOTH BORE OPEN TO THE AIR AT ONE END.**

Inside the open bay the rack runs along the back wall, framed from angle iron with timber boards laid across for pipes and sections.

A length of lead pipe lies on the rack with a bend at one end, the straight about four feet long and the bend turning through a right angle over about six inches. The pipe is of the old kind, heavy in the hand, with a bore about an inch across that runs clean through from the straight end round the bend to the mouth.

The mouth of the bend stands open to the air and shows a smooth inner face where water ran for years. The outer face of the bend is wrinkled on the inside of the curve where the metal compressed in bending, and stretched thin on the outside where it drew out. Neither wrinkle nor thin place has split.

The straight end of the pipe carries a wiped joint about a foot from the bend, where a collar was formed round the pipe in the making. The collar stands proud by about a quarter inch and carries a bead round its lower edge where the solder ran. Below the collar the pipe darkens where it lay against the rack board.

Lead pipe dulls the finger cold and heavy, slow as winter dusk. Forged iron at the upright feels cold swiftly, factory-bright and impatient to fingertips. The rest plank underneath sips scant warmth, exhaling a whisper of oil.

The rack boards are of rough softwood, blackened where pipes have lain and pale where they have been shifted. One board carries the print of three pipes in dust, and the dust there is deep enough to show the curve of each. An iron upright at the end of the rack carries bolts through each board, and the bolts have rusted into place.

The bay around the rack is open along its front, with a concrete dado along the back wall and corrugated sheet above it. Light comes in from the yard and falls across the pipes in bands, bright on the crowns and dark between. Dust hangs in that light when the air moves, and settles again when it stills. The bands of light move across the pipes through the morning as the sun clears the far roof, brightening each crown in turn and then leaving it to dull again.

A second pipe lies beside the bent one, straight throughout and capped at one end with a wooden plug. The plug has swollen with damp and cannot be drawn without splitting it, and it has been left where it is. The grain of the plug runs across its end in rings, and each ring stands proud where the softer wood between has shrunk. Dust has settled into those rings in pale lines that wipe away at a touch and gather again within days. A third length stands upright against the rack end, lashed with cord sunk into yielding metal where hauled taut. The upright length is shorter by a foot and carries no joint along its length, and its lower end stands on the concrete where it has left a dark ring. The ring is of damp and dust pressed together, and it marks where the pipe has stood through two winters without moving.

Twine on lead marks it quickly, because the metal gives under pressure and keeps the mark. The tie there has left a bright ring round the upright length, with the weave of the twine showing in it. The ring does not go deep and does not weaken the wall.

The floor of the bay is concrete with a fall toward the mouth, and water that blows in runs out again without standing. Grit has gathered against the dado where the broom cannot reach, pale and sharp, with here and there a flake of paint from the sheet above. The paint flakes are thin and curled, blue on one side and grey on the other.

Old lime pointing along the dado has gone hard and cracked in one joint, and the crack runs full of dust that spills onto the floor when touched. The dust there is soft and grey, and a fingertip drawn through it leaves a clean line. The concrete beyond is rough underfoot and rings faintly when struck. The dado above it was shuttered with boards when cast, and the grain of the boards shows in the face in long lines. One line runs deeper than the rest where a board warped, and dust has gathered along it in a pale thread that breaks when touched and settles again.


Bay light moves in bands across the pipes as the morning advances, each band brightening a crown then abandoning it. The bands derive from gaps between yard buildings, narrow slits that project sun sharply. Dust motes drift through those bands perpetually, bright going one way and dark the other. A cobweb spanning two pipes harvests that dust, greying through the month till rain beads it bright again.

The concrete dado behind the rack preserves shutter grain from its casting day, plank ridges running horizontally. One ridge carries a pencil note from the shutter carpenter, a level mark with an arrow, preserved in reverse on the concrete face. The mark means nothing now but survives because concrete forgets nothing. Grit lodged along that ridge spells the wind direction for the week.
---

The middle finger goes into the bore of the bent pipe from the mouth and works round the bend as far as it will go.

Straight entry first, then curving with the metal, wall deciphered by fingertips not eyes. It moves slowly, because the bore turns and the finger must follow without forcing.

First finding is the reach round the curve. The finger goes in to the second joint along the straight and then a further inch round the curve, where the bore tightens on the knuckle and will not let it pass. The straight beyond can be seen as a disc of daylight when sighted from the mouth, small and bright.

Second finding is what lines the bore at the limit. Down the greater reach the bore runs glassy and arid, a slim rib marking one flank where drawing shaped it. Near the limit of the reach there is a ring of scale, rough under the tip, where water stood and dried through years.

Scale ignores the nail point, surrendering only a powder when scrubbed hard. The ridge beside it is smooth and draws no skin. The lead around both draws heat steadily and feels dense against the finger.

The digit retreats borewards lead-striped down a single side, else naked. The bore continues as found, scale undisturbed save a hairline scored by the tip.

The pipe carcass rests unbudged on timber, tonnage pinning it and collar chocking it to its neighbour. A palm shove jogs it a whisker, dust print re-seating it promptly. The print itself is of two lines where the pipe touched the board and a pale band between them where air passed beneath. The band is free of dust while the lines are deep with it, which shows how the pipe bears only along two ridges of its own curve.

Bay-roof precipitation plunges past the aperture, never on timber; hence arid pipes and deep powder. The outer yard spreads in flags dipped alternately, and the central gutter sheds rainfall clear of the bay. A pigeon crosses the yard through the morning and is gone before midday. Dew never breaches the bay; the bore keeps year-long dryness.

---

The booking clerk stands drum-adjacent bay-mouthward, steel cylinder portside, pipe-rack ahead, hands unladen.

The knuckle clears the lead mouth and the wrist drops to the knee while the clerk turns from the drum toward the pipes. Conversation turns on whether the post still comes twice in the day, and whether the office parcel was left at the yard, with each reply given freely.

The clerk lingers drumside, venturing no closer to the metal. The drum beside her is rusted at its rim and dark with oil below, and the rack stays where it is with its pipes lying still.

The bore of that bend runs round its curve and keeps its scale where the water left it, where the daylight meets the dark inside the pipe.
