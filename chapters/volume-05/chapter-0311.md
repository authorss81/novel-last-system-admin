# Chapter 0311 — What He Said He Would Do

Wednesday the fifth of April 2017, from about ten past eight in the morning, the low end of Peverell Street in Tideglass.

**HE SAID OUT LOUD AT THE TOP OF A LANE THAT HE WOULD TAKE UP A STEP THAT HAS BEEN DOWN SINCE BEFORE CHRISTMAS, AND HE WROTE ONE LINE ABOUT IT IN A PAD HE HAS NOT WRITTEN IN BEFORE, AND HE DID NOT DO IT, AND A WOMAN WITH A CLIPBOARD TOLD A MAN IN A HALL ON A THURSDAY THAT HE HAD SAID IT, AND HE TOLD HER HE HAD NOT, AND NEITHER OF THEM WAS LYING.**

The step is outside a shop at the low end of Peverell Street that has been shut since about the summer before last, and the freeholder of that shop is an outfit that keeps itself to a town a long way off and has not come up that street since before the summer before last, and the outside corner of that step has sunk until it is lower than it was, and the lip you get is about the height of two fingers, and it is where people put their right foot coming off the footway, and the whole top of the step is sheeted over with a length of blue lino whose near end has lifted and stood up like the lid of a tin, and the step itself is about two foot by three.

The man in his thirties has been in that street more than anybody would think in the last two months. He said a list of nine things aloud on that pavement on the twenty-second of February and the step was the fourth of them, and he has walked past it since, and he has put his boot on it since, and he has not knelt down at it until the morning this happened.

---

It was not cold and it was not wet. It was about thirteen degrees and the wind had gone round to the north-east and there was a bit of hard on the lino at the crack.

He knelt down on it on the Tuesday morning with his bag on the pavement and turned the loose end of that lino back with the blade of a screwdriver, and there was black mastic under it that had gone hard, and the mortar under the top of the step at the outside corner had let go of the tarmac in about a foot of it, and it was not a hole, it was a lip.

"That's a lip," he said, to the man in a fleece going to the works, who was standing about eight foot off with a coffee in a paper cup. "That's not a subsidence. That's just that the mortar's come away at the edge, and the whole thing wants about three inches of screed on it and a week to set in."

"Are you going to do it?"

"I'm going to take it up," said the man in his thirties, "and put a bit of screed on it, and it'll be done by the middle of next week."

"Aye," said the man in a fleece, and drank his coffee.

At about ten past eight on the Wednesday morning, at the open end of the hard at the top of Marsh Lane, with the woman with a clipboard about ten foot off with her back to the road, he said it again to a man who was not the man in a fleece, and he said it in the same words, and he said it in a louder voice than the first time, and he did not know that she was ten foot off.

He had the pad of work notes out of the front pocket of his coat that morning for the first time since the summer, with the elastic still round it, and he took the elastic off with his teeth and wrote one line on the top sheet in pencil, and it was the top of a step at the low end of Peverell Street, and he put the elastic back on and put the pad away.

The man in a fleece, who was going to the works, said, "You put a bit of screed in that and in a month you'll not be able to tell you've done it, and there'll be a man in a white van here in ten years saying it's always been like that."

"That's the whole idea of it," said the man in his thirties.

"It's the whole idea of it," said the man in a fleece. "It's not the whole idea of it, but go on then."

He did not do it that week, and he did not do it the week after, and by the middle of April the lino at the crack had gone back down onto the tarmac on its own with a bit of rain and was lying flat enough that nobody tripped on it, and the step was still two inches down at the outside corner and would go on being two inches down.

He had a reason and the reason is not a good one and he knows it is not a good one. He can put a screed on a step and charge for it, and this step is outside a shop that is shut, and there is no freeholder of that step in this borough to send an invoice to, and the man in his thirties could not put one in anybody's book if there was, and a man who takes a step up outside a shop he does not trade with is a man that shop has had something done to it by a stranger, and the next thing that happens to that step is that a woman comes out of the parade and asks him about it and he has to have an answer, and the answer is going to be about a shop he does not trade with.

He worked out the second week that he would do it on a Sunday when nobody was on the parade. He did not do it on that Sunday either. He went and did a job in a kitchen in Corrance Lane that had come in on the Monday and it took two days and he did not think about the step again until the April bank holiday, when it rained.

---

The woman with a clipboard was at the open end of that hard on the Thursday because her easel went round the back of the bank in February and she has been on a folding stool with the board on her knee since, and her course finished with the end of April and this was the Thursday before the last week of it.

The man in his forties was in that hall on the Thursday evening and the woman who works in a shop was in early with her coat on, and the woman with a clipboard came in at about half past eight, which she has never done, and sat down.

"You've been counting buses at the top of Marsh Lane since January," said the man in his forties.

"People," said the woman with a clipboard. "In and out, at the door. And it's the end of April after this, so that's the end of it."

"Right."

"I counted four hundred and something on Tuesday in the hour and a half before the rain," said the woman with a clipboard. "And a man in a fleece and a man in his thirties were stood at the top of that lane talking about a step outside a shut shop, and the man in his thirties said he'd take it up, and he said it in a voice you could hear over a bus, and I was ten foot off with my back to the road."

The woman who works in a shop looked up.

"He did not."

"He did," said the woman with a clipboard. "I do figures all day. I am the last person in this borough who would make up a man saying he'd take a step up. He said it, and he said it twice, and he said it to a man in a fleece, and the man in a fleece said aye and drank his coffee."

The man in his thirties came into that hall at about ten to nine and the woman with a clipboard had not seen him come in.

"I never said I'd do that step," said the man in his thirties.

"You said you'd take it up."

"I said I'd have a look at it. There's a difference and you know there's a difference, because looking at a thing costs me a boot and about two minutes, and doing a thing costs me a whole Sunday that does not come back, and I am not going to stand in this room and let a woman tell me what I said to a man in a fleece at a bus shelter in the open air."

"You said it at the top of Marsh Lane and I was standing at the open end of it with a board on my knee," said the woman with a clipboard, "and I am not going to be told that I heard a thing I did not hear, because I have been on that hard every day since the middle of January and I could tell you what happened on any one of those days, and I do not need to be right. I only need to have heard it."

"I'm not saying you didn't hear anything," said the man in his thirties. "I'm saying what I said isn't what you said I said."

The man in a fleece was in that room by then and had been since about half past eight, and the man in his thirties looked over at him.

"Tell her what I said."

"You said you'd take it up," said the man in a fleece. "And that is what I heard, and that is what I told the woman with the board I had heard it, and I could not tell you what you were doing at that lane that morning because I go to the works and my hours are not yours."

The man in his thirties stood in the middle of that floor with his hands open.

"Nobody's asked me to do a step," he said. "I'm doing a step in a street in about six weeks, in my own time, and nobody's asked me and nobody's waiting on it, and if the two of you want to carry it round this room like a job, carry it. There's nothing in it. A step is a step."

He took the pad out of his coat at that and put it flat on the table face up for about a second, with one line penciled on the top sheet and nothing else written on that sheet, and then put it back in his pocket with the elastic on it.

Nobody said anything, and the woman with a clipboard put her board on the table face down, and the man in the parade, who was at the far end of that table, said that the tea was cold again, and it was.

**AND A PAD WITH AN ELASTIC ROUND IT IS IN THE FRONT POCKET OF A COAT WORN BY A MAN STANDING IN THAT ROOM, AND THE STEP IS STILL DOWN, AND ON THE MONDAY AFTER THAT A WOMAN WITH A CLIPBOARD GAVE HER COUNT IN AT THE BACK OF A BANK AND DID NOT CARRY IT ON.**
