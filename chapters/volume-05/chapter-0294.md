# Chapter 0294 — Still As It Was

Monday the thirtieth of January 2017, from about a quarter past nine in the morning, a building by the water in Tideglass.

**THE BOROUGH DID WHAT IT DID ON A MONDAY, AND A MAN COUNTED A NUMBER OUT LOUD IN A ROOM OFF A CORRIDOR, AND FOUR ANSWERS WENT ROUND A TABLE AT NIGHT, AND NONE OF THE FOUR COULD BE PUT BESIDE ANY OTHER, AND THE ONE THAT CAME IN OFF A PIECE OF HARD WAS A COPY OF ONE OF THE OTHER THREE, AND THE ONLY PERSON WHO KNEW THAT WAS A WOMAN IN A GREEN COAT WHO WAS NOT IN THAT ROOM.**

The shop that shuts early had gone back up on the fourth and had been open every day since, and it shut at one on the Saturday and the boards that had gone over three windows on the parade in the last week of December had come off the bank and off the shop that sells radios and were stacked flat against the inside of the shuttered one where they have stayed.

There was nothing on the hard at the top of that lane, and nothing had been on it since the third of January, and the line had been there on that Tuesday from about twenty past eight to about ten to nine, and the woman who works in a shop, who had told two people on the Friday before, still told nobody anything about where that had come from.

At the bottom of Peverell Street a man put grit at the corner by the bank at about twenty past eight with a shovel and went on up the parade, and the grit bin at the top of Peverell Street was still there and was still empty in the bottom, and nobody had been up that street with a shovel since the Saturday in December, and the front room of number forty-one had its light on with the curtains open at a quarter to nine on a Monday morning and the letterbox on that door still had a soft leaflet in it.

Just after ten there was a van standing at the top of that parade with a ladder in the back of it, and it did not go up Peverell Street either.

---

The man in his thirties went into the building by the water at about a quarter past nine to get a line out of the register for a job sheet of his own, and Nell Ardent was in and she copied it out for him in her own hand, and he asked her, with the job sheet on the top of the desk, whether there was a date against it, and she said that the book did not have one for that line, and he said go on then, and she turned round and got the book down.

She did not go down the column with her thumb. She had it open on the desk on its side, and she was looking at the foot of the written part of it, and the last thing written in that book is a yard light, and the bulb in it is in a column headed maintenance, and the bulb is out.

"You could put anything against a yard light," she said. "That's the trouble with that one. If I wrote a date on that you wouldn't know what it was a date of."

"Last time anybody looked at it, then," said the man in his thirties.

"It's the column above it that tells you," said Nell Ardent. "It's the last one in the book and everything above it has a name on it and this hasn't, because it's a lamp."

There is no date against that last entry, and the only thing on the page under it is the ruled line the book makes by itself, which comes out from under the writing and goes on down towards the bottom margin and gives up about two inches short of the edge of the board on a book that is about two inches deep.

The man in his thirties put his pencil on the corner of his own job sheet and ran it up against the date at the top of the form, and then said the date out loud, and then said another one out loud, and then said the number of days between them, which was seven hundred and ninety-six.

Nell Ardent put the corner of her own form over the figure on his sheet and said, "That is a long time for a lamp."

"That's since the lamp, then."

"That's since the lamp," said Nell Ardent, "and there's no name in the margin opposite it, and there never was, and if you were writing one in now it would be your name against a lamp."

"It's not that I'm after," said the man in his thirties. "It's that I was doing the job sheet and I wanted a figure for it and I thought, that one's been sat there a while."

He shut the book, and gave it back, and she put it back on the shelf it lives on among the others, and the register did not move and the margin opposite that last line is still empty, and the job sheet went out of that building with a figure at the top of it in his own handwriting and a line under it that was not a name.

---

At the top of that building, on the first floor, a woman of about fifty-five was in a room at the end of a landing with the light on, and there was a chair against a wall in the yard below with a blanket over the back of it, and the water that had come down that wall in the first week of January had dried into a mark about a foot long which had gone the colour of the ceiling.

The hall off the parade was full at about half past seven and the meter running and nobody in a hurry to be out of it, and there were nine of them in it, and not one of them was there on a matter that anybody in that room could have set down and shown to somebody else.

The man from the parade came early. The man in his forties came about half way through and stayed about an hour, as he does, and had a bit of paper in his pocket with the price of a length of lead on it that he had not given to anybody. The woman with the key had her coat over a chair. The man in his sixties looked out of the window at the parade. The woman of about fifty-five was in by about ten past eight. The man who works nights was in with his coat still on. The woman who works in a shop was at the end of the table by the fire with her back to the window.

There were four of them going round that table and there had been three for a month, and the fourth one came off a bit of hard at the top of Marsh Lane on the twelfth of January, said by a woman in a green coat at a table of bulbs to a man with a barrow of potatoes who had said the same shape of it to two other people before she got there.

The man from the parade was on his feet with his cup in both hands, the way he is at that end of a room, and said that somebody in there must know by now which of the four of them it was, and that he had been saying it since about the summer and he would keep saying it.

Nobody gave him anything. The man in his forties looked at the boards of the table. The woman who works in a shop turned her chair about the way she does and said that there were four of them on that table where there had been three, and that she could not put a number on the one that had come off a street because she had never heard it, and that anybody who could tell them which of the four was the same as anybody who could tell them which of them it was.

"Who says it's three," said the man from the parade.

"You do," said the woman who works in a shop. "You've said three since December. There are four because somebody said it on a bit of hard with nobody from this room on it, and it's the same sentence, and it's worse than ours because ours came out of a room."

"Nobody's improved on it," said the young woman of about twenty, at the end of the table, without turning round, and drank her tea, and the man from the parade asked her whether that was still the answer she had and she said it was, and he asked her whether she had put it to anybody in the last fortnight and she said she had not, and that was the whole of what passed between the two of them about any of it.

The man from the parade said he had heard it in October, and the man in his forties said he had heard it in December, and neither of them said it twice, and the young woman of about twenty said that was all right, and the two of them went back to what the table was doing.

At about ten to ten the man in his forties said to the woman of about fifty-five, without any preamble at all, that he hoped she was all right for the month, and she said that she was, and he said right and went back to the price of the lead.

The meter went off at about ten past ten and the padlock went on after it, and the chairs went up, and the man in his forties carried six of them and the man in his sixties had carried three and had gone at half past nine.

**AND THE MAN IN HIS THIRTIES CARRIED THAT JOB SHEET OUT OF THE BUILDING WITH A FIGURE AT THE TOP OF IT IN HIS OWN HANDWRITING AND A LINE UNDER IT THAT WAS NOT A NAME, AND HE PUT IT IN THE VAN WITH THE PENCIL, AND ON THE WEDNESDAY HE WROTE THE SAME FIGURE AGAIN ON AN OFFCUT OF HARDBOARD BECAUSE HE COULD NOT REMEMBER WHICH WAY ROUND UP HE HAD IT, AND HE PUT THE OFFCUT IN HIS POCKET AND TOOK IT OUT OF THE VAN AGAIN WHEN HE GOT TO THE END OF THE PARADE.**
