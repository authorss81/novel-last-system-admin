# Chapter 0850 — Turned Over In The Sack

Sunday the eighth of October 2023, inside that shed at number ten from the time the light gets past the door until the middle of the afternoon goes off the floor, and the soil turned over inside the sack is the whole of that Sunday.

**A SACK OF TOPSOIL STANDS IN THE CORNER OF THAT SHED AT NUMBER TEN AND HAS SETTLED INTO A CONE WITH A HOLLOW DOWN THE MIDDLE OF IT AND SHE MEANS TO TURN THE BOTTOM OF IT OVER INSIDE THE SACK WITHOUT EMPTYING IT, AND HER SISTER SETS A BARROW DOWN IN THE SHED DOORWAY AND TAKES IT AWAY AGAIN OUT OF THE YARD.**

That sack is hessian, about four feet to the top when it was filled, and it was filled off the front bed in May after the lilac stool came out of the other side.

It is not emptied and it is not going to be emptied today.

It has stood in the corner of that shed since May with nothing done to it at all, and topsoil off a bed does not stay as it was put in a sack. The fines go down to the bottom and the top goes hollow, and the mass in the middle takes the shape of the sack as the sack takes the shape of nothing.

A sack that has settled will not run. It comes out in a lump with a hole in the middle of the lump, and a lump of soil with a hole in the middle of it has to be chopped out and it will not go where it is wanted in that condition, and the condition does not improve by standing.

So she turns it. In place, on the floor, where it stands.

She gets both arms in to the elbow and that is the whole difficulty of it. A sack of dry soil is not so heavy as it looks until it is against your chest and your arms are in it and there is no way to get any purchase on the middle of it. She gets one arm in and works the other past the wrist and then takes the weight of the sack against her knee and pushes.

The bottom of the cone comes up.

She does it in three goes and not in one, because it will not do in one. The first third comes up off the floor and over onto itself and the sack drops a little under her arm and she goes at the second. The second third comes over on top of the first and the shape of the sack under her forearms goes from a point to a roll. Then the middle, which is the heaviest part and which comes last because there is nothing left to keep it up but the third and her two hands.

The hollow goes down last of all. It comes up out of the middle and slumps sideways into the roll and the soil has no shape at all in there for about as long as it takes to get her arms out.

Her shoulders are the price of it and they tell her about it for an hour afterwards. Turning soil in a sack is not heavy work by any honest measure. It is awkward work, and the awkwardness is all in the arms, and the arms were not designed for a bucket with no handle.

Her sister comes in through the shed doorway in the middle of the day with the barrow, sets it down in the doorway clear of the sack, and stands there for the length of the third go without saying anything at all.

Then she picks it up and goes back out of the yard with it, and the light on the floor closes up again behind the door.

She gets her arms back in and finds the turn she has already done.

Down the middle of the sack there is a hollow the size of a fist and it goes straight down, and she can put her fist into it and there is nothing within three inches of it on any side. That hollow is the chimney the soil made when it slumped away from the centre and down onto the walls of the hessian, and it is the whole reason the sack is in this condition and not merely heavy.

The bottom of the hessian is thin.

She can feel it with her fingers, turned up round the inside of her forearm, and it is the texture of cloth that has been damp and has dried and been damp again, and the fold that the bottom has been folded in since the day it was filled has gone soft and will not stand up square any more.

She does not empty the sack to mend the bottom. Mending the bottom wants it open and out and the soil somewhere for a day, and the soil in it is the best of that bed and is going back on the bed in the spring when the edging goes down.

What she does is push the soil in against the bottom all round, hard, with the heel of her hand, so the weight of it sits on the cloth and not in a hollow with a slack bag under it, and the soil is packed up into the corners of the fold where the corners have knitted themselves into a ridge that will stay put.

Then she works round the outside of the sack with both hands and picks the hessian off the soil, so it hangs loose and does not suck to the shape underneath.

The turn is what she came for. Next time that sack is opened the soil will come out of it soft, in shovels, the way it came off the bed in May, instead of in a lump with a hole in it.

She thinks about the soil while she works, how it is the only part of that bed worth having and it has been standing in a corner since May going exactly nowhere and getting exactly worse at a rate nobody could see from the doorway. Turning it over takes half a morning and two arms and nothing bought, and it makes no difference to the bed at all and all the difference to the sack.

She pulls the mouth of the sack over and folds the hessian down on itself where it has been folded since the spring, and the fold takes and stands, and that is the crease that has been in it since May showing where the sack was folded on the day it was filled.

She puts her hand in one last time, down to the wrist, and feels for the hollow.

It is not there. The soil is turned and loose and comes up to her forearm all round, and where her hand goes in it goes in easily and comes out easily, and there is nothing left in that sack with a shape of its own.

She stands in the middle of the shed floor in the last of the light off the door and looks at what is there. A sack standing in the corner with the soil turned over loose inside it, and the hessian creased where it has been folded since the spring.
