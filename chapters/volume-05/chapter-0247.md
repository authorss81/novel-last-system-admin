# Chapter 0247 — The Hose

Tuesday the twenty-first of June 2016, from about half past eight in the evening, the low end of Peverell Street in Tideglass, and a wall.

**A MAN HAD A BURST HOSE, A WASHER AND NO WATER TO PUT IT IN, SO THE ONE QUESTION HE COULD ACTUALLY ASK WAS THE WRONG ONE, AND HE SAID SO, AND IT WAS ANSWERED BY A MAN WHO CAME OUT OF A DOOR HE KEEPS OPEN A HAND'S WIDTH.**

Jonas Mercer came down Peverell Street at about half past eight because there was nothing else to do with his evening, and he stood at the top of it the way he has stood at the top of it before, and he did the sum against the wall with a biro on the back of his hand because that is what he does.

It came to five hundred and seventy-three, and he wrote it on a piece of paper and put the piece of paper in his coat, and it is a line under the nineteenth entry of a register in a building by the water and it is still blank, and nobody has ever asked him for that number, and he is not going to put it in front of anybody who is going to write it on anything.

At number thirty-eight a man on a stepladder had a length of hose of his own that had burst at the coupling and had been dripping down the side of a fence for about an hour, and he had come down the ladder and was standing in the road holding the two pieces.

"I've got a washer but I've not got a coupling and there's no water in the house to prove it in."

"There is in the yard," said the man in the doorway of number forty-one.

He was inside doing something with a radio and he came out into the doorway, which is open a hand's width anyway, so it was not so much coming out as shifting sideways, and the door stayed the width it always is.

"There's a standpipe in my yard and the stopcock's open," he said. "Get the one off the wall."

The one off the wall is a length of hose that has been on that wall since the wet week in the November before last, and it has not been touched since the water went down, and there is a grey line of dirt on the wall behind it where it has been lying in one place.

He took it down and pulled the loop out of it and gave it to the man in the road, and the man in the road looked at it and said, "This'll do," and went off up the street with his own broken one over his arm.

The woman at number thirty-eight was in her back yard at the same time with two washing lines full and no water in the house, and she had been in that yard since about seven and had got the length of it done out of the cold water off a butt she keeps behind the coal shed, and when the man on the ladder came up her side of the road with the borrowed hose she put a washing line down and held the other end of it while he worked, and neither of them asked the other anything.

---

About ten minutes later a man from a shop on the parade came up the low end on his way home and stopped at number forty-one, and he stood on the step and told him that the water was off at the top of the street and at number thirty-eight and had been since the middle of the week, and that there was a notice about it on the door of the parade shops.

"Who's that then."

"The man off the shop with the shutter that goes up and down."

"Aye," said the man in the doorway. "I'll say."

The man in the doorway has been at the bottom of that street for the thirty-odd years it has got houses on it, and in all that time a man coming off the parade has been the only way anything has ever come down it, and neither of them was ever asked to do it, and there is nobody in that street either of them could point at.

Then the man on the ladder called down from number thirty-eight to ask whether the water was on, and the man in the doorway said it was, and the man on the ladder said right, and went back up his ladder, and the two of them were in the road together for a while with the borrowed hose and a roll of tape, and the man from the shop went off up the street and did not stop again.

And the man in the doorway said, to the man on the ladder and not to Jonas Mercer, who was standing in the dark at the top of a wall doing a sum with a biro:

"If your lot are out on a Tuesday, I'll come down and bring that hose out, and I don't want it on that board."

The man on the ladder said, "Right," and went back up his ladder, and that was the end of it, and it was the only sentence anybody said down there all evening that was about anything other than water.

---

Nobody asked him a question.

Jonas Mercer had been at the top of that street for about half an hour and had asked nothing, and he had come down it on the two previous evenings in the week and asked nothing, and nobody up there had asked him anything for seven weeks either, and the two of those facts sat in the road together and neither of them did anything about the other.

The door went to its usual width and the radio went on inside it, faintly, the way it does.

**THE HOSE WENT UP THE LADDER AND THE WATER CAME, AND THE WOMAN AT NUMBER THIRTY-EIGHT PUT A SECOND WASHING LINE OUT, WHICH SHE HAD BEEN DOING ONTO A BUTT BEHIND THE COAL SHED SINCE SEVEN O'CLOCK, AND AT THE TOP OF THAT STREET A MAN WALKED HOME WITH A PIECE OF PAPER IN HIS COAT THAT NOBODY IN THAT BOROUGH WAS EVER GOING TO ASK HIM FOR.**
