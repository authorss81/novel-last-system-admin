# Chapter 0318 — What the Two of Them Said

Sunday the thirtieth of April 2017, from about half past ten in the morning, a bus stop at the end of Marsh Lane in Tideglass, and a parade of shops.

**A MAN IN A FLEECE TOLD A STRANGER AT A BUS STOP THAT THE SHOP THAT SHUTS EARLY WAS GOING TO BE A CAFE AND THE RADIO SHOP NEXT DOOR WAS GOING TO GO WITH IT, AND A MAN WHO PUT SHUTTERS UP FOR A LIVING TOLD A STRANGER OUTSIDE THAT SAME SHUTTER THAT THE RADIO SHOP WAS GOING TO BE A CAFE AND THE WOMAN WHO RAN THE OTHER ONE HAD BOUGHT IT, AND THE TWO OF THOSE WERE SAID WITHIN A QUARTER OF AN HOUR OF EACH OTHER ABOUT A QUARTER OF A MILE APART AND NOBODY PUT THEM TOGETHER.**

The stop at the end of Marsh Lane is a pole and a plate and about a foot and a half of kerb, and there is no shelter on it, because a shelter on that end would stand in the way of the turning for anything coming in off the low end of Peverell Street.

The man in a fleece was on the kerb at about half past ten with a paper cup in his hand and a man and a dog standing about two foot off him on the same side, and the dog is a grey thing about as big as a bicycle that has decided to sit down in the road.

The man in the fleece had not been on that kerb for two minutes before he said it.

"You know that parade, the one you come down off here," he said. "Where the shop shuts about two."

"Aye."

"That shop's going to be a cafe. There's a man come up from the city looking at it for somebody. And what he's after is both of them, the cafe and the radio shop next door, and the man in the radio shop's got about till August and then he's out of that parade."

The man with the dog said the man in the radio shop had been in there that morning, and the man in the fleece said he had, and had a set on his counter with nothing on it, and that a man going every day is a man who has been told something.

"That's the two of them, is it," said the man with the dog. "Because you can have the one without the other. You can have the cafe on its own."

"You can," said the man in a fleece. "I don't know. It's not mine and I'm not having it, I'm just telling you what I was told at the works on Friday by a lad who's off to a woman who's doing it up on a loan, and that's three goes and three comes and I'm the fourth one and that's all I am."

The dog got up and went about nine inches and lay down again in a different orientation.

They stood there about six minutes and then the man in the fleece looked at the display on the shelter-less pole, which is a plate on a post with a timetable in a plastic case on the other side of the road, and got on the number that came, and the man with the dog got on it with him, and neither of them was the other man anywhere in this borough.

---

The man who puts shutters up does the parade on a Monday and a Thursday and has done for about nine years, and he is not in this borough five days a week, and the other two days he is at a yard off the Cleeve road with his own gear.

He was up a short ladder at the shop that shuts early at about ten to twelve that morning, hooking the roller up to its top bar, and the bottom of that roller had been down for four days.

He came down the ladder and put it in the passage and there was a young man at the end of the parade with a rucksack on looking at a card in his phone, so the man who puts shutters up waited, and then the young man put the phone away and looked at the shutter and then at him.

"Sorry."

"You're all right."

"I've not seen that open for a while."

"It opens," said the man who puts shutters up. "I shut it at two o'clock every Monday and Thursday. That is the whole of my relationship with it."

The young man said the shop next door, the radio one, was going to be a cafe, and he had heard it in a place he did not name, and he was sorry, and the man who puts shutters up put his hand flat on the roller.

"Had a bit of that myself on Friday," he said. "Different version of it, though."

"What's the difference."

"They've had the other one to me," said the man who puts shutters up. "That it's the woman who's in there, that's bought the radio shop and the radio man's got to be out by the end of June, and that when she takes the two of them together she's doing the parade as one and the cafe is the far end of it. That's what came to me, and it came to me from a man who fitted these shutters, and he had it off his brother-in-law, and his brother-in-law did the joinery on a shop in the city in February, so it's second hand as well."

"So which of them is it," said the young man.

"It's not mine to know and I'm not being the man to know it," said the man who puts shutters up, and picked the ladder up off the pavement. "I'll tell you what I do know. The two shutters on that parade come off the same bar and they were put up in the same week in about 2011, so whoever's buying anything is buying a parcel, and a man who sells radios will not go and buy a cafe, and a woman in a shop that shuts at two is not going to be running a radio shop. So one of the two men you spoke to has it backwards, and it isn't the one whose version is more interesting."

The young man said the one at the bus stop was more interesting, and the man who puts shutters up said that was the one he had expected, and went up the parade with the ladder on his shoulder and put a hand up to the third shutter, which is the one the radio man keeps half down, and knocked on it twice with the flat of his hand, and the radio man turned a set off and looked out and lifted the shutter a foot, and the man who puts shutters up said the bar had dropped a half inch at that end and wanted looking at, and the radio man said it had been like that since before Christmas, and the man who puts shutters up said he would shim it on Thursday, and went on up the parade.

He did not know that a man in a fleece had said the opposite of what he had said about a quarter of a mile away with his back to him, at about half past ten, and the man in the fleece did not know that there was a man in this town who had put the other half of it together, because the man who puts shutters up only knows the second half and has been given it as fact.

Nobody went into that shop. Nobody knocked on a door. Nobody rang anybody, and nobody was told anything, and no paper of any kind came into that parade in that week or the week after.

The shop went up on the Monday at about ten to eight and shut at two, and the radio shop beside it came up with its own shutter at about the height of a man's chest and a man behind it and no sound on the set, and that is the whole of what either of those two men had to show for it.

**AND A LADDER WENT UP A PARADE AND A SHUTTER CAME UP BEHIND IT AND A NUMBER CAME DOWN A STREET, AND A CAFE WAS DESCRIBED IN TWO PLACES IN ONE MORNING WITH THE SUBJECTS THE WRONG WAY ROUND, AND THE WOMAN WHO RUNS THAT SHOP HAS NOT BEEN TOLD ANY OF IT BY EITHER OF THEM.**
