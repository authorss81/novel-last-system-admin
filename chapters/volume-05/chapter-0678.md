# Chapter 0678 — A Clean Rectangle In The Coal

Saturday the thirtieth of October 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from first light until dark with nobody in it, and the whole of the day is eight feet of coal shed she has to go into because she wants coal and there is a rectangle in the coal where something sat.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT GOES INTO HER OWN COAL SHED FOR A SACK OF COAL AND FINDS A CLEAN RECTANGLE IN THE COAL WHERE THE PARCEL HAD BEEN, AND FINDS THE PADLOCK STILL HANGING ON THE HASP, AND DOES NOT GO DOWN THE ROAD, AND DOES NOT ASK.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is on her own all day except for three children at the door for about four minutes and a message on the machine in the passage.

---

She goes round the side at about half eight with her coat on over the apron, and both drop bolts are in the apron pocket and she leaves them there.

The grass on the walked-flat line is wet right through and the air has got a bite in it that was not there on Monday, and the gate is standing back against the wall of the coal shed out of the way, and the path is clear, and the road has nobody on it.

The shed door comes open about four inches at the top corner and then the rest of the way, the way it did on Monday, and it has not been shut since Monday.

She goes in and puts her hand in at the back for the shovel, and the shovel is where it was.

Then she stands there with her hand on the shovel handle.

---

The coal is against the back wall of the shed and there is most of a sack of it, and in the middle of it there is a rectangle about the length of her two hands side by side and narrower than that, where the coal is a different colour.

It is not lighter. It is darker, the way a thing looks when it has been sitting on it, because the coal is dust and the dust has been disturbed round the edges of whatever was on top of it, and inside the rectangle the dust is flat and has the print of a corner of a box in it.

It is not deep. It is the print of a box that sat there from the Monday to the Saturday.

She puts the shovel down.

She looks at it for long enough that she has gone round the whole of it twice, and there is nothing else in the shed to look at, and the hasp is on the front of the door with the padlock hanging open in it where it hung when she left it on Monday, and the key to it has been on a hook behind her own back door since before Christmas and she did not go and get it then and she is not going to now.

---

Here is what she wants.

She wants to walk up her own path and out through her own gate and about forty yards up the road to where the road turns, and then back again, looking at the grass and the kerb and the front of the houses, on the offchance.

She is aware of wanting that with a clarity that surprises her, and what stops her is not that it would look strange, because a woman of seventy-eight walking forty yards up a road and back would not look strange to anybody. What stops her is that she would have to do it twice — once now, in daylight, when she would see nothing, and once at night, when she might see something and could not then go and ask about it.

So she does not go up the road. She goes in the shed, and she stands with her hand on the frame of the door, and she does not go up the road.

---

The parcel went off a van for number twelve on the twelfth of October and came up her path at night in the hands of a lad who lives at number fourteen, and it has been on this coal since Monday, and it is not on the coal.

That is the whole of what has happened, and all of it happened between the Monday and the Saturday, and the coal has the print of it in it.

She stands in the shed and does the arithmetic of the week, which she does not want to do and does anyway.

Monday she put it there at about three in the afternoon. The gate has been standing open since the first of September and the shed door has been unlatched since the summer. There was rain on Monday night and again on Tuesday and a shower on Thursday. The road has nobody on it at this hour, and at eleven at night the road has got a dog on it and nothing else.

She does not get as far as an explanation. She gets as far as the fact, which is that a thing that was in her own shed is not in her own shed, and there are four possible reasons for that and she is the only person alive who knows all four of them and she cannot tell anybody any of them because the asking is the part she has not got.

---

She gets half a sack of coal out of the shed in two goes and drags it round to the back door on a bit of newspaper she pulls off the parcel that is not there, which means the coal is going to be carried across her own clean floor and she has decided that as a thing she will deal with afterwards.

She gets it in and puts the bucket on it and fills the bucket from the bucket and the hotplate takes about four minutes.

There is a new element in the hotplate. The old one dropped out in the middle of cooking in the summer, in the pan, and did not get hotter and went off, and a man she has never seen the face of put a new one in for a price she was given and did not argue about.

The plate gets warm. That is all it does. It does not get hot enough to boil a pan and she does not expect it to.

---

There is a knock at the front door at about one and it is two girls and a boy of about ten with a cardboard box on a strap between them, and it is the fourth Saturday in a row it has been somebody.

"Can I do the lamps?"

She stands in her own passage with the brush behind her and does not open the door all the way.

"What lamps."

"For the old folks." The girl has the list on a clipboard and a pen on a string. "It's two pounds twenty."

"I don't need a lamp."

"It's for the hospice."

"Right."

They stand on the step. This is the part she cannot do, because saying no to a child about a lamp is a conversation, and saying yes to a child about a lamp is also a conversation, and the whole of what she is doing on this step is trying to find the third thing.

Then she goes and gets her purse off the shelf in the passage, which takes about a minute and a half, and puts two pounds in and does not have the twenty, and the girl says that's all right, and she says it's not all right and goes and gets the twenty, and the boy takes it off her and does not count it in front of her.

They go up the path. The boy says *thank you* at the gate and the girl writes something on the clipboard.

Then she shuts the front door, which is shut when she shuts it and does not latch, and stands in her own passage again.

That is the only time anybody has been at that door this month and she has given a child twenty-two hundred people nothing, because the boy did not count it in front of her.

---

She washes the newspaper off the kitchen floor with a hot cloth and it comes away grey and there is a smell of coal on it for a while after and then there is not.

She puts the cloth on the rail of the oven and it stands out from the oven the whole length of the front of it and does not go soft, the way the other one did not.

She looks at the parcel mark on the table, which is now a pale ring with a stain in the middle of it, and she puts the cloth over it and leaves the cloth over it, which is a thing she has done about that mark twice and which is a thing she has now done about two things.

The gas envelope is face up square with the edge of the table, pencil side up, six things on the back of it and nothing crossed off, and there is no pencil in that house and there has not been since before Christmas, and the second list on the front of it has its top corner gone, taken by tape, in a hand that is not her own, and neither woman can write on that paper again.

Four of six is done. The gate. The window in the back room. The hotplate. The passage light, four minutes on a Wednesday in June.

The front room is not done and it is not a painting job and there is a pale patch on the ceiling of it just inside the door, and she has not sat in that room since the sixteenth of July.

The bath tap is not done, and has not been done since before Christmas, and there is a bowl under it with a folded cloth in it, and she empties it morning and night.

---

She goes up the stairs in the afternoon with the cloth and wipes the bathroom ceiling round the mark, and the mark has a hard edge and there is grit on the cloth at the end of it, and she puts the cloth in the bowl and the bowl will be emptied in the morning and the grit will go down the drain with it.

She has a bowl, a cloth, a hotplate, a coal shed with a padlock she never puts on, and a gate that stands open, and a front door that does not latch, and a passage light that is on all day because she has never found the switch that turns it off without going under the stairs, and she has been up under the stairs twice this year and there is nothing up there.

---

She comes down at about four and looks out of the kitchen window at the side of the coal shed through the kitchen glass, and from where she stands she cannot see the inside of it at all, and she stands and looks at the side of a building she owns.

The gate is in front of it, back against the wall, open.

She has not shut the gate since the first of September and both drop bolts are in the pocket of the apron she is wearing and have been since August, and the door that does not latch is at the front and has been like that since before Christmas, and the shed door does not latch either and she has not locked it since before Christmas, and the coal shed is the only building on that road with a lock on it and she has never once used it.

---

The machine goes in the passage late in the afternoon and it is one short ring and then nothing, and it is not the sort of ring that means somebody has rung the bell.

It is the sort of ring that means somebody has rung the bell and let it go.

She stands in the kitchen and lets it ring twice more and then goes out and answers it, because a thing that has rung in this house since before Christmas is either somebody at the door or a fault in a line, and there is no fault in the line.

It is a message. It is from a woman with a bright voice and it goes like this.

"Hello, this is a message for the occupier of number twelve, the water side, from a building survey. I'm sorry to leave another one. I did say I'd try you again and I have. There's a mark on a first floor ceiling at that property that I logged in the summer and I'm not able to get the report closed without going back in, because the report has to say whether it's getting worse or not and that can't be written down in August. I'm in your area on the twenty-sixth of November. It's still a free call and it's still about twenty minutes and there's no charge for the visit. If you don't want me to come, that's fine, but if you do want me to come I'll need to hear from you, because I can't put that in a report either. It's for the freeholder, not for you, and we need to be sure you're happy for us to look."

It stops. The machine says the message is from a woman whose name she has never written down, on a date in the week, and the line goes dead.

She stands at the sink with the cloth in her hand and looks at the machine on the side table with the little green light still on.

She has now had two messages from this woman. The first was in August and she answered it with a recording in which she said she had not asked anybody to look at it and was not going to, and she listened to that recording twice and then kept her finger on the button until it said the message had been deleted, and it said it, and she put the machine back the way it was with the side toward the wall.

The second is this one, and it is different from the first, and the difference is that this one has a date on it.

There is a printed card in the pocket of the apron hanging on the back of the front door, and it has a free number on it and a bit of a survey company's address, and a man in a white van gave it to her on the fifteenth of October and said it was a local call and no charge and about twenty minutes, and she has not rung it, and she did not decide about it, she decided it in about a second and a half on a Friday afternoon with a man standing on her path.

The card is in the apron. The number is in the machine. Both of them are the same number.

She plays it twice.

The second time through she hears it, because she has been listening to it since the summer and knows where the woman's voice does what it does, and what she hears is that the woman says *a first floor ceiling* and not *the first floor ceiling*, and that she says it the way a person says a thing they are reading off a list.

And then she hears the woman say *the twenty-sixth of November*, and she knows today is the thirtieth of October, and she works out the number of days between the two and does not write it down.

Then she puts the receiver down on the side table and she stands there.

What she is thinking is not *do I want her to come*. What she is thinking is that a woman with a bright voice is going to be four feet from the chair in her front room on the twenty-sixth of November, and there is a pale patch on the ceiling of that room, and she told a man with a clipboard on the fifteenth of October that there was no water, and he wrote it down, and she watched him write it down.

---

She empties the bowl under the bath tap at six and puts the cloth back in it and the tap goes on dripping into it, and she does not look at the ceiling this time, which is the first time since June that she has emptied that bowl without looking at the mark.

She eats her tea at the table under the window on the green chair with the cushion on it, because the cold has gone off slightly by then and the cushion is a thing that is there.

The plate on the table is quite clean and she leaves it there, and the envelope is face up square with the edge, pencil side up, and the parcel mark on the wood is covered by a cloth, and the coal is in the bucket by the range, and the machine in the passage has a woman in it who is coming on the twenty-sixth of November to look at a ceiling in a room she has not sat in since the sixteenth of July.

Four of six is done and nothing has been crossed off.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS LOST A THING SHE CANNOT ASK ABOUT OFF THE TOP OF HER OWN COAL, HAS WORKED OUT FOUR REASONS AND NOT ONE OF THEM OUT LOUD, HAS LISTENED TWICE TO A WOMAN SAYING A DATE OUT LOUD IN A MACHINE IN HER OWN PASSAGE, HAS STILL NOT RUNG THE NUMBER IN THE APRON, AND THE PADLOCK IS STILL HANGING OPEN ON THE HASP.**

There is no pencil in that house and there has not been since before Christmas.
