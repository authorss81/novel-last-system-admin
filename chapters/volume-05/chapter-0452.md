# Chapter 0452 — The Door, Hung and Not Finished

Saturday the tenth of November 2018, most of one morning, and the best part of an hour of a second day. An upper floor, in Tideglass.

**HE PUT THE TOOLS IN THE BAG IN THE MIDDLE OF THE JOB AND WENT OUT, AND SHE CAME IN AT ONE, FOUND THE DOOR HUNG AND NO LOCK IN IT, AND SAID NOTHING TO ANYBODY ABOUT EITHER.**

The door is a front door of a flat on the top floor of a building about two streets back from that parade, and it had been off its hinges since the summer, propped against the wall of that landing, and the frame had gone soft along the top on the outside where the weather had been at it.

The man in his forties was on that stair at about eight on the Saturday morning and had the old one down and the new one up on the hinge side by half past nine.

What he did then was the whole of the job as anybody in that trade would have described it. He took the new door off the top hinge. He packed the hinge end of the frame with three slivers of thin offcut, folded and driven in and trimmed flush with a chisel, and he did it well, and it took the bow out of the head of the frame and the door came up square in it. He hung it on three hinges, all three of them working, screwed down, and closed it, and opened it, and closed it.

The whole of the left side of that job was finished by ten past ten.

---

The lock was the other half of it and the lock was the trouble.

It is a mortice lock, and a good one, and his customer had chosen it, and it has to be let into the edge of a door, and the edge of that door is a piece of softwood that has swelled and dried and swelled again over about eight years and has gone uneven along its length.

A door edge that has done that will not take a lock square. You can mark it and you can chisel it and you will get most of the way, and then the front of the case stands about a sixteenth proud of the wood and the door will not close without lifting, and it will shut on the top of the case and not on the bottom.

The proper work is to take the edge off that door on a lathe, or take the frame off the brick and pack the head of it, and the first of those is a man with a machine in a van and two days' notice and the second of those is a morning with a chisel and dust and half a day of noise in a landing.

He is not frightened of either of them. He has done both.

What he did at about half past ten was put a driver down on that landing, and then put the other one in the bag, and then zip the bag, and then stand up and look at the door for about a minute, and then go downstairs and out.

He has not been able to give anybody a reason. He has been over it. He put it down because his hands were not working, and that is a thing other men have said and he does not believe it of himself. He put it down because a lock that stands a sixteenth proud on a swollen edge is a lock that will be a problem in a year and a bigger one after that, and because every door in that street that has a lock in it that is doing what that lock would do was fitted by somebody who did not stop, and because of a thing he will not say even to himself, which is that a door with no lock in it and a bare keep on the frame can be brought back in twenty minutes on any dry day for the next eight months, and the job is the same job and the difference is a chisel.

He was not going to carry a chisel up two flights and start a noise in that landing on a Saturday morning in a building where somebody lives.

That is the whole of it and there is nothing under it.


She came in at about one.

The woman who has that flat came in at about one with her shopping and there was a door on the frame with a bare keep screwed to the frame where a lock should be, and the old door was still against the wall of that landing where it had been standing since the summer, and the new one was hung, and closed, and opened, and closed.

She looked at it for about a minute.

Then she went in, and put her bag down, and came out and knocked on the door of the flat across that landing and asked the woman next door whether the man had been and gone, and was told he had been there since eight and had gone before ten.

She said, "Right."

She did not go down to the man's van, and she did not telephone him, and she did not write anything down, and she has not mentioned it to anybody, and she has not mentioned it to him.

---

He was back in that building on the Thursday of the fifteenth at about four in the afternoon with the intention of getting the lock into that edge, and he had a chisel in the bag and he had known on the Monday that he had it in the bag on the Monday.

She opened the door before he had knocked at.

She said, "It's all right."

He said, "Right."

She said, "I wasn't going to bother you about it."

He said, "It's not a bother."

And that was the whole conversation, and it was about eight words each, and he put the bag down in that landing and looked at the edge of that door for a minute and a half with his hands in his pockets and then put the bag back and went out, and he has not been back since, and she has not asked him again.

The bill was whatever they had agreed the door was going to cost, and that is what he took, and it was taken on the Friday, and he did not say a word about the difference and neither did she.

**HE WENT OUT OF A FLAT AT FOUR IN THE AFTERNOON ON THE FIFTEENTH OF NOVEMBER WITH HIS BAG STILL MOSTLY FULL, AND SHE STOOD IN THE DOORWAY AND LET HIM GO, AND THE KEEP IS STILL SCREWED TO THAT FRAME WITH NOTHING IN IT.**
