# Chapter 0777 — The Strut And Four Turns Of Wire

Tuesday the sixth of December 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house and yard at number ten from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a leaning run of boundary boarding propped with a strut and tied on with wire, and it cannot be opened at all.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES HAS A RUN OF BOUNDARY BOARDING LEANING OUT OF PLUMB AND MEANS TO DRIVE A WEDGE UNDER THE POST AND PACK THE HOLE, HER SISTER TAKES THE WEIGHT OFF IT WITH A RAKING STRUT AND FOUR TURNS OF WIRE, AND THE WHOLE RUN CANNOT NOW BE TAKEN APART TO GET AT ANYTHING BEHIND IT.**

She is a woman in her early thirties at number ten on Tuesday. Her sister is at the far end of the run when she comes out into the yard.

---

The back door shuts true on its latch. Nothing has come out of the drawer under the worktop in a long while, and what is in there is a hammer, a screwdriver and a folded sheet with three lines written on it. The line across the yard is up and bare. The piece of ply leans against the shed front, fastened to nothing, and the felt above it is still lifting at its top corner. The sack of gravel is at the back of the shed with a shovelful gone out of it in the summer. The three boards are still down the path and the hole the water made at the corner of the shed is deeper than it was.

The run is along the bottom of the yard where it divides her ground from the ground next door. It is close boarding on posts, six feet of it, put up by somebody before she came, three horizontal boards on a frame between four posts, and the posts are square sawn softwood going into the ground as deep as the length of her forearm with nothing round them but earth.

The far post is leaning. It leans in toward her own yard and it has got further since the summer. It stands a hand's width out of true at the top, which is the width of her palm laid flat, and the frame has gone over with it, and the top board has come a coin's thickness out of its housing where the ground behind it has gone.

Her plan is the post. She means to dig round the base of it, get a bar under the bottom and lever it upright, drive a length of batten in under it as a wedge so that it cannot go over again, and pack that hole with concrete, because a post packed in concrete stands up and then she can paint the frame and forget it.

Her sister is at the far end of the run with her hands in her pockets when she comes out.

---

"You are not going to pack that post."

"I am going to pack that post."

"You are going to dig round the bottom of it, get a bar under it, heave it back upright, put a batten under it, and pack a hole a foot wide in ground that takes water off those boards every time it rains." Her sister does not move off the end. "It will be the best-packed post on this boundary until the first wet week. Then it will be on the old angle again and you will have packed it twice."

"Then what would you do with it."

"Nothing to it." She comes along the run and puts her hand flat on the frame and runs it along to the leaning post. "Leave it leaning. Take the weight off it."

---

"She means to put a prop on it," the sister says, "and she is right, and I want that said now, because I am not going to be told afterwards that I did this."

They both look at the run.

"The prop goes on your own side."

"It goes on your own side, and that is the whole of it." The sister walks out into the middle of the yard and stands where the water comes off the three boards. "The ground where that post is going in is the wettest ground you have got. If you put a prop on the far side you will be putting it in the next door's ground and he will have something to say about it, and rightly. So it goes on this side."

"Then it stands in the same wet."

"It stands in the wet for about eight inches. That is all. A post has to go down far enough not to move in the ground, and a prop has only to go down far enough not to fall over, and those are two entirely different things and one of them is about three times the other."

---

She digs the leaning post out, meaning not to, and she has to, because the prop goes against the top of it and there is no room on the back of it where the frame is. The hole is a foot across and about as deep as her forearm and full of black water at the bottom. She scoops it out, knocks a lump of brick in the bottom, and puts the post back in on top of it, and it comes up a coin's thickness truer than it was.

"Now leave it."

"Now leave it and let it lean."

"Now let it lean and take the lean somewhere else." The sister is marking out a length on the ground with the back of her boot. "That post has got one job and the job is to go in the ground and not come out. It has never propped a fence up in its life and it is not going to start. What is going to keep this run up is the prop, and the post is going to stand there and do nothing, and the two of them together will last exactly as long as the two of them together last."

---

The prop is a length of sawn post about the thickness of her wrist, thick at one end and tapered at the other, and she does not bring a saw to it.

They set the thick end in a hole she digs on the near side of the run, a foot and a half out from the post and as deep as the prop is wide. She gets it down with the bar, packs earth round it and tamps it with the back of the spade, and the prop stands out of the ground at an angle of about a foot in three.

Then she leans it against the post.

It does not touch the post. It stands about the thickness of a finger off it.

"A bit more."

"It cannot come to you any more. I have it in at the angle of the fall and the thickness of the post. It touches the side, not the front." The sister gets her hand between the two. "It is bearing on the side. That is all it has to do. It is not going to keep the post up in front of anybody. It is going to stop the post going over sideways, which is the only direction it has ever gone."

---

Then the wire.

They tie the prop to the leaning post with four turns at the top, right at the joint. Four turns. It goes round the post, round the prop, and round itself three more times, and each turn is pulled up hard and the end is bent back along the wire and tapped round with a screwdriver so it cannot pull itself open.

It takes a long time to get four turns up tight on wire that has not been annealed, and they do it with a bar round the post and a lump of lead on it as a weight.

"That is enough."

"That is four. It wants four." She puts the lump down and stands back. "Two turns ties a thing. Four turns ties a thing to a thing that has to stay tied while nobody is looking at it. And nobody will be looking at it, because you will not see it again until the grass grows up in the spring."

---

The run stands.

She does not believe it at first. She pushes it hard with both hands at the frame and the whole six feet of it comes a little and stops against the wire, and the frame comes back square against the posts and stays square, and the top board that was out of its housing goes back in.

She goes round it and looks along it. It is straight, and she has been watching that run go over since the summer.

"That is better than it was."

"That is better than it was when it was new, because there was nothing keeping that post from going over sideways but the post." Her sister is wiping her hands. "And it will be better in four years' time too, provided that prop stays in that ground and that post stays in its hole. The only thing that will ever shift it is somebody digging in that corner of the yard, and there is nothing buried there."

---

Then she goes along the run with a claw hammer in her hand, because the bottom board at the far end has gone soft at the near ground and she wants to see behind it.

She has never been able to get at anything behind that run. It has always leaned, the boards are close, and there is a gap under the bottom board where the ground has washed away, which was a hand's width in the spring and is about half of one now. She gets her fingers into the gap as far as her first knuckle.

She cannot get them any further. The prop is on the near side of the post, the post is on the near side of the run, and the wire is round both of them at the top, and there is nothing she can do to it with her fingers.

She puts the claw of the hammer against the topmost turn where it lies against the post and pulls.

The wire has bitten into the post. The turns have sunk into the wood and the wood has come up between them, and the post's own surface stands proud between two of the turns.

She puts the hammer against it again and pulls harder, and nothing moves.
