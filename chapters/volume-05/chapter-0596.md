# Chapter 0596 — The Bit She Said

The Wednesday of the thirtieth of September 2020, from about half past seven in the morning until about seven in the evening, at a house on a road of nineteen in this borough.

**SHE SAID ONE SENTENCE TO HIM IN THAT KITCHEN ON THE SATURDAY BEFORE LAST AND HE DID EXACTLY WHAT SHE SAID AND IT IS THE WRONG AMOUNT OF IT, AND SHE CANNOT SHOW HIM IT IS THE WRONG AMOUNT, BECAUSE WHAT SHE SAID WAS NOT WRONG AND WHAT HE DID WAS NOT WRONG AND THEY ARE HOLDING THE SAME SENTENCE.**

She is a woman in her early fifties. He is a man in her early forties and he is her son. He has been painting the outside of that house since the last week of August and it is nearly finished.

The thing is a shop at the bottom of that road with a man in his late fifties who keeps it, and the two of them have been arguing about a door on the side of that shop for about six weeks.

---

Here is the sentence.

She said it on the Saturday before last, at about ten in the morning on the Saturday before, standing at the end of the kitchen with a cloth in her hand, and she was not looking at him when she said it and he was not looking at her.

She said, "Tell him the front's fine and it's the side door he wants."

And he said, "Right," and went back to the front door, and that was all of it.

On the Sunday she mentioned that the man in the shop had been in a mood on the Friday before that, and he said he would have another go at it, and she said no, leave it, and he said right again, and neither of them said anything else about the shop.

What the man in his early forties did with that sentence is this, and it was done properly.

He went down at about ten on the Monday, which was the day before yesterday. He had a piece of chalk in his pocket and a small tin of paint that he had stirred the night before, and he had done the front once already and knew the colour of it.

He worked from the left. The shop's side door is at the end of the front, past a downpipe, and the front is about four foot wide and the side door is the same four foot, and the two of them meet at a corner of about two inches of brick.

He did the front first. He did the same four foot he had done before, one coat of it, and it went on well because the colour is the one he mixed himself and he had it down to a shade he was happy with. Then he did the corner, and then he went along the side of the building to the side door, and the side is about twelve foot, and he did all of it, right up to the door and round the door, and he did the bottom of it, and he came back at about one and did a second one on the front because it needed it.

He was there from ten until about ten past three, with a break of an hour, and the man in his late fifties came out at about twelve and stood beside him for a while with a cigarette, and said nothing about the front, and said nothing about the side either.

Then he knocked on the side door at about half past three on the Monday, which he had never done, and the man in his late fifties opened it and looked at him.

He said, "That's the side done. I said the front was fine, so I've left it."

And the man in the mid fifties said, "Right."

And he said, "The side's had two coats and a bit at the bottom. You'll not want to look at it again for a fortnight."

And the man in the mid fifties said, "Right," and shut the side door.

And that was the whole of it.

---

He has done it. It is done. There is nothing wrong with anything he has done.

The whole outside of that building is now one colour, which it has not been since before either of them can remember. It is a good job. The colour is right and the corner is done and the frame of the side door has been painted round, and the bottom of the side, where the brickwork is damp and has been damp for years, has been done in a way that will not last as long as the rest, and he knows that and said so.

It is the wrong amount. Not the wrong colour and not the wrong door. The wrong amount.

Here is what she wanted, and it is worth being exact about it because she cannot be exact about it herself and that is the whole of the difficulty.

What she wanted was for the man in the mid fifties to be told that the front was the one that was finished, so that he would see it when he came in and say something about it, and the something would be the end of six weeks. She has never in her life wanted a surface painted. She wanted a man in a mood to be given a reason to come out of it.

What was said was: *Tell him the front's fine and it's the side door he wants.*

The front is fine. He is the one who wants the side door. Both halves of that are true and neither of them carries a person. What she meant by the first half was a shape, a picture of a man walking past that side of the shop and being pleased with it, and what he heard was a surface, because he is a man who does surfaces.

And she cannot say so now, and the reason she cannot is not that he would be angry.

If she says *I didn't mean the painting, I meant him looking at it*, then she is saying the sentence she said had a thing inside it that she did not tell him about, and that is true, and he will accept it, and it will make the sentence she said into a different sentence from the one she said, and the one she said is in the room.

And if she says *you've done the side door, I only wanted the front done again*, then she is telling him his work is no use, and he will say it is not no use, because it is not, and he will be right, and the two of them will be in a kitchen arguing about a colour.

On the woman in her early fifties side there was one evening, and here it is entire.

She said nothing on the Monday. Nothing on the Tuesday, and nothing at all yesterday, which was the Wednesday.

On the Wednesday night at about half past eight, which was last night, she said, "That was decent of you."

And he said, "It wasn't anything. It wanted doing."

And she said, "The side door's the one he wanted."

And he said, "I know. That's the one I did."

And she said, "All right," and the evening stopped there. She washed the cloth and hung it over the tap and came in and sat down, and the two of them watched something for the best part of an hour, and she did not raise it again. 

She is not a person who stops. She is also not a person who is going to say it. And here is the thing about it that she has not put into words for anybody, including herself: if she says the sentence was not the sentence she meant, then she is a person who says things she does not mean, and she has looked down that road and has not gone down it, and she will not go down it for a man in his late fifties and a wall.

The outside of that building is one colour. It is a good colour. He is right and she is right and the sentence is the same sentence they both have, and it is in that kitchen with them, and it is not going anywhere.

He painted what she said and it is the wrong amount of the same job. The only person who knows it is the wrong amount is the person who said it. She said *that was decent of you* and meant something else, and neither of them is going to raise it again.
