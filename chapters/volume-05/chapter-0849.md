# Chapter 0849 — Four Slabs Swept

Wednesday the fourth of October 2023, on the four slabs by the shed at number ten from the time the dew has come off them until the shadow of the eaves is across the whole run, and the faces swept and the joints full again is the whole of that Wednesday.

**THE FOUR SLABS BY THE SHED AT NUMBER TEN HAVE A FILM OF SOIL BAKED ON THE FACES AND NO SAND LEFT IN THE JOINTS AND SHE MEANS TO SWEEP THE FACES AND LAY THE SWEEPINGS BACK INTO THE JOINTS WITHOUT LIFTING A SLAB, AND HER SISTER COMES OUT OF THE BACK DOOR WITH A FOLDED SHEET OVER HER ARM AND PEGS IT OVER THE BOUNDARY RAIL.**

Those four slabs are an old delivery laid by somebody who is not coming back, down on sand and a screed, edge to edge with about an inch between them, making a standing place at the shed end where the barrow can be put down.

The faces of them are not dirty in any way a person would describe. They are grey, and the grey is a film of soil off the yard that blew into the tooling marks and baked on through a summer and has now gone hard enough to need a brush.

The joints are the other thing. Sand brushed into a joint between slabs about an inch apart does not last a season on ground that takes the washing off a shed roof, and what has come out of these three joints has gone down into the screed and taken the sand under the edge with it.

She starts on the faces with a stiff brush and a pan, and a sheet on the grass to put the sweepings on.

A face is nothing like a joint for this. Dust in a joint lifts and hangs and goes everywhere, and dust on a face has been pressed into the marks of the making and it lifts in a film, in one piece where it is thick and in a wipe where it is thin, and it comes off without going up into the air at all.

She goes along the run doing the four of them, and the sheet fills with a grey powder that has the whole summer in it, and the faces under it come up pale and true with the marks of the making standing out again as if they were new.

Her sister comes out of the back door in the middle of the morning with a folded sheet over her arm and two pegs in her hand, crosses to the boundary rail at the back of the yard, and pegs the sheet over it, and comes back in by the door she went out of.

She does not stop and she does not put a foot on the slabs, and the band person does not turn round.

---

The filling of the joints is the whole of the rest of the day and it is not one material.

What comes off a slab face is fine and it is not all of the same thing. Most of it is the soil film and that is fine enough to be put in a joint by itself. Under it and mixed through it there is grit off the yard, and the grit is what has been picked out of these joints a foot at a time all summer by boots and barrow wheels, and grit put back into a joint comes out of it again inside a month because that is what it is.

So she puts the coarse in first.

She lays the sweepings on the sheet and lifts the coarse off the top with the edge of the pan, the stones and the hard bits, and those go into the joints with the trowel and are worked down to the bottom of the gap. Then the fine goes over the top of them, a finger deep, and the fine is what a boot meets when it meets the joint, and fine under a boot stays where it was put.

A joint filled the wrong way up is a joint that empties itself in a month. A joint filled coarse at the bottom and fine on the top is a joint that is still there in the spring, because what a boot takes out of it is the fine and the fine goes down onto the coarse and stops there.

She does the three joints between the four slabs and then the two at the ends where they meet the ground, and by the third one her hands have learned it and she stops looking at the sheet.

The third slab has a chip out of it.

The chip is at the arris along the front edge, about the size of a thumbnail and a quarter of an inch deep, with the arris broken away in a curve rather than a square. It has been there since the slabs went down and it has not opened further this year.

She does not fill it. A fill goes into a joint, which is a gap with a face either side of it and something to grip both sides. A chip in an edge is not a gap, it is the edge itself gone, and anything stood in the hole would sit proud of the arris and be off with the first barrow wheel, and then it would be in the yard instead of in the chip.

She leaves it bare and does not sand it back to a line and does not touch it, and it is the only thing on that run she has not put right today.

The test is the heel. She puts a heel across each joint in turn and leans her weight on it, and where a joint is full to the top the heel prints nothing and no dust comes up and the joint takes the weight without giving.

Two of them did give at first. She went back over those with another finger depth of fine and the heel stood on them the second time and the print stayed in the film of soil that came off the faces and nothing came out of the joint itself.

She sweeps the faces and the arris over the ends of the slabs, and leaves the chip bare in the middle of it.

She thinks about the slabs while she works, how a slab face is not paving and does not pretend to be, and how the whole trouble with it is that it lies flat enough to be walked on and to be left things on, so it takes the weather and the grit and the feet, and it has no joint pattern to protect it the way flags have and no bedding to run into. Everything that lands on a slab face stays on it until rain puts it somewhere else, and the joint is the only thing about the arrangement that can be made to keep what it is given.

---

The pan and the brush go back in the shed, and the sheet of sweepings goes out to the side, and the four slabs stand where they have stood all summer.

She stands on the grass by the shed when the shadow of the eaves is across the run and looks at what is there. Four pale faces with a fine grey line running between them at the level of the slabs, and one chip out of the arris on the third one, left bare.
