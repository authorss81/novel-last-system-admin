# Chapter 0677 — The Mark On Her Landing

Tuesday the twenty-sixth of October 2021, in this borough. He is in a house on the far side of the ring road from first light until dark fitting eight doors and a pair of kitchen frames, and the whole of the day is a question asked on a landing that he does not answer.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES IS ASKED BY A WOMAN ON A LANDING WHY THERE IS A BROWN MARK ON HER CEILING AND GIVES HER AN ANSWER THAT IS TRUE AND SMALL AND LEAVES THE ONLY THING HE ACTUALLY KNOWS UNSAID, AND DOES NOT RING A CARD HE HAS CARRIED IN HIS SHIRT POCKET SINCE THE SIXTH OF OCTOBER, AND GOES BACK TO PLANING A SILL.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is on his own all day except for one woman who is in her own house, a man at the bench opposite his unit, and a man on the shop telephone.

---

The job is eight doors and a pair of frames for the kitchen and it is on a road he has driven four times, and the woman who booked it works shifts and is not in the house.

"You'll be all right with the latch on that one?"

"It'll do what it does."

"You'll not put the old one back on?"

"I'll put the old one back on. You always put the old one back on."

"Good. That one's only for the airing cupboard and it's got to shut, because the cats get at it."

"I've a bit of card on the hinge for that if it wants it."

"You've a what?"

He does not answer that, and the woman takes it as a joke and goes to work, and he gets on with the first of the eight doors.

---

The shoebox is at the unit and the element is in it and it stays there.

He gets to the unit before anybody else because he has to fetch the router and the hinges and the eight door leaves are already stacked on site from Friday. The man at the bench opposite is in early, the way he is every day, and the radio is on low at that bench with talk he does not follow.

The shoebox is on the top of his own bench where he cannot come in without seeing it, and the lid is on it, and the element is down inside in a bed of screwed-up newspaper, and it has been on that bench since the third of August.

He has a key on a ring of his own with five keys on it. The unit. The van. The old van he does not have. The house on the estate he has not had anything to do with since the spring. And one that opens nothing he has been able to find out about in about two years.

He has thought about that key about four times in two years and he has never thrown it out, and the reason he has never thrown it out is that he cannot remember which door it was, and a key with no door is not rubbish, it is a key.

---

The woman comes back in the early afternoon and finds him on the second landing.

She has been on the landing twice, both times standing at the top of the flight with her arms folded looking at the ceiling, and both times she has said nothing about it, and both times he has watched her do it out of the corner of his eye while he was cutting a hinge recess.

That is what she opens with.

"That's come down a bit, hasn't it."

He looks at the ceiling. It is a landing ceiling, painted, and there is a brown mark on it about two foot across with a defined edge and a paler ring round the outside of it.

"Aye."

"Can you tell me what that is?"

"It's water."

"I know it's water. Everybody's told me it's water." She has not moved from the top of the flight. "Can you tell me where it's coming from, that's what I'm asking. My mate says get an architect and I've been to two pages of them. Is it coming from the roof or is it coming from my upstairs neighbour's shower?"

She asks it the way people ask it, which is as though there is one answer that has been kept from her by somebody.

He puts the chisel down.

---

Here is what he knows, and it is the whole of what he knows, and he has had it since the summer.

He has been in three kitchens in this borough and in all three of them the water in a ceiling has come from a pipe or a tank and not from the tiles, and he has never once been in a roof space in his life, and he knows what a ballcock does because his mother's did it, and he knows that a cistern in an unheated tank in October is the coldest thing in a house and the only thing in it that is above the room where anybody sits.

And he knows a thing about a woman of seventy-eight in a house on a road three miles from here, who has a bath tap that has dripped since before Christmas, and a passage with a brush on the floor of it, and a light on a bracket by a gate that stands open, and a kitchen table that he put a card in the leg of with no glue and no filler while she watched and neither of them said a word, and he has not been back to that kitchen and cannot go because going is a visit and a visit takes the gate off somebody else.

He could say, right now, on this landing, to this woman, about four sentences that would save her about four hundred pounds, and every one of the four sentences would be a thing he knows from a different house.

---

He says: "You want somebody up in the tank with a torch."

"Is that a person?"

"That's a roofer or a plumber, one of the two, and it's not me, because I don't do roofs and I've told you I don't do roofs."

"You've told me."

"I've said it."

"Right." She looks at the mark again. "They came round in August. There was a woman with a bright voice on the phone, she left a message. And I rang her back and she came, and she was about twenty minutes, and she put a camera on a pole."

"What did she say?"

"She said she'd send it to the people that own it and she'd have to come back in the winter to see whether it gets worse." The woman shifts her weight. "That's the bit. That's what I've been thinking about. Why would you come back in the winter to see if it gets worse?"

"Because it gets worse in the winter."

"Right."

And that is the conversation, and it is a conversation, and it is the only conversation he has had since the beginning of September that has lasted more than a minute, and about a third of it is a thing he is saying and about a third of it is a thing he is not saying, and he can tell the difference between the two thirds from where he is standing, and he does not close the gap.

The woman goes back downstairs.

---

After she has gone he stands on the landing with the chisel in his hand for a while.

What he has not said is that if it is a tank, then a tank in a cold house in the winter is a thing that will find out whether anybody ever goes up that ladder, and that if it does find out, it will find out on a night, on its own, with nobody in the house but a woman who cannot ask anybody to be in the house with her.

And what he has said is that he does not do roofs, which is true, and he is glad it is true.

He goes back down and fits the third door.

---

Late in the afternoon the shop telephone rings and it is a man about a pair of gates.

He takes it standing up with the filler going off behind him.

"Joiner."

"I want two gates. Hinges, the whole thing."

"Right."

"They've got to be oak. Oak's what's asked for. They've been through the wringer longer than anybody can remember, which is what gates do."

"Oak's fine if it's oak. What's the width?"

"I don't know. They're on the wall outside a cottage. I've had a look at them."

"You've had a look at them, right."

"I've had a look at them."

"Then bring the width and the height off one of them. Both. And take a bit of the hinge side with you, about that much." He puts his thumb and forefinger up and the man on the other end of the line takes his time working out how much. "There's a garden place off the ring road that'll do it in oak, you tell them you want it mortised for a padlock on the hinge stile and not on the latch stile, because on the latch stile it lifts out in a few years."

"That's a thing, is it."

"That's a thing."

"Right. Bits and pieces, then."

"Bits and pieces. You'll want it by the spring and he'll want the money up front."

"Right you are."

He puts the receiver back and runs it back in his head, and it was the right thing, and the man will not be quoted ten minutes for two gates because ten minutes is what he said about a cupboard door in September and it has been doing him like a stone in a boot ever since.

---

Then he sees the card.

It is in the shirt pocket of the coat on the back of the door, and he puts his hand in the pocket for a pencil the way he has done a hundred times, and it is a letting agent's card with a boy on it in a shirt, and it has been in that pocket since the sixth of October, and the lad on the estate gave it to him in a front room with dust sheets down and eight doors leaning against a wall with a damp patch in the corner nobody has mentioned.

He has had it in that pocket since the sixth of October and has not rung it.

He does not ring it now either, and the reason is not that he cannot be bothered, and it is that the card is for a man who owns eight flats on an estate where three ceilings have come down in eighteen months, and that man is always in, and that ringing him would make him a man who goes round asking after roofs, and he is a joiner, and a man's not a roof.

He puts the card back in the pocket of the coat.

---

He fits the fourth door and the fifth and the sixth, and he planes a sill for the seventh because the frame is out and there is a sill to be done, and the frame is good stock and the sill is oak and it is off a piece that came out of a window frame, and he works it with the grain and not across it and the shavings come up pale under his hand and he puts a cup on it when he has finished the way he always does, because a cup is what you put on a table and a table is what you put a cup on.

The cup does not walk about.

He does not think about that for longer than it takes to pick the cup up and put it down on the sill again, and then he starts on the eighth door.

At the end of the day he drives back with the van empty and the drawer under the bench still open, and the shoebox still on the bench where he cannot come in without seeing it, and the canvas bag in the footwell with the stranger's brush still in the side pocket and the stone in the leather bottom of it.

There is a new brush in a plastic bag flat along the back of that open drawer and he has not touched it and he is not going to, and the drawer has been open since the third of August.

And there is a woman on a landing on the far side of the ring road who has a mark on her ceiling about two foot across and a bright voice on an answerphone and a plan to come back in the winter, and he has told her he does not do roofs, and he did not tell her one single other thing, and he did not tell her the thing he knows, and there is nobody in this borough who is ever going to find out that he knew.

---

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES HAS BEEN ASKED WHERE A MARK ON A CEILING COMES FROM AND HAS SAID HE DOES NOT DO ROOFS AND HAS SAID NOTHING ELSE, HAS PUT A CARD BACK IN A SHIRT POCKET INSTEAD OF RINGING IT, HAS BUILT A SILL TODAY AND PUT A CUP ON IT TO SEE IF IT WALKED, AND THE DRAWER UNDER THE BENCH IS STILL OPEN.**
