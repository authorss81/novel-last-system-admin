# Chapter 0418 — Taped Over

Thursday the seventh of June 2018, beginning at about ten past twelve in the morning and running on into the middle of July, a block of eight flats on a side street, and a shop off that parade, in Tideglass.

**TWO MEN SAW A CRACKED PANE OF GLASS ON AN INSIDE STAIR IN THAT BLOCK AND EACH OF THEM LEFT IT THERE FOR A REASON OF HIS OWN, AND A THIRD PERSON CAME ALONG ON HER OWN ACCOUNT AND TOOK THE PANE OUT AND HAD IT CUT AND PUT IT BACK PROPERLY, AND NEITHER OF THOSE TWO MEN HAS EVER FOUND OUT THAT SHE WAS IN THAT BUILDING.**

Eight flats and a bin store, built about 1910, with the door on a side street. The stair rises from the passage in three flights, each with its own landing.

A window on the top landing, and a casement on each of the two below it. The first landing has the one that matters: a three-light casement, its opening light painted shut, looking out over the back roofs of the next street.

The opening light of that window is about a foot wide and about two foot high. It is cracked across a corner, from about two inch in at the bottom left corner up and across to about six inch from the top edge.

It cracked in the second week of April. Nobody in that block saw it happen and nobody in that block has ever found out what did it, and the woman who lives in the second flat thinks it was a ball and a man who lives on the top floor thinks it was a house move, and the two of them have never discussed it.

Somebody put two strips of brown parcel tape over it from the bottom corner up about five inch, and pressed the edges down with a thumbnail, and the tape has gone grey and has lifted at one corner and has let a run of water come in and go out again in the wind.

---

On the sixth of May, at about a quarter to twelve, he went up that stair for a running cistern on that same floor that he had been in twice before and had not been paid for.

He stopped on that landing because the window was making a noise, which it does, because the opening light is loose in its frame and it moves in the wind, and the noise is the tape lifting and slapping against the glass.

He knew in about a second what it was and could have told anybody standing there what it was.

And he is not going to do anything about it, and here is the whole of why, and There is no rule behind it and no principle and he has not turned it over since.

It is a landlord's. That is the short answer and it is a real answer, because he does not touch other people's property and has never once in his life, and if he puts a pane in that window and the man who owns that block does not like the price he paid, or the bead, or the fact that it is a different thickness, that is a conversation he will have to have with a man he has never met in an office he would have to go to.

But that is only the first half.

The second half is that glazing is not his trade. A pane of glass in a window in a stair in a block of flats wants a man with a glazier's frame and a running bead and a putty knife and about an hour of clear space, and he has not got the frame, and the frame is the whole of it.

And the third half is the one that is actually the reason.

A man in his thirties cannot be the man who did that window. If he does it, then that block knows that he was in it and that he saw the tape, and there is a thing between him and eight households that did not exist before, and in about two years somebody in that block will want something done in a hurry and will remember it wrong. He would have put a pane of glass in a window to avoid that and would have got the whole thing instead.

He went on up the stair and did the cistern and came down and shut the door behind him, and he has not been on that landing since and is not going to.

On the second of June, at about ten in the morning, he was in that block for about nine minutes in total, all of it on the ground floor and all of it in a passage.

He was waiting on a delivery of a wardrobe for a flat on the ground floor and there was nobody in and a telephone going and no answer, and he stood in that passage with a piece of paper in his hand for about four minutes.

The window is on the first landing, three or four steps up, and he went up those steps because a man standing in a passage waiting for a telephone to be answered goes up a step, and he went up about four and stopped, because the window was in his face and it was making that noise.

He looked at it for about three or four seconds.

He could have done that window. He has a pair of putty knives in his own van and he has put four panes in on his own account since 2014 and he knows exactly what a running bead is for.

He did not do it, and the reason is the plainest thing in this chapter and it took him about a second. It is not his.

He was in that passage for somebody else's delivery, on a ground floor, for nine minutes, and the thing that needed doing was two flights up, and to have done it he would have had to ask a stranger whether he might come into her landing with a ladder and spend an hour on her window, and he would have had to have a conversation with that stranger about a crack she has known about since April and has not mentioned, and whether she knows or does not know, it stops the same conversation.

So he went back down to the passage and the telephone went and he got his delivery in and he shut the door of the block behind him and drove off, and he did not think about it again.

On the Thursday of that week, at about twenty to twelve in the morning, she went into that block.

She was not going in to see anybody. She had a bolt of a particular size out of a shop off that parade that a woman who lives in the second flat of that block had been asking after for a fortnight, and the woman who lives in the second flat is not her friend and is a customer, and the bolt was in a bag and it was the wrong bag for that passage and she was not going to leave it on a step.

She went up the first flight with it because the light on that landing is at the top and she knew there was a light at the top.

The opening light was moving in the draught from the passage door, and the tape at the bottom corner was standing off the glass about the width of a thumbnail, and there was water on the inside of the sill, and there had been water on that sill long enough for the paint on the sill to have gone chalky and lifted in a crescent about four inch long.

She stood on the bottom step of that flight and looked at it for about a minute.

And then she went back down and put the bolt behind the letterbox of the second flat with a scrap of paper round it and a note on that paper that said the size and nothing else, because the woman who lives in that flat works days and would not want a ring on the door, and she went back to her shop.

And then she did not think about that window again for about a week, and she thought about it in the shower, and the thing that brought it back was not the window at all.

It was that she had a length of 5 mm polished sheet in a cupboard under her own counter that has been there since the January because she bought it by mistake in a hurry and it has never gone anywhere, and she has a glazier's putty knife in the same cupboard, and she has a hacksaw in her own van, and she does not need a glazier's frame to cut a small pane because a small pane is the one size of glass a person can cut on a table with a straight edge and a square and a light touch.

She thought about the fact that she has a glazier's putty knife in a cupboard and has used it four times, all of them on her own place, and she thought about the sill.

And what she thought about, and this is the part that is the whole of it and that she has never put to anybody, is that nobody in that block is going to do that window, and that is not anybody else's business and is not hers either, and that if she does not do it then it does not get done for about five years, and she does not have to be asked and she does not have to be a person who was asked, and there is no form in this that anybody fills in.

She did not tell anybody she was going to. She did not ask the woman in the second flat. She did not put anything in a book about it and there is no book. Nobody in this borough knows she has a tape on that stair.

---

She did it in three goes over about three weeks and she did it properly, and this is what proper means on a stair window that has had a bit of tape on it for two months.

The first of them was the Saturday after, about ten in the morning, with the sun off that side. She put two sheets of cardboard on the stair below the window, because there is nowhere else to put anything in that stair, and she took the tape off in pieces rather than peeling it, because peeling it takes the paint, and she put what came off in her bag and took it away.

She took the pane out whole on the six screws that hold the beads and carried it down two flights in her own arms with a blanket round it, and shut the block's front door behind her on the latch, which is a thing she can do and has never told anybody she can do.

She had it cut early the following week in a shop off that parade, in about twenty minutes, for nine pounds, out of her own purse, and it went on that shop's invoice in a column with about nine other small jobs, and that is the only record of it anywhere in this borough.

She bedded the new pane in on the Saturday after that, standing on a step with a board across the two above her, and she ran a bead of putty on the outside, and she did the beads, and she put two screws in the bottom member where the sash catches, and she wiped the glass and the sill and carried the old pane away in her own van.

Then she came back the Saturday after that with a small brush and a pot of whatever colour the sill was, and touched the crescent of chalky paint where the water had been standing, and came away.

Three goes. Two Saturdays and a weekday. About four hours of her own time and nine pounds.

The light in that window has not shut and it is not going to, because it is painted shut and it was painted shut before the pane went in, and she knew that when she put it back and she did not scrape it and she did not cut it out and she did not say one word to anybody about that either.

Nobody in that block asked her. Nobody in that block saw her, because that first flight is empty between about half past nine in the morning and half past five in the afternoon on a weekday, and the man on the top floor is out at work, and the whole of it is a day of a woman's life and nine pounds.

In the middle of July a man from the top floor of that block came down for his post, stopped on that landing, and looked at the window, and it looked like a window, and he went back up.

The man in his thirties has not been on that landing since the May. The other one has not been in that block since the second of June and does not know he stood four steps under a window that wanted doing. The woman who lives in the second flat found the bolt behind her letterbox and left it on the hall table where it is.

**SHE TOOK A PANE DOWN TWO FLIGHTS IN HER OWN ARMS WITH A BLANKET ROUND IT, AND NOBODY IN THAT BLOCK HAS ASKED HER ANYTHING, AND NOBODY IN THAT BLOCK KNOWS IT WAS HER, AND THE LIGHT IN THAT WINDOW STILL DOES NOT SHUT.**
