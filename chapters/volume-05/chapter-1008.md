# Chapter 1008 — Take Me Off The Book

Undated in this draft.

**THE PANTRY AT NUMBER NINE HAS ITS SHELVES TWO FOOT DEEP AND A NARROW DOOR.**

The pantry off the kitchen at number nine is a small square room with three runs of shelves on three walls and no window of any kind, and the shelves are about two foot deep and are fixed on brackets let into the wall so that there is nothing underneath them to slide, which is a thing somebody thought about once and then never again. There is a bulb on a flex from the ceiling in the middle of it and the bulb is the same low-watt bulb as the one in the kitchen and gives about as much. The door of it is on the kitchen side and it has to be got round the corner of the wall to come into the room, and it is the width of one person's shoulders and no more, so a man carrying anything at all cannot get through it, which whoever built it knew perfectly well and did. The shelves were fitted in nineteen-something by a man who set the bottom run against the wall without setting it true, so the whole of that run stands about half an inch out of true at one end and the jars on it have been going over towards the wall on their own for as long as she can remember.

She got back from the water four days ago. She has not asked anybody for anything in the four days, which is a thing she has been telling herself about, and the first thing she did with them was go up to the lock-up with a basket on her arm, and stand in front of the open book in the office, and read the column from the top down, and go back the end of that street with her hands in her apron and think about it for two days without once deciding anything, which for her is the most she has ever done about a thing.

"You were not going to come," she told him. "You have not come up this frontage on your own once in four weeks and I have made sure of it by being out."

"I was coming up this frontage to ask a woman at number twelve for a wall."

"Then you have come about a wall and I am asking you about a book, and both of those can happen in one afternoon, and if you cannot manage both then the wall will keep." She had her hand on the pantry door and she was not going through it. "Put it into words out loud and then I will let you put the other one into words."

---

"There is a woman at that yard who keeps it."

"Yes."

"Take me off it."

He did not answer that, and the reason he did not answer it was not the one he was going to give her about ten minutes later, and he knows that now. What stopped him in the doorway was that he could not think of one single reason why he was the man to do it. He has never asked that woman for one thing in his life. He has stood in her office and been refused and been refused again and argued with her and taken his own work to her to be refused on, and the whole of that is a thing between them, and this would be a third thing with a shape of its own that he would be carrying across a yard in his mouth.

"I do not know her well enough to ask her that. And I have not asked that woman for anything either. You could go yourself."

She was seventeen when her mother put her on it, and the reason her mother did it was that there was a man in that borough who fitted a boiler in this street in the winter of that year and would not do it unless the house had a name against the work, and her mother's name was already on the book and hers was not, and she was seventeen and she was standing in the kitchen listening to it. That was the first time anybody ever came into that house for a reason. There have been four times since. The last one was in the spring and it was a man from the same yard to look at the front step, and he stood in the kitchen and would not come further in, and she gave him a cup of tea at that table and he drank it in about a minute and went, and she has thought about that cup of tea more in the last ten years than about anything else that has ever happened to her.

"I have been on that book since I was seventeen and I have never once been asked whether I wanted to be on it, and the reason I know what is on it and what is not is that I can read." She came through the pantry door then, because she had decided to, and she came through the gap edgeways with her shoulder and stood in front of the bottom run of shelves with her hand on the front edge of it. "A name comes off it when the season shuts, and the season shuts on Friday, and I have four days to work out whether I am going to be a person who got herself off a book or a person who is still on it in the spring. That is what four days is. And I want somebody to come in there who is not me, because if I go in there on my own two hands she will hear it in the first sentence and I will not get past it."

He put his hand along the front edge of that bottom shelf the way you put a hand along a lath, and the shelf was not straight, and he found the dip about a third of the way along it, which is where the dip has been since before either of them was born and which is the dip she has been shoving the jars back from for thirty years without ever once thinking about it.

"All right. What do I tell her."

"That you have been asked by a woman who has been on the book since she was seventeen to be taken off it, and that she is asking you to ask, and that I am not coming with you because I am not going to be the one who asks." She let go of the shelf. "And then you stand there and let her ask me, in whatever way she likes, because that is all of it, all I am after. Nobody has ever had to ask me for anything in my life and I want to know what it is like to be asked, once, by somebody with the right to do it, and I am not fussy about what the answer is."

He promised her the morning, and he promised her in the plainest words he had, which is not a high thing to promise anybody anything. She let him have it without a face, which after the morning she had given him is a thing he will not forget.

He got as far as the front door and stopped, because there is one thing about a man who agrees to carry somebody else's want that he had not thought about when he agreed to it. Nothing on this frontage will show that he carried it. No paper covers it and no room does and nobody standing on this street a month from now could tell that a woman at number nine asked a man to go up to a yard and ask to have her own name taken off the one page in this borough that anybody in this borough could produce, and that he went, and that she never once thanked him for it and would not have wanted to.

Although the door of that pantry is too narrow for anything a man would want to carry out of it, she has had it open every day since she came back, and the dark between the two lowest shelves has not been disturbed once.
