# Chapter 0585 — Mending

The Monday of the tenth of August 2020, from about half past nine in the morning until about half past five in the afternoon, in a first-floor flat off a main road in this borough, and on the landing outside it.

**SHE SAID ONE SENTENCE IN THAT KITCHEN A WEEK AGO AND THE TWO OF THEM HAVE LIVED A WHOLE WEEK INSIDE IT WITHOUT LOOKING AT IT, AND THE ONLY THING THAT HAPPENED IN THAT FLAT TODAY WAS A ZIP.**

The flat has a hall, a room at the front, a room at the back, and a kitchen you can stand two people up in if neither of them is turning round. It has been hers since about 2016 and it has been his since the March, when he came to stay for a while and then did not go.

She is a woman in her early thirties. He is a man in his early forties. They are not married and neither of them has ever used a word for what this is, and it has held for five months on nothing more durable than the two of them turning up.

The sentence was said in the kitchen on the Monday before last with the tap off. She said, "I have put in for the one in town," and then she said, "I haven't heard, and I'm not going to chase it after."

He said, "Right," and put a plate away on the rack.

Since then the days have gone the way days go. He leaves at seven. She is in and out. They have eaten at the same hours and spoken about the same small things — the bin men missing again on the back stretch, a man at work who has started parking over the side of her sister's drive — and neither of them has said the word flat or the word town or the word chase once, and the sentence has been in that kitchen every single evening without ever being in it.

She has noticed that neither of them raises it and she has not decided whether that is a kindness or the two of them being tired.

---

He goes at seven and the door goes on the latch behind him the way it has gone for five months.

She has a job that is three days a week and it is not today. She gets up, does the washing, puts it out, comes in, and stands in the kitchen with the radio on very low, which is how it goes when a flat is empty and she is not.

At about ten to twelve she goes through to the front room and comes back with the sewing tin off the top of the cupboard, which is a biscuit tin with the picture off the side of it and holds what it has always held: a needle with the thread in it, some hooks, two buttons, a bodkin, and about a yard of tape with a pin in the middle of it.

She does not sit down to do it. She stands at the end of the table by the good window with the front door of that room open behind her.

The zip on her work trousers has been going since the June. It has been catching on the underside of the table at home twice and once at the shop, and she has been meaning to have it seen to since the June, and she gets the tin down at about a quarter to twelve on a Monday morning in a flat on her own and it takes her until about twenty to one.

She unpicks the bottom three inches with the bodkin, which takes longer than the sewing does. She opens the slider, eases the teeth apart, and works the closed teeth down the split and back up the other side. It is not difficult work. It is the kind of work a person does with one hand and thinks about something else with the other, and what she thinks about is nothing in particular, and that is also the kind of thing that happens.

She finishes at about twenty to one. It pulls true both ways. She puts the needle back in the tin with the thread in it, because that is how she found it, and she puts the tin on the top of the cupboard.

Then she stands there.

There is a knock at about twenty past three.

It is the woman from the second floor, a woman in her late forties who has lived on that landing since before either of them came, and who has a key of her own to the cupboard where the post is, and who has a way of arriving at three in the afternoon exactly when there is nobody in.

She says, "Is the lad in?"

She says "the lad" about the man in his early forties, and she has called him that since about April, and neither of them has ever asked her to.

"No," the woman in her early thirties says. "He's not in till about six."

"You'll want to get him to look at the fitting over your own door," the woman on the landing says, coming two steps in because the door is open. "It's been flickering on and off all afternoon. Two of the ones underneath have been going since the weekend."

"Right," the woman in her early thirties says.

"That's the one over the second door. It's the one over your own, when he gets in."

"Right," the woman in her early thirties says again.

The woman from the second floor looks at the tin on top of the cupboard for a moment, which everyone in that building looks at, because it has always been there, and she says, "You'll be wanting the steps painted before the weather turns, the both of you," and goes down.

The man in his early forties comes in a little after six and puts his bag down in the hall and comes through to the kitchen and says, "All right?"

"All right," the woman in her early thirties says.

He eats. He asks her about a man at work who has been off since the middle of the week. She tells him, and it is a good deal of nothing, and he listens to it in the way he does.

At about half past six she says, "The woman from the second floor's been."

"What did she want?"

"The fitting."

"The one over the second door," he says, not a question, and looks up.

"The one over your own," she says, "when you get in."

He says, "Right," and goes out into the hall and stands on the stairs and looks up at it for a bit, and comes back in and says it's a bit loose in the fitting and he will see to it at the weekend, and she says there was a zip on her trousers that had been catching, and she says it in the middle of nothing, the way you put a thing into a room, and he says, "Get the man in town to do it," and she says, "Done it," and he says, "Good," and that is the whole of that.

Neither of them says one word about the flat in town. She has not heard and she is not going to chase it after. That sentence is still in the kitchen where she left it on the Monday before last, saying exactly what it said when she said it, and it has not been picked up and it has not been put down, and it is still there at half past six on this Monday evening, unchanged and unargued with, and neither of these two people has done a single thing about it.

What happened in that flat today was a zip. A woman stood at the end of her own table in the front room with a biscuit tin off the top of a cupboard and did a small job on her own trousers and put the needle back in the tin with the thread in it, and that is the whole of what she did, and it had nothing whatever to do with the sentence, and there was nobody in that building she could have told it to and nobody who would have understood what was being told.

**THEY GOT THROUGH THE WEEK AS THOUGH SHE HAD NOT SAID IT, AND AT THE END OF IT SHE SAT A STITCH, AND HE CAME IN AND ASKED WHETHER A MAN IN TOWN COULD HAVE DONE IT, AND THE SENTENCE IS STILL IN THAT KITCHEN, AND NEITHER OF THEM HAS THE SLIGHTEST INTENTION OF IT.**
