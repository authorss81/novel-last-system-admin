# Chapter 0235 — The Board

Thursday the twenty-first of April 2016, from about half past eight in the morning, a boathouse off a yard in Tideglass, and the car park behind it.

**THE THING THIS BOROUGH IS CALLED FIRST FOR IS ON A BOARD IN A LOCK-UP, IN A HAND, ON CARDS, AND THE FIRST TWO LINES ARE PEOPLE AND THE THIRD IS A PLACE, AND UNDER THE THIRD ONE THERE IS A NAME THAT IS NOT THERE ANY MORE.**

The lock-up is a brick hut with a hasp on the door and a padlock on the hasp, and the number to the padlock is known to four people in this borough, none of whom have it written down anywhere. There is a bench along one wall, a coil of hose on a nail, a pump on a barrow, and a board about the size of a tea tray hanging on the back wall at a slight angle that nobody has straightened.

Nell Ardent got there at about half past eight. She came on foot. She had not come from an office, because she has no office, and she had not telephoned, because there is no number on the door, and she got there by walking up the parade and asking two men in a wet shop who have both been in this borough longer than she has, and who said the same thing to her separately, which was that if you want to know what goes first, ask the boat, and if the boat won't tell you, ask the engine.

The boat crew were in already, two of them, wringing something out on the handrail. The engine crew came in at five to nine, set a box down on the end of the bench and did not open it. Nobody had rung any of them. Nobody had sent for them. They had come because it was Thursday and Thursday is what they do, and four people in this borough would have told her that on Monday if she had asked them on Monday.

She did ask. It took until about ten past nine.

The board has a strip of card tape across the top and a pencil line under it, and on the strip there are three cards, all of the same length, all cut with something that was not scissors.

The first card says BOAT. The second says ENGINE. The third says LOW PEVERELL, and under the third one, in the same hand and smaller, there is a gap where a name was.

"Is that all of them?" said Nell Ardent.

"That's all of them," said the woman who has the key to the lock-up and has never been asked what she is called and has not offered. "That's been on there since I was a girl. I don't cut them. I put the padlock back and I shut the door."

"You cut the first one."

"I did not." She said it without any heat in it. "Somebody cut it and put a card in and wrote on it, and that's not a thing I did, and I wouldn't have done it, because the day you cut a card for something somebody asks who cut it, and I'm not going to be the one somebody asks."

"Who is on the first one?"

"Whichever three turn up. The boat is whoever gets there first on a Thursday."

"Then the cards aren't people," said Nell Ardent. "They're doors."

"They're doors," the woman said. "And the third one's a place, and the third one's the one they ask me about, and I'll tell you what I tell everybody, which is that the low end goes first because the low end goes first, and there is nobody to ring, and there isn't going to be anybody to ring, and the pump at number forty-one is a man's own, and he is the only reason anybody is going down there at all."

At about ten past nine the man from number forty-one came in.

He is not on anything. He has lived at the bottom of Peverell Street for about thirty years, and in the wet week of the November before last he put a pump in his own doorway and ran everybody below his landing off it for two days, and when the water went down he shut the stopcock off and left the pump and the hose where they were, and the pump is still standing in that doorway, and he has not mentioned any of it since and is not going to.

"I hear you've been asking after the card," he said.

"Who told you that?"

"A man in a shop. Half of this borough is told everything by a man in a shop." He looked at the board for a while. "It was my name under the third one. Somebody come round with a pen about three years back and asked me what I did, and I told him I don't do anything, I just live here, and he wrote something, and I took it off a fortnight after and it's in a drawer in my kitchen and I have not put it back and I'm not going to."

"Why did you take it off?"

"Because a name on a board is a thing that gets asked about," said the man with the pump. "I've been on enough lists."

**AND THE PRIORITY IS A PERSON AND A PLACE, AND NEITHER OF THE TWO CAN BE GIVEN TO ANYBODY, AND THE MAN UP THE ESTUARY WHO HOLDS THE FIVE THINGS HAS HIS OWN NAME UNDER A LINE ON A WALL AND WAS TOLD A YEAR AGO THAT NONE OF IT COULD BE HANDED TO ANOTHER PERSON, AND THE SAME SHAPE OF SENTENCE IS TRUE IN THIS LOCK-UP AND IT IS A DIFFERENT SENTENCE, AND NELL ARDENT IS THE ONE WHO HAD TO HOLD BOTH OF THEM AT ONCE.**

Nell Ardent stood in front of the board with her bag at her feet and said nothing for about half a minute.

"The first of the two," she said, "is that a duty can be given to a man who is standing there. That's the whole of what up the estuary is about, and it's a very good arrangement, and I have never heard a soul in that town say a word against it. You can hand a man his own work because he is in the corridor. That's what a name under a line on a wall is for."

"And the other one."

"The other one is that you can't hand a man to anybody either, and you can't hand a place to anybody, and there is no way of doing either of those from this borough, and it has never found one and it is not going to." She put a finger under the gap without touching it. "The low end of Peverell Street doesn't belong to anybody. It isn't this borough's, because there is no such thing, and it isn't the water office's, and it isn't anybody else's either. And the man at the bottom of it doesn't belong to anybody. And that is why he is the first thing on that board, and that is why he took his name off it. He can't be sent anywhere, and he can't be given away, and neither can the place. And it's the same shape of sentence as the one in that town and it isn't the same sentence, and if you put them side by side you'll get both of them wrong."

Nobody in that boathouse said anything. The engine crew did not open the box. The man at number forty-one looked at the board for a bit longer, and then went out into the yard, and did not say good-bye, which is a thing people in this borough do.

---

Jonas Mercer was in the car park behind the boathouse from about nine until about half past eleven, in a coat, with a piece of paper in his inside pocket that has a date and a number on it. He did the sum standing up against a wall with a biro and got five hundred and twelve, which is the same sum he has been doing since the summer and has never had a different answer out of.

He was not in the boathouse. He had been asked to come and had not said he would not, and he was not asked again, and there is a difference between those two things that about four people in this borough could explain and not one of them would bother to.

At about half eleven Nell Ardent came out into the car park and stood beside him and gave him the whole of it, and he asked her one question, which was what the man at number forty-one had actually said, and she told him that too.

"They're two different sentences," he said.

"They're the same shape," she said. "That's what makes it hard, and that's why I've been in there since half past eight. One of them is a wall in a corridor in a town up the estuary with a man's own name under the second line, and the second one is a gap under a third card on a board in a brick hut, and they are the same shape and they are not the same sentence. The second one I've just spent three hours being told about by a man who lives at the bottom of the street, and he took his own name off that board three years ago, about a fortnight after a man came round with a pen, and he was not frightened doing it, and there was nobody standing over him."

She got a taxi. She has a clinic at two and she is very good at being two places.

**AND THE NAME IS IN A KITCHEN DRAWER FOUR DOORS UP THE STREET FROM A BOARD WITH A GAP UNDER THE THIRD CARD, AND THE GAP IS STILL A GAP, AND THE MAN WHO PUT THE PUMP IN HIS OWN DOORWAY HAS NOT MENTIONED IT SINCE, AND NEITHER THE MAN NOR THE PLACE CAN BE HANDED TO ANYBODY, AND THIS BOROUGH HAS A THING IT COULD PUT ON A TABLE AND THE THING IT HAS IS A GAP.**
