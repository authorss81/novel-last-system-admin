# Chapter 0692 — The Only Question Anybody Asks

Tuesday the twenty-eighth of December 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house at number ten from first light until dark with her sister in the kitchen for most of the evening, and the whole of the day is a drawer with a bit of ply in it and the one question anybody has asked her since Friday, which is answered with nothing.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES TELLS HER SISTER TWO THINGS ABOUT THE HOUSE AT THE END OF THE ROAD AND NEITHER OF THEM IS ABOUT THE STEP LADDER, IS ASKED BY HER SISTER WHERE THE LADDER WAS FROM AND SAYS SHE DOES NOT KNOW WHICH IS TRUE, WORKS OUT THAT A FELT EDGE ON HER OWN SHED ROOF IS THREE THINGS SHE CANNOT NAME YET AND DOES NOT PUT A DAY ON IT, AND STANDS IN HER OWN BACK YARD IN THE DARK AFTER HER SISTER HAS GONE AND REMEMBERS THAT SHE DID NOT SWITCH HER OWN OUTSIDE LIGHT ON ON SATURDAY MORNING AND STOOD IN THE DARK LOOKING FOR A LIGHT AT THE END OF THAT ROAD, WHICH WAS ON.**

She is a woman in her early thirties. She is at home all day. Her sister is in the kitchen for most of the evening.

---

The shed is still doing her roof in.

She knows that without going out to it, because she has been in that kitchen every day since Friday and she can hear it, or she thinks she can, and she has been the sort of person who thinks she can hear a thing like that since she was a girl and has never once been wrong about a draught and has never once been right about a tap.

The bit of ply is in the drawer under the worktop on top of the tape roll with about four inches of brown tape left on it. The hammer and the screwdriver are in the same drawer and have not been out since September and are not going to be out this year.

She does not open the drawer on the twenty-eighth.

---

What she does do, in the morning, with a pencil and the back of a bit of paper off a pad that lives on the shelf above the worktop, is work out what a felt edge on that shed would be.

A roll of it. A tin of the stuff that goes over the join. A bit of a timber off-cut or a piece of ply to sit under the new edge so there is a lip for the water to come off and not run back under.

She writes the three things down and she does not write down what any of them are, because she does not know, and she is not going to stand in a builder's merchant on a Saturday morning and say *I want a bit of felt for a shed*, because that is a sentence with about nine words in it that she does not have yet.

She puts the pencil down.

Then she looks at the bit of paper for a while, because three lines with nothing on them but the shape of a problem is a worse thing than nothing at all, and a person who writes down three things she cannot name has started something she will not finish and will not throw away.

She folds it in half and puts it in the drawer on top of the ply, on top of the pencil, under the tape roll.

That drawer now has in it a bit of ply that will not do the job, a roll with about four inches of brown tape left on it out of a roll neither she nor her sister has ever said where it came from, a hammer and a screwdriver that have not been out since September, a pencil, and a folded piece of paper with three lines on it that say what a thing would be and not what it is. It is the fullest that drawer has been since she moved in.

She shuts the drawer.

---

Here is the thing about it and it is the whole of the day, and she can say it in one line because it is a small thing and she is not making it into a large one.

A repair with no day on it is not a repair.

She did that to herself on the Friday when she got up on the ladder and found the front edge of that felt gone and put the ply back in the drawer. And that is the same as a man on a street on Monday morning who lends a step ladder out of a van and does not say when it is coming back. And it is the same as a woman at number nine who took a thing out of a porch in the rain and could not put it back.

Nobody in this borough has agreed anything. Everybody has been reasonable. That is the entire of what has happened to that shed and that ladder and that box since Friday, and it is going to be exactly the same as it is now when she goes to bed.

She looks at the pad on the shelf for a while and then she puts the pencil in the drawer on top of the ply, which is a stupid place for a pencil and which she will find again.

---

Her sister comes at the end of the day with a form in a bag, goes round the back without knocking and lets herself in off the yard, and the door shuts true behind her the way it always does.

They do not go into the front room. There is a settee in it, a table with a lamp on it, and a wall, and neither of them has any business in there on a Tuesday in December.

She puts the kettle on.

"You've been quiet," her sister says.

"I've been at the shed."

"Did you get up on it?"

"I got up on it on the Monday. It's no use. The felt's gone at the front edge. Ply'll do nothing."

"Then you'll want to get some felt."

"Aye."

"You could get some off the man at number eleven. He had it on his bench all summer, I saw it through the window."

"I know."

"You could ask him."

"I know."

Her sister sits down and puts her feet under her and looks at her.

"You went up the road," she says.

It is not a question. Her sister has not been up that road since either of them can remember either, and there are two people in this borough who have never been up it and both of them are in this kitchen, and the one who has been has the kettle in her hand.

"Aye."

"And?"

---

And here is where the whole of the week comes to, and it is not a big moment and it is not going to be a big moment, and she is not going to make it one.

"Two things," she says.

"Go on."

"The gate's off its wall. It's standing back off the shed, and it's been like that since September, and there's a padlock on the shed hanging open in the hasp, and the shed door's shut. She shut it herself back in November. She's not been in it since, she said."

Her sister looks up.

"She shut a shed door."

"That's what she said."

"Weird, that."

"It's not weird. It's a shed."

"And the other thing."

---

"The kitchen's at the back," she says. "The front room's in between the door and the kitchen, and it's cold and there's a mark on the ceiling. She didn't go in it. She stood in the doorway. I put the ladder in there and she didn't go in after it."

"You never been in that house before."

"Never."

"Then how did you know to go up there?"

"I didn't know. You told me about it in the summer. Twice."

Her sister does not say anything to that. Her sister said it twice, in a kitchen, in about August, in the way that people say a thing they have said before to somebody they are trying to help, and she has never once been up that road to see whether the thing she said was right.

---

"What was she like?"

"About my age. A bit older. She didn't ask me anything." She turns the tap off. "That's the thing. She didn't ask me a single thing. Not who I was, not where it was from, not what I wanted with it. She just stood there and let me put it in the room."

"Wouldn't you think she'd want to know?"

"I'd have thought so."

---

"And the ladder," her sister says. "Who was that from?"

And that is the question. Since Friday, and that is the only question anybody in this borough has asked anybody about a step ladder, and it has been asked by a woman who has no interest in the answer, in a warm kitchen, at the end of a day, and it is asked in exactly the tone of somebody asking about a bin day.

"I don't know," she says.

"You don't know."

"I don't know. It came off a porch at your end and the man who emptied the house was on the Friday and the woman who took it out of the porch on the Sunday didn't ask him either, and when I asked her she said she couldn't put back what she couldn't give and that was the end of it."

"So it's somebody's."

"It's somebody's."

"You could go back and ask her."

"I could go back and ask her," she says, "and she'd say she doesn't know, because she doesn't, and then I'd be stood in a gateway having said the whole thing out loud twice, and she'd know I'd been and there'd be nothing at the end of it."

Her sister thinks about that.

"Fair enough," she says.

And that is the end of the ladder.

They stand and drink their tea in the kitchen because the kitchen is the warm room and neither of them has ever lit a fire in the other one's front room since Easter, and the talk goes to the bin and to the price of it and to a form, and her sister says the tip off the ring road is on different days this year, and she says that is the first sensible thing anybody has said all week.

At about the middle of the evening her sister looks at the drawer under the worktop.

"Is that ply still in there?"

"It's on top of the tape."

"Are you going to use it?"

"No."

"You might as well use it if it's in there."

"It'll do nothing on the front edge," she says. "I've been up on it. I've seen the front edge. If I put a board across the middle of that roof it stops one draught and lets two more in at the front and then I've spent the ply and it's still a shed."

"Then you'll want felt."

"I'll want felt."

"When?"

---

And there it is, and it comes out of her sister without any weight on it at all, and it is the fourth time in a year that somebody in this borough has asked a question like that to somebody who cannot answer it, and it is a good question and it is the exact question.

She looks at the wall.

"In the spring," she says.

"Right."

"Aye."

"Right."

---

She does not put a day on it. She does not put a week on it. She does not open a calendar, because she has never in her life had a list on a wall and she is not starting one in the last week of December with four inches of tape and a bit of ply in a drawer.

And she knows exactly what that means, because she has watched her sister cross the first line of a pad off twice since March and never once properly, and she has a hammer and a screwdriver and a roll of tape in the same drawer that have not been out since September, and she has a shed with a draught off the top of it that a man from a coal yard has now told her about twice, in November and on the thirteenth.

"In the spring," she says again, to nobody, and washes the cups.

---

She goes out into the yard after her sister has gone to see her off and stands there in the dark with the back door shut true behind her.

The outside light over that back door is off. It is a Tuesday, so that is nothing.

What she does in that yard on a Tuesday is not what she does in it on a Saturday morning, and she knows that, and she stands there anyway.

On Saturday morning she puts her coat on over her nightdress and goes out and switches that light on and stands and looks at it and switches it off again. She has done it since the spring. She did not do it on Saturday.

And the reason she did not do it on Saturday is not that she forgot, and she has turned that over twice in the yard and she has not got a better reason than the one she already had, which is this: that she was stood in her own yard in the dark before there was any light looking for a light over a gate at the end of that road, and there was one, and it was on, and she looked at it for a while, and she did not go up the road to see what was making it.

There is a woman in a house at the end of that road who has never opened her front door to a lamplight man and did on Friday, and that woman is owed a knock by somebody, and it was not going to be her standing in a dark yard on a Saturday looking at a light from thirty yards away.

So she went in and shut a door that was already shut and the shed went on doing her roof in.

---

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES HAS TOLD HER SISTER TWO THINGS ABOUT THE END OF THAT ROAD AND NOT ONE OF THEM ABOUT THE LADDER, HAS BEEN ASKED ONCE SINCE FRIDAY WHO THE LADDER WAS FROM AND HAS SAID SHE DOES NOT KNOW AND THAT IS TRUE, HAS WORKED OUT WHAT A FELT EDGE WOULD TAKE IN THREE ITEMS ON THE BACK OF A BIT OF PAPER, HAS SAID *IN THE SPRING* TWICE AND PUT NO DAY ON IT, AND HER SHED IS STILL DOING HER ROOF IN.**
