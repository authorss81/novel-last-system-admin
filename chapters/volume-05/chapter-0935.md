# Chapter 0935 — Into The Cut Socket

Monday the twenty-sixth of August 2024, at the cut socket in the plinth of the back wall at number twelve with the sun off the wall. Into a cut socket.

**SHE HAS A SQUARE SOCKET CUT INTO THE STONE PLINTH AT THE BACK OF NUMBER TWELVE AND SHE MEANS TO DRIVE THE HAND IN WITH THE FINGERS SHUT TOGETHER AND THE THUMB LAID ON THE STONE ALONGSIDE IT, AND THEN TURN THE HAND ROUND ON ITS OWN AXIS INSIDE THE HOLE, TO FIND OUT HOW FAR THE SOCKET GOES AND WHAT STANDS IN THE FLOOR OF IT.**

The plinth was dressed and laid in the week the house went up, and the socket was opened in it the same week, and both of those are now seventy-odd years behind the front door. The wall above has had two men to it since, and neither of them went near the plinth. The socket has not been touched since the pipe came out of it.

It is a course of sandstone about a foot high, dressed on the face and run the whole length of the back of the house under the render, with a socket taken out of it every eight feet or so where the rainwater pipes went down into the ground. She wants the socket, which is the square hole that two inches of pipe once passed through.

A pipe let into a plinth is bedded and never pointed, because a lime joint set hard against iron eats the iron. So the fingers go in shut and the thumb lies along the near lip. The arris at the socket's mouth is blunt where the bolster opened it and has been blunter still since, and the stone inside is cool and gritty and dry to whatever depth a hand can measure.

Then the hand turns a quarter on its own axis inside the hole, and the fingers come up against the far wall, which is dressed stone and not ground. The socket is four inches deep at the middle of each face and shallower at the two corners, because a square hole opened with a bolster is never cut to one depth and nobody corrected it.

In the floor of it stands a ring of lead gone as hard as the stone round it, poured round the pipe while the pipe was in and left to take the pipe's shape when the pipe was finally cut out. Inside the ring the lead has gone down about the depth of a thumbnail and kept the round of the pipe anyway, so that the floor of that socket carries the outline of a thing that was taken away from it sixty years before the paint on the sill went matt.

---

Lead round an iron pipe is the older way and the only way that really works, and it is a good deal older than cement and a good deal better than it. A pipe bedded in lime is a pipe with a joint harder than the pipe itself, and iron in a hard joint goes quicker than iron anywhere else, because the rust has nowhere of its own to go.

The lead was poured and not hammered, and the man who poured it stood at that plinth with a ladle and a brazier and about half a pound of metal for every socket on the house. It goes in hot enough to take the pipe's round cleanly, and it shrinks as it cools, which is the whole of why it takes the pipe off the iron and grips it rather than merely sitting round it.

When the pipe was cut out and the lead left behind, the ring dried on the pipe's shape and the pipe's shape is a hole in it. A ring that dries on a pipe it no longer has is a record, and it is as good a record of a two-inch pipe as anybody could draw to-day.

A plinth course is a separate thing from the wall above it and it does the wall's work differently. The wall above is brick and it stands on the plinth; the plinth is stone and it stands on the ground. Everything that goes wrong at the bottom of a wall goes wrong at that one course, and a socket is the one place in the course where anybody has been at it with a tool.

The socket was opened from the outside face with a bolster and a cold chisel, and the mason stood with the back to the wall and worked downwards at it, which is the wrong way to open a socket that has to take a pipe square and the wrong way to finish one as well. A square socket cut downwards has its arris at the top lifted and its arris at the bottom crushed, and a pipe going through one has to be dressed afterwards to make it fit at all.

A man cutting a socket works downwards because the bolster wants to be driven and a sledge wants to fall, and the second lump of the chisel is put where the hand can get at it. Cutting upwards puts the hand under the chisel, and the only way to manage that is to work on your knees with the arm turned back, and even then the last inch is all chisel and no bolster at all.

The socket at number twelve has the marks of both. There is a line across its floor about halfway down where the mason's bolster finished and the cold chisel began, and above that line the walls of the hole are square, and below it they are slightly bell-mouthed where the chisel was worked from one side. Nobody corrected it, because a pipe going through a bell-mouthed hole is easier to get at and nobody ever looks at the inside of one.

---

The arris at the top of that mouth is lifted by about a millimetre and the arris at the bottom is crushed flat, and seventy years of rain have taken the coin off again and left the whole edge blunt. It is blunt all the way round now, which is what a top arris becomes once the water has stopped being able to get a purchase on it.

The lead in the floor of that socket has not moved and is not going to. Lead this old does not creep the way soft metals creep. It goes hard, and then it goes quiet, and then one day in a century or two somebody puts a ladder against that wall and finds it has shifted a hair at one point along the ring and nowhere else.

Under the ring is the socket's own floor, which is sandstone, and the sandstone is bedded in a mortar of lime and sand gone hard and is about as firm as the stone above it. Under the plinth course the ground is the ground the whole house stands on, and it has not been dug out from under that wall in living memory.

So the hand finds a floor, and what is on the floor, and the far wall of the socket, and a depth, and nothing else whatever. No nest in it, no water, no metal past the ring, and nothing in the arrangement that wants doing about. The socket is a hole that a thing was in once and is not in now.

She takes the hand out and puts it down at her side, and the grit of seventy years comes off it dry. The ring of lead in the floor has the print of nothing on it except a socket's own shape, and the print of the pipe that was lifted out of it is the only writing in that hole that anybody ever put there.

Where a pipe is bedded in lead and then cut off flush, the lead is normally dressed back and the ring is lost. This one was not dressed back, because the man who cut the pipe was not the man who poured the lead and the second man took the view that the ring was doing no harm where it stood. A dressed-back ring is a neat job and an undressed one is a socket that still has the pipe's size in it.

Her sister comes out through the porch door with nothing in either hand and goes away down the front path towards the gate, and the porch door stands open behind her the whole length of that path. She goes past the plinth at the pace she came at, and the wall gets nothing.

A gate at the end of the lane went down that path twice over, once as itself and once as a question about whether somebody has put it back a foot from the kerb, and neither end of that touched the plinth or the square hole cut in it.

The socket keeps its four inches and its ring of lead, and a hand will go into it as many times as she likes and come out on the same dressed stone each time, which is the least complicated thing about it and the only thing there is, lest somebody come along one day and take it for a hole that wants something putting back in it.
