# Chapter 0307 — A Fire Blanket

Saturday the eighteenth of March 2017, from about ten past seven in the evening, a first-floor landing in a building by the water in Tideglass.

**A MAN CAME INTO THAT BUILDING TO LOOK AT A CUPBOARD AND THE CUPBOARD HAD A WOOL FIRE BLANKET AND A FOOT OF FLAT BAR IN IT, AND THE BLANKET WENT HALFWAY ALONG THAT LANDING AND STOPPED THERE, AND TWO PEOPLE SAID A DIFFERENT THING ABOUT IT AND BOTH OF THEM WERE RIGHT, AND IT IS STILL ON THE ARM OF A CHAIR.**

The cupboard is under the stairs on that first floor, past the fire door, and it is a built-in one with a flat door and a knob on it and no lock, and inside it on the top shelf there is a fire blanket in a plastic bag with a printed label on the bag, and under the bag on the same shelf there was a length of flat bar about a foot long in a box of screws, and under that a coil of two-and-a-half inch cable ties and a coil of wire, and behind that a bucket.

The man in his thirties had come in at about ten past seven to look at a box on the landing for a job on the Monday and he saw the cupboard door open, and the cupboard door is not locked because there has never been a lock on it, and there is a hook on the inside of that door with a key on it, and the key is on the hook, and the key has been on that hook since the building went up and nobody in this borough has ever taken it off that hook to do anything with it.

He had the blanket out and the bar out and both of them on the landing floor by about twenty past seven, and the woman in the room next door along, counting past the fire door, whose own door stands open three inches with a chair against it, came out into the landing because she had heard the door of the cupboard and she does not sleep with that door shut.

The blanket is about a metre square and it is wool and it has been in that bag since the building went up or near enough, and it weighs nothing for its size. The bar is a foot of flat steel, an inch and a half by an eighth, and it is cold, and it is not a thing you can get a strap round.

He had put the bar in the bag he came in with and it went in about four inches and stopped, and he stood on that landing holding it and the bag with his hands out to either side like a man holding two heavy things he did not want to drop.

"I can't get that in this," he said. "I'd have to come back with the big bag off the van, and I'd have to come back at a time when the van's here, and I know when the van's here because I put it there."

The woman in the room next door along looked at the bar and then at the landing.

"You could put that up the sleeve of a coat and carry it in your hand like a bottle," she said.

"I could," said the man in his thirties, "and then I've got a bag with a fire blanket in it and a bar in my hand, and I'm going down that outside stair, and the light on that stair is about what it is."

She looked at the bar for a bit.

"You could leave the bar," said the woman in the room next door along. "That's a cupboard. If you go up the street and ask, there's a dozen of those bars in a dozen of those yards behind a dozen of shops, and every one of them's got a hole in the middle of it where a guy cut it and went round again. The bar is not what's in that cupboard. The blanket's what's in that cupboard, and there's one of those on this landing and none at the bottom, and that's the one you want to take."

"That's not a reason to leave it for somebody else to find."

"It's the best reason there is in this building," said the woman in the room next door along. "If you take the bar and leave the blanket, then one day somebody will come up that stair to be let in and there'll be a fire blanket in a cupboard with nothing in it but a coil of cable ties, and that's a thing somebody could make a noise about. If you take the blanket and leave the bar, there's a bar in a cupboard and no fire blanket, and every building in this town is short of one of them or the other and nobody's ever said a word about any of it."

He put the bar down on the landing floor and stood up straight.

"You're saying take the one that's harder to explain."

"I'm saying I'm not having this conversation at half past seven on a Saturday about a foot of steel," said the woman in the room next door along. "I'm on my own next door and I do not want a cupboard on this landing emptied out around my door on a Saturday night. And I can see the light's bad on that stair and you've said so yourself. And if you say you'll come back for it then you'll not come back for it, and if you say you won't then we're both stood here agreeing with each other about nothing."

He stood there.

"If I take the blanket," he said, "you've got to know I've taken it."

"You've taken it," said the woman in the room next door along. "I'm not going to ask you to take it and then pretend afterwards that I hadn't noticed you taking it, and I'm not going to say it's all right when it isn't a thing I can say all right to, and I'm not going to put my name to a bar of steel for the rest of my life. It's gone. That's the end of it."

---

The man who works nights came through the ground-floor passage at about a quarter to nine on his way in and found that street door shut on the same wedge of timber in the corner that it is shut on now, sitting a good four inches clear of the plaster, and came up.

He did not say anything about a fire blanket to anybody and did not know there was one and would not have wanted to.

There was a light burning in the room off the far end of that corridor and a bag sitting on the bed in it, and the woman of about fifty-five was in there, and the man in his thirties came along that corridor with a fire blanket in a plastic bag under his arm and did not knock on her door, and the reason he did not knock on her door is that he had been in her passage at about ten to seven one morning in February with a screwdriver and a bag of plaster and she had been in her dressing gown at that hour and the two of them had said about nine words between them, and he was not going to put a man on that landing at half past eight on a Saturday night to be handed a box.

The woman in the room next door along said, at about twenty to eight, "There's a woman at the end of this landing I could ask."

"You could," said the man in his thirties.

"And you're not going to."

"I'm not going to," said the man in his thirties, "and that's not because of the box."

He went down that stair with the fire blanket in a plastic bag and the woman in the room next door along shut her own door until it stood open three inches again, and put the chair back against that door.

---

On the Monday at about half past eight in the morning, the man in his forties came into that building to look at the fire door and the frame round it, and the man in his thirties was in the passage and had come in on his way to the low end of Peverell Street.

The flat door of that cupboard is set back about nine inches in a recess and the fire door is hung on the other side of that landing with a bar across it, and the bar comes out about four foot and stops, and there is nine inches of nothing between the end of that bar and the edge of the cupboard door, and you cannot get it to.

"That's a fire door with a bar on it that can't reach," said the man in his forties.

"That's a fire door with a bar on it that can't reach," said the man in his thirties, "and that key's on a hook inside the cupboard it's supposed to be locking, and if you want to know the rest of it, this cupboard has never had a lock on it and never has had anybody in this building who comes to look at it, and the round is done by a firm from over the county border and they come twice a year and write a bit in a book."

"Somebody could make a noise about that," said the man in his forties.

"Two of us could make a noise about that," said the man in his thirties, "and I am not one of them, and you're not either, and we'll both be at that bar in about nine years if nothing's been done to it by then."

The man in his forties did not take the bar off. He put his hand on it and pushed it and watched it come to a stop in the air, and said, "Right," and went down the stairs to the shop he was going to.

**AND A WOOL FIRE BLANKET IS ROLLED UP AND PUT ON THE ARM OF THE CHAIR AGAINST A DOOR THAT STANDS OPEN THREE INCHES UPSTAIRS FROM THAT PASSAGE, AND THE KEY IS STILL ON ITS HOOK, AND A MAN PUT HIS HAND ON A BAR AND WATCHED IT STOP IN THE AIR.**
