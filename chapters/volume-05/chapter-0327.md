# Chapter 0327 — Two Hands on a Landing

Tuesday the sixth of June 2017, from about half past nine in the morning until about half past four in the afternoon, a first-floor landing in a building off the parade in Tideglass.

**A SASH WINDOW WOULD NOT GO UP AND A DOOR WOULD NOT GO BACK ON THEIR HINGES ON THE SAME LANDING ON THE SAME MORNING, AND BOTH OF THEM WANTED SOMEBODY TO HOLD SOMETHING, AND THE MAN WHO LIVES BELOW THAT LANDING CAME OUT OF HIS FLAT TWICE AND WENT BACK IN TWICE, AND NEITHER PIECE OF WORK IS DONE.**

The building is one of nine off that parade and it goes up three floors, and the first floor is a landing with a step up into each of two flat doors, a self-closer at the foot of that flight, and a window on the outside wall at the end of it about four foot off the floor.

The man in his thirties was on that landing at half past nine with the new sash in, because a window out of a building off a parade is the one thing anybody in that parade will let a man who knows what he is talking about come in and do.

The old one had been out since the Thursday and had gone down the alley to a man who wanted a frame for a shed. The new one was in and beaded and the top sash would not go.

It is not a rotten window. It is a good window that has been painted shut about four times by people who did not know and could not be told, and the top sash is painted down on the outer stop of the bottom rail, and the bottom rail has swelled about an eighth of an inch into it, and when you push, the top corner of the sash goes out of the frame before the bottom rail will pass the stop.

He got a trestle up on the landing and a staging board across it and got himself up and put both hands under the head of that sash and lifted.

It came about half an inch and stopped.

He tried it four times. On the fourth he got his shoulder under it and lifted with his legs and it came an inch and the top left corner came out of its rebate about the width of a finger and he had to drop it back down onto the outer stop before it went further, because past that the whole thing is out of the square bay and it does not come back.

You cannot do that with a mallet and a piece of wood. You do it with somebody on a trestle holding the head of the sash down and level while you work the bottom rail back with a block plane and a chisel, because the moment you let the head go, the weight of a double-glazed unit comes down and the corner goes out.

He came off the trestle and stood in the middle of that landing and looked at it.

There is a man in the ground floor flat of that building and he has held the head of a sash for this man three times, in a house two streets away and once in a house off the Cleeve road, and he was given a cup of tea and he was not given anything else and he was not on anything then and he is not on anything now.

He came out of his flat at about ten with his shoes in his hand, on his own, and stood on that landing in his socks.

"I heard your frame going," he said.

"Aye," said the man in his thirties. "It's a top sash and it's painted shut and the bottom rail's swelled and I can't shift it."

"That's a two-man job," said the man in the ground floor flat.

"It is."

"You've not got anybody."

"I've not got anybody."

The man in the ground floor flat stood there in his socks with his shoes in his hand for a while.

"I'm on at four," he said. "And that's my front door that's got your trestle and your board across it, and that's a working morning I'm doing it in my own time, and I'm not stood on that trestle in front of that window for nothing at half past ten in the morning because I've done it three times and there's no fourth one coming after that."

"Nobody's asked you to do it for nothing," said the man in his thirties. "I said I can't do it. I haven't asked you. I said I can't do it alone, which is a different thing, and I'd be saying it to any man on that board."

"You'd be saying it to a man who's already come out of his flat in his socks," said the man in the ground floor flat. "That's not the same as you not asking me. You put a trestle up there at half past nine and you've been at that window for half an hour and there's a man coming up that stair at the end of it whether I like it or not, and if I stand in here and shut this door, then I'm a man who let a window go, and if I stand out here, then I'm on that trestle. You've made me a part of it by not asking."

"That is fair," said the man in his thirties.

"I'm going to shut this door," said the man in the ground floor flat.

He shut it, and it was not slammed, and the man in his thirties was on that landing with a window he cannot close and a trestle in the way of a door.

---

The woman in the middle flat of that building has been in and out of that flat for about ten years and has taken her own front door off its hinges twice, and the second time was on the Sunday because she was moving a wardrobe and it would not go past through the frame with the door on.

It was lying against the wall of that landing at about a quarter past twelve with the old paint marks on it and the hinges still screwed to the frame and about two inch of play in the top corner.

The man in his thirties was in there with his trestle in the way and the woman in the middle flat came up the stair with the ends of a wardrobe under her arms and then could not get them up the last four steps and stood there.

He put the trestle against the wall and took one end.

"That door's off its fixings," he said, looking at it on the way past.

"It is."

"Whose was it."

"Mine," said the woman in the middle flat. "I took it off Sunday to get a wardrobe up and I can't get it back on."

He got the board down off the trestle and knelt at the frame and put his eye on the top hinge.

"Those screws are into a frame that has dropped at that corner," he said. "Somebody has put something heavy on that lintel over the years and the corner has come down about half an inch, and every one of those screws has gone into air on the top left one. That's why it spins. And the reason you can't hang it back is that if you get it up and screw it, it will go up now, and in a month the corner will be down again and it will be back on the floor, and it will have been up the whole time in between and you will have been hanging things off that door."

"Can you do it."

"I can pack that corner and ease that hinge and take up the slack in the frame, and that's a job on my own, and it takes a morning," said the man in his thirties. "What it wants is a second pair of hands for about twenty minutes while I take the slack out, because I cannot hold a door square against a frame that wants to close and take the slack out of the same hinge at the same time. You are not that second pair of hands."

"I am not standing in that hallway holding a door while a man works a chisel about two inch from my fingers," said the woman in the middle flat.

"You are not anything in that job," said the man in his thirties. "It is not you I am short of. It is him."

He said that with his thumb at the wall of that building, downwards, at the ground floor.

The woman in the middle flat looked at the door on the floor.

"He's held a window for you before," she said.

"Three times."

"And he said no just now."

"He said no before I said anything, and he was right, and I would have said the same."

"Then who is it you're waiting for," said the woman in the middle flat, "because there's nobody else in this building."

The man in his thirties put his bag down on the landing and said nothing for about a minute.

"There isn't," he said. "That's the whole of it. I do a trade on my own, and a top sash is a two-man job and a dropped corner on a frame is a two-man job, and in this town I have got one man who will hold a thing for me for a cup of tea and I have never once asked him to hold a thing for me on a day when he had to be somewhere else at ten o'clock, and I am not going to start now, because the first time I do it is the last time he ever picks up a telephone."

---

The woman who works in a shop was in that parade at about half past four and he came by the front of it with his bag and told her, standing in the doorway, because he does that.

She listened to the whole of it and then she said, "How many."

"How many what."

"How many of them have stood on that landing."

"That one," said the man in his thirties. "The window and the door, that's two on one morning, and they're both stood on that landing at this minute, and I've had a look at it and the door's on the floor and the window's an inch out of its rebate with a trestle across that stair."

"That is not what I asked you."

"I know what you asked me," said the man in his thirties. "And I have not counted them, because I have never counted anything in this town, and neither has anybody else, and that is not modesty. It is that nobody has ever stood on a landing and been handed a pencil. There's nothing to count them on and nobody to count them for, and the day somebody counts them is the day this becomes a thing you can be shown."

"So it could be dozens."

"It could be dozens," said the man in his thirties, "or it could be four, and I could not tell you which, and neither could the man who lives on the ground floor of that building, and that is the thing I cannot get past at the end of a day like this one."

The woman who works in a shop looked at the floor of her shop for a bit.

"I don't want to know that," she said. "You'd think I do because I've asked you twice. I don't. I asked because a man can stand in a doorway for five minutes and say a number, and you stood there and didn't, and now it's in my head instead of in yours, and I have a shop to shut."

The man in his thirties went back at about half past four and took his trestle off that stair and left it on the landing against the wall, and the door is still on the floor of that landing with the paint marks on it and the wardrobe is in the front room of that flat with its foot through the wall, and at the top of that landing a new sash window has a top sash that will not go up and will not go down without being put back into its rebate by hand, which nobody in that building can do.

**A SASH WINDOW ON THAT LANDING IS AN INCH OUT OF ITS REBATE AND A FRONT DOOR IS LAYING ON THE LANDING WITH THE HINGES STILL SCREWED TO THE FRAME, AND A MAN IN A PAIR OF SOCKS PUT HIS SHOES ON BEHIND THAT DOOR AND WENT UP HIS OWN STAIRS.**
