# Chapter 0736 — The Third Chalk Mark

Thursday the twenty-third of June 2022, in this borough. He is in his own unit from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a run of six foot with a quarter inch in it and a man at the gate.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES LAYS HIS OWN TAPE ALONG A SIX FOOT RUN AND GETS A QUARTER INCH AS HE ALWAYS DOES, TELLS A MAN WHO HAS COME ABOUT THE GATES WHAT TRUE MEANS AND SAYS THE QUARTER INCH OUT LOUD TO A MAN WHO CAME TO THE GATE TO ASK FOR IT, FINDS THREE CHALK MARKS ON THE BACK OF ONE GATE AND THAT ONE OF THEM IS WRONG, RUBBED THAT ONE OUT WITH HIS THUMB AND LEFT THE OTHER TWO.**

He is a man in his early forties in his own unit on Thursday. A man who lives in the units comes to the gate in the middle of the morning and he speaks to him out there.

---

He is in the unit with the roller door up and the light off until he needs it, and the first thing he does is put his hand on the bench and walk it end to end without touching anything.

The shoebox is on the bench where it lives. The drawer under the bench is shut, and has been shut since November, and there is a brush in a bag in there that he has not looked at since he shut it.

The pencil is behind his ear. The scrap of scribing card is in his pocket with the twelve on one side and the room on the other, and it is not a record of anything, it is a thing he has carried so long that putting it in a drawer would feel like throwing a number away.

The oak gates are leaning along the back wall where they have leaned since the spring, chalk gone pale on them.

He does not go near the gates first. He goes to the run.

---

The run is a length of frame he made in the spring for the front of a unit two doors down, and it stands on trestles in his own place while he gets on with it, and it is proud over its six foot by near enough a quarter inch at the long side.

He lays the tape along the top of it at three points and looks at the tape at each point, and the reading is the same at the hanging end and the same in the middle and the same at the shutting end, and it is a quarter inch.

He has got a quarter inch before. He has got a quarter inch in March and he had it in the winter. He has never got anything else and he has never once got less.

He puts the tape away and stands looking at the run and then goes and gets the pencil from behind his ear and puts it on the bench.

He does not write anything. There is a shoebox on the bench and there is a scrap in his pocket and there is a run in front of him, and there is no number on anything that says what it was in March.

---

The man who lives in the units comes at about the middle of the morning on foot, with his hands in his pockets, and stands at the gate and looks in at the gates leaning along the wall.

"You are not doing roofs," he says.

"I am not doing roofs," he says.

"You said that to me in the yard in March."

"I said it in the yard in March and I have not got better at it since," he says. "What do you want."

"I want to know if I could hang one of those," the man says. "I have got a frame at mine. It is for a gate about the width of a door. I have not got a gate and I have got the frame."

The man in the early forties does not answer at once. He comes out into the doorway and stands with his arm along the frame of the roller door.

"How is your frame," he says.

"Straight, near enough. It came off a house that is gone."

"How near enough."

"It is a frame," the man says. "Frames are straight. That is the whole of a frame."

"Come and look at it," he says.

---

They walk the two doors down and the man in the early forties takes the tape out and lays it along the top of the man's frame and looks at it twice, and puts it away.

"It is out," he says.

"Out how much."

He feels for the tape and does not take it out again.

"A quarter inch," he says.

The man does not say anything for a second.

"You have said that twice," the man says.

"I have said it once," he says. "You said it back at me."

He says it again, to make the man hear it twice, and finds that he can do that, and that the reason he can do it is that the man has already heard it once. He has said the figure to the man at the bench opposite in a yard in the spring and he has said it to himself over this run more times than that. What is different about this is that the man at the gate came here to be told something and has gone away with a figure he will repeat to a builder.

"That is the run," he says. "Six foot. It is proud by that at the long side, and it has been proud by that since I made it, and I left it that way because it is not my frame to close up."

"What is true, then," the man says, "when you call a gate true."

"A gate is true when it will go on a frame that is straight," he says. "That is all it means. It means the joints are cut so the leaves shut square and the stiles are the same length, and it means nothing about a quarter inch in a run that is not mine."

"And if my frame is out a quarter inch."

"Then your frame is out a quarter inch," he says, "and a true gate will sit proud at one end on it, the same as any gate would."

---

They walk back to the unit and the man stands in the doorway again and looks at the gates along the back wall.

"Three marks on the back of that one," he says. "What are those."

"Those are the sizes," he says. "For me. So I know which way round they go."

"And the three."

"The three is wrong."

He had not expected to say that and he said it before he had a reason.

"The three is the width across the middle," he says. "It is a foot and it is not, it is eleven inches and it is the one I chalked in a hurry in February and I never came back to it. It is wrong by an inch and it has been wrong since February and there is one gate hanging off it."

"Are you going to do it up again."

"No," he says. "The frame is on the ground at my customer's and it has been since March, and it will be there in the autumn, and the gates go on it in the autumn when there are two of us. Until then the eleven is the number I work to and the one foot is on the back of a gate in a cold unit."

He put his thumb on the one foot mark and rubbed it out. It took a fair bit of rubbing because it had been on there through two wet months, and the chalk came off in a grey smear on the ball of his thumb and left the oak clean and a shade darker than the rest of it.

"That is two marks right and one mark that used to be wrong and is now nothing," he says. "The gate is exactly as good as it was at the middle of February and exactly as bad. I have only stopped being able to get it wrong a second time, and there is nobody in this borough who would have seen a thing in it either way except me."

---

The man looked at the clean patch on the oak for a while.

"Could I have that one," he says. "The one with the marks on. If I can get somebody to hang it."

"You can have the frame's own advice for nothing," he says. "You cannot have a gate out of this unit between now and the autumn, because when I hang them I want both leaves and the pair closes as a pair, and one gate on its own in somebody else's yard is a thing I would be thinking about instead of work."

"That is a no."

"That is a no for the gates," he says. "The measuring is not a no."

He went back in, put the tape in the van where it goes, and stood at the bench with the pencil still lying on it where he had put it.

He picked the pencil up and put it back behind his ear. The quarter inch and the eleven inches were in the room in the words that had been said in the doorway and in the twelve on a scrap of card in his pocket, and nowhere else, and the clean patch on the back of one gate was the only place in this unit where a figure had ever been taken off anything.
