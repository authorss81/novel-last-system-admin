# Chapter 0737 — The Box That Moves

Monday the twenty-seventh of June 2022, in this borough. She is in her own lock-up from first light until dark, and the whole of it is a box under rags that moves half an inch when a knee goes under it.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FORTIES PUTS A KNEE UNDER A BOX THAT HAS SAT BEHIND HER OWN BENCH SINCE OCTOBER AND FEELS THE CONTENTS OF IT SHIFT AND SETTLE, LEAVES THE LID WITH ITS CORNER STANDING AN INCH AND DOES NOT OPEN IT, TELLS THE MAN WITH KEYS IT IS NOT HEAVY AND IS NOT GOING TO BE FOUND OUT, RULES TWO MORE LINES ON THE INVOICE PAD AND WRITES NOTHING ON EITHER, AND PUSHES THE RAGS FLAT SO THAT THE CORNER STANDS WHERE SHE CAN SEE IT.**

She is a woman in her late forties in her own lock-up on Monday. The man with keys comes to shut the place in the evening and she speaks to him out there.

---

The van is outside with the back open and the boards and the bolster in under the bench, shut, where they go, and the rag bag is tied on the boards and the floor is swept.

Behind the bench in the dark there is a box under rags, and the box has been in that corner since October and has not been moved by her hand or anybody else's.

She is not going near it at the start of the day. She works round it, the way she works round it, lifting the rag that is on top of the corner of it and setting that rag down again a hand's width further along.

At about the middle of the morning she wants the other rag, the one underneath, and instead of lifting everything off she goes down on one knee on the floor of the lock-up and puts the flat of her other hand under the near end of the box and lifts.

The box comes up about an inch and inside it something moves.

It is not a small movement. It is a whole thing going across a box and settling at the other end and stopping, and it makes a noise like a bag of sand put down on boards, and she has it back on the floor before she has decided anything.

She kneels there with her hand still under the corner of it.

---

The lid is not nailed. She has never seen it nailed. It is a lid that goes on and is kept on by its own weight and a strip of gummed paper across the middle of the join, and the gummed paper has let go along one whole length and the corner at the far end is standing up about an inch, and there is a dark line of air along that edge.

She does not put her hand on the lid.

She gets her fingers under the corner instead and lift it the quarter of an inch further and let it go, and the thing inside moves again and comes to rest in a different place from the one it was in before.

Whatever is in there is not packed. Whatever is in there is loose and it has been loose for eight months and it has been finding its own bottom.

She closes the corner down with two fingers so that it is where it was and not higher, and she gets up off her knee, and her knee has the grit of the floor in it and she brushes that down before she touches anything else.

---

She rules two lines on the invoice pad at the bench in the afternoon.

The pad has a hall line on it with nothing under it, and it has a cupboard line with a day on it, and the day is gone now and the room is gone with it and she has been paid and it is written in her own book at home and not here, because card work stays in the van.

She rules two more lines under the cupboard one. There is nothing to go on them and she does not put anything on them, and she does not rule them badly, and they are as straight as the other three.

Four lines on a pad and one of them with a thing on it, and the other three are lines of their own with no reason on them that anybody else would understand.

She puts the pad back in the door pocket of the van and comes back in and does not take it out again.

---

The man with keys comes at the end of the day to shut the lock-up, which is his job and has been since before she started.

He has the ring on him with the padlock for the door on it and a couple of keys he has never offered to anybody and never will.

He looks in past her at the box in the corner behind the bench, which he has looked in past at a hundred and something ends of days.

"What is under that," he says.

"A box," she says.

"That is not what I asked."

"It has a number on it," she says, "and the number is mine, and I have read it twice and I have not written it anywhere."

He puts the key in the door and stops with it half turned.

"Is it heavy."

"I can lift one end of it with a knee," she says. "That is not the same as heavy. That is how I know what is in it is not a stone."

"And how do you know what is in it."

She puts her hand on the bench and does not turn round for a moment.

"I lifted it," she says. "There is something loose in there and it went across the box when I lifted one end. And I am not going to find out what it is."

---

He finishes turning the key and does not go out yet.

"Eight months," he says, "with something loose in a box in a cold corner, and it will get in under the lid and go damp, and then it is not eight months, it is nothing."

"I know it will," she says.

"Then open it and take the things out and put them somewhere dry."

"No," she says.

He looks at her.

"You have opened it," he says. "You have had your knee under one end of it. There is nothing in that that says no."

"I have not opened it," she says. "I have moved it, which is different, and I have moved it once and I am not going to move it again, and if you had gone out of that door tonight and come back in a week you would not know I had, and that is the reason I did it and not any other reason."

He stands in the doorway with the ring in his hand.

"You are going to be sitting on a wet box by the winter," he says.

"Probably," she says.

---

She waits for the gate and then goes back in and does the one thing she has been thinking about since the middle of the morning, which is not opening it.

She takes the two top rags off the box and lays them out flat on the concrete, the way they were, and then she puts the corner rag back down over the top of it and presses it flat along both sides with the flats of her hands until the air is out of it and the shape underneath is the shape of a box and not the shape of a lid standing open.

Then she lifts that one rag at the far corner, only that one, and folds it back on itself so that the corner of the lid stands proud of the rags in the light from the bulb above the bench.

She can see from where she is standing which way the lid folds over on, and which side the gummed paper is across, and that the paper is gone along one whole length and not at the join.

That is all she has got for eight months and a knee, and she could have had the rest of it any day since October, and she takes the rag back up and puts it down flat over the corner again and pats it once, and shuts the lock-up and goes out to the van.

The bulb goes off behind her and the box is in the dark again, in the corner, with a lid that is not nailed and a corner she knows about.
