# Chapter 0312 — A Saucepan for the Man Who Works Nights

Saturday the eighth of April 2017, from about half past five in the afternoon, a hall off a parade in Tideglass.

**A MAN CARRYING A SAUCEPAN HE FOUND IN A YARD CAME INTO THAT HALL AND PUT IT ON A TABLE AND SAID IT WAS FOR THE MAN WHO WORKS NIGHTS AND WENT HOME, AND THE WOMAN WHO TOOK IT IN WAS NOT THE MAN IT WAS FOR, AND IT WAS USED ONCE ON A RING IN A KITCHEN AT THE BACK OF THAT PARADE AND PUT ON A SECOND SHELF, AND NOBODY IN EITHER PLACE WAS TOLD ANYTHING.**

It came out of a yard behind a row where a skip was standing open on a skip lorry, and the man with the van had been in that yard for about an hour moving a fridge out of a passage that was being done up, and the saucepan was standing on its side by the skip with about nine inches of frozen water in the bottom of it, and there was no lid anywhere near it and there was not one in the house it had come out of either.

He put the water out in the yard and picked it up and turned it over and looked at the underside of it.

There is a code stamped into the base of a pan like that, a letter and six figures, and a date, and the date said twenty sixteen, and the six figures are not anybody's name and are not a postcode and are not a telephone number, and there is no maker's name on it anywhere, only the stamp.

It is a saucepan about eight inches across and four deep with a handle that has a rivet through it about a third of the way down and a bit of black it has never properly come out of.

He put it in the back of the van on the boards with the freezer bags and came up the parade at about half past five because he knew one thing about the man who works nights, which is that he was in a coal line at the end of Marsh Lane in January and took nothing out of it, and the coal line is not where you find a man at half past five on a Saturday, and the hall off that parade is.

---

There were five of them in there and the meter was on and the urn was on.

The woman who runs that shop had a table out of the way and a folding one against the wall, and the man in his forties was at the end of the long table with a bit of paper folded into the pocket of his coat, and the man from the parade was by the fire that is not lit with his hands round a cup, and the woman of about fifty-five was in early with her coat on the back of the chair.

The man with the van came in with rain on the shoulders of his coat and stood by the counter, and he was let in, and he stood there for about four minutes with the pan in his hand and nobody offered him a cup and he did not ask for one.

"Which of you's the man that works nights," he said.

"None of us," said the woman who works in a shop, who had come in off the parade about ten minutes before him. "He isn't in. He comes in about half past ten."

"Then who's this for."

"You said it was for him."

"It is for him," said the man with the van. "It's off a skip in a yard behind a row of them and there's no lid on it and it's got a code in the bottom of it and the code's no use to anybody, and I know the one thing about him which is that he was stood in a coal line in January and didn't take anything out of it, and he's the only man in this town I've heard of that stood in a line like that and went away with nothing, so I thought of him."

He put the saucepan on the table at the end nearest the door, the right way up, with the handle towards the middle of the table.

"Twenty sixteen is stamped in the bottom of it," he said. "That's the only thing on it worth knowing."

Then he went out into the rain and shut the door, and the lamp in that porch came on behind him and put its circle on the matting and died again a few minutes later, when the lamp at the corner outside came up over the door and did what it does.

Nobody in that room said anything about a saucepan. The man from the parade said that the tea in that hall has been going cold on him in about four minutes a week since the summer and that he could not see how anybody drank it, which he had said before. The man in his forties said that the meter in that porch would need seeing to before the summer.

The man who works nights came in at about twenty past ten that night with his coat on, the way he does, and the woman who works in a shop had not put the pan in the cupboard with the urn and had not put it in the kitchen and had left it where it was, on the end of the table by the door where a man had put it.

"That's come for you," she said, and nodded at it.

The man who works nights looked at it for a second. He did not touch it.

"There's no name on it."

"You never gave him your name," said the woman who works in a shop. "He asked which of us you were and I said none of us, and he put it down and went."

He took it over then, and turned it over, and looked at the base of it the way anybody looks at the base of a pan, and read the stamp without saying it out loud.

"Aye," he said. "Well."

---

He had a ring at the back of the kitchen at the back of that parade and no more than that, and he was in at about ten to midnight with the pan and about a third of a tin of something out of the tin, and he heated it and he ate it out of the pan standing up, and he washed it out with the hot tap and dried it on the cloth and put it on the second shelf of the cupboard under the sink with the lids, at the back, behind the two lids that do not go with anything.

The kitchen at the back of that parade is a working kitchen and the person who cooks in it is a man who is in at about ten at night most nights of the week and the cupboard under that sink is where the lids go, and he put a pan at the back of it because it had no lid and because it was not his and because a man does not leave a thing that is not his on a table in a room with nine people in it.

He told nobody that he had used it. The woman who works in a shop went in on the Wednesday and the pan was not on the table by the door and she did not ask where it had gone, and the man with the van was at the end of Marsh Lane on the Thursday with a tarpaulin over an empty back and nobody told him anything about a saucepan in a cupboard in a kitchen at the back of a parade.

**AND A SAUCEPAN WITH A CODE STAMPED IN THE BOTTOM OF IT IS ON THE SECOND SHELF OF A CUPBOARD UNDER A SINK BEHIND TWO LIDS THAT GO WITH NOTHING, AND THE MAN WHO PUT IT THERE USED IT ONCE, AND A DOOR IN THAT PARADE SHUT AT ABOUT TEN TO MIDNIGHT.**
