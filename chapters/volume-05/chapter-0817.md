# Chapter 0817 — Three Lengths At The Top End

Saturday the third of June 2023, along the boundary behind that lock-up from the time the shutter goes up to the time it comes down, and three lengths of paling that have been knocked out of line are the whole of that Saturday.

**THERE ARE THREE LENGTHS OF BOWED PALING ALONG THE BACK BOUNDARY OF THAT LOCK-UP THAT HAVE BEEN KNOCKED OUT OF LINE AND SHE MEANS TO PUT THEM BACK ON NEW POSTS, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS IN THE OFFICE WITH THE DOOR SHUT ALL DAY AND IS HEARD ONCE THROUGH IT, ASKING SOMEBODY A QUESTION ABOUT NEXT WEEK.**

That boundary is about thirty feet of palings on two rails, four feet high, along the back of that yard from the corner by the office end to the gate.

Three lengths of it are out of line.

They went out of line in the spring, all three at once, and there is nothing about that which needs a reason. A fence at the back of a yard takes the side of a lorry, the side of a trailer, the push of a barrow with one wheel, and the whole run has to be soft somewhere for any of it to move anything, and in the spring it was soft all along the bottom of it after a month of rain.

What moved is the three lengths. The posts they were on have gone round at the ground, the rails have dropped off them and the palings have knocked across each other where they overlapped, and they are now standing in a shallow bow out into the yard, about four inches proud of the line of the fence either side of them.

The palings themselves are bowed as well, and that is a different thing and it is the thing this Saturday is about.

They are bowed because the two end stiles of each length have spread. That is what a length of paling does when it is built and left and rained on for thirty years; the frame racks, the palings nailed across it keep the racked shape, and what comes off that boundary is not a flat board but a length of fence with a curve in its face.

The gate at that end is on a latch and a drop bolt and has been for years and it stays exactly where it is, because the run is set out from the corner and not from the gate, and setting a run out from the gate is how you end up with a fence that goes true at the gate and out of line at the corner where nobody can see it.

---

She starts at the middle of the three, because that is the one that is worst and because it has to come out before the two either side of it can go back.

She has a spade, a bolster, a lump hammer, a saw, a short length of spirit level, a straightedge, two pegs and about forty feet of line on a reel.

The first thing she does is take the whole run apart, and there is no way round that. A length of palings comes off a post when the post moves, and a post moves when the ground round it moves, and a post does not come out of a hole that has got gravel round the bottom of it.

She knocks the bottom rail off the first post with the back of the bolster and gets a saw under the palings and cuts them, and they come off in about nine pieces, and she breaks those down and puts them in the barrow, and then she has the post.

The bottom of that post is grey and soft for about four inches above the ground line and sound the whole way above that. She can get a thumbnail into it at the front and she can put a chisel into it and turn it.

She does not throw the post away. She cuts four inches off the bottom of it and keeps the rest, and a post with four inches of rotten wood taken off it is a perfectly good post for a rail and is not a post anybody would hang a length of palings on and walk into.

That happens five times over the morning and there are five posts in the barrow with their rotten ends cut off and their tops cut square, and the top five feet of that fence is made out of what used to be the bottom of that fence.

The new posts come out of a gap at the back of that yard where they have been stacked for a year with a bundle of rails on top of them.

They go in the same day, in the same holes, which are dug out and washed down and then filled back in.

The digging is the longest part of it and it is not the post that takes the time. It is the gravel.

Thirty feet of four-foot paling with rails on it weighs a great deal more than it looks, and every year the bottom of it collects the blown leaves and the grass that has got up through the ground and the grit that comes off the yard, and all of that goes in round the posts and packs itself hard enough in a wet winter to be part of the post.

She puts a bar in every hole and works it round before the post goes back in, and if the bar will not go down she digs it out by hand, and there are two holes where she does that and neither of them is one of the three she is here for.

The packing is the other half of it and it is the half people get wrong. Broken brick and the rubble out of that yard, broken down to nothing bigger than a fist, in layers, packed with the back of the spade, not hammered, because a post packed hard is a post that rocks the first time anybody leans on the fence.

She packs them in three layers and checks each one with a straightedge on the level before the next one goes in.

---

That is where the Saturday stops being a Saturday about a fence.

It is about half past the middle of the morning and she has the three new posts in and packed and the three bowed lengths standing against the office wall waiting to go back on, and she stands in the middle of that yard with a straightedge in one hand and looks at the run.

She puts a peg in the ground at the corner and a peg at the gate, and she runs the line between them, and she sights along it, and the run in front of her is out by about four inches at the middle where the three lengths are.

She takes the line out of the ground and puts it back in the middle of the three, and looks along that.

It is not the posts. The posts are new and they are in the holes the old ones were in and they are packed on broken brick and rubble out of that yard and they are straight, and she has a level on them and they are true with each other.

The bow is in the palings and it is in the three lengths and there is nothing she is going to do about it, because the bow is in the frame and the frame is thirty years old and the palings are nailed across it and they will come off it exactly the shape they went on.

So the question is not how to make that fence straight. It is where a fence that is not straight should have its not-straight part.

She tries both and it takes her twenty minutes.

The first time she sets the three lengths in the middle of the run. She hangs them on, checks them with a level and a straightedge, packs the posts, cuts the line, and then she goes to the gate and stands at it and looks down thirty feet of paling.

The bow is in the middle of what she is looking at, about fifteen feet away, and it is the only thing in that view that is not straight, and it stops the eye dead.

She undoes it and takes the two off again.

The second time she sets all three at the top end of the run, one length next to the corner and the other two along after it, and the twenty-seven feet between that corner and the gate goes on as it was, in a straight line, and she puts the line in from the gate and sights it and checks it with the straightedge from about eight feet back.

And the bow is not there.

It is at the top end, past the point where the eye stops taking in detail, and from the gate it is foreshortened down the run to nothing, and what you see from that gate is a fence that goes true from you to about twenty-seven feet and then goes on to the corner.

She leaves it there and does not touch it again for the rest of the day.

The rest of that Saturday is the run going back together and the run being ten feet better than it was in the morning for one reason and worse for another.

The three bowed lengths go on at the top end and they are on new posts and they are true with the corner and they are as good as three bowed lengths can be made.

The fourth length — the one at the middle of the run, the sound one — had to come off to get its post out. Its own post was not rotten, but it stands between two rotten ones and it could not be reached without taking the palings off it first.

She puts it back on the same line, on the same sound post, with the same rails.

It is the straightest thing on that whole boundary. It was always the straightest thing on that boundary. But it has been sitting in a run with four inches of bow four feet away from it and the eye had been using it as the reference, the way an eye will take a straight thing and measure everything else against it, and now there are three bowed lengths within eight feet of it instead of four inches.

It has made itself worse.

She stands at the gate and looks at it for a while and there is nothing she can do to it that would help, because the only two repairs available are taking the three lengths out again, which is worse, or putting a bow in the sound length to match them, which is not a thing anybody would ever do.

That is the price of the twenty-seven feet and she knew it when she made the choice and she would make it again.

The barrow of palings and the five cut-off post ends go out through the gate and behind that yard, because there is a skip of somebody else's at the end of the row and it takes whatever is put in the top of it.

The shutter goes down at the end of the day and runs without a sound, as it does, and the four pallets are still on their bearers and the grit is still at the leg of the rack where it has been, and nobody in that building has been told anything about any of it.

The line of that fence comes true from the gate for twenty-seven feet.

It does not come true after that, and the three lengths where the not-straight part has been put are at the top end, out of the sight of anybody who opens that gate.
