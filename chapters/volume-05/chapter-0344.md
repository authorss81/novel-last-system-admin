# Chapter 0344 — A Day Like the Others

Wednesday the sixteenth of August 2017, from about ten to six in the morning until about half past four in the afternoon, a parade, a building by the water, a hall on Carrow Lane, and about four hundred yard of street, in Tideglass.

**NINE HUNDRED AND NINETY-FOUR DAYS WENT UNDER THE NINETEENTH ENTRY OF A REGISTER IN A BUILDING BY THE WATER AND THE LINE UNDER IT IS STILL BLANK, AND NOTHING HAPPENED ANYWHERE IN THIS BOROUGH ON THAT DAY THAT WAS NOT ALSO HAPPENING ON THE DAY BEFORE IT.**

It was warm at ten to six in the morning and the cloud came in off the water at about half past nine and stayed, and there was no rain in it, and it was the kind of day that gets described afterwards as a day nothing happened on.

A van was brought up that parade at about ten to six in the morning and parked half on the footway outside the second shop, and it was still there when the man who puts shutters up arrived with his ladder at about a quarter past six, and he could not get a ladder up against the shutter of the second shop with it there, so he did the third and the fourth and came back at about half past eight and did the second off the tail of it, and the van went at about ten past nine without anybody getting out of it.

A man came through that parade at about a quarter past six with a sack over his shoulder and stopped outside the shop that shuts early and stood there about two minutes doing nothing at all, and then went on up the parade with the sack still on his shoulder, and nobody in that street has said one word about those two minutes and nobody will.

The woman who works in a shop has a key to a flat above the three shops and went up the outside stair at about half past seven and came down at about twenty to eight and shut that stair door, and the man in a fleece was at the stop at the top of that parade at about the same time with a paper in his hand and neither of them was on the same side of the road and neither of them looked over.

The man in his thirties was in a yard at the top of Marsh Lane at about a quarter past eight when a man with a barrow came through the gate with four lengths of treated batten on it and put them down in the middle of the yard, about nine foot from where that yard keeps them, and went off up the road without saying where he was going. The man in his thirties left them where they were for about a minute, and then he carried them across the yard himself, one at a time, and put them against the wall where they go, and got on with the day.

Down the outside stair at about a quarter to nine went the woman of about fifty-five with two empty jugs, one hooked over the other, and she came up at about ten past ten with a jug in each hand and a kettle over the other, as she has done twice a day since the middle of June, and nobody offered her anything on the way up, and she did not ask anybody for anything.

The man who works nights was at the stop at the top of Marsh Lane at about ten with his coat on, and a bus came at about twenty past ten and stopped and went on, and it was not his bus, and he stood there for about half an hour afterwards.

The woman with a clipboard was on that street at about ten past ten with the board on her knee and a pencil behind one ear, and she was at the far end of what she had, and there were about two lines of room left on it, and she used one of them on a stand and did not use the other one.

The man in his sixties and the man in his forties were in the hall on Carrow Lane at about one in the afternoon and there was a table out and the urn on and no meeting until the Thursday.

"How many are there of those now," said the man in his sixties, standing up with his cup.

"Four," said the man in his forties, without stopping what he was doing with a cloth.

"I'd have said five."

"There are four," said the man in his forties, "and there have been four since January, and I have not added anything to that and I have not taken anything off it, and you have not been in this borough long enough to be the man that was here in January, and neither has anybody else who was not in this room in January."

The man in his sixties said that was fair enough and went and had some more tea, and neither of them said one word about the other three, and neither of them could have said what they were if the other one had asked.

The man from the parade was in a doorway on that parade at about two in the afternoon eating out of a paper bag with his fingers, and a man he has never spoken to went past and said good afternoon, and he said good afternoon back, and neither of them knew the other's name.

A woman came into that hall on Carrow Lane at about ten past two to ask whether it was open on Wednesday, and the man in his sixties told her it was, and she came in and sat at the back and read a newspaper for an hour and went at about a quarter past three, and he did not ask her for anything and she did not ask him for anything, and that is a thing that hall does about twice a week.

At about a quarter past four the man in his forties came up the parade from the low end and the man in a fleece was at the corner.

"Nobody in this town ever counted anything," said the man in a fleece, out of nothing, the way he says things. "Has anybody ever told you why it is four."

"No," said the man in his forties.

"Right," said the man in a fleece, and went on down the parade.

---

At about half past four the man in his forties let himself into the building by the water and went up the outside stair and along a first-floor corridor with a concrete floor and a painted skirt and went into the bay at the end of it and opened the door of the bay, which does not shut over its catch.

The book is on the second shelf up, and it is open at the nineteenth entry out of nineteen, and the nineteenth of them is a yard lamp that somebody wrote in as gone in about November two years ago against a heading kept back for things of that sort, and there is nothing under that heading and there has not been since. Below that entry the paper runs on for the width of two fingers and then gives up well before the bottom edge of that book, and nobody left that gap, and the book has got it.

He did not write anything on it. He put his thumb on the edge of the last page to hold it and worked the sum out in his head, the way a man works out a sum he has worked out before, and it came to nine hundred and ninety-four days, and the line under that entry is blank and it was blank on the Saturday morning in July and it is blank now, and it is the only thing in that building that has not moved while a door stood in an angle and a cupboard under a stair lost a blanket and a landing lost a gutter and a tap got put back across at the wrong end of a weekend.

He shut the door of that bay, which caught on its own frame the way it always has, and went back down that outside stair and left the street door on the latch behind him, and went up the parade, and the lamp outside the hall on Carrow Lane came on of its own accord at about a quarter to five and burned until about half past six in the morning.

**A LADDER WENT DOWN A PARADE AND UP AGAIN TWENTY FEET FURTHER ON, AND A JUG WENT DOWN FOUR TREADS AND CAME BACK UP FULL, AND A LINE UNDER THE NINETEENTH ENTRY OF A BOOK ON A SHELF IN A BAY AT THE END OF A CORRIDOR IS AS BLANK AS IT WAS ON THE MORNING OF THE SIXTEENTH OF AUGUST.**
