# Chapter 0662 — Where The Stool Came From

Monday the twenty-third of August 2021, in this borough. She is in from a late at ten past midnight and in her own kitchen until about half past one, and the whole of it is a woman of seventy-eight who cannot be thanked and a stool that has been in that kitchen since the Thursday of the twenty-seventh of May and is not in it at the end of the night.

**A WOMAN IN HER LATE FIFTIES COMES IN FROM A LATE AT TEN PAST MIDNIGHT AND IS TOLD BY HER SISTER, IN THE MIDDLE OF A FORM, WITHOUT BEING ASKED, THAT A MAN HAS BEEN AT NUMBER TWELVE, AND SHE WORKS OUT STANDING AT HER OWN SINK THAT THE MAN WHO HAS BEEN IN THAT PASSAGE IS THE MAN WHO BUILT THE STOOL SHE HAS BEEN EATING AT, AND SHE CANNOT SAY THANK YOU TO A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT WHO CANNOT BE TOLD ANYTHING, AND SHE CANNOT ASK HER SISTER ONE QUESTION, AND SHE CARRIES THE STOOL OUT OF HER KITCHEN AND PUTS IT IN HER FRONT ROOM.**

She is a woman in her late fifties. She is with her sister, who is at number ten. Nobody else is in that house or in that room.

---

She gets in at ten past midnight and the kitchen light is on and her sister is at the table with a cup in front of her going cold and a form in front of her and a pen in her hand.

And that is the whole of the reason there is somebody in that house at ten past midnight on a Monday, and neither of them ever mentions it.

Her sister knew what night it was. Her sister has known what night it is since they were girls. And she came round at about half past ten and put the heating on and made a cup of tea and sat down with a form from the council and has been working at it for an hour.

---

And the woman in her late fifties puts her bag down on the back of a chair and says nothing about that and puts the kettle on, and there are about four minutes of that kitchen.

And then her sister says, without looking up:

"Somebody's had a light put in."

---

And the woman in her late fifties says, "Where."

And her sister says, "Number twelve."

And the woman in her late fifties says, "Right."

---

And here is the whole of the first part of it, and it is about ten seconds long, and it is the reason the rest of the night goes the way it goes.

She has not asked. She said *where* and that is the only question anybody in that kitchen ever asks each other, and *where* is a place and a place is not a fact.

If she had said *who*, she would have had to be told a name.

---

And her sister goes on with the form for about a minute and a half.

And then she says, unasked, and she says it to the form:

"She couldn't have done that herself."

---

And about two seconds.

And the woman in her late fifties says, "No."

And her sister says, "Could she."

And the woman in her late fifties says, "Not that."

---

And here is what the woman in her late fifties is doing, standing at her own sink at about ten past midnight on the twenty-third of August 2021 with her hands in the water, and it takes her about three minutes, and she works it out in the same order a person reads a house, which is the order the people are in and not the order the facts arrived in.

There is one man who goes into that passage.

That is not a figure of speech and it is not a belief. She has never seen him. She has never been up that road in her life. But her sister is at number ten and her sister is at number twelve sometimes, and her sister has told her, in the middle of something else, on two occasions since April, about a man at number twelve, and there has only ever been the one.

He did the passage light. He put a bulb in in about four minutes on a Wednesday in June and he did not do the rest of the list, and her sister has never said why not and has never been asked.

---

And then, standing at the sink, she gets to the second half, and it is the second half that finishes her evening.

A woman of seventy-eight gave her a stool.

That was the Thursday of the twenty-seventh of May and it was on the step and the woman in her late fifties asked where it came from, and she was told, and what she was told was the only true thing anybody has said to her in this borough in a year.

*I had two and I couldn't stand the room with them in it.*

And then the woman of seventy-eight said, without being asked anything, that the stool was ash with oak rails, three out of four oak and one ash, and that the pale one is the ash and you can see it from where you are standing.

And she said: *He made it himself. On a bench in a unit off the industrial estate, on a Monday and a Tuesday.*

---

And the woman in her late fifties dries her hands and does not turn round.

And what she has just worked out, at about ten past midnight, at her own sink, is that there is a man.

Not a stranger's kindness. A man. In a unit off an industrial estate, on a Monday and a Tuesday, with a saw and a planer and a bench, who built a stool, and who has been in that passage five or six times since the May, and who has never once been mentioned in that house in front of the woman who has got his stool in her kitchen.

---

And she stands at the sink with the towel in her hand for about a minute.

And what is in that minute is not gratitude and it is not a plan. It is the thing that everybody in this borough has been walking about in since the Wednesday of the nineteenth of May and nobody has ever said.

A woman of seventy-eight has a green dining chair that a woman in her late forties put at her kitchen table on the last day of May, and a stool that a man made, and she has never had the two in the same room, and she told a woman in her late fifties about the chair and told her about the stool and did not say that the two of them had been in the same house.

And a woman in her late fifties has a bin and a hedge in common with her sister and a stool in her kitchen and has never asked where the stool came from, and got told the truth, and did not ask the next question.

---

And there is one more thing in that minute and it is the reason she cannot go up that road in the morning, and it is this.

She has a stool. It cost her nothing. A man made it and a woman of seventy-eight put it on her step and neither of them has ever asked her for anything and neither of them ever will.

And that is the best thing that has ever happened to her, and it is best because it costs nothing, and it is best because there is nothing to be grateful for in the ordinary way, and you cannot be grateful in the ordinary way for a thing that somebody gave you before they could be asked.

---

And there is a sister at that table who has just told her, in the middle of a form from the council, that there is a man in that passage, and it now costs something to have that stool.

Because now she knows that a man made it. And now she knows the man has been in that house. And now she knows that the woman of seventy-eight has known all of that since the twenty-seventh of May and has said one true thing about it and nothing else, and has never asked the man for anything either, and has never told the woman in her late forties about the chair, and has never told the man about the stool.

---

And there is a thank you in all of that.

And she can see exactly where it is and she can see exactly what it would cost, and it is not available.

She cannot go up that road and say thank you for a stool. Not because the woman cannot hear it — though she cannot, in any practical sense, she has not managed a thank you since before Christmas and she has not been able to practise.

It is that a thank you needs one of the two people to say *I know what this is.*

And the woman of seventy-eight cannot say *I know what this is*, because she cannot say *I know what this is* about anything, and she has proved that since the Wednesday of the nineteenth of May to everybody who has ever been in her kitchen.

And the woman in her late forties cannot say it for her, because the woman in her late forties is not somebody the woman in her late fifties can go to, and because asking her would be asking her to explain, and nobody in this borough explains anything.

---

So there is a stool in a kitchen and a thank you in it that nobody will ever be able to give, and the two women who are owed it are the two women in this borough who cannot say a word.

---

And the woman in her late fifties does not ask her sister one single question.

Not where he comes from. Not what he was doing. Not whether he is the one who has been doing other things in that house. Not whether the woman of seventy-eight knows he is the one who built the stool.

She asks her sister where the light is, and her sister says the passage, and her sister says she can see it from the doorstep, and that is the whole of the information that passes between them that night.

---

And at about twenty to one her sister takes the form away and puts it in her bag and says, "Are you all right for the door."

And the woman in her late fifties says, "Aye."

And her sister says, at the door, "I might call in on Thursday."

And the woman in her late fifties says, "Right."

And the door goes and the latch is on her sister's side, and that door is on the latch, and the woman in her late fifties has not asked her sister about that either.

---

And then she does the last part of the night, and it takes her about four minutes, and she will not have told anybody she did it, including her sister, and there was no audience.

She walks through to the front room and turns the light on.

And the stool is against the wall by the table in the kitchen, on its feet, facing the room, the way it has been since the twenty-seventh of May, and she has eaten at it or stood beside it or moved round it ten thousand times.

---

And she thinks about it for about a minute standing in the doorway of her own front room with the light on.

The kitchen is where she eats. That is the whole rule and it is the rule of every house she has ever been in since she was seventeen, and it is a good rule.

And the front room is where you put a thing you do not know what to do with.

---

And she goes back into the kitchen and puts her hand on the top of the stool and lifts it, and it is not heavy, and it is ash and three rails are oak, and it is a good bit of kit and she said so on a step in May and she meant it.

And she carries it through the house into the front room and puts it down against the wall opposite the window, and she does not put it by the wall as a place to sit. She puts it down where it is not a place to sit.

And the front room has a chair in it and a settee in it and a rug, and there is now a stool against the wall opposite the window with nothing on it and nowhere for it to go.

---

And she stands in the middle of that front room and looks at it and works out, about a minute later, standing on her own rug, what the difference is between the two rooms in her own house.

In the kitchen a thing is a thing you use. You wipe the surface round it. You put your plate on the table beside it. It gets moved when you need the space. That is what a thing in a kitchen is.

In the front room a thing is a thing you look at.

And that is a different job, and she cannot do it, and she has known that since the Thursday of the twenty-seventh of May and has been eating next to it ever since.

---

And she goes to bed at about half past one.

And there is no stool in her kitchen in the morning, and there is one in her front room against a wall with a chair on one side of it and a settee on the other, and there is a sister three doors down with a form from the council in a bag, and a woman of seventy-eight in a kitchen off the parade who has kept two chairs apart since the start of May and who cannot be thanked for a stool or a chair or anything else in this world.

**A WOMAN CAME IN FROM A LATE AT TEN PAST MIDNIGHT AND WAS TOLD THREE THINGS IN THE MIDDLE OF A FORM AND ASKED ONE QUESTION THAT WAS ONLY A PLACE, AND SHE NOW KNOWS THAT THE STOOL SHE HAS EATEN BESIDE SINCE THE TWENTY-SEVENTH OF MAY WAS MADE BY A MAN WHO HAS BEEN IN A PASSAGE FOUR MINUTES AT A TIME ALL SUMMER AND NEVER MENTIONED IT IN THAT HOUSE, AND SHE CANNOT ASK HER SISTER ONE QUESTION ABOUT IT AND CANNOT ASK THE WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT ANYTHING AT ALL, AND THE STOOL IS IN A FRONT ROOM AND NOT IN A KITCHEN, AND THE FIRST THING ON A PIECE OF PAPER IN THAT HOUSE IS A GATE, AND THE GATE IS DONE.**

(End of chapter 0662)
