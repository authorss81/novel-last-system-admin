# Chapter 1002 — Whether She Has Ever Been On A Book

Undated in this draft.

**THE FRONT ROOM AT NUMBER TEN HAS BEEN SHUT SINCE THE SUMMER WITH THE SASH DOWN ON THE SILL.**

Number ten's front room has one window, in the far wall, and it faces the back yards, and it has been shut since the summer because they wanted the front room shut when the partition came down in it and because shutting it was cheaper than heating it. The sash has been down on the sill ever since, which is where a sash goes when the room is shut and the curtains are off for the summer, and the sill is a hardwood sill about two inches thick with a moulding on the front edge that has been painted over three times and has started to lift where the paint has got wet behind it and come away from the wood in flakes you can lift with a fingernail. The room has the cold of a room that has not had a fire in it since before the summer in it, and the sill in that window is even along its whole length for about four feet of it and then is not, and it is that kind of cold that gets into a man's back between the shoulders after about a quarter of an hour and stays there.

He is in the room with the sash lying where it lies and the dust standing on it in a stripe where the light comes in, when she comes in at the front door with the sash out of its frame in her two hands. He can hear her come through the passage before he can see her, because she has to turn sideways to get the thing past the newel, and she is out of wind from having gone up the stairs with it, and she does not put it down until she is asked, and she is not asked.

He is also, in that minute, aware of two things at once that he has been keeping in separate places since the middle of one afternoon last week. The first is that the arrangement he has worked on for ten years was that nobody asked anybody anything on this frontage, and the only person who has broken it in six weeks is the woman standing in front of him with a sash in her hands, and the second is that he has come into this room four times since the summer and every one of the four times he came out with the offer, and never once the request.

She has it by the top rail and the bottom rail, and it is heavier than a sash looks, and it has been taken out of the frame the wrong way round, which has put the bottom corner of it through the paint of the inner architrave and left a long pale scratch in a place she cannot see and will not be able to mend. She brings it into the room and stands it against the wall under the window with its glass in the light, and the room fills up with the size of the thing.

"I did it yesterday with a chisel and a block of wood and it took me until half past three," she tells him. "I wanted you to see it before you tell me anything."

"Then I will tell you the thing you have not looked at." He got the fold of the rule out and put it down into the corner of the frame where the sash sits, and the sash had covered the whole of it for two months. "Your sill has gone. Not all of it. About a hand's width of it at this end, underneath, where the frame sits down into it, and the frame has been sitting in the wet because the paint on the outside of that architrave has gone and the water comes in at the top and lies along the sill and goes into the corner, and that corner has gone soft to a depth of about an inch and a half, and none of that is visible with the sash lying on top of it, because the sash is what has been hiding it all summer."

"How long?"

"Since the spring, at the rate of it. Before next winter it wants cutting back to solid and you would want a new piece let into it, and the reveal lined out square, and then a board across the opening and the room can stand shut for six weeks with nobody in it." He took the rule back and put it away. "That is about two days for me and half a day for anybody else, and the new piece is about four foot of hardwood, and it is not difficult."

---

"That is the first thing anybody has told me to do that costs money and involves work since my sister went up the river," she told him, "and I have heard how that sounds, and so have you, and neither of us is going to act the deaf about it, so let us both be grown about it."

"I have not put it that way."

"You stood there and let me carry a sash across two rooms to show you, which is what a man does when he wants a woman to know he is worth the walking." She leaned the sash back against the wall. "What I actually want to know is whether the room can be let."

"To who?"

"To anybody at all. I have not asked anybody yet and I am not going to be the kind of woman who goes round asking. But there is a family at the end of the row going up the river in about a month and if somebody wants a shut room with a fireplace in it for six weeks in the middle of the season, that is a thing people take."

"You have thought about this more than you have told me."

"I have thought about nothing else since the end of last week because I have been in this house alone with the fire out since the winter and a fire in a room is not a thing you can have on your own for six weeks." He let that sit, because she had put it down on the floor between them and not picked it up again.

"You have never been on a book." It came out of him before he had decided on it, and he heard how it sounded in his own ears, four days after he had stood in a hut at the far end of that same yard and heard a version of the same sentence.

"No." She did not take any longer over it than that. "Whatever that is. No. And I don't know that anybody has."

"Nobody has asked you, and you have never asked anybody, which is the sum of it, and it is no complaint; it is a fact about the two of us that I have known about for a week and you have known about for thirty years."

"I have had plenty asked of me." She put her thumb and forefinger on a flake of paint on the sill and lifted it and let it go. "Not asked. Had it asked of me. There's a difference and you have been on the wrong side of it since Friday." [break]

"Right." He put his hands in his pockets because there was nothing in that room he wanted to touch. "Then you will take that about half an hour, and I will not push it, and I will not come back to this house on a Tuesday and ask you a question about it, which is what I did to a woman at nine and what I have been told about since. But you should know what it is, because it came to me in the middle of one afternoon and I have not put it into words anywhere, and it is a thing that a man ought to put into words in front of somebody once. There is a book at the lock-up. It has one ruled column down the side of a page and a man's name against each ruled line and a day against the name, and a man goes in it for work somebody asked him for, and it shuts at the end of the season if no work goes on it, and at the moment both of the people who were in it are up a river and the column is empty and the season is closing."

"And you want to be in it."

"I have wanted to be in it since I was twenty-three and I have never once put that to a person, and I got as far as the frontage of the lock-up this morning with nothing in my hands at all." The sill at number ten is sound the whole length of it apart from the corner underneath the frame, where the paint has lifted off in flakes and the wood underneath has gone down to a soft core an inch and a half deep, and the sash is stood against the wall below it with a long pale scratch in the architrave that she cannot see and that will not come off.
