# Chapter 0985 — The Step Outside Number Two

Undated in this draft.

**THE BENCH UNDER THE ROOF LIGHT OF THE STORE AT THE UNIT HAS A DRAWING-BOARD ON IT WITH ONE CORNER OFF SQUARE.**

The store at the unit is one long room with a bench the whole length of the far side under a roof light, and the light lies on the bench from the middle of the morning until the sky over the yard goes the colour of a wet slate. Timber samples hang on the wall above it in a rack, one of every thickness, and the ends of them have gone the colour of tea where the wall has been damp at the bottom of the rack for years. He has been at the bench since the rain stopped, with the drawing-board square in front of him and the rule and the pencil and a folding rule he has not opened, doing the one thing he had set the morning aside for. A man at the lock-up wants a wall taken out and a doorway put through, and wants it priced by Friday, and he was going to do the sum twice and have the answer on the desk before the shutter went up.

The roof light over the bench is dirty on the outside, which is why the bench is never bright, only grey. Somebody washed it twice and it made no difference, because the dirt is on the weather side and no amount of water on the inside reaches it. The pencil behind his right ear has been there since the yard, and every time he turns his head it catches the light off the roof light, and twice this morning he has put his hand up to it without meaning to and taken it out and put it on the bench.

She comes in past the wide door, which stands open against the wall on its hinge, and stops at the end of the bench with the yard light behind her and the roof light over both of them. The step outside number two has gone down in the middle. Not cracked and not lifted, gone down, so that it dishes about an inch and a half across the width of it, and every shower sends the water across the flags and into the passage under the door, where it stands against the inside of the door and comes up the skirt. She has a bucket for it and has had the bucket since before the weather turned.

"I want it made safe. Not mended — made safe."

He turns the near corner of the drawing-board over with his fingertips a hand's width and looks down the edge of it.

"Whoever mends it will tell you it's mended. It's a cast flag and the bed under it has gone. You could set it up on a fresh bed of grit and it would be level for a good many years, or you could cut a new one, and then you are into the passage and the door's off its line."

"Which is which."

"The grit is a morning. The new one is three days and a man who does nothing else all three." He puts the corner back and lines it up with the edge of the bench by eye, and it is square again. "Give me the six and I'll do it Thursday. Thursday's my own."

The figure she names is not three pounds and she does not pretend it is. He hears it and she can see him hear it, and they both stand in the grey light with the rain beginning to tick on the roof light over the bench, and he turns the question round to the place it belongs to.

"Who else would I go to? You know the answer to that."

"That man from the weighbridge would do it well. He'd also do it in the spring, and he'd want paying, and he'd be fair about the figure."

"Whenever he got to it, then."

"You'd take three pounds for the grit," she tells him, and the number in her mouth is not three pounds, and the difference between them is the whole of what he has been doing for ten years.

---

He does it the same afternoon, in a drizzle, with the passage door taken off its hinges and laid over the flags so nobody walks into it, and a sack of grit from the yard and a length of batten to level it on. The old flag comes up at one corner and away whole, which it should not, and the void under it is a bowl of silt and old mortar gone to sand, the mortar hard as a stone and the sand under it not. That bed was laid in one afternoon by two men a long time back and has been washing out at the low corner ever since, and every year the dish deepens by the width of a thumbnail, and this year the water has started going under the door.

He scrapes the silt out with the end of the batten and his fingers and packs the side of the hole with the new grit, and levels it by eye against the jamb, which is out of true by a quarter of an inch over the width of the door. That quarter of an inch is why the water went in and not along. He packs the low corner and lifts it and packs it again until the flag sits proud of its neighbour at the door end and proud of the passage wall at the far end, and the whole of it now runs away instead of across.

Afterwards he gets a stub of chalk from the box on the bench and marks the low corner, so that whoever looks at it in a year knows where the trouble was and does not go looking along the whole front of the house for it. She comes out of the passage with the hessian over her arm and looks down at the line.

"That'll last the winter."

"It'll last a good many years if the grit was well graded. It wasn't." He dries his hands on the hessian. "I used what was in the yard."

"That'll last the winter," she tells him again, and there is nothing wrong with her for saying it twice.

"I'm not on the book." He has the bucket emptied outside and the door hung back on its three hinges and the passage swept out with a hand broom, and he is standing at the low corner of the step with the chalk still in his hand when the thing he has been carrying since he was twenty-two comes out of him with no preparation at all.

She looks up at him from the passage flags.

"The book at the lock-up. Nobody's ever put me on it. That man from the weighbridge has been on it since before I came down here and he'll be on it when I'm not, and I do the same work, and I have never once asked anybody to put me on, which is the whole of it." He gets his hand up to the pencil behind his ear and stops it there. "Ten years on that yard."

"You have never asked."

"No."

She puts the bucket down inside the doorway and comes out onto the step with her back to the open door, and looks at the chalk line the way she looked at the dish, and does not offer him anything else, which is the correct thing and is not much use.

"There's something on," she tells him. "I had it off the woman that books work. She's after men who can go up the river and come back — not this side of it, up it. She takes names at the office and she takes them herself, and I've seen her do it twice. If you want on that book you go and ask her, and you don't ask me, because I can't put anybody anywhere and I never could."

---

He puts the chalk in his pocket and the rule and the folding rule in the bag and stands with his hand on the bench for a second, because there is nothing on it now but the light. Six pounds. A morning. And a job at the lock-up that wants pricing by Friday, and about four hours of a day that had been nobody else's because he had not promised it away. So as it goes it is only money and a day, which is the easy way round it, and he can see himself going round it that way while he is standing there with his hand on the bench.

The chalk line along the low corner of the step is still clear in the drizzle, and the water is going past it into the passage instead of across it, and the passage door is hung back on its three hinges and shut against nothing, and the bucket is standing outside on the flags where she has left it.
