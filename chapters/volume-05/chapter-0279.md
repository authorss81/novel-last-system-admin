# Chapter 0279 — The One They Keep

Tuesday the sixth of December 2016, from about half past seven in the evening, a hall off a parade in Tideglass.

**THE SAME MAN ASKED THE SAME QUESTION AGAIN IN A ROOM THAT ALREADY HAD AN ANSWER IT WAS HAPPY WITH, AND THE ROOM HANDED HIM A DIFFERENT ONE, AND HE WAS TOLD BOTH, AND HE WENT HOME WITH THE NEW ONE.**

The hall off that parade is warm and free and is on a meter, and it is open on a Tuesday evening now as well as a Friday, and nobody puts anybody's name anywhere and nobody is asked anything at the door.

There were about eight of them in it. The man from the parade came at about half past seven. The man in his forties turned up about half way through and had brought nothing with him, which is what he does. The woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall was early and had her coat over the back of a chair. A woman who has been in that hall about two years came off a late. A man who works nights had come straight off and still had his coat on.

At about ten past eight the door of that hall went, because that door has swung both ways since before the summer on a catch that has never once held, and a gust came up the parade off the front of the bank and took it wide and put about a foot of cold straight down the length of that room.

The jug went off the table. The man in his forties got a hand on it. The woman of about forty-two put her back against the door and held it, and the man in his forties kicked a flattened cardboard box out of the side of the stage and the door came back onto it and stopped, and stayed stopped for the rest of the night.

While they were doing that, the young woman of about twenty came in with her coat running water onto the boards and the woman of about fifty-five arrived behind her with her bag and a folded towel over her shoulder, and neither of them had been in that room since October, and nobody asked them why.

---

The man from the parade had it at about a quarter to nine, standing up, because there are chairs in that hall but he has never once sat down in it.

"It's the same question I put in October," he said. "Of the things we have dated. Somebody, one day, is going to be able to stand up and say they know. Which one. And I'm not after an argument with anybody. I want to know what the answer is now, because there's one going round and I want to hear whether it's still that."

"It's the day," said a woman at a table by the wall. "The twenty-eighth of June. That's the one that's going round."

"I know that's the one that's going round," said the man from the parade. "My aunt's got the other one. She's had the register one off a man off a lorry since about the summer and she's still got it and she'll still tell you. So there's two. I want to know if anybody's come up with a third."

Nobody came up with a third, for about a minute, and the radiator under that window has been out since about 1998 and nobody was going to put it on.

Then the woman of about fifty-five put her bag on the floor and said:

"It isn't going to be one of ours."

"You've lost me," said the man from the parade.

"It's going to be one of theirs," said the woman of about fifty-five. "That's what I've been thinking about for a fortnight. Not the register and not the day. Somebody's going to go through a drawer in a building on the other side of that water and come out with a bit of paper with our postcode on it, and that's the one, and it won't be the best one, it will be a bit of paper, and none of us will ever know what it was and none of us will ever be able to stand up and say that was us."

"That's a wind-up," said the man in his forties.

"It's a handover," said the woman of about fifty-five. "That's what that is. It's the only one of the three that isn't sitting in a cupboard in one of our own rooms. The book, you can shut it. The day, you would have to be all stood in the same rain, and we would not remember it right, and in about ten years there will not be one of us left who can get a bus. That one asks nothing of anybody in here. And that's not me being clever, that's me saying that we've been sat in here for a fortnight handing a thing round like it's a jar of something."

"That's worse than the day," said the man from the parade.

"It's more honest than the day," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"The day's not a lie."

"The day's not a lie," she agreed, "and it isn't yours either, and neither is that. It's just the one that doesn't ask anything of anybody, and I'm tired."

The man from the parade stood with his cup in both hands and said, "Say that again," and she said it again, and he wrote nothing down because he has never written anything down.

The young woman of about twenty, who is the one who gave that room the day in October, and who is the only person in it who was in a room in April, said nothing for a long time.

At about ten past nine the man from the parade turned round and said, "You started the day one. What do you make of that one."

"I make of it that the day's better than that," she said.

"Right."

"The day's better than that," said the young woman of about twenty, "and I don't care how many of us have heard it now, and I'm not going to sit here and say it again to make it go round again, because it went round once and it did its work and now there's a third one and you'll both be back here in a month with two wrong answers and none of them any use to anybody."

"That is the second time this year," said the man in his forties, "that you have told this room where the floor is."

"I'm not telling anybody where the floor is," she said. "I'm telling you which of two of your own ideas I'd rather you had, and I have said it, and I am not going to say it a third time."

And the man from the parade said, "Right," and drank his tea, and nobody in that room said anything else about any of it, and the woman who has been in that hall about two years said, "I like that, the other one, the one of theirs. It's the one I'd have given."

"You'd not," said the woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall, without turning round, from the door, where she had gone to put a pound in the meter.

"Would I not."

"You'd have given the day," said the woman of about forty-two, "same as the rest of us, and you'd have given it quicker."

The woman of about fifty-five said, at about half past nine, to the middle of that room, that they had not told the man who does the estimating any of it, and that they were not going to, and that in July he stood in a street and told a shopkeeper he could not get his own answer in two pieces, and that not one person in that hall had been in that street.

Nobody argued with her about that either.

At about ten to ten the young woman of about twenty picked her coat up off the back of a chair, and on her way to the door she stopped by the woman of about fifty-five and said, quietly, that the woman had only heard the one about the register and the other.

"I've heard three," said the woman of about fifty-five.

"You've heard three and you've kept one."

"You as well," said the woman of about fifty-five.

The young woman of about twenty went out into that cold, and the door came off its cardboard box about a foot and let the whole of the front of the bank in.

**AND THE BOX WENT BACK UNDER THE DOOR AT TEN PAST TEN BY A WOMAN WHO KICKED IT AND STOOD ON IT UNTIL IT WENT, AND SHE WENT OVER TO THAT METER AFTERWARDS AND TURNED THE GAS OFF AND LOCKED IT, AND WENT DOWN THE PARADE AND WAS GONE, AND THE DOOR CAME OFF THE BOX AGAIN AT HALF PAST TEN AND STAYED OFF IT, AND ON THE SUNDAY MORNING A MAN WALKED THE LENGTH OF THAT PARADE LOOKED DOWN AT THAT BOX, WHICH WAS WET THROUGH, PUT A FOOT ON IT, CARRIED IT IN, AND DID NOT SAY WHY.**
