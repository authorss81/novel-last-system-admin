# Chapter 0656 — A Bad Brush Out Of A Bucket

Monday the twenty-sixth of July 2021, in this borough, and not in it, because she is on the far side of the ring road for the whole of the middle of the day. She is in a shop on the ring road at about ten in the morning and in a house with the stair already stripped from about half past ten until about half past four, and the whole of it is a wire brush she buys for a gate out of a bucket by the door of a shop, and the Monday she spends with it is spent on a wall.

**A WOMAN WHO SAID IN A KITCHEN THAT SHE WOULD DO A GATE BUYS THE TOOL FOR IT OUT OF A BUCKET, AND SHE DOES NOT RING THE MAN WHO HAS ONE BECAUSE HE WOULD SAY YES, AND SHE HAS KNOWN SINCE THE FRIDAY EXACTLY WHAT SHE WOULD HAVE TO DO WITH A YES, AND THE BRUSH SHE COMES AWAY WITH IS A BAD ONE, AND SHE SPENDS THE WHOLE OF THE MONDAY WITH IT ON A WALL.**

She is a woman in her late forties. There is a man behind a glass case in a shop for about two minutes of the morning, and a woman who has booked the house asks her one question about a brush at about three o'clock, and that is the whole of the talking.

---

Her van is outside a house on the far side of the ring road with the tailgate up and the blue roll-up out of the boot and half of a staircase in pieces on the front drive, and she has been in that drive since about half past seven, and at about ten she goes out to the shop because she has run out of the thing she needs.

That is the whole reason and it is a small one. She has a bolster chisel off her own board in the van. She had a wire brush for years and it went in a skip in a house in Chorlton in the spring with the handle snapped off it. She has not bought a new one. She does not own a new one.

She has three brushes on the board in the van and two of them are bristle and one is a bottle brush with a handle like a pencil, and none of them is a wire brush.

---

And the drops on the back of that frame are one solid lump.

That is not her figure. That is his, and he gave it to a kitchen on the Friday of the sixteenth of July in front of two people, and she wrote it down that night in the back of the pad in the van with the other things she writes down.

*one solid lump with old gloss over them. chisel and a wire brush.*

She has read it since. She has read it in a van on the ring road with the heater going and she has got the way with the lumps off the back of a frame in her hands from about twenty years of other people's gates, and she knows the figure is right, and she knows that the figure is not the whole of the job either, because you cannot leave four inches of loose gloss on the back of a frame and hang a gate back on it and expect it to swing in the cold.

---

And here is what she cannot get past, and it is a small thing, and she gives it about ten minutes in the shop car park.

A brush is not the work. The work is Saturday morning and a chisel and two and a half hours on a back step with the sun coming round the side of somebody's house, and the brush is about four minutes of it at the end.

So the brush is not the reason she is frightened of the Saturday.

The brush is the reason she cannot do the Saturday at all, and it is a brush she could have had on the Friday for nothing, and that is the whole of the trouble.

---

She goes into the shop at about ten past ten.

It is one of those places with a glass case along the side and three aisles and a yard out the back, and there is a galvanised bucket by the door with brushes standing up in it in sawdust, and there is a card on a wire stuck in the top of the brush handles, and the card says something about being suitable for a number of things.

She stands at that bucket for about a minute and a half before she touches anything, and what she is doing is looking at the handles, because the handle is the thing that matters. You want ash. You want it long enough that your hand is not over the work, and you want the ferrule tight, and you want the crimp to be deep so that the wires are set in something and not floating.

Every one of them in that bucket is beech. Every one of them has the same crimp and the same two inches of ash and a stamp on the butt of the handle that says a town off the ring road.

---

And the man behind the glass says one thing.

He says, "They're the same as that one, the big ones are over there."

And she says, "Which ones are the big ones."

And he puts his hand round the bucket and moves three of them about six inches to the left.

And she says, "Right," and picks one of the three, and pays for it, and that is the entire conversation of a Monday morning in that shop.

---

The one she picks has about nine inches of bristle in the crimp and it is soft, and soft is what it is for. It is a wall brush. It is for taking the flat off a distempered wall and for getting into the corner behind a radiator, and it is not for a gate frame with oak under it and a great many years of gloss in the wood.

She can see that across the glass. She can see that the crimp is about a quarter of what it should be, and the wires at the ends of it are already splayed where it has been in a bucket and used by somebody who did not know what it was for.

She takes it anyway, because it is a brush, and it is four pounds, and it will be enough.

---

And here is the thing about enough, and it is the thing that has run this whole summer, and she stands in a shop on the ring road with a bad brush in her hand and cannot put it down.

A brush that is not the right brush is worse than no brush, because no brush is a reason and a bad brush is a thing in your hand that says you have started.

---

She is back on the far side of the ring road by about half past ten and the stair is in three piles and the woman who booked the house is in the kitchen with her coat still on.

And here is the Monday, and it is worth setting down because it is eight hours and not one minute of it is the gate.

She does the back room first, because the back room is the room with the paper on the walls. The paper comes off in sheets and every sheet under the last is the same paper as the last, and the only way off a wall like that is to score it, get a blade under the corner, and then lift with a scraper and a hand, and it comes down in about two hours and fills three bags and stinks.

And she is doing that on her knees on boards with a blade and a scraper and a bucket, and the wire brush is in the van, and it is not doing anything, and she does not bring it in for the first two hours.

---

And then, at about half past one, she does bring it in.

She has run out of wire. She is on a picture rail in the front room with a scraper and a chisel, and the paint on the rail is a hard cream gloss, and she goes out to the van and gets the brush and comes back in, and she puts it on the rail.

And it moves nothing.

That is the honest finding and it takes her about four minutes. The glaze on that rail is thinner than the lump on a gate frame and the brush still will not shift it, because the crimp is soft and the wires are splayed and the brush is spending itself on the flat instead of into the corner where the glaze has run down into the wood.

She gets the chisel under the edge of it instead, and it comes off in a strip, and the rail is done in about another half hour, and the brush sits on the top of a bucket for the rest of the afternoon with a curl of cream gloss in the middle of it.

---

And the woman who booked the house comes in at about three o'clock and stands in the doorway of the front room with a cup in each hand.

And she says, "You're not using that on the wood, are you."

And she says, "No."

And the woman says, "That's a wall brush, that."

And she says, "I know."

And the woman says, "You've not got a wire one?"

And she says, "I've got a chisel."

---

And the woman who booked the house looks at the brush on the bucket for a second and then goes out, and she does not ask where the wire one is, and she does not ask where the money came from, and she does not ask what a woman in her late forties is doing on her front room picture rail in the middle of a Monday afternoon in July.

That is the arrangement. She has been doing this for nineteen years and the arrangement is that neither of them asks the other a single question, and it is the only arrangement in this borough that has never once cost her anything.

---

And the front room is bare by four o'clock, and the picture rail is off, and there is a room at number twelve that has not been painted since before Christmas, and she is on the far side of the ring road with a wire brush she bought for it.

---

And here is what happens at about half past four, and it is in the front of her own van with the door shut, and it takes her about a minute, and she does not say it out loud.

She puts her hand on the brush on the parcel shelf.

And what comes up is not a gate.

It is a door.

---

A door on a road off the parade, off its latch, which it has been since before Christmas and which nobody in that house has ever mentioned.

She has known that about the door since June. She stood in that passage in June and put her hand on it to go out and it went under her hand, and the woman in her late seventies was at the sink and did not turn round, and neither of them said one word about it then either.

And she stands in her own van on the far side of the ring road and works out, standing up, with a brush in her hand, that she does not have to knock.

That is the whole of what has been standing in the way since the Friday. There is no knock on that door. There has not been a knock on that door since before Christmas, and the door is not shut, and she could walk up that path at any hour of any day of her life and put her hand on it and it would come away from the frame under her hand.

And she is not going to do that, and she has got it down to the reason in about a minute, and the reason is the one she would not be able to explain to anybody and is going to explain to herself exactly once, here, with the engine off.

---

If she knocks, she gets what the knock is for.

That is all it is. A knock on that door is a way of making a woman who cannot say no to a knock open a door. It does not ask anything. It has never once asked anything. Everybody who has stood on that step since before Christmas has knocked, and the door has gone every time, and not one of them has put a question in it, and one of them came with a screwdriver in her pocket because she had seen a blanket through a window.

He came into that kitchen on the Friday and said *nothing* to a woman who asked him *how much is that*, and it was the right thing and she has been sure of that every day since.

He fitted a bulb off the bottom of the list so that he would have had a reason to have been in the house.

And a woman in her early thirties taped a window in May and was told *no* in three seconds about a gate and went back down the road without asking why.

Every single thing that has been done at that gate since the Wednesday of the nineteenth of May has been done by somebody arranging to be let in.

And she has got a chisel and a bad brush in a bag and a Saturday coming, and if she knocks she is doing the same thing, and she will be doing it better than any of them, because she is the one who put the green chair at that table.

---

And there is the other half of it, and it is the half that took her the minute.

A woman of seventy-eight cannot turn round and say no.

She cannot say no to a woman in her late forties who has come about the first line on a list, because there is no version of that Saturday on which she says no, and everybody in that room knows it, and the whole of this summer has been built out of her not being able to say no.

So if she knocks, then the gate gets done, and the gate getting done will cost that woman something, and it will cost her the thing she is least able to pay, which is being turned down.

And she is not going to put a woman of seventy-eight in a position of having to refuse her.

---

And that is the reason and it is not the door, and it is not a fear, and it is not about the bench on the parade and it is not about the agreement the two of them made in May and it is not about the chair or the stool or the man.

It is that she cannot be a person who knocks.

---

She sits in the van for a bit and then she puts the brush in the bag with the chisel and shuts the tailgate and goes home, and it is in the bag on the floor by the passenger seat from that Monday evening, and it stays there.

---

And here is the part that makes the Saturday, and she does not know yet that it makes it, and it is about nine o'clock at night and she is in her own kitchen.

She gets the pad out of the door pocket to write the day down, the way she does, and she gets as far as the Monday, and she stops.

And what she writes is: *brush. bad one. ring road.*

And then underneath it, before she can talk herself out of it, she writes a second line.

*mon. gate. wire brush.*

And the second line has a Monday on it, and there is no Monday on it that she is going to be able to keep, and she does not cross it out either, and she leaves the pad open on the table with the two lines on it and goes to bed.

**A WOMAN BOUGHT THE TOOL FOR A GATE IN A BUCKET OFF THE FLOOR OF A SHOP AND SPENT THE WHOLE MONDAY WITH IT ON A WALL ON THE FAR SIDE OF THE RING ROAD, AND THE REASON SHE DID NOT RING THE MAN WHO HAD A GOOD ONE IS THAT HE WOULD HAVE SAID YES AND SHE WOULD HAVE HAD TO EXPLAIN WHY SHE COULD NOT BE IN THAT KITCHEN WHEN HE CAME UP, AND THE REASON SHE DID NOT GO UP THAT ROAD WITH IT IN HER BAG IS NOT THAT THE DOOR WOULD NOT OPEN, AND THE DOOR HAS NOT BEEN ON ITS LATCH SINCE BEFORE CHRISTMAS, AND IT IS IN A BAG ON THE FLOOR OF A VAN AND SHE HAS WRITTEN A MONDAY ON A PAD AND HAS NOT CROSSED IT OUT.**

(End of chapter 0656)
