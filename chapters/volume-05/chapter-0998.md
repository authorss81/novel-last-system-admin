# Chapter 0998 — What A Man Has To Have

Undated in this draft.

**THE WEIGHBRIDGE HUT AT THE LOCK-UP HAS A BENCH DOWN ONE SIDE AND A WINDOW ON THE YARD.**

The weighbridge hut stands at the weigh end of the lock-up yard on four brick piers with a floor of softwood boards off the top of them, and it is eight feet by six inside and it has been painted twice and is due a third. There is a bench down the left-hand side when you go in, a shelf over the bench at hip height with nothing on it, and a window in the end wall that looks out square at the weigh plate with its frame in one corner of the opening so that a man sitting on the bench can see somebody getting out of a cart without standing up. The plate itself is a slab of steel in a surround of angle iron set into the concrete of the yard, and it comes up about a quarter of an inch proud of the concrete at the far side, which is how a plate is meant to be and the only thing about it that is still right, and the joint all round it is even along its whole length where it has not started to open, and the surround has opened along the top edge by the width of a coin over the years and the corner of it stands up like the lip of a tin.

He is on the bench with the bag down between his boots when the woman who books work comes across the yard from the office, and she does not come because she was looking for him. She comes because the office has two radiators and neither of them is on and the hut has the one door shut, and she stands in the doorway of the hut with her hands in the pockets of a coat she has not taken off since the morning.

"You have come about the book."

"I have come about being on it."

"Sit down again and ask me the whole of it then, because you have had two goes at it from the frontage and you came to the office door twice and stood in it." She stayed where she was. "A man goes in the book against work. Not against wanting work, not against being ready, not against the offer of a man who will work if he is let. Against work. Actual work with a day against it and somebody's name against that day, and the name on the day has to be the name of the man who did it."

"And who asks him?"

"Somebody on the book. In front of the others."

"Who is on the book now?"

"Nobody is on the book now. Two lines went up the river on Tuesday in one bag between them and there has been nothing on it since, and the column is ruled and empty, and whether anybody wants it shut or not it is going to shut, and nobody is going to be in it." She turned round and shut the hut door behind her, which took the draught off the back of his neck. "That is the first half. Do you want the second half or are you going to let me save you a quarter of an hour."

---

"Give me the second half."

"The second half is the asking, and the asking is the part that cannot be done from a bench in a hut." She came and sat down on the end of the bench with her back against the wall and the coat still on. "I have put a man's name in that book against four days of work on a Tuesday, in a room with two other men in it, after one of the men in that room asked him for the work out loud with me standing there. How it is done is that, in full, and no rule is ever going to be put up in a frame on a wall about it. The last crew I asked came back up the river at the end of six weeks and their names were not in the book again, because the work had stopped on this side while they were gone and there was nothing to enter them against, and nobody in that room did that, least of all me. It is what the arrangement does." He looked at the window with the plate in it and put his hand flat down into the crack where the angle iron has opened away from the concrete, and closed it on nothing, and the nothing was wider than his hand by the width of two fingers on the near side.

"Can a man go in against work he has done on his own?"

"No."

"Against work he has already done and been paid for?"

"That is work. If somebody asked him for it at the time and it was done, it is work and it goes in." She looked at the side of his face. "You have not done one thing this season that anybody asked you for. Have you."

"Four days of wall at this end of your yard."

"Who asked you for that?"

"The man in the second office."

"Then he asked you. Put that in front of me on Monday and it goes in the book on Monday and your name is against those four days for as long as the book is open." She got up off the bench. "And it will not be me who asks you, because I have told you the reason I will not, and the reason is a room with nobody in it, and I would be asking you in a hut at my own weigh end with the door shut. That is worse, not better."

There is a thing he can put his hand on, while she is still standing at that doorway with her hands in her pockets, and it is the wall behind the middle bay. It is a partition of brick and block with a doorway wanted through it and the whole job took him four minutes to price in his head in the first week and about two seconds to understand the rest of. It is the only work anybody has put in front of him this year. Every other house on this frontage got what it got because he turned up.

He has not asked anybody for one thing in his life. That is no boast and no hardship; it is simply the other side of the same arrangement, because asking is a thing a man does when he has something at stake and a man with nothing at stake can ask anybody anything, and thirty years of asking for nothing has left him as the only person on this frontage who cannot put a question to anybody, and he has got to that position the way a man gets to most positions, which is one offer at a time.

She went out across the yard to the office with the door shut behind her by the draught, and he sat where he was with the bag against his ankle and looked at the plate in the window and understood, about nine minutes after she had gone, that he had come up the frontage to be told what a man has to have, and that a man has to have somebody entitled to ask him for something, standing in a room, and that every person on this frontage with that entitlement is either up a river or is the woman who has just walked off across that yard and would not do it. The two of them are six weeks gone and the woman at ten and the sister at twelve are the only ones left standing in the street, and one of those two has spent the morning paying a man in coin off a folded square and the other one has a coal shed and a house with the fire out and a mother gone up the river on Friday. That crack runs the whole width of the plate and it is the same width everywhere it is not going to give way, which is what old cracks do, and the steel is a cold colour under it all morning and about four degrees colder in the middle than at the ends.
