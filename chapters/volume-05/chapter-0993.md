# Chapter 0993 — The Book Closes At The End Of The Season

Undated in this draft.

**A LENGTH OF FLOORBOARD IS ON TWO TRESTLES ACROSS THE FLOOR OF THE OFFICE AT THE UNIT.**

The office at the unit has been his for nine years in the sense that the key is his and the roof over it is his problem and nobody has ever stood in it except the woman from two doors down and a man from the council who came about the damp and was gone inside ten minutes. There is a desk with a lamp on it that has never worked, one chair, and a length of floorboard on two trestles across the floor that came off a job in the spring and has been waiting on the plane ever since. Behind him the window is a single sash with a card jammed behind the lower joint where he put one nine years ago, and one chair is all the chair this room has ever had.

The board is four feet long and an inch and a quarter thick with a quarter sawn figure running the length of it, and it has lain flat on the trestles since the middle of the spring with a square of paper over one end of it to keep the dust off. It is out of true along its length by a quarter of an inch in the middle and next to nothing at the two ends, which is the shape a board of that width takes coming off a stack that has lain the wrong way up. An hour on that plane would take it to nothing. The plane is on the bench behind the door, three feet away.

She came in at the office door from the yard with the door open behind her and stopped at the desk, which is the other way round from the store where the whole thing started, and he knew that the moment she was through it.

"You didn't come to ask me for anything."

"No."

"You have come to tell me a thing you have been carrying since Friday."

"I have come to tell you all of it and I have not had it in one piece before, so it will come out in the wrong order."

"The sister at number ten has taken a line and filled it in herself, with a man walking up the frontage beside her. The one at number nine has taken one and has not told a soul. One line remains unfilled and stays unfilled until five o'clock this afternoon." He turned a trestle a quarter turn without meaning to.

"I asked the one at number nine whether she would go. She didn't answer, and she put her name down herself two days before I got there. I carried the other one's sheet up the frontage and I never asked her. One done, one done without me, and one not done at all."

"And you want the empty line."

"I wanted it on Monday. I want it today more than I wanted it on Monday, and the part I cannot get round is that wanting it."

"So what do you want now?"

"You." The board is still under his palm and he leaves it there. "I want you to tell me why I asked you at all. I have been over it nine times since Saturday and every time I come out in the same place, which is that I never asked you. I asked two other people and not you, and I have spent two days telling myself that a man being in a room is not a man asking." He looked at the board instead of at her, because he had put that question three times in four days and got an honest answer out of nobody, himself included.

"You never asked me."

"No."

"A step and a mirror in a cold grate and two hours in a doorway. None of that was asking. All of it was arranging."

"There is a thing I want to put to you and you will not enjoy it. Are you on the book?" She does not sit down either.

"No."

"Then nothing in it is to be liked or disliked. Come round this side."

She got a hand into her apron pocket, brought out a folded sheet and put it on the corner of the desk, and he came round and stood on the far side of it from her.

"I am on it. I was in that office on Thursday, ahead of anybody else. I never told you. The woman at the lock-up does not know either. That page has been folded and unfolded in the front of my handbag since Tuesday, and it has been in and out of that bag every day since."

He had been carrying the empty line round his head for four days like a coin in his fist, and there was nowhere in that room to put down what she had just handed him.

"Then why —"

She withdrew her hand from the desk altogether.

"Do not do that. There has been nobody in that house but me and my mother since the summer she came out of hospital, and you have come twice a week for ten years without once putting a question to me, and this week I found out that the woman who books work asks everybody on this earth except you. So do not ask me why."

"Then what took place on Thursday morning."

"They asked me. In an office, on their own, with a sheet with a heading on it." She stopped, and took it up again a moment afterwards. "They want somebody to sit with a mother four afternoons a week while they do her hip. Up the river. Six weeks. And I'm to be the one who sits with her. And my mother is the one who would be left here."

The office was very quiet, and through the glass the yard was doing nothing in particular. Her eyes went down to the boards at her feet, and stayed there.

"So you have taken it."

"I told her I would think it over. I have not, and thinking it over is not the same as a yes."

"And before you start, I know exactly what it is. Somebody's mother. That is the job. That is what this is, right the way down."

"Yes."

"I know." She straightened up. "And here is the other half of it, and it is why I came up here instead of putting my bag down and going. If the two men go up the river, the work stops at that yard for the season, and you know that better than I do."

"It shuts in the spring, and it shuts on me and on every man that is on it. If those two stay there will be work before the year turns, and if I am not on the page when it comes in, somebody else will put a name against it and be standing there when the work does."

"Say that again in fewer words, because I did not follow the first of it."

"If I do nothing, that page closes on me. That is the price and it is a real one, and it is not going to be paid by anybody on my behalf."

"I'll go." He put both hands on the board on the trestles, one at each end, and left the plane on the bench where it was, and he had about six things in his chest and got one of them out.

"No."

"You haven't—"

"You'll not." She did not raise her voice and she did not move. "Nobody is going to set your name down in place of mine. Do you understand what you have just done? You have wanted that line since Monday and you would take it off me in a sentence if I let you, and the reason I am not going to let you is that it would be the one thing that page is unable to do. Not one line exists for a man to go in place of a woman who has been on since she was twenty-two. Ask for it and there will be one in a week, and then there's a way of doing this thing that never asks anybody at all, and I have seen two good trades turn into that."

"Then I'll do as I am told."

"No." She is honest with him now, which would have been easier if she had shouted. "You'll do as you decide, which is what you have been not doing for ten years. I would rather you asked me one question and heard me refuse it than stood there and took it out of my hands because you have decided it is for the best."

"All right," he told her. "Then I need you to ask me one."

"I did that in the store, with a bucket in your hands."

"That was a favour and you knew it was a favour, and so did I, and I have spent nine years pretending that sort of thing is different from asking." He swung round and laid a palm on the board. "Put one thing to me. Anything. I will give you an answer and I will not go behind your back afterwards, and there is nothing else behind it at all."

"Not now," she told him. "Go on. I'll think of something by the time you've got that board planed, and you'll have plenty of time, because I have seen that bench." She did not have one ready and that was the best thing that happened to him in four days.

Four days ago he had wanted his name on a page. Tonight he had wanted an answer out of a woman about a woman up a river, and she had taken it out of his hands because he was reaching for it. Neither of those is the same as the first one, and he has got no page and no line and no name in either place. He walked her out to the gate, came back, and stood in the office door with his hand on the frame while the whole week arrived on him at once, which is how it always arrives, all at the end.

The board is on the trestles and the plane is on the bench behind the door, and she is going to think of something to ask him, and he has not the least idea what it is.
