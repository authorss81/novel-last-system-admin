# Chapter 0485 — The Man at the Last House

Saturday the thirteenth of April 2019. Half past nine in the morning until about six in the evening, at the top of that borough, in Tideglass.

**AT ABOUT HALF PAST NINE ON THAT SATURDAY A MAN LET HIMSELF INTO THE SIDE DOOR OF THE LAST HOUSE IN THAT ROW WITH A KEY HE HAD BEEN GIVEN IN FEBRUARY, AND A MAN WHO HAD PARKED HIS VAN AT THE TOP END HALF AN HOUR EARLIER STOOD AT HIS OWN GATE AND WATCHED HIM DO IT FOR ABOUT NINE MINUTES AND THEN DROVE ON DOWN THE ROW TO HIS WORK.**

The row is about fourteen houses long and it is the top of that part of the borough, where the ground stops going up and there is a field behind it with nothing on it but a wire and some thistle.

The last house is their mother's. She has been in it since about 2011 and she was widowed in about 2004 and she did the widowing slowly.

He is her older one. He has been in the row since he was small, he works at a place four miles off that does not shut, and he is never home before six and most Sundays not at all.

She is the younger one. She has two children and she lives about fifty minutes away on the other side of the water, and she rings on a Wednesday and sometimes on a Sunday.

The two of them have not discussed the man at number fourteen since early March, and not one of them has raised him with anybody outside this row.


He has been at that house most days since the middle of March.

That is not a figure of speech. On a Monday he is there at about half past eight and it takes him until about half past ten, and on a Thursday he is there from about ten and it takes him until about one, and twice a week since the end of March he has taken her into town and come back by the same road and put her down at her own kerb.

Her grass is a strip about thirty yard long between the house and a wall and he cuts it with his own machine, which he brings in a van and takes away again, and he has done it four times.

He got the key in February. It is a key off an old ring of about nine of them that he had picked up at a house clearance in 2017, and he asked her for it in a way that was not asking, and she gave it to him at the door and did not say anything after she had done it.

Here is what he does not know and has never asked about.

Whether she is glad to see him.

He has decided twice that he is, on the grounds that she puts her coat on. And he has noticed that she puts her coat on for the man who delivers the bread, and that she does not put her coat on for him when he knocks, and he has decided that this is because of what he is being let in to do rather than who is letting him in, and he has not gone any further into it than that and he is not going to.


What the brother has worked out took him about a fortnight and it began with the grass.

There is a strip about four foot wide between that garden and the house next door and the woman in the next door has complained about it twice in nine years, and in the second week of March he noticed that it was about a foot higher than it had been at the back of the previous summer.

He did not say anything to his mother about that. He went and looked at it on his way to work one morning in March, at about seven, and stood at the end of it, and then he got in his van and went to work.

Then there was the second thing, which was a car outside that house at about half past nine one evening when there was no reason for a car to be there, and the third, which was that his mother had asked him at the end of February whether he knew anybody who could do a bit of work at the front, and he had said he would ask at work, and he had asked at work, and nothing had come of it.

That was the last of it, because it told him where the grass had come from and who had cut it.

He has known since about the middle of March. He has kept it off his sister. He has kept it off his mother. He has been at number fourteen twice since then, on a Sunday and on a Wednesday, and has sat in her front room for an hour and an hour and a half with a cup of tea and has talked about nothing, and both times he has looked at the side door while he was there.

---

She worked it out on the second Sunday in March.

Not from her mother. From a woman at her children's school who had been sitting in front of her in a car park at about half past eight on the Monday, and who said, in the way people say a thing they have decided is a thing somebody ought to know, that there was a man at that row who was at the last house every morning and had been since about the middle of February.

And she said, "Has he got a key?"

And the woman said, "I don't know. Sorry."

That was on the Monday. She thought about it for nine days.

What she decided on the second Sunday was that she would ring her brother, because he was the one who lived there, and that she would say *there is a man at mother's and he has a key and I do not think she has said she wants one*, and that he would then have to do something about it, and that she would rather he did.

And what she also decided, in about the same four minutes, was that she did not want to have that conversation at all, and that she would prefer her brother to raise it, and that if he raised it she would then be able to say something about how it had gone and would not have had to be the one who put it into the air.

The first decision took about nine seconds. The second took the rest of the Sunday and most of the Monday.

She did not ring him.


On the morning of a Wednesday in that same March he was at his mother's at about half past four with his coat already on, and his sister rang at about half past five.

He had it half way up his chest. He had it about as far as *I have got something to tell you about mother's and it is about a man*, and he had it that far because he had been holding it for about nine days and a man who has held a thing for nine days has it ready.

And then she said, "Is she all right?"

And he said, "She's all right."

And then there was a gap of about four seconds in which the whole of it could have gone into that call, and she was on the other end of a telephone fifty minutes away with two children asleep upstairs and he could see her doing the thing she does with her hand while she is on the phone, which is holding the back of her neck.

And he said, "I'll tell you next time I'm up."

And she said, "Right."

And he got into his van and drove to work and was twenty minutes early and had to sit in the car park with the engine off for about ten of them.

Neither of them has any idea that the other one had the same idea on the same morning. Neither has asked the other. Not one person in this borough has been told any of it by either of them.


He got back to that row at about half past four on the Saturday of the thirteenth of April because he had a reason, and the reason was a bag of something he had left in his mother's hall the week before, and it was not a good reason and he had made it up in the van at four miles off.

He parked at the top end about half past four and walked down.

The man at number fourteen was in the front strip with a line he had started at the wall and was coming back along. He had a garden fork, not the machine. The machine was in his van at the kerb and the van was facing the wrong way for the top end, which meant he had come in the way he always comes in.

The brother stood at his own gate.

He was about ten houses away, which is a bit over a hundred yard, and the only thing between that gate and the front strip is a low paling and about nine foot of somebody's parking.

He watched the man knock. He watched the side door open before the man had finished knocking, which was the part he had not been ready for, because he had expected the wait. He had expected a woman putting shoes on.

The side door was already open and the man was inside in about four seconds and the door was shut behind him, and that was all he saw.

He stood at his own gate for about nine minutes.

A woman came out of number nine with a dog and went past and did not look at him. A van came up the row and turned round at the end because there is nowhere to turn at the bottom of it, the way there is nowhere to turn at the top either, and went back down.

Then the door opened again and the man came out with the fork over his shoulder and put it in the van and shut it, and the side door shut, and the brother got into his van and drove down the row.

He did not knock. He has thought about that for the rest of that day and it is the thing of the day he keeps coming back to, and the reason is not courage, and it is not that he did not care, and it is that if he had knocked, then within about half an hour his mother would have known that one of her children knew, and she is the kind of woman who would then have had to decide something at about ten past five on a Saturday evening on her own, and he has worked out, from about nine years of her, that she will decide whatever she is going to decide about ten minutes before she has to and not one minute before.

So he did not put her in that position. And he has not mentioned any of it to anybody.

---

He rang his sister at about eight that night.

He did not think about it before he picked the telephone up. He picked it up and put the number in and it rang and she said, "That was quick."

And he said, "I'm up next Sunday. Do you want me to bring anything."

And she said, "No. Nothing. You'll only bring something you've brought before."

And he said, "Right."

And she said, "How is she."

And he said, "She's all right. She's got somebody in the garden about four days a week now. He's the one who did the front."

And there was about two seconds on that line.

And she said, "Right."

And he said, "It's the fellow with the van. He cut the front and now he does the back."

And she said, "Ah."

And he said, "I'll see you on Sunday," and put the telephone down.

And she sat on the end of her own bed with it in her hand for about four minutes, and neither of them has ever gone back over those two seconds, and neither of them has ever mentioned the man to the other one since.


At about six on that Saturday the man at number fourteen came out of the side door with the machine in his hand and put it in the back of his van, and the curtains of the front room at that house moved once and did not move again, and the brother was at the kitchen window of number nine with his tea in his hand and watched him reverse out of the row with a bag of grass in a carrier by the side of the machine, and he did not put the curtains back either.

**A MAN SHUT THE SIDE DOOR OF THE LAST HOUSE IN THAT ROW AND STOOD ABOUT FOUR INCH OFF IT WITH HIS HAND STILL ON IT, AND THE BROTHER OF THE WOMAN WHO OWNS THAT HOUSE DROVE ON DOWN THE ROW TO HIS WORK AND SAID NOTHING TO ANYBODY ABOUT IT THAT NIGHT.**
