# Chapter 0624 — The Woman on the Other Side of the Table

It is Friday the twenty-sixth of February 2021 and it is in this borough, and it starts at about a quarter past seven in the morning and it does not finish until his wife has gone up.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY SIXTIES HE TALKS TO AT A CANTEEN TABLE TOLD HIM ACROSS IT THAT HE HAD BEEN ASKING AFTER HER KNEE AND THEN STOPPED, AND HE KNOWS HE HAS NEVER ASKED AFTER ANYBODY, AND HE HAS SPENT THE DAY TRYING TO GET AT A MONTH HE WAS IN FOUR TIMES.**

He is a man in his late twenties. He lives in this borough and drives a van out of a yard and he is married, and there is a daughter in her early twenties who does not live with them and who he is on terms with.

The woman in her early sixties is the only person in the last year who has talked to him for longer than it takes to hand something across a table.

---

The whole of what was said took very little of the morning, at a table by the window of a canteen with two hundred people going through it, at about twenty past eight.

He had done the first lot of the day and had come in, and she was at the table by the window with a tray she had not started.

And he said, "Can I sit down."

And she said, "Go on."

And he sat down and she moved her bag off the chair.

And he said, "How's the leg."

And she said, "It's the same."

And he said, "It'll be the cold."

And she said, "It's February."

And he said, "Well."

And she said, "They tell you it's the cold."

And he said, "Is it not."

And she said, "My mam had that leg for years and she went straight down that hill in December and there was nobody on it to stop her."

And he said, "Right."

And she said, "You asked after me, you know."

What he did with it is worth setting down as it came, because he has been back over the whole day in that order and has never got past the fourth item.

One: he said no.

Two: she repeated it, and he said no again, and she then said, "Well, you did, a good while back, and you didn't ask how it went, and that's what I mean, and now I'm telling you how it went."

Three: he said, "I don't remember asking after you."

Four: she said, "You came up to me in the queue about a month ago. You asked how my knee was. And I said it was no better. And you didn't ask anything else. And that is why I'm saying this."

And he said, "Right."

And she said, "Because you were right to ask."

And he said, "Was I."

And she said, "You did the right thing and you did it once and then you stopped, and I have thought about that more than you would want to know."

He has got four things and he has had them since about half past eight.

One: he cannot remember it.

Two: he is almost certain he cannot remember it, because he knows what he does with a queue and he does not stop and talk to anybody in one.

Three: it is possible he did it and has forgotten, because he was tired, because it was a Monday, and because there have been a good many Mondays.

Four: and this is the one that has taken the day, and it arrived on the walk back to the yard.

Four: she is not talking about a queue.

She said *you did the right thing and you did it once and then you stopped*. She said *I have thought about that more than you would want to know*. She put a tray down on a table in front of her and made a joke about her mother, and she did all of that while telling a man in his late twenties that he had been thinking about her leg.

Nobody thinks about a stranger's knee. That is the whole thing. She has built a shape out of a sentence he may or may not have said, and she has been living in it, and she has not been wrong about her own life, and there is nothing in it she has got to be ashamed of.

And he did not do it.

Or he did do it, once, and meant nothing by it, and she has had a month of a man thinking about her.

He cannot tell which, and the not being able to tell is the whole of what he has carried from about half past eight to now.

He went back to the yard and he did the rest of the day.

He was out from about ten and he was in about twenty places, and he can tell you every one of them and he can tell you what he said at each of them, and at not one of them did he ask after anybody.

He has been over that twice this afternoon.

That is the fact that undoes it, and he did not have it until early afternoon, and it took him the rest of the afternoon to be sure it was true.

If he had asked after her, he would have expected to do it again. That is what asking after somebody is. You ask, and it puts the person in your head, and you see them again, and you ask again, or you don't, and either of the two is normal.

If she is right about the shape of it — that he did it once and then stopped — then something stopped him, and he does not remember anything stopping him.

And if he never did it at all, then she has built a shape out of a stranger being polite in a queue, and she has had a month of it, and there is no event anywhere that would prove her wrong.

At about four he went back and she had gone and her tray was cleared and he stood at the window where the table had been.

At about half past five his wife came through the canteen on her way out and he was still standing there.

And she said, "What are you doing."

And he said, "Nothing."

And she said, "You've been stood there since I came in."

And he said, "Have I."

And she said, "You were stood there at about ten to five as well."

And he said, "Was I."

And she said, "You're stood there like a man waiting on a bus."

And he said, "That's about it."

And she said, "Is that a joke?"

And he said, "No."

And she said, "Who was it."

And he said, "Nobody."

And she said, "Who was it."

And he said, "The woman off the third table with the tray."

And his wife said, "Ah."

And he said, "Why."

And his wife said, "No reason."

And he said, "You know her."

And his wife said, "I know of her. Everybody knows of her."

And he said, "What's her name."

And his wife said, "I don't know."

He went home at about twenty to seven and his wife went up at about half past and he did not follow her straight away.

He stood in his own front room for a while.

At about seven on a Friday evening, in his own front room, this is the whole of what he has, and he has been back over it.

There is a woman in her early sixties in this borough who has spent about a month on the belief that a man in his late twenties asked after her knee, and thought about it at her kitchen table, and told him about it over a table in a canteen with two hundred people going through it, and was not embarrassed and made him a joke first.

He has never asked after anybody. He is certain of that this evening in a way he was not certain of at ten o'clock this morning.

He cannot prove it either. He was in about twenty places today. He has been tired a lot of this year. She was specific about the queue and about a month ago, and he has been in that canteen four times in a month, and he has spoken to her for longer than it takes to hand something across a table, and he cannot remember which of the four it was.

And he has not asked her. He had until about four this afternoon and did not use it, and can give no reason he believes in, and has not gone looking for one.

And he has not told his wife, and his wife knows a woman at the third table and does not know her name, and looked at him and said *ah*, and he has been standing in his front room for a quarter of an hour.

**SHE TOLD HIM ACROSS A TABLE THAT HE HAD BEEN ASKING AFTER HER KNEE AND HAD STOPPED, AND HE KNOWS HE HAS NEVER ASKED AFTER ANYBODY, AND HE COULD HAVE ASKED HER AT FOUR O'CLOCK AND DID NOT, AND HE HAS SPENT THE DAY ON A MONTH HE CANNOT PUT HIS HAND ON AND DOES NOT KNOW IF IT WAS HIS.**
