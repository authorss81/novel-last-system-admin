# Chapter 0675 — The Lid Has Not Shut

Monday the eighteenth of October 2021, in this borough. She is in her own house from first light until dark with nobody in it, and the whole of the day is a parcel on the left of her own kitchen table that is not fish any more and a bin lid that has not shut since the summer.

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT PUTS A TINNED FISH SHE DID NOT ORDER AND CANNOT GIVE BACK ON TOP OF THE COAL IN A SHED SHE HAS NOT OPENED SINCE BEFORE CHRISTMAS, DOES NOT PUT THE PADLOCK ON THE HASP, TELLS A MAN AT THE KERB SHE HAS NO PARCEL, AND GOES ROUND THE SIDE IN HER COAT TO DO IT.**

She is a woman in her late seventies. She is on her own all day. One man speaks to her at the kerb for about a minute and tells her three things she did not ask him about.

---

She wakes with the passage light still on and the brush still in the middle of the runner, and she steps over it on her way to the kitchen, right foot then left, without looking down, the way she has done since the first of September.

It is colder than yesterday in a way that has come in overnight rather than gone out during the day, and she puts her cardigan on over her dressing gown before she puts the kettle on.

She empties the bowl under the bath tap with the folded cloth in it, and carries it through to the bath, and wrings the cloth into the bowl again and puts it back under the drip. The tap has not stopped since before Christmas and she has not expected it to. She looks at the brown mark on the bathroom ceiling while her hands are busy and the mark is about the same as it was on the fifteenth and she can stand that.

Then she comes down and stands in the kitchen doorway and looks at the left-hand side of her own table.

The parcel is where it has been since the twelfth. It is about the size of two loaves and it came off a van for number twelve and was brought up the path at night by a lad who lives at number fourteen, and there is no name on it and no order number and nothing written on the side of it in her own hand, and there is not going to be.

What has happened to it since the twelfth is that it has stopped being cold.

That is the whole of it, and it is the whole of the day. It arrived with a smell of ice and newspaper coming off it and a white skin on the top of the box where the cold had got into the card, and now the cold has gone out of it and something has come up out of it instead, quietly, overnight, and the bottom corner of it on the table is damp.

There is a damp mark on her own kitchen table under the corner of the box, about the size of a coin.

She wipes it with the cloth and the mark does not lift, and she wipes it again with the dry side, and it does not lift.

---

She wants to put the thing in the bin, and she decided about that twice already in the summer, once in the morning and once at night, and both times she decided not to.

She wants to open it, and she has a knife in the drawer with the string and the fuses and the tea bags, and she has had a hand on that drawer twice since the twelfth and has not taken the knife out, because a box of tinned fish is a thing a person opens and eats and there is nobody in that house to eat it with.

She wants to know who sent it, and that is the one of the three she is not going to do at all.

The envelope on the table is face up square with the edge of the table, pencil side up, six things on the back in pencil and nothing crossed off, and there is no pencil in that house and there has not been since before Christmas, and the envelope does not move either.

The table stands dead flat. A man put a card in the leg of it in the summer and planed a piece of ash to a quarter of an inch and sawn it flush under the window-side foot, and a tradesman can see that from six feet away, and a damp mark on the top of it can be seen by anybody at all.

She puts the cloth over the box and then takes it off again, because a cloth over the box would be a thing she had covered it with.

---

She gets the bin out at about ten, which means carrying it down the passage and stepping over the brush with one hand on the wall, and out of the front door, which is shut when she shuts it and does not latch and never has, and down the path and out through a gate that is standing back against the wall of the coal shed with both drop bolts in the pocket of her apron.

The lid has not shut since the summer. She has known that since June. She lifts it with her heel and it comes up about two inches and stays there, and the bin has to go out with the lid up like that, on the front of the collection, kept in place by nothing.

She puts it out and stands on the grass and looks down the road. There is nothing on the road at this hour.

---

The man comes at about eleven and does the bin and comes back up the path, and he is about forty-five and she has seen him twice before this year.

He puts the bin back and looks at the lid.

"That's not shutting, that."

"No."

"You'd want a new hinge on that, or a new bin. That's the hinge, look at it, that's gone soft." He lifts it two inches with one finger. "It'll come off in the wind one of these."

"Right."

"Have you not got a bin you can close?"

"I've got this one."

He looks at the road and then at the gate standing back against the coal shed wall with both bolts in her apron pocket, and she watches him look at it, and he decides the way people decide, and he goes back down the path to the lorry.

Then he stops and turns round.

"There's a parcel in there."

She looks at him.

"That box, that's got a parcel in it."

"It's got something in it."

"I can see it's got something in it." He is not unkind about it. "That's not bin stuff, love. That'll not go in with the general. They'll take it out and put it back on the kerb and you'll have the same box back by Friday."

"Right."

"You want to take that down the tip."

"I know."

"The tip's the one off the ring road, past the garden place. You want it in a bag, not a box, because they don't want boxes. Boxes go on the pile." He waits for her to say something about the tip and she says nothing, because she has been on that road her whole life and there is no tip on it, and asking a man in a lorry at the kerb where to take a box of fish she did not order is a sentence she has not got.

"You'll want to go in a car," he says. "You'd not want to carry it."

And he goes back down the path.

She says nothing at all about a parcel, and the man has told her three things she did not ask him about, and she says thank you and means nothing by it.

---

She stands in her own passage with the door behind her and the brush in front of her and thinks about a tip off the ring road past a garden place, and about carrying a box down a road, and about a car.

Then she thinks about the coal shed, which is eight feet from where she is standing, because the gate leans against it and the shed is between the gate and the side wall of her own house.

She has not opened it since before Christmas.

It has coal in it, most of a sack, and a shovel, and the padlock hanging on the hasp with the hasp down, because she has never locked it either, because there has never been a reason to and because the key to it is on a hook behind the back door that she has not needed since before Christmas.

She goes out through the front door again and round the side on the walked-flat line, and the shed door is stiff at the top corner and comes open about four inches and then comes the rest of the way, and the cold in it is the outside cold and it goes over her face.

She puts her hand in for the shovel and then stops, and leaves the shovel, and goes back for the box.

The parcel comes up the path in two goes, with a hand at the near corner and a hand at the far corner, and it is heavier than it was on the twelfth, which is the one thing about it that has certainly changed.

She puts it on the coal, at the back, against the wall, with the printed side up so she can see the corner of it when she comes in with the shovel in the dark.

Then she stands in the shed doorway with her hand on the frame.

She could put the padlock on the hasp. It is the work of a second and she has the key in her pocket on the hook behind the back door. If she puts it on, the box is shut in.

She does not do it. She lets the door come back against the frame and she does not push it, and the padlock stays where it has hung since before Christmas, and she stands there a while longer than the reason for standing there.

---

She comes in and puts the kettle on and takes the tape sheet off the back room window and shakes it out, because there has been rain on it in the night and the top corner somebody else's tape has come away again.

She wipes the tape sheet down, dries it on the cloth on the drainer, puts it back on the same corner, presses it down along the whole length with her thumb, and presses it a second time because that is how she does it.

The towel on the drainer has gone stiff in the corner. She wrings it out and hangs it over the rail of the oven and it stands out from the oven about three inches the whole length of the front of it and does not go soft.

She is in her own kitchen at midday with a cold house and a coat on and nothing to put in front of anybody, and she eats toast standing up because the green chair with the cushion on it has got a cushion on it and she is not going to sit on a chair in a cold room on purpose.

She puts the second toast on the plate and looks at the parcel mark on the table, which is still there and is now a ring rather than a mark, because the water has been out into the wood and come back into the wood again.

She has not opened the drawer for the knife. She has not gone down the road to look at the gate and the coal shed with somebody. She has not asked a soul where the tip is or whether a man in a lorry might carry a box for her.

Four of six is done. There is no pencil in that house and there has not been since before Christmas, and a parcel of tinned fish she did not order is sitting on top of half a sack of coal behind an unlocked shed door in a garden with a gate standing open on a road with nobody on it, and there is no way on this earth of writing it down.

---

**A WOMAN OF SEVENTY-EIGHT HAS PUT A THING SHE DID NOT ASK FOR AND CANNOT GIVE BACK ON TOP OF HER OWN COAL, HAS ASKED A MAN AT HER OWN KERB FOR NOTHING AT ALL AND BEEN TOLD THREE THINGS BY HIM THAT SHE DID NOT ASK FOR, AND HAS LEFT THE SHED DOOR AND THE PADLOCK EXACTLY AS SHE FOUND THEM, AND FOUR OF SIX IS DONE AND NOTHING HAS BEEN CROSSED OFF.**
