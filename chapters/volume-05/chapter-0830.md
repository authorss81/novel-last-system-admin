# Chapter 0830 — Two Bars And An Empty Rack

Friday the twenty-first of July 2023, in that bay from the middle of the morning until the shutter comes down, and two lengths of bar leaning by an office door that are going somewhere are the whole of that Friday.

**TWO LENGTHS OF BAR ARE LEANING BY THE OFFICE DOOR AT THE LOCK UP AND SHE MEANS TO MAKE A RACK TO REST THEM ON, AND THE WOMAN WHO BOOKS WORK IS IN THE OFFICE WITH THE DOOR SHUT ALL DAY AND IS HEARD ONCE THROUGH IT.**

That bay is the open end of the lock up with the bench along the front and the shutter over the door, and by the office door inside it there are two lengths of scaffold bar leaning against the wall.

They are going with the office when it goes.

One is about six feet long and the other about five, both steel, with paint on the ends and rust in the middle where hands have been. The first bar, the one she stood on in the winter of another year, is not the subject and is not used and stays where it was by the far wall.

She means to make a rack to get these two off the floor and out of the way of feet.

A rack for bars is two uprights with cross timbers between them, off the ground, with the bars laid across the top. The height of it is set by nothing but the bars themselves. Too low and the ends trail in the dirt. Too high and the whole lot topples when one bar is lifted off. The right height is the length of the longer bar and nothing else, because a bar laid across a rack wants to bear along its whole length and not hang off the ends.

Here is the whole of it. The longer bar is six feet, so the cross timbers go at a height that will take six feet without the ends touching. The shorter bar will lie inside that with room to spare, which is how it should be, because two bars of different lengths want one measure and the measure wants to be the longer one.

She cuts two uprights from a broken pallet board and two cross timbers from the same, and she frames them square with screws out of the tin in the drawer of the bench, not nails, so they can come apart again with a driver.

The uprights go against the back wall of the bay on either side of the door, clear of the shutter guides, with the cross timbers between them at the height of the longer bar, levelled with her eye along the top.

The woman who books work is in the office with the door shut all day and the shutter up over her, and is heard once through the door in the middle of the day asking after a bill, which is office noise through a shut door and nothing about the bay. She does not come out and she does not look at the bay twice.

“Has the post been?” she calls.

“It is on the bench by the door,” she tells her, without opening it.

---

The bars go up in the afternoon.

She lifts the longer one first, carries it flat to the rack, and lays it across the two cross timbers with an equal overhang at each end. Then the shorter one beside it, inside the ends of the first, so the two lie side by side with air between them.

She steps back and sights along them. Neither end touches the floor and neither end hangs past the timber by more than a hand, and the rack stands square under them without rocking.

The pallet bay with the four pallets on edge is not touched in any of it, and the bench with its spliced legs is not touched, and the box behind the bench is not opened.

When the shutter comes down at the end of the day she stands in the bay and looks at what is there. Two bearers are down at the height of the longer bar with the two lengths across them and nothing else on them, and the middle of the bay is empty round them.
