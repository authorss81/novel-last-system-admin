# Chapter 0612 — The Lamp and the Armchair

The Friday of the twenty-fifth of December 2020, in this borough, at about nine in the morning and through to about six in the evening.

**SHE CARRIES A SMALL CAR AND A CLOTH BAG DOWN ONE TERRACE AND UP ANOTHER ON FRIDAYS AND HAS BEEN DOING IT SINCE THE JUNE, AND THE HOUSE AT THE BOTTOM OF THAT TERRACE HAS HAD SOMEBODY IN IT SINCE THE SEPTEMBER, AND TODAY IS THE FIRST DAY SHE HAS FOUND OUT.**

She is thirty-one. The house belongs to a woman in her mid seventies who is in a place about eight miles off and has been since the September, and the house is not empty in the sense anybody means. It is got a woman in it, an old one, who is the sister-in-law of the woman who is away.

The thing she carries is not shopping. She has never once carried a bag of shopping up that hill.

---

Here is what she does.

The arm is a standard one with a shade on it that she has to reach and she cannot reach it from a chair, so she gets up on the chair, and the lamp is a small one with a short flex that is plugged in behind the chair and it needs unplugging and plugging in again.

That is the whole of the lamp and it takes the length of a cup of tea.

The chair is a wing chair with a cushion on it and the cushion is the reason she goes, because she has decided since the June that a chair with a cushion on it in an empty house is a thing that gets sat on by somebody with a cold in it.

She sits on it long enough to have counted and gets up.

She has done that on Fridays since the June, and she has never told the old woman and the old woman has never asked, and the two of them have been in the same house on a Friday a good number of times.

Here is what happened before the summer, because that is where the carrying came from.

She was asked. That is the part she has not been able to make sound, however many times she has turned it over.

The old woman did not ask for the chair and did not ask for the lamp and did not know there was a chair and a lamp. She asked her sister-in-law's daughter-in-law — that is what she did — to go and see whether the house was all right, and said it in a kitchen, and meant it entirely.

And she went. And she found the house all right. And she went the next Friday.

And she has never worked out where the going next Friday came from and she has looked for it, and the only candidates she can find are these.

One: she did not like the thought of the house sitting there with nothing done to it.

Two: she liked it, and liked is not a reason you can give a person in a kitchen.

Three: she is thirty-one, she is not at work, and she lives alone in a flat with a view of a car park, and of everything she could have got herself this was the one that came to her hand.

She has never said three out loud to anybody and she has not ruled it out either.

Here is today.

She went at about nine in the morning because she has found that the house is better early, when the sun is coming over the ridge and the whole of the back of it is lit, and there is a bus that goes up at about twenty to nine that gets her to the bottom of the terrace.

She had the cloth bag. She put it down on the step and got her keys out, and she stood on the step with the keys in her hand.

That is the whole of what happened and she has thought about it since more than she has thought about anything else.

She has a set of keys and she has had them since the June and they are the old woman's and the old woman gave them to her in a car park at the end of that road, on the way to signing the paperwork, with a plastic carrier bag of her own stuff in the other hand.

What the old woman said was, "Have got a key for me?"

And she said, "I don't know how I'd use it."

And the old woman said, "You'd go in and put the heating on."

And she said, "Right."

And the old woman said, "And the back one's the same."

And she said, "Right."

And then the old woman got in the car and did not say anything else about it, and she has not said anything about it since, and that was in the June.

So she stood on the step at about a quarter to ten with a key in her hand that opens a house she has no reason to be in.

Here is what she did about it, and it is worth setting out because she has been accused by herself of all three of these at various times since the June and she can only prove one.

She did not go in and do the heating. That was not the arrangement and she was never going to make it the arrangement, because the arrangement was the chair and the lamp on a Friday and nothing else, and the moment she turned a key in that door the key stopped being about the chair.

She did not put the key in her pocket and go down the hill. She has been down that hill without the key about three times since the June and she has known every one of them that she was doing it and it was not the same as having put it in the step.

She did go in.

---

The house was cold, and she has been in it every Friday since the June, and it has always been cold, and that has never once stopped being strange.

The front door was on the latch, and she has never worked out whether that is because somebody in that house leaves it on the latch for her or because it has been on the latch since long before she had a key.

She went through to the back and switched the lamp on, and the shade is a paper one and it made the circle on the wall it always makes. She moved the newspaper off the top of the cupboard and cleaned it, which she does, and she did the top of the fridge, which she does.

She got the chair out from the wall and set it in front of the light and dusted the seat with the cloth from the bag.

And she unplugged the lamp and plugged it in again so that the shade is where it should be, which is not really a thing and which she does anyway.

And she sat down on it long enough to have counted, and got up, and put it back against the wall, and unplugged the lamp, and she was in and out of that house in the time it takes to look at a chair twice.

She did not do it for anybody. There was nobody there. She did it and she was alone in a cold house with a bag of her own cloth and a pair of her own hands and there was not one moment of that in which anybody had asked her to.

And that is the part she has not got past, and it is not a noble thing and it is not even an interesting thing, and here it is.

She has worked out that she has been coming up that hill for something to be needed. She has worked out that she is not needed. She has worked out that the old woman has a sister-in-law who lives in that house and who is in it most of the week and who has the heating on and who has known the lamp and the chair all along and who has said nothing.

And she got in her car at the bottom of the terrace at about ten past ten with her keys still in her hand and she did not put them in her pocket, and she put them back in the bag, and the bag went on the passenger seat, and she has not used them since and she has not given them back.

The rest of the day is a kitchen and a table and a woman coming in at about two, and one of them says something.

And the other one said, "You'll not go up there."

And she said, "No."

And the other one said, "Not since she went."

And she said, "I've not been since before."

That was not true and she did not correct it and she went on cutting onions, and the other one did not press it, and that is the arrangement between the two of them, which is that things said in that kitchen are taken at the value they were put in at.

**SHE WENT UP THE TERRACE AT A QUARTER TO TEN WITH THE OLD WOMAN'S KEYS IN HER HAND AND DID THE LAMP AND THE CHAIR AND CAME DOWN AT TEN PAST TEN AND PUT THE KEYS BACK IN THE CLOTH BAG ON THE PASSENGER SEAT, AND THERE WAS NOBODY IN THAT HOUSE AND NOBODY ASKED HER AND SHE HAS NOW DECIDED SHE IS NOT NEEDED AND HAS NOT DECIDED WHAT SHE IS GOING TO DO ABOUT IT.**
