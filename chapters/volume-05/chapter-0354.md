# Chapter 0354 — The Twenty-Fourth

Sunday the twenty-fourth of September 2017, from about ten in the morning until about half past nine at night, a parade, a shop with a shutter, the low end of a street, a hall on a meter, and a first-floor corridor in a block by the water, in Tideglass.

**A MONTH WENT THROUGH THIS BOROUGH AND ON THE LAST SUNDAY OF IT A WOMAN AT THE END OF A CORRIDOR COUNTED A SUM SHE HAD BEEN COUNTING SINCE THE WINTER, AND THAT SUM BELONGS TO A LINE UNDER AN ENTRY IN A BOOK THAT IS OPEN ON A SHELF IN THAT SAME BUILDING, AND THAT LINE IS STILL BLANK, AND SHE WROTE NOTHING AND TOLD NOBODY.**

The shutters on that parade went up at about half past seven, and the man who puts them up put the ladder against the fourth shop, then the fifth, and then came back to the third one halfway through the morning and did that one off the end of it, because the third one takes longer than the other two and it always has.

The man in his forties came up that parade at about ten with his bag and went into the shop and asked for a length of four by two and the woman who works in a shop cut it and he paid for it, and neither of them said anything else for about a minute.

"Four," said the woman at that counter.

He did not answer it, and he took his length and went out into the parade, and that is the whole of that and it has happened about nine times since the twelfth of January and neither of them has ever said what the four are and neither of them could name two of them between them.

It is four. The number is the same one it was in the middle of January and neither of them has put a thing on it or taken a thing off it, and nobody in this borough could stand any two of the four next to each other and see what the pair of them makes of, and nothing about it moved today.

About nine people were on nothing that day and not one of them was on the same thing as any of the others. The man in his forties is on nothing and has been since the middle of the year. The woman who works in a shop is on nothing. The man who puts shutters up is on nothing but his own rounds. The man in his sixties is on nothing and is in that hall on a Sunday morning for an hour with a cup.

The man in a fleece was at that bus stop on the top of that parade at about ten on his way to work, and the man who works nights was standing at the same stop at the same hour and got a bus at about a quarter to ten, and neither of them looked at the other.

At about half past two the man in his thirties was at the low end of a street with his bag on the wall beside him, doing nothing whatever, and he stayed there the best part of half an hour. Two women went past him and a boy went past on a bike and not one of them said anything to him. He picked the bag up afterwards and walked back up the parade past three shops that were shut, and there was nothing at the low end of that street he had come down there for and there has not been this month.

The man at the end of a street was at the top of that parade at about three on a Sunday for no reason anybody has asked him about. The man from the parade was stood in a doorway at about four with a bag of chips, eating out of it with his fingers. The woman of about fifty-five was at the end of a first-floor corridor at about half past nine in the evening with a jug in each hand, and she has not been on anything since she came into that room and nobody put her on anything.

---

The hall on Carrow Lane was open from about ten to about four on the Sunday and there was no meeting and the man who does that hall had them up off their brackets by ten and the urn going, with a cloth on his shoulder, going round the edge of one.

The man in his sixties came in at about half past ten, sat in the split seat with his cup, was on his own for about fifty minutes, said good afternoon to the man who does that hall on the way out at about ten to twelve, and went up the road.

The woman who works in a shop was in that hall on the Sunday afternoon from about half past two until about half past four, and the woman of about fifty-five was in that hall at about half past three for about twenty minutes, and the two of them were in that room together for twenty minutes and did not speak, and the woman who works in a shop has said since that she did not see her, and the woman of about fifty-five has not said either way.

At about half past two the man who works nights came into the shop off the parade for a length of pipe and the man in his forties was at the counter for a box of screws at the same time, and the man who works nights said it before he had asked for anything.

"What is the fourth one," said the man who works nights.

The woman who works in a shop looked up.

"Ask him," she said, and the man in his forties said, "I could not tell you that in this shop."

"You could tell me most things."

"Not that one," said the man in his forties, and paid for his screws, and the man who works nights said right, and carried his pipe out into the parade.

Nobody asked him a second time and he has not asked anybody else, and he did not know while he was standing at that counter that there were four of anything.

At about a quarter to five the lamp outside that hall came on of its own accord and burned there until about half past six the next morning, and a man went up the parade at about five past five with a bag on his shoulder, and there was nobody at the window to have seen what was in it.

At about half past five the man in his forties put his own key in the street door of that building by the water, and climbed that outside stair and went along the corridor, and stood about nine foot from the end wall with his hands in his pockets for a couple of minutes, and then he came back down, left that door on the latch and went home.

He did not go into the bay at the end of that corridor. He has not gone into that bay since the middle of August and there is nothing in it that concerns him, and the book on the second shelf up in there is lying open where it has been open since before the summer.

The register is at nineteen entries out of nineteen. It goes in a yard lamp, written down as gone in about November two years back, and it goes in under a heading somebody has been keeping back for that class of thing, and the column under that heading is empty and has been since the lamp went in. Past that entry the page carries on for two fingers and then runs out well short of the bottom of the book and nobody closed that gap and the book has got it.

That line is the one fixed thing in that building, and it is blank, and it was blank at the beginning of the month and it is blank now, and the book was not opened today by anybody at all.

---

She did it at about half past nine in the evening, on the landing, sitting down on the second stair with her back against the wall, the way anybody counts on their fingers.

She has not been in that bay since before the summer and she is not going. She does not know what the other eighteen of them say and she has never once tried, and she has not laid a hand on that book, and the one thing she does know about it is the last line of it and how long that line has been waiting.

She has been counting since before the summer. She counted it in her kitchen off a calendar in April and counted it again in July and told the number out loud in that hall in July and was told that nobody was going to check it, and she said that she was not asking anybody to, and that is all of that.

She counted it again tonight, on the second stair, on her fingers, and the figure is a thousand and thirty-three, and that is what it is, and there is no other way of arriving at it and nothing on the page to set it beside.

Then she got up off that stair and put her hand on the rail and went down to the bottom of it for the water, with a jug in each hand and one hooked through the other, and she brought the street door back to behind her at the foot of it the way she has since June.

**A WOMAN COUNTED A SUM ON HER FINGERS ON THE SECOND STAIR OF AN OUTSIDE STAIR ON A SUNDAY NIGHT AND WROTE NOTHING DOWN, AND NOTHING WAS TOUCHED IN THAT CORRIDOR TODAY, AND THE LAST ENTRY IN THAT BOOK IS STILL A LAMP AND THE LINE UNDER IT IS STILL BLANK.**
