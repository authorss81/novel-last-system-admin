# Chapter 0759 — The Bed At The End Of The Yard

Tuesday the twentieth of September 2022, in this borough. She is in her own house and yard at number ten from morning until the middle of the day, and the whole of it is a bed dug too deep with the fieldstones left in the bottom of it.

**A WOMAN IN HER EARLY THIRTIES DIGS THE BED AT THE END OF HER OWN YARD AND A BOY OVER THE FENCE IS LET IN AND DIGS IT HIS WAY, AND SHE FINDS THE STONES SHOWING IN THE BOTTOM AND DOES NOT GO OVER IT AGAIN.**

She is a woman in her early thirties at number ten on Tuesday. The boy over the fence comes to the gate in the morning and is let through it, and works the bed with her.

---

She is in the kitchen from early with the washing in and folded on the table. The drawer under the worktop is shut and the hammer and the screwdriver and the folded paper with three lines on it are all in it and none of them comes out. The line stands bare across the yard. The strip of ply stands against the front edge of the shed where the felt has gone, and the felt's own corner is still lifting at the top of it.

At the end of the yard behind the shed there is the bed. It has been on the same ground for years and this year it has been beans and a row of courgettes that went hollow and a nasturtium that went everywhere, and what is left of it is the stalks cut down a month ago and gone brown at the top, and couch grass coming through from the path side in mats as thick as a door mat.

She has a fork and a spade and a barrow, and she means to fork it through from the top down, take the mats off the path side out whole, get the couch out in its length, and leave the fine stuff in a heap to go down over the winter.

The boy is at the gate before she has the fork into the ground.

---

"You have not asked me."

"I have not asked you."

"You have not asked me and you are going to dig it." He puts his fingers through the gap in the gate where the latch does not reach. "I can do the other end."

"You have never dug."

"I have dug." He gets his fingers further through. "I dug for my uncle in a garden with a wall on one side and he told me it was the wrong way and then he made me do it the right way and then we dug it up again."

"That is not the same as this."

"That is the same as this and worse." He looks at the barrow. "Can I have the barrow."

She looks at him through the gap and then at the bed with the stalks standing in it, and then she undoes the latch, which she has not done for him since the summer.

"You will not take the spade." She hands him the fork and keeps the spade against her own leg. "That is not a fork job and you will do yourself an injury with it inside ten minutes. Fork only, and you go slow, and if I say stop you stop."

"You will not have to tell me."

"You will want me to by the second flag." She goes down the side of the shed. "Come on then, and do not walk in the bed. Walk in the path and step off it sideways."

---

He is better than she expects and worse than she hoped. He can lift the mat of couch with the fork and shake it and get the whole root mat up out of the bed in one piece without it breaking, which she cannot do and has never been able to do, and he puts the mats in a row along the shed wall with their roots all one way and tidy, and after a while she stops looking at the edge he is doing and gets on with the middle.

Then the soil gets into the stones.

"What is that." He has stopped with the fork in the ground.

"That is the fieldstone coming up."

"It is under me."

"That is what I have been telling you for ten minutes. Keep it out of the bottom and put it on the path." She pulls her own fork out and knocks the clod off. "You cannot leave it down there. It goes down first and it is there for the winter and the frost gets at it and it heaves the bed up and the bed will be a lump of stones in March."

"Where do I put it."

"On the path. Along the wall. Not on the grass."

He puts it on the path, and for about twenty minutes that is how it goes, and then he finds a rhythm of his own. He goes down deeper than she does. He opens a spit and a half where she takes a spit, and the bottom of it goes the colour of a wet pipe, and the couch goes into the sides of the bank instead of coming up in mats, and the stones come up mixed through the whole of it rather than kept out, and the fine stuff she wanted left in a heap goes nowhere at all, because there is no separate fine stuff down there any more; it is all mixed into the bottom.

She says nothing about it for the length of two rows.

"You have gone down a foot and a half."

"You have not got deep enough."

"I have got deep enough for a bed of beans."

"You have not got deep enough for anything." He leans on the fork. "My uncle went down as far as his boot and he told me that was right and I have never seen a bed go wrong from being too deep."

"Your uncle has never had stones in it."

The boy looks down at the bottom of the row he has just opened, where the pale fieldstones are lying all along it in a single layer with the fine stuff packed over and round them, and does not answer that.

They finish the bed by the middle of the day. It is not a bad bed and it is not her bed. The couch is out of it, which is the thing she wanted, and that is because of the boy and not because of her. Underneath that it is deep and heavy and stony, and the bottom of it will not come down to the depth she wanted it at, and the stones in it cannot be got out now without walking the whole bed again from one end to the other and picking them out by hand, which is two days she has not got.

He stands at the end of the bed looking down it.

"That is good dug."

"That is good dug." She puts her fork against the shed and stands beside him. "That is deeper than it needs to be and it has stone in it and we are not going to find that out again until March."

"You are not pleased."

"I am not pleased and I am not going to be, and neither are you, and that is the whole of this morning." She looks at the row of mats along the shed wall with their roots all one way. "You have done the top of that bed better than I could and the bottom of it worse than a dog would, and both of those are true and I am not going to pretend otherwise to keep you here."

He looks at the fence and then back at the bed.

"Can I still come."

"You can still come." She picks up the barrow. "Not to dig. You can come and sit on the shed step and I will not have you on this."

---

She sends him out of the gate and stands a moment at the back door with the barrow in her hands, and then she goes back down the yard to the end of the bed, because there is one thing she wants to see before she goes in.

She looks down into it. The bottom is dark and wet and there are the stones showing along the whole length of the second row in a pale line, packed over and round and already settling where she has walked the length of it.

Then she walks the whole bed, once, from the near end to the far end, at the top, without putting the fork in. From here the first row and the third row are a good colour and even, and the second row along the middle is a paler stripe with the stones showing through it in a line, and she can see from one end of the yard to the other that that is the row the boy dug and not her.

She stands at the far end of her own bed looking back down it, and she does not pick up the fork.
