# Chapter 0825 — Stones In The Order They Came Off

Monday the third of July 2023, in that side passage at number twelve from first thing until the heat has come off the paving, and about six feet of brick edging that is going to be dug up anyway is the whole of that Monday.

SIX FEET OF BRICK EDGING ALONG THE SIDE PASSAGE AT NUMBER TWELVE IS LOOSE AND SHE MEANS TO TAKE IT UP AND SORT WHAT IS THERE, AND HER SISTER IS IN THE KITCHEN WITH THE DOOR OPEN AND IS CALLED TO THE FRONT GATE ONCE WHILE THE STONES GO DOWN THROUGH IT.

That passage is four feet wide between the house wall and the old brick wall at the side, and it runs from the back door to the front gate, and along one side of it there is a line of edge stones where the paving meets a narrow bed that has not grown anything for years.

She laid that edging herself out of brick on end, cut halves and whole ones mixed, set in sand with their tops level with the flags. It has been there since before the paving was lifted in May, and it kept the sand under the flags from washing out into the bed every time it rained hard.

The line runs on the house side of the passage, which means the working side is the bed, and the bed is a foot wide at the front and narrows to a hand's width at the back where the lilac roots have taken the ground and the wall has taken the rest. She has dug against that bed before. A bar put into it comes up with a root in it every time, so the spade is the tool for the sides and the bar is for the bottom of each stone where it has set.

The stones are loose now along about six feet of it. She can put a toe under three of them and lift them without bending, and two more rock if she stands on them, and one at the far end has sunk until its top is below the flags. Six feet of run at the width of a brick stood on end comes to sixteen stones, and sixteen is what comes off.

She starts at the back end with a trowel and a short bar and a bucket, and a piece of sacking on the flags to put the good ones on.

The first stone comes up with soil on it. Brick on end does not lift clean after ten years in sand. The sand has gone into the pores of it and the soil has come up from below, and the bottom inch of it is dark where it has been in the ground.

She knocks the soil off against the bucket and looks at the arris.

An edge stone lives or dies by its arris. The top outer corner of it is what the eye runs along, and it is what the boot strikes, and it is what keeps the line of the passage true. A stone with the arris gone is not a bad stone for a wall or for the bottom of a bed, but it is no use on an edge where anybody walks, because the corner goes on crumbling and the sand goes with it.

This one has the arris gone all along one side. The corner is rounded off to the width of a thumb, and under the thumb the brick is soft and comes away in crumbs. She puts it on the flags to the left, with the bad side up so she knows it at a glance.

The second one is better. The arris is sharp on three sides and only a little worn on the fourth, and the faces are flat and the brick rings when she taps it with the trowel. She puts it on the sacking.

A whole brick and a half are not judged the same way. A whole one stood on end has four arrises to it and a foot of thickness to stand on, and it will go back into a line and keep it. A half is narrower at the top than at the bottom because she cut it with a bolster and did not face it after, and it has two good corners and two that are only cut, and a cut corner is not an arris however square it looks in the hand.

That is why the halves are the ones that go first, and the third stone off is a half, and its two cut corners are sound and its two arrises are not there at all. She feels it with her thumb twice to be sure, because a square corner and a sharp corner are two different things and only the first of them can be felt by hand. The cut faces leave a grey crumb in the print of her thumb and the brick itself under it is as good as anything on that line. It would do for the bottom of a bed and nothing else, so she puts it on the flags with the bad ones.

The fourth is a whole one and it rings off the trowel like a plate, and the arris on it is sharp enough on the outer side that she can run a thumbnail along it and feel the nail bite the skin and stop dead. She lays it on the sacking on its end and it does not want to lie down.

That is the sorting, and that is the whole of the morning.

Stone by stone she works along the six feet, lifting, knocking clean, turning in her hands, feeling the top edge with her thumb. A good stone has a square corner that bites the thumb. A stone that is done has a corner that gives under it, soft as biscuit, and leaves red dust on her skin.

The sand under the stones has not gone anywhere. That is the thing about it, and it is why the stones have come loose and not gone. The wash under this line happened before the edging was laid, and the edging stopped it, and everything under these stones is as dry and as pale as the day it was put in. The stones are loose because the tops have gone round and there is nothing left to pinch the sand, not because anything has been lost out of the bottom.

She puts each one back down on the sacking in the order it came off, head to tail the way they stood, because she will not know the order again once they are mixed, and a line of edge stones laid out of order is a line that runs off in a curve where the whole ones are on the outside of the bend.

The one at the far end that has sunk is the last of the sixteen and it is the worst of the lot. It has gone down about an inch and two, which means there is a hollow under it where the sand has washed in from the bed side and taken the support away. She gets the bar under it and lifts, and it comes up with a lip of dry sand stuck to the bottom of it in one piece, and under the lip the sand is grey and close packed and has never been wet. She knocks the lip off, finds the arris soft along its whole outer side, and it goes on the flags with the others.

By the middle of the morning she has eleven good ones on the sacking in the order they came off, head to tail the way they stood, and five bad ones on the flags.

She looks at the two rows for a while from a crouch. Eleven will lay about four feet and a half, which is short of the six she took up, and the five are not going to make the difference. Whoever digs this passage will find a heap of eleven and a scatter of five and a trench six feet long, and there is no way to say that in the ground.

Her sister is in the kitchen with the door open the whole of it. She can hear the chair move and a cup go down, and the wireless low, and none of it comes out into the passage.

“You have left your basket on the step,” her sister calls.

“I have left it there till the barrow is through,” she tells her. “Leave it where it is.”

She calls out once to the front gate.

Her sister comes out through the house and stands at the front gate while the stones go down through it. She does not bend and she does not pick one up, and she does not look at the passage twice. She stands with one hand on the gate while the barrow goes past her, and then she goes back in through the house to the kitchen, and the door stays open behind her.

The barrow goes down the passage and back four times, and the wheels find the same line every time because the paving has not lifted and she has not moved. The bucket goes down first with the bad ones in it, and then the barrow comes back with the sacking of good ones on it, and she does not put the stones straight into the barrow because two bricks with a good arris will knock each other off that corner in the first ten yards, and an arris once knocked off is an arris once gone.

There is nothing asked about the passage from either of them, and nothing about the stones, and the hour goes as it came.

---

The bad ones go first. She takes them down to the bottom of the back garden in the bucket, two at a time, and lays them along the foot of the wall where the broken brick bed is, for footing under nothing in particular. They will do there, where no boot goes and no eye runs.

She sets them on edge in the footing rather than flat, because a brick with a rounded arris laid flat is a brick that rocks under a spade and one stood on end is a brick that stays where it is put. It is five courses of nothing at all at the foot of a wall, and it is the one place in that garden where a soft brick is better than a hard one.

The good ones she carries to the front in a stack on the barrow, in the order they came off, head to tail, with sacking between every three so they do not chip.

It is hot by then. The heat has come off the paving in a shine, and the sand under where the edging was is pale and dry, and along the bottom of the shallow trench there is the line where the stones stood, straight as a ruler for six feet.

That line is the reason the whole morning went the way it did. A brick edging does not stay because the bricks are good. It stays because the line stays, and the line is made by every stone being the same width from the front of the house to the gate, and one stone a quarter of an inch narrow puts that quarter of an inch into the run behind it. She is leaving the eleven in the order they stood so that whoever lays them again can put the narrow one back in the narrow place without a set square and without measuring anything.

She sets the stack down by the gate clear of the passage. She lays them head to tail the way they stood, eleven of them, with the best arris uppermost, tight together so they cannot roll.

On top of them she lays a short length of blue string tied in a loop.

She does not explain it. She lays it across the top stone with the loop to the front, tucks the ends under, and steps back. It is not there to tie them. A heap of eleven brick laid on edge does not want a thing tied round it, and if it ever wanted one it would want a wall and not a length of string. It is there so that whoever finds the heap can tell at a glance that somebody stood over these and put the good ones on top and took the bad ones away somewhere else on purpose. It means nothing to anybody but her, which is how she wants it, and it will still be a blue loop on a heap of stone in the spring.

The paving laid in May is not touched in any of it, and the lilac against the house wall is not touched, and the trench where the edging was is swept clean with a brush and left open to the air.

She stands at the back end of the passage when the heat has gone off it and looks down the six feet to the gate. The heap is by the gate in the order the stones came off, the poor ones gone out of it, and on top of it the loop of blue string with its ends tucked under, saying nothing.
