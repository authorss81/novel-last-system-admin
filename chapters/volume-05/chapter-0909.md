# Chapter 0909 — Behind The Cupboard Back

Monday the thirteenth of May 2024, in the cupboard under the bench in the store at the unit while the door stands ajar on the yard. The pocket behind a back board.

**HE HAS A CUPBOARD UNDER THE BENCH IN THE STORE AT THE UNIT WITH A POCKET OF AIR BEHIND ITS BACK BOARD AND HE MEANS TO WALK FINGERTIPS BACKWARD INTO THAT POCKET WITH WRIST BENT AND THE WOMAN FROM TWO DOORS DOWN IS AT THE STORE DOORWAY FROM THE YARD WITH A BROOM IN HER HAND.**

The cupboard has stood under that bench since the store was lined, and the bench above it has been mended once while the cupboard has kept its place.

It is a cupboard of pine boards about three feet wide, with the door hung on the front and the back board set inside the carcass a finger from the wall.

He wants the pocket behind that back board, where the wall sweats and the air sits still.

The back of a cupboard is the one face that gets looked at last of all, and it is the face that says where the box was made to stand.

So the fingertips step backward behind the cupboard back with wrist bent and elbow high.

The air in there feels cool on the skin, the way shut air stays cool past noon, and the back board drags where dust coats it.

His fingers travel back near two inches before meeting firmness, and what is against them is brickwork, cool and coarse, with the paint on it shedding in scales and the joints sandy where the pointing softened seasons past.

He shifts the tips first left, then right along the brick, which yields nothing beneath the nails before turning damp a touch lower.

What makes it damp is sweat. Brick throws damp in spring when warm air meets a wall that spent winter cold, and each morning beads it where the air cannot move.

So the back board never once touches the wall. It stands a finger off the brick with air between, and that moving air has kept the board from rotting at the back.

The woman from two doors down is in the store doorway with a broom in her hand, come from the yard, and she does not sweep.

"Is the cupboard open past dinner?"

"It stands open till the light goes."

"Our shelf boards warped in the damp."

"They warp worst where air cannot get round them."

She turned the broom in her hands and stood it against the doorway without letting the head touch the ground, and stood there with her hands in her coat pockets while the draught came through the store, and then she took the broom back up the yard with the head off the stones.

---

What his fingers find further along, about a foot from the side, is the batten behind the board.

Not a second board. A runner of pine, fixed across behind the back to keep it from bowing, about two inches wide and set upright where the board meets it.

He gets the bent wrist round it and feels the face of it with the pads of his fingers through dust and cool still air.

It is a back runner, put there when the cupboard was made, to throw the back off the brick and let air up behind it.

The cupboard stands a finger clear of the wall along its whole back because of that runner, and the finger of air behind it smells of dust and cut ends that fell down there years back.

A cupboard that stands clear of the wall will take a winter without going soft at the back. Air goes up behind it and keeps the back dry, while one pushed hard to brick sweats at the back and goes dark in two years.

With air behind it, that back has stayed pale and flat, while the door fronts of the same cupboard have gone dark with oil and use.

Each runner stands on edge against the back with its width square to the grain, and pine set against pine that way passes damp across the joint instead of keeping it in the end grain.

The brick behind stays rough where the paint covers it and soft in the joints where water gets in.

Brick stays sound under paint because paint throws rain while bare joints drink it, and each winter the bare ones go a little deeper.

Paint gone soft feels sandy under the nail and comes away in crumbs, while paint gone hard rings a little when tapped with a knuckle.

Old paint flakes in scales where damp gets behind it and lifts it, while paint on dry brick stays flat for ten years.

Scales curl up at the edges where the damp pushes, and each scale leaves a pale patch that darkens again by next rain.

---

The boards of the cupboard sides run true from front to back, with the grain running along them and the ends housed in the front frame.

Side boards cup when one face stays damp and the other dries, because the damp face swells and the dry face shrinks, and the board curls toward the dry.

These sides have cupped a little toward the store, which lets more air in behind and dries the back faster each year.

Cupped sides throw air to the back in the day and the back throws it up at night, so a cupped carcass airs its own back every time the door opens.

Flat sides throw nothing, and the back stays shut and sweats, which is why flat-sided boxes want a runner and cupped ones stand without one.

The back board here lies flat to its runner and is kept off the brick by it, while the sides, which cup a little, throw their share of air behind on their own.

The flat back board speaks its own lesson. Dust lies on it after dry weeks and brushes off in sheets, and each brushing leaves the face a touch flatter than before, for loose dust leaves and hard pine remains.

A face that lies flat to still air gathers dust even across it by evening, while one that stands in a draught gathers it in streaks.

Dust lies on that back board even from top to foot, with its flat pine shut to the wind, while the door fronts carry it in streaks where hands pass.

Three screws fix the bench top down and all three have been turned once since it was made, and each one has gone slack a quarter turn in the damp and drawn back when the pine dried in June. A screw turned once and then left goes slack every winter and every spring for as long as it sits in that bench, because pine swells across the grain in a wet month and shrinks across it in a dry one. The rust under those heads has bloomed out along the top of the board in a soft orange, and it stops dead at the first clear inch of pine, which is the line the damp got to and no further.

The cupboard hinges turn on brass pins gone green at the knuckles, leaves screwed to door and carcass with mismatched screws. Pins wear oval over decades, dropping the door a hair till its lower corner kisses the frame. A kissing corner polishes bright while a clear one gathers dust, showing where the swing settles.

The shelf boards above the cupboard sit flat where they meet their battens, with the flat pine to flat pine throwing the dust off instead of gathering it. A shelf that meets its batten face to face stays true through summer, while one that meets it edge on dips in the middle under books.

He brings the hand out of the pocket, wipes the dust off against the bench edge, and stands in the doorway looking at the cupboard with the yard behind him.

At eye level the cupboard looks square and true, with the door shut flat to the frame and the flat boards each side of it level with the bench legs.

Flat to the frame like that the door keeps the dust out by day, and each year the hinges wear a hair lower under the load and the flat pine round them burnishes bright.

A door that meets its frame face to face closes without a rattle through spring winds, while one that meets it edge on drums when the yard wind gets in past dusk.

The cupboard keeps the place it was made in, with its back a finger off the brick and the air behind it cool as it stands, in that shadowed corner where the walls sweat least of all.
