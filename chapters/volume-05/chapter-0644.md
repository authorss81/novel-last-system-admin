# Chapter 0644 — The Card With Nothing On It

Monday the thirty-first of May 2021, in this borough. She is at the end of a road off the parade at about half past nine in the morning and she is in a kitchen at number twelve from about ten past ten until about half past ten, and the whole of it is an envelope on a shelf and one chair in a room.

**THE WOMAN WHO BROUGHT THE FOLDING CHAIR SEES THE STOOL ON THE STEP AT NUMBER NINE AND GOES UP TO NUMBER TWELVE INSTEAD, AND TO KEEP THE WOMAN THERE OUT OF IT SHE TELLS HER THAT THE MAN WHO CAME FOR THE FRIDGE DID COME, AND THAT THERE IS A CARD, AND THAT THE CARD HAD NOTHING TICKED ON IT, AND THE WOMAN IN HER LATE SEVENTIES LOOKS AT HER AND SAYS NOTHING AT ALL FOR THE WHOLE OF WHAT SHE IS TELLING HER, AND THEN ASKS HER WHICH OF THE TWO PEOPLE COMING UP THAT PATH SHE WOULD TELL.**

She is a woman in her late forties. The stool is on the step at number nine because the woman there put it out on the Saturday morning to air it, facing the road, the way a person puts a chair out.

---

She has been up that road twice since the Wednesday of the nineteenth of May and both times she has stopped at the end of it and both times she has not got out. What she has come for both times is a table, and a good sentence about a table, and it is the only thing she has been able to think of for a while that is not about a chair and a stool and two people who do not know about each other.

And then she parks at the end of the road at about half past nine on the Monday and there is a stool on the step at number nine, standing on its feet on the top step facing the road, the way a chair is put out, and it is not a thing you can mistake for anything.

And what happens to her in a van at the end of a road is a thing she will not be able to account for later: she feels that somebody has taken a thing out of a house and put it on a step, and she knows the house. And there is a woman three doors up that road who has been left with nothing, and who is seventy-eight, and the kitchen is a different size this morning than it was on Wednesday.

---

She knocks at about ten past ten. Two knocks and a pause and one more, which is not the woman's knock, and which the woman in her late forties has heard from the other side of four doors and has never once copied.

The door opens in about three seconds.

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Come in, I've not got the table out of the way."

Which is the first thing about the table.

And the woman in her late forties says, "That's what I've come about."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Well, come in and we'll have it out."

The corner by the sink is empty.

That is the first thing she sees, standing in the doorway of a kitchen, and it is the corner where a stool has been stood on its side against the wall on top of nothing since the Wednesday of the fifth of May.

And then she looks across the room and the green folding chair is at the table.

Under the window. With the cushion on it. Where a chair goes.

And it has not been there since the Saturday of the twenty-fourth of April. It has been against the dresser on a Saturday and it has been in the corner by the sink on a Wednesday, and it has been in and out of that kitchen four times a day since the start of May, and it is at the table, and the cushion is on it, and the table is under the window with a cloth on it.

And the woman in her late forties stands in the doorway and does not say anything for longer than is comfortable.

And the woman in her late seventies does not make it easier. She says, "Is it all right."

And the woman in her late forties says, "It's spotless."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "I have been on my knees."

And the woman in her late forties says, "You've done it, then."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Thursday. On the Friday you were here you said it wanted doing again before long and I said no, and then I did it Thursday, and I have got a burn on both knees that is going to be on them until September."

And the woman in her late forties says, "You should have rung me."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "I should have rung you. I didn't want you in for a floor."

And that is the whole of the first ten minutes and it is the best ten minutes the two of them have ever had, and neither of them knows it.

There is not one false thing in that kitchen between ten past ten and about half past ten on the thirty-first of May. There has not been one in it since the Thursday. The last one was the council about the damp and that was the Wednesday before last.

And then it goes, and what replaces it is on the shelf by the door where the post comes, and it has been on that shelf since the Wednesday of the nineteenth of May, and it is an envelope from a firm that takes old appliances away, about four inches by seven, with a lorry on the front of it.

And the woman in her late forties saw it on the Wednesday, because she sat in that front room for fifty minutes with a bag of frozen vegetables on the table beside her and the shelf is by the door, and she has been looking at it in her own head since the Wednesday before last.

---

And she says it, and she has not decided to say it, and that is the part she cannot get past afterwards.

And she says, "Can I tell you something and it's a bit daft."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Go on."

And the woman in her late forties says, "That man did come."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Which man."

And the woman in her late forties says, "The one for the fridge."

Here is what the woman in her late seventies does with that, and it is the whole chapter.

She does not move. She has got a cloth in her hand and it is half way to the oven and it stops where it is.

And the woman in her late forties says, "I know what you told me. I know what you told the man on the Wednesday about me, and I took it, and I have been carrying that as well, and I am not here to get at you for it. I want to be straight with you about the fridge because it is going to come up."

And the woman in her late seventies says nothing.

And the woman in her late forties says, "He came in March. He was in a van. And I had said to a woman at number ten that he came for the fridge and I have been sick about it, and I went back this morning and I looked at the shelf where the post comes, and there is a card."

And the woman in her late seventies says nothing.

And the woman in her late forties says, "It's got his name on it and it's got a number and there is nothing ticked on it. No tick, no date, nothing. Which means he never rang anybody about you. Which means nobody came."

And here is where the woman in her late seventies works out what is happening, and it takes about four seconds, standing in her own kitchen with a cloth in her hand.

The envelope is from a firm that takes old appliances away. It came through the letterbox. It was there on the Wednesday and it is there now and it has been on that shelf since the Wednesday, and it is the only piece of paper in that house with a lorry on it, and it has nothing ticked on it, and the woman in her late forties has picked it up and read it and worked that out.

And the woman in her late forties is now standing in a kitchen telling her, to her face, that the man who came for the fridge did come.

And she has not said the words *I know you are lying*.

And she is not going to.

---

And the woman in her late seventies says, "He never came."

And the woman in her late forties says, "No."

And there is about a minute and a half of that kitchen where the two of them are both saying things that are not true, on purpose, to each other, and neither of them has said what they are doing.

And the woman in her late seventies is doing it because she has been given something. That is what she works out in the four seconds, and it is the only time in this whole business that she has been given anything at all, and it has been given to her by a woman who came up that road to move a table and has ended up defending a fridge.

And the woman in her late forties is doing it because there is a stool on a step at number nine and a woman of seventy-eight has an empty corner by a sink, and there is going to be an afternoon when that woman is asked a direct question by somebody, and the only answer she is ever going to be able to give is a man in a van, and a man in a van is not on the page.

And so two women in a kitchen at number twelve spend about a minute and a half agreeing, in the plainest words, that a man came for a fridge, and they both know the other is doing it on purpose, and neither of them will ever refer to it.

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Leave that."

And the woman in her late forties says, "It's got nothing on it."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Leave it where it is."

And the woman in her late forties puts it back on the shelf.

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Right."

And the woman in her late forties says, "The stove's the other one to look at. You said last thing."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "The stove is sound."

And the woman in her late forties says, "It's not sound. The hotplate on the left is going. It did the same in April and you told me the man at the shop said it would go at the end of the summer."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "That's the same as my saying it."

And the woman in her late forties says, "It is."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "You'll get a burn on both knees if you get under it."

And the woman in her late forties says, "I've had worse."

And she is at the door at about half past ten with her bag over her shoulder and her hand on the latch, and behind her the chair is at the table and the corner by the sink is empty and the envelope is back on the shelf with nothing on it.

And the woman at number seventy-eight says, "You've not asked me about the stool."

And the woman in her late forties does not turn round.

And the woman in her late forties says, "No."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Why not."

And the woman in her late forties says, "Because it's not there."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "No. It isn't."

And there is about ten seconds of a passage on a Monday morning where neither of them says anything, and it is the longest silence in this business, and both of them know exactly what is in it.

And then the woman in her late seventies asks her for the one thing, and it is not what either of them expected, including the woman who asks it.

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Can I ask you something and it's not a job."

And the woman in her late forties says, "Go on."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Which of you do I tell."

And the woman in her late forties says, "Tell about what."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "The stool. It is gone. I gave it to the woman at number nine and I have not told anybody, and there is a man who made it, and there is a woman who has been unable to ask me about the table since the Wednesday before last, and one of them is going to ask me where it went, and it is going to be you, because you are the one that comes."

And the woman in her late forties says, "I might be."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "And when you do, what do I tell you."

And the woman in her late forties does not answer that.

She stands in the passage of number twelve with her hand on the latch for about four seconds, and she is aware of the whole of it — the green chair at the table, the corner by the sink, the envelope on the shelf with nothing on it, and the woman behind her who has not moved off the cloth.

And she says, "I don't know."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Right."

And the woman in her late forties says, "I genuinely don't."

And the woman in her late seventies says, "Well. Neither do I."

And the woman in her late forties goes out into the light and the gate goes.

And the woman in her late seventies stands in her own passage with a cloth in her hand and does not go back into the kitchen.

---

Here is what she has at about half past ten on the thirty-first of May, and she has been aware of every bit of it since half past six this morning at her own window.

Three: there are two people who come up that path and there is going to be a question at one of them about a stool that is not in her house, and she has just been asked which of them she will tell, by a woman of seventy-eight, in her own passage, at about half past ten on a Monday morning, and she cannot think of an answer that is not *both*.

And both is the one that does not work, because both means telling the woman who brought the chair that a man in a van made a stool, and telling the man who made it that a woman who clears houses gave a chair, and then the two of them will know, and they will meet, and they will both come, and there will be a woman of seventy-eight in a kitchen with a green chair at a table and a corner by a sink with nothing in it, and the question will have been answered for her.

So she cannot say both. And she cannot say one, because she does not know which, and because the reason she cannot say one is that she has been choosing every morning and every evening since the fifth of May and has picked neither, and a woman of seventy-eight has found that out and has put it in one sentence and walked it past her.

And there is a third thing, and it is the one she will be carrying.

A woman in her forties came up that road to move a table and has spent twenty minutes in her kitchen defending a fridge that never came, in front of a woman who has been lying to her since the fourteenth of May, and neither of them said one word about what they were doing, and they are going to carry that between them for as long as they are both alive.

That is what a lie is when it stops being one. It is not a thing you are caught in. It is a thing two people carry.

And she has got a cloth in her hand and a corner by her sink with nothing in it, and the sun is on the table where the chair is, and it is the thirty-first of May, and she goes into the kitchen and puts the chair back under the window where it is going to stay, and that is not the end of anything, because the question is still in the passage, and what she has put back under the window is a chair, and what she has put on the shelf is an envelope with nothing on it, and neither of them is an answer.

**SHE TOLD A WOMAN IN HER OWN KITCHEN THAT A MAN CAME FOR A FRIDGE AND THE WOMAN SHE WAS TELLING IT TO DID NOT SAY ONE WORD WHILE SHE WAS SAYING IT, AND THEN ASKED HER WHICH OF THE TWO PEOPLE COMING UP THAT PATH SHE WAS GOING TO TELL, AND WHAT SHE GOT BACK FOR IT WAS *I DON'T KNOW* AND *WELL. NEITHER DO I*, AND A LIE IS NO LONGER A THING YOU ARE CAUGHT IN, IT IS A THING TWO PEOPLE CARRY, AND THE QUESTION IS STILL IN THE PASSAGE AND IT HAS BEEN ANSWERED BY TWO PEOPLE WHO CANNOT ANSWER IT.**

(End of chapter 0644)
