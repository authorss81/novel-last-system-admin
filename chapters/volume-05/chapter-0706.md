# Chapter 0706 — The Run A Quarter Inch Out

Friday the twenty-fifth of February 2022, in this borough. He is at his own unit from first light until dark and the whole of the day is a run of units a quarter of an inch out and a card not written on.

**A MAN IN HIS EARLY FORTIES WORKS A RUN OF UNITS A QUARTER OF AN INCH OUT, KEEPS A SCRAP OF SCRIBING CARD WITH TWELVE ON ONE SIDE AND ROOM ON THE OTHER AND A PENCIL BEHIND HIS EAR AND WRITES NOTHING, AND SPEAKS TO THE MAN AT THE BENCH OPPOSITE ABOUT A BORROWED LADDER AND ABOUT WATER UNDER A BATH TAP.**

He is a man in his early forties. He is at his own unit all day. The man at the bench opposite is in and out of the unit all day. He speaks to him at the bench.

---

He gets to the unit before there is any light in the yard and opens up with his hands cold.

The shoebox with the element in it is on the top of the bench where it has been since July. The drawer under the bench is shut, as it has been since the sixteenth of November, with the nine pounds of brush in the plastic bag inside it. He does not open it. The stranger brush is in the side pocket of the canvas bag in the footwell of the van outside, stone and leather at the bottom of it. The bolster is on the board under the passenger bench with the leads and the screws. He leaves all three where they are and gets the rule out.

The run of units along the back wall wants fitting. He set them out in January and they are a quarter of an inch out at the top, out over six foot, enough to show at the cornice. He sights along the top with one eye shut and marks nothing. Marking it would mean cutting it, and cutting it would mean packing it, and packing it wants a pair of hands he does not have.

---

He works round the run from first light to the middle of the day without cutting a thing.

He takes the rule and the square and the level and lays them on the bench in a row. He measures the run at the bottom and at the middle and at the top and writes nothing down, because the figures are in his head and have been since January. A quarter of an inch out at the top over six foot. He could pack it with card and nobody would see it but him. He has packed worse with less and been paid and thanked.

The scrap of scribing card is in his shirt pocket with the letting agent card with the boy on the shirt on it. The scrap has twelve on one side in his own writing and room on the other side and the pencil is behind his ear. He takes the scrap out and looks at the twelve and puts it back. He has not written on the other side of it since October, when he put the twelve on it. There is room for a number and a street and he has not put either of them there.

---

He spends the middle of the day with the gates against the wall.

He takes the rule and measures post to post in his head, latch stile to drop bolt, and marks nothing. The pencil stays behind his ear. The scrap stays in his pocket. The drawer stays shut under the bench with the brush in the bag inside it. He lays his palm flat on the top gate and feels the oak cold through his skin. The numbers chalked on the backs show pale. He wipes a smear of dust off one with his thumb and does not touch the chalk.

The man at the bench opposite comes over with offcuts and stacks them.

"You still not written," he says, nodding at the pencil.

"I have not written," he says. "Twelve on one side and room on the other. Room for a number and a street. I have had it since October."

"Write the cottage number."

"It is not the cottage number that wants writing. It is his number. Lent without taking it. Left in a porch. Now under a window. I cannot write what I never took."

---

At the middle of the day the man at the bench opposite comes over with two cups and puts one down on the bench.

"You look sour," he says.

"I am sour," he says. "This run is out."

The man sights along the top with one eye shut.

"A quarter," he says.

"A quarter," he says. "Over six foot. At the top."

"Pack it."

"I would pack it if I had hands. I cannot keep the top true and pack the bottom on my own."

---

They drink the tea standing up with their backs to the cold.

"You lent your ladder out," the man says. "Twenty-ninth of November."

"Aye."

"Where is it."

"In an open porch on the third of December with no note on it. Then out of the rain. Then up a road on the seventeenth of December. Then folded under a window in a front room at number twelve. Tape round the second rail. Chalk on the third tread. Folded and squared and not opened since."

The man looks at him.

"How do you know all that."

"Because the woman of about thirty told me in January. And because the woman at number twelve has a bath tap that drips into a bowl she empties at first light and at dark, and I saw the bowl when I was in that house in July, and I think about it whenever I run a tap in here."

---

The man at the bench opposite turns his cup round on the bench.

"You could ask for it back," he says.

"I could ask whose it is first. I lent it without taking his number and I cannot give back what I cannot ask for. I turned half a job down for the space in the van and I have not thought about it since December, except I think about it every time I need a ladder, which is every time I have gates to hang."

He takes the pencil from behind his ear and puts it back behind his ear. He does not write on the scrap. The letting agent card stays in the pocket with the boy on the shirt on it since the sixth of October, not rung. He has said nothing about how much since the sixteenth of July and what he decided in four seconds is still right. He has said about ten minutes and not ten. He has said fifty pound about the old man gate in July and never charged and did not put it right.

---

He eats standing up at the bench with the rule beside his plate.

The van is outside in the yard with the stranger brush in the footwell. He looks at it through the small window while he eats. Stone and leather at the bottom of a canvas bag since July. He has never taken it out and never put anything with it. Taking it out would mean asking whose it is, and he does not ask.

He thinks about the bowl while he eats, white china under a bath tap, emptied at first light and at dark. He saw it in July when he was in that house to look at the table foot. The bead grew and let go while he stood there with the rule in his hand. He said nothing about it then. Saying it would have meant offering, and offering would have meant hands, and he had no hands to spare.

---

They go back to the run and sight it again.

The posts are already in the ground at the cottage, and the pair of oak gates are leaning against the back wall of his own unit with a number chalked on the back of each. The man who has paid wants them by the spring and he cannot hang a gate on his own without a ladder. The stool he built will not do it. The top run being a quarter of an inch out will not do it either, because a gate hung true against a run that is out shows the run up, and a run packed true without hands shows him up if it slips.

"You do roofs," the man says.

"I do not do roofs," he says. "I have not been up that road since the seventeenth of June."

---

He spends the end of the day with the rule in his hands and the pencil behind his ear.

He measures the gates against the posts in his head, post to post, latch stile to drop bolt, and finds the latch stile wants a pair of hands on a ladder. He measures the run again at the bottom and at the middle and at the top and finds it still a quarter of an inch out. He puts the rule down and picks up the square and puts the square down.

The drawer under the bench stays shut. The shoebox stays on the top of the bench. The stranger brush stays in the footwell. The scrap with twelve stays in his pocket with room on the other side of it.

"What would you do about the bowl," he says.

"What bowl."

"The bowl under the bath tap at number twelve. Emptied at first light and at dark. Full again between."

"I would mend the tap."

"Aye. And who would keep the frame while she goes up the fixed four rungs beside the hatch to see the cistern that weeps at the far side of a joist."

The man has nothing to say to that, because there is nothing to say to it. The water keeps coming and the bowl keeps filling, round and white and cold, and asking about it would mean saying the job out loud, and neither of them says it.

---

They sight the run again at the end of the day with the light going.

The quarter of an inch shows at the cornice where the shadow cuts. He runs a finger along the top and feels the lip where it stands proud. Packing it with card would take it true. He has the card in his pocket. He does not take it out, because packing the bottom while keeping the top true wants two pairs of hands and he has one.

"What would you do," he says, "with a tap that drips and a bowl that fills."

"I told you. Mend it."

"And the frame. Who keeps the frame while she goes up. The fixed four rungs beside the hatch. The cistern weeps at the far side of a joist. Nobody has been up since the spring."

The man looks at the run and at the gates and at the pencil.

"Same as this," he says. "You cannot keep the top true on your own."

"Aye," he says. "Round and white and cold. Twice over. Waiting."

---

He sweeps the unit at the end of the day with the yard broom.

He sweeps round the bench legs and under the offcut stack and into the corners by the back wall where the gates lean, working the broom flat so the pale dust lifts and settles. The dust comes up pale in the low light and catches on the oak where his palm smoothed it. He gathers it in a pan and tips it in the bin. The floor shows the oil marks round the bench where the work has stood. He sets the broom back by the door. The drawer stays shut. The shoebox stays on the top of the bench. The scrap stays in his pocket with twelve on one side and room on the other.

---

At dark he locks up with his hands cold and gets in the van.

He sits in the cab with the engine off and takes the scrap out and looks at the twelve on it in his own writing. There is room on the other side of it for a number and a street. He puts the pencil behind his ear and puts the scrap back in his pocket and does not write on it.

He drives out of the yard with the stranger brush in the footwell and the bolster under the bench and the drawer shut under the bench in the unit behind him. The run is a quarter of an inch out and will be a quarter of an inch out at first light, and the gates are leaning and will be leaning, and the bowl is filling and will be full, and all three wait where they were not meant to stay.
