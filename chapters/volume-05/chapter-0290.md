# Chapter 0290 — Ninety-Four Pounds

Wednesday the eighteenth of January 2017, from about ten past two in the afternoon, a kitchen at number six in a street off Marsh Lane in Tideglass.

**HER BOILER WENT AGAIN IN JANUARY AND SHE RANG THE FIRM AND THE FIRM SENT THE MAN WHO LIVES TWO DOORS UP, AND HE DID THE WORK FOR THE FIGURE THE FIRM HAD QUOTED HER IN DECEMBER, AND THEN HE SAT DOWN ON THE ARM OF HER SOFA AND TALKED FOR FIVE MINUTES ABOUT SOMETHING HE HAD BEEN TURNING OVER SINCE ABOUT THE SEPTEMBER, AND THERE WAS NO ANSWER TO ANY PART OF IT IN THAT KITCHEN.**

The boiler at number six in that lane is a wall-mounted combi with a clock on it that runs two hours fast since the summer, and the pressure light had been on that morning and the hot tap at the kitchen sink had gone to cold and stayed there, and it was about twenty past eight on a Wednesday in January and about four degrees outside.

She had rung the firm at about half past eight and the woman on the telephone asked her the postcode and the make off the sticker on the front, and then said the same thing as she had said in December, which was that there was a figure for coming out after four and a different one before four, and read the after-four one out, and said it was ninety-four pounds, and it was exactly what she had been quoted in December.

"You've not changed the price."

"No, love," said the woman on the telephone. "It's the same price. It's the same everything. You'll get the same fella."

She put the phone down and sat on the arm of the sofa with the fire lit in the grate, and there was a hot water bottle under a blanket, and the washing came out of the machine and went on a clothes horse in the front room because that is where it dried in that house in January, and the gas fire was on in the other room, and the clock on the boiler said twenty past ten.

At about half past ten she got the notebook she keeps by the telephone off the shelf and looked at a gas and heating firm written in the back of it in a hand not her own, and then she looked at the number she had written herself, and then she picked the telephone up and rang the second one, and a woman answered it, and she asked whether they were any good, and the woman said they were not on that road, and she gave the address anyway, and the woman said she would put it in the book for the Wednesday afternoon and would not promise a time.

---

He came at about ten past two with a bag of tools and a van outside with a sign on the side of it, and the sign had his own name on it and a landline number and nothing else, and he had been in this house before and had never been in this kitchen.

He put his bag down and went straight over the boiler and put his hand on the top of it and listened to it for a second and then got the cover off.

"You've got a cold feed," he said. "It's the valve, not the boiler. I'll get a new one off the merchant today and I'll be back inside the hour, and it'll be ninety-four pounds, because that's the figure the office sets, and I haven't set it and I can't move it."

"That's the same as December."

"That's the same as December," he said. "Downstairs, that's the valve. It'll be about a ten-minute job and I might as well do it now so I know what it is."

He did the valve at about half past two with the front off it and a torch on the floor, and he got the part off the merchant's website and had it delivered to a shop on the parade that same afternoon, and he went down the lane and up the parade and came back with it at about half past four, and he fitted it and bled the system and ran it and there was hot water at the kitchen sink at about ten past five.

The clock on that boiler said twenty past seven.

"Right," he said, and got his bag off the floor and put his coat on.

"Can I ask you something," said the woman at number six.

"You can ask me something."

"You came past here every morning in December," said the woman at number six. "With your bag. You used to say good morning and I used to say good morning back, and I stood in that front room on the Friday morning you went past with the fire going and I saw you and you didn't come to the door, and I've thought about that every day since, and I want to say something about it and I've been trying to work out how for about six weeks."

He had his bag at his feet and the zip half up and he stopped with his hand on it.

"What I've been trying to work out," he said, "is not what you did. You have done nothing. You were sat in your front room with your fire on and you let a man walk past your door in the cold, and that's your business and it has nothing to do with me. What I've been trying to work out since about the September is what I am."

"There's a sign on your van."

"There's a sign on my van," he said. "I paid for that. That's the whole of the paper about me anywhere and it's got a landline number on it and nothing else, and it goes out of sight the second I turn the van round in a street."

He put his bag down again and sat down on the arm of the sofa, which nobody had asked him to do.

"There isn't anything on me," he said. "Not a patch. I've looked. If there was a patch on me it would be somewhere anybody could put a piece of paper, and there isn't, and if there isn't a patch on me then I am not on your list either, and if I'm not on your list then the fact that I can mend a boiler at number six at ten past two on a Wednesday is a fact about a Wednesday and not about me. That's what I wanted to say. I wanted to say it in December, and I never said it, because you were in your front room and I was on the other side of the road, and I have not thought about anything else since about the September."

The kitchen was very quiet. There was washing going on a clothes horse in the front room and the fire had gone to a red bed and the clock on that boiler said twenty past seven.

"I didn't know," said the woman at number six. "I'm not saying that as a fault. I didn't know and I would not have done anything with it if I had. I'd have said, oh, he's the one at number two. That's all it would have been."

"I know," said the man at number two. "That's the bit that's done me in. There was nothing to be done. Everybody in that lane knows my name, and the name was never going to be in anybody's mouth because there was never a moment where it would come out."

"So what do you want me to do."

"I want you to do nothing," said the man at number two. "I want to have said it. That's the whole of what I wanted out of a boiler going on a Wednesday in January."

---

She paid him the ninety-four pounds out of the money box in the front room, in notes, and he counted it on the worktop and put it in his inside pocket, and he wrote nothing down, and there was no receipt book out, and she did not ask him for one, and he said it was on the office and it would be on the statement and the statement would come to her through the office and not through him.

He was at the door at about ten past five and she was stood at the worktop with the box open and the lid back.

"About the money," she said. "I want to say one thing about the money and then I'll leave you."

"Go on."

"You charged me the same as the firm charged me in December," said the woman at number six, "and you know you did, and you told me you hadn't set it, and I believe you, and I want it said out loud in a kitchen that somebody has now been told that a man came twice for the same money and the second time he said he had never been asked for anything."

"Right," said the man at number two.

"Whatever you were after."

"It wasn't after anything," said the man at number two. "That's what I've been telling you. There was never an after."

**AND ON THE MONDAY HE CAME BACK AT ABOUT A QUARTER TO NINE AND SHE HAD HOT WATER BY TEN PAST, AND ON THE MONDAY AFTER THAT SHE PASSED HIM ON THE FAR SIDE OF THAT LANE AND SAID GOOD MORNING AND HE SAID GOOD MORNING, AND AFTER THAT IT WAS GOOD MORNING AND NOTHING ELSE, AND THERE IS NOTHING IN THAT LANE THAT SAYS HIS NAME ON A LIST, AND THERE NEVER WAS, AND THE STATEMENT WENT TO HER THROUGH AN OFFICE EIGHT MILES OFF WITH A FIGURE ON IT AND NO NOTE OF ANY KIND.**
