# Chapter 0894 — The Keep Standing Proud

Tuesday the nineteenth of March 2024, at the front door of number nine, from the middle of the day until the street has gone quiet. The gap beside a door.

**EVERYBODY GOING OUT OF NUMBER NINE LIFTS THAT FRONT DOOR AND HAS DONE FOR YEARS, AND SHE MEANS TO TURN ONE HAND ON ITS EDGE AND PUSH IT INTO THE GAP BESIDE THAT DOOR, THE BACK OF IT LEADING, AND WALK IT DOWN THE WHOLE HEIGHT OF THAT GAP, AND THE WOMAN WHO DOES HER SHOPPING IS AT THE SIDE GATE.**

That door has to be lifted.

It has been lifted every time anybody has gone out of the front of that house for years, and everybody who goes out of it does it without ever registering it, the way a person lifts a hand over a step they have never once looked down at.

She has watched two people do it in a week and has decided to find out why.

It also makes a noise. A door that shuts on its own weight goes into the frame and stops. That door drops the last inch before it arrives, and then clacks, and then stops.

The gap beside a door is the one place on the outside of a house where a hand can go and learn something, because a door stands a hair off its frame, and a hair is very nearly the width of the edge of a hand.

So the hand goes on its edge, the back of it leading, and pushes into the gap beside the door, and it goes in.

---

The woman who does her shopping comes through the side gate from inside the garden about four in the afternoon, with the gate already open behind her, and stands at it a moment with her back to the house.

She looked along the front of that house from the side gate to the door and across the front path and back again, and she stood a while at the front step with the bag at her feet, and then she went out and the gate was where she had left it.

The first thing the hand finds going down from the top is that the gap is a hair.

A hair is a hair. The edge of a hand goes into it about half the length of the first joint and no further, and a door that shuts properly gives exactly that.

The fingers go down a foot and the gap is still a hair.

A foot and a half and the gap has opened to the width of a thumbnail.

Two feet and it is the width of the end of the little finger.

And all the way down at the bottom of that door, from about four inches off the flags, it is a quarter of an inch from end to end, and a quarter of an inch goes in past the first joint and on to the second.

So the gap beside that door is not one gap. It is a hair at the top and a quarter of an inch at the bottom, and it goes from one to the other steadily, the whole way down, without a step anywhere in it.

There is a way to check the whole of that from the step without a hand at all, and she has tried it, and it tells her less than she wants. Stand square to the door from three feet off and look down the side of it, and you can see the daylight at the bottom and you cannot see the daylight at the top, and you can see the keep standing proud as a dark line down the whole edge.

The dark line is the only part of that door that a person could tell you about from the path, and it is the least important half of what is wrong with it.

Which is what a door hanging high looks like from the side.

Then the fingers come to something, on the way down.

Not a stop. A ridge.

A hand run on its edge down a door feels a proud edge the way it feels a nail head, because the nail of the finger rides over the proud part and drops into the hollow behind it, and the size of that drop is the size of the step.

This one is about a sixteenth of an inch.

And it does not end.

She walks the hand down the whole height of that gap and the ridge comes with it, all the way from the top joint to about four inches from the bottom, the same sixteenth the whole way.

That is the keep.

The keep is the half of the latch that is fixed to the edge of the door, and it is meant to be let into that edge with its front flush with the face of it, so that when the door shuts the keep goes into the strike plate on the frame and the two of them take the weight of the door straight into the jamb.

The keep on that door was never let in.

It is screwed on to the edge and stands out a sixteenth, from the top joint to the bottom joint, and the screws in it are slotted and a little rust on two of them.

Two faults, then, and they are of different ages.

The gap is the newer one. A door hangs on three screws in a frame of softwood, and softwood screws in softwood work loose over years, and a door that has come down on its screws is the commonest fault there is and the least likely to be reported, because a door that has dropped still shuts.

So that gap is a hair at the top because the door has not gone down as far as it used to at the head, and it is a quarter of an inch at the bottom because the door has gone down by that much at the foot. The whole of it is one thing. The door has dropped on its screws.

The proud keep is the older one. A keep that was never let into the edge is a joiner's mistake made on a day in a hurry, and it has been there since the door went in, and it does not get worse and it does not get better.

Which is why the door has been lifted rather than fixed, and why it has never been mentioned: a door that has dropped a quarter of an inch in forty years is not a fault anybody brings up, and the fix is three screws and a quarter of an inch of packing, and the packing is not there.

Both faults are corrected by one movement.

Lift the door a quarter of an inch at the foot and the edge comes down onto the frame again, and the keep has to come up a sixteenth on its way to do it, which it does, and then the door comes the rest of the way on its own weight and shuts instead of dropping.

So that door has been lifted every time it has been opened since the frame settled, and the lift has become part of the way out of that house, the way a step has become part of the way up to it.

In the quarter inch at the bottom of that gap there is a line of hard grey stuff, which is the paint of the door's edge from the last time that door was painted, ground hard by however long it has been swinging against the frame.

Above that line the gap is empty, from the quarter inch down to the sixteenth, and stays empty the whole height.

The door is sound. The frame is sound. The hinges are sound and working, and the door swings freely on them and stops where it stops.

The thing that will go wrong with that door is the painter. The painter will be told it sticks, and will take a plane to the top edge of it, and the gap at the top will be twice what it is now and the lift will be a quarter of an inch bigger with it.

That door will close a quarter inch short and a sixteenth proud, the frame sound and the hinges sound and the gap under the latch doing exactly what it has done since the keep was screwed on, a sixteenth in ten years and a quarter inch at the bottom.
