# Chapter 0293 — One Thing

Friday the twenty-seventh of January 2017, from about twenty to eight in the morning, a kitchen at the back of a parade in Tideglass.

**HE WAS IN FIVE PLACES BEFORE HALF PAST EIGHT IN THE EVENING, AND SOMEBODY AT A TABLE ASKED HIM ONE QUESTION, AND HE ANSWERED IT, AND NOBODY AT THAT TABLE DID ANYTHING WITH THE ANSWER.**

The kitchen at the back of that parade had about nine plates in the sink at half past seven on the Friday morning and the machine that heats the water has a timer on it that nobody has ever reset, and the clock on that wall says ten to nine when it is about a quarter to nine.

He was there from half past seven. He is on the dishes and that is a thing he does for money and it is the only thing he does. The woman he washes up for had been away since the Friday before Christmas and was not back until the Monday.

The plate rack was down over the sink when he came in and he put it up. He washed the plates. The hot water went off at about nine because of the timer and he finished the last of them in cold, and his hands were still cracked across the knuckles from about November and they were worse.

There was a pair of fingerless woollen gloves in the left pocket of his cardigan, and they were grey, and the wool had gone shiny at the tips of the thumb and the heel, and the paper band was still on one of them, and they had been in that pocket since about the middle of December.

---

He was on the floor of the shop that shuts early at about ten, because the shop does the breakfast and the room at the back has a door that comes through into the shop, and when the breakfast is over the two of them put the chairs up together and he is the one who does the chairs.

There were five people in that shop at that hour and one of them asked him whether he was doing anything on Saturday night and he said he did not know yet, and the woman who asked him said there was a quiz at the Methodist hall on Carrow Lane with a team in it, and that they were all going, and he said that was all right.

He carried six chairs through and stacked them against the wall by the door and put the mop in the bucket and carried the bucket out to the passage, and nobody told him what to do next and he went and did the next thing himself, and the next thing was the floor of the passage, and after that it was his own time, and he sat on an upturned crate in that passage with his cardigan over his knees and got his hands out of his pockets and looked at them and put them back.

At about half past one he was in a kitchen in Corrance Lane, where there were about sixteen plates and a good deal besides them, and two people at that table asked the woman who he was and she said the boy who does the dishes, and nobody else in that house said one word to him from the time he came in to the time he went, and he had it done by about ten to three and washed his hands and dried them on his sleeve.

At about ten past three he was on the first floor of a building by the water with a mop and a bucket, because the woman who holds the key to that building's ground floor has a woman in cleaning on Fridays and has never once been in the room the woman in the cleaning goes into, and the woman of about fifty-five was in that room that afternoon with her bag and her back to the door.

She said, without turning round, "Is that the water on."

"Aye," said the man of about eighteen. "It's the hot on. That's the cold one's got the green thing on it."

"Right," said the woman of about fifty-five.

That was all that was said in that room, and he did the landing and the bottom of that stair and the top of it, and the light that flickers at the top of that stair flickered, and he did not put his hand on it, and he went down at about half past four and shut that door on the latch.

He was in the hall off the parade at about half past seven in the evening, with about nine of them in it, and the meter on and nobody letting out for the night.

The man in his forties was at the end of the table talking about the pipe under the floor, and the man from the parade had a hand up in front of the fire about half the time he was talking, and the woman of about fifty-five had come in early with her coat still on and her bag down by the chair, and the woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall was at the door with her hand in her pocket going through for her keys before she would want them.

She saw him come in with the tray of mugs and she stopped.

She had not seen him in that hall since the middle of December, when he had come off the bus with his coat still on, and he had carried mugs round that room and she had not had a word with him then either.

"Are you in here Tuesdays," said the woman of about forty-two with the key to that hall.

"I don't know," said the man of about eighteen.

"You might be."

"I might be," said the man of about eighteen.

"Right," said the woman of about forty-two, and went and found her keys, and came back and put them in her pocket and sat down.

He went round that room with the tray and everybody at that table took one off it and one or two of them said what they wanted and one said nothing and got water.

He went round that room with the tray four times. Nobody at that table said one word to him about himself, and nobody said the word lad all night, and nobody said the word boy, and the man in his forties looked up at him every time he came past the end of the table and did not stop him.

At about half past eight the woman of about forty-two said to the man in his forties, with her chin up the room, "That's the lad that does the dishes at the parade."

"Ay," said the man in his forties.

"That go round in the daytime, or that go round in the evening?"

"He does the morning," said the woman of about forty-two. "I've seen him at the front of that shop at about ten."

"Right," said the man in his forties, and went back to the pipe under the floor.

He came back round with the tray a third time, and everybody at that table took one off it, and the young woman of about twenty was at that end of the table and drank her tea, and he was not asked anything else that evening, and the thing he had asked in that room in June, which was about a thing he had been told about and could not get an answer to off anybody, was not put to anybody in it again.

He took the tray back to the kitchen at about ten past ten, and the water in the machine had been off since about nine and he filled the tray out of the cold tap and washed it, and the clock on that wall said ten to twelve.

**AND HE WALKED DOWN A DARK PARADE PAST A SHUTTER THAT HAD COME DOWN AT ONE AND PAST THE BANK AND GOT THE BUS AT THE BOTTOM OF THAT STREET, AND THE GLOVES STAYED IN THE POCKET OF THAT CARDIGAN AND HE NEVER PUT THEM ON AGAIN.**
