# Premise

## Logline

When a modern city discovers that its ordinary infrastructure obeys a living network of civic promises, a municipal network technician who repairs damaged permissions becomes the only person able to mend the rules that keep the city running—and the person most likely to inherit a system built for control.

## One-sentence premise

In the estuary city of Nacre, 32-year-old network technician Jonas Mercer can repair magical permissions that decide who may cross a door, use a clinic, draw water, or receive emergency help, but every repair shifts responsibility toward him while an unseen civic authority tries to make one permanent administrator responsible for the whole city.

## Central promise

This is a grounded urban fantasy about a useful profession, not a chosen-one power fantasy. Jonas solves problems by understanding people, infrastructure, records, consent, and the practical consequences of a rule. He can open a permission or restore a missing term; he cannot open every door, command every mind, or solve a city’s problems without asking others to carry part of the burden.

The recurring question is simple and physical: **who is allowed to do this, who pays for it, and who gets counted when the system works?**

## Tone and genre

The tone is contemporary, procedural, humane, and occasionally uncanny. Ordinary work—inspecting a cabinet, tracing a fiber, arguing over a form, taking a statement at a hospital—should sit beside magic. The city is lived in: buses miss their stops, parents wait outside schools, patients argue about referrals, shopkeepers lose access when a contractor changes a contract, and technicians carry home repairs in their bags.

The action is grounded in the struggle to keep promises physical. A bridge, clinic, shelter, school, or water pump is not a disposable dungeon room. If a permission is broken, people and consequences enter the story.

## Starting situation

Jonas Mercer is a probationary municipal network technician in the Nacre Department of Civic Continuity. His work usually involves replacing fiber modules, resetting civic access plates, and repairing the junctions where ordinary power and the city’s hidden **Civic Lattice** meet. He wants permanent employment, a clean record, and enough control over his own life that he never has to repeat his father’s disappearance.

During a routine inspection in Volume 1, a pedestrian bridge refuses an ambulance because two old permissions contradict one another. Jonas makes an attended, temporary repair; it opens a bounded clinic route and reveals a missing term, but it does not give him a supernatural claim. A later first civic repair, made only after a visible responsibility bond, mends a sealed clinic door and assigns him a named maintenance claim. The opening investigation also finds a separate, live service route through **Lowglass**, which exists in the infrastructure but not on the official city map.

## Story engine

Every volume moves through a different civic pressure while the same central mystery develops:

- a district loses access to a service;
- a person is treated as a bearer of a rule they never agreed to;
- a repair succeeds technically but creates a political or moral cost;
- an emergency permission saves lives and is quietly made permanent;
- an ally’s independence challenges Jonas’s need to control the solution;
- the Lattice’s history becomes evidence in a public argument about who deserves authority.

The immediate promise of a volume is resolved even when the larger mystery remains unresolved.

## Ending direction, held for the author

The city is offered a permanent root administration that could keep essential services running through any disaster. It is controlled by the surviving civic identity of the first administrator, Iona Vey, and promises safety by making individual refusal impossible. Jonas ultimately rejects that bargain.

His final act is not to destroy the Lattice or become a benevolent dictator. He uses his root access to write a narrow, renewable enabling clause that makes citywide authority dependent on multiple district stewards, living witnesses, public renewal, and expiring terms. He gives up the ability to edit the rules directly. Nacre survives with a distributed civic system that is less seamless, less obedient, and more capable of surviving its founder.

## Narrative guardrails

- The System may state a rule, warning, or choice, but it never solves the scene.
- No instant mastery, unexplained bloodline, hidden ultimate power, or stat-driven leveling.
- No real software jargon; “network” refers to physical and civic infrastructure, not programming.
- No romance outside the single slow-burn relationship between Jonas and Dr. Sanaa Iqbal.
- No new final enemy. Iona Vey, the proxy question, and the fate of the first administrator are seeded in Volume 1.
- Every major victory must create a new obligation, a damaged relationship, a political reaction, or a larger question.
