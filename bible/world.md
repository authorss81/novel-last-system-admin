# World Bible: Nacre and the Civic Lattice

## Nacre at a glance

Nacre is a modern estuary city of roughly 1.3 million people. The Alder River reaches the sea through a broad fan of docks, grain silos, tidal barriers, and low neighborhoods that flood easily. The city has ordinary rail and bus service, fiber networks, hospitals, schools, markets, apartment blocks, small farms on the reclaimed edges, and a large informal economy of repair, delivery, and care.

Nacre has eight official administrative districts, nine service districts in the Lattice, and one legally defined civic core, the Old Ward. The city’s official map is clear, orderly, and increasingly expensive. The physical city is more complicated. Streets disappear at the river, businesses share addresses with multiple wards, and thousands of people live in rooms or work in buildings that the city calls “unassigned.” The infrastructure remembers them even when the paperwork does not.

The Lattice’s service districts are the first eight official districts plus the unlisted ninth service district, Lowglass. They are:

1. **River Stacks** — dense housing, repair yards, and small clinics; Jonas’s home district.
2. **Bellow Market** — markets, workshops, kitchens, and a large informal exchange economy.
3. **Saint Orra** — hospitals, schools, civic offices, and the city’s main medical center.
4. **Tideglass** — port, ferries, cold storage, and a volatile refugee reception network.
5. **Morrow Fields** — greenhouses, market gardens, water pumps, and food distribution.
6. **Juniper Flats** — new housing, transit yards, and the city’s most politically volatile redevelopment zone.
7. **Kestrel Hill** — offices, universities, private clinics, and wealthy residences.
8. **Gantry Ward** — warehouses, rail, repair industries, and large employers.
9. **Lowglass** — a real neighborhood of old warehouses, worker housing, and a buried canal, omitted from the civic map and population register for eighteen years.

Lowglass is not a hidden fantasy kingdom. It is inhabited by people who work in Nacre, attend its schools, use its hospitals, and pay into its services while lacking ordinary proof of residence. The omission began as a flood-response decision and became a system of political convenience.

## The Civic Lattice

The Lattice is a citywide layer of enforceable permissions woven through Nacre’s physical infrastructure. It is not a computer simulation and it does not create matter from nothing. It makes social promises operative inside doors, roads, wards, pipes, transit lines, wards, clinics, and other shared systems.

A permission is a rule with six necessary parts:

1. **Holder** — the person, office, institution, or place that receives the permission.
2. **Scope** — the exact action or boundary it permits.
3. **Witness** — the living person or jointly kept record that makes the promise credible.
4. **Term** — the duration, trigger, and conditions of validity.
5. **Claim** — what becomes owed if the promise is broken or the permission is misused.
6. **Precedence** — the public rule that says which permission wins when two rules touch.

A missing part creates a **seam**. A seam may be harmless, but it can let a door open at the wrong hour, a pump shut off during a shortage, or a quarantine rule erase access to education. The Lattice reports the symptom more reliably than the cause.

### What the Lattice can do

A valid permission can:

- open or close a physical boundary, such as a gate, bridge, ward, tunnel, or checkpoint;
- authorize access to a public service, such as water, a clinic, a shelter, a school, a transit platform, or an emergency ration;
- make an existing civic rite or technical safeguard valid under stated conditions;
- assign a duty to a role, such as night watch, flood inspection, medical triage, or repair verification;
- create a bounded right of return, care, shelter, or compensation when a service is taken or a promise is broken;
- establish precedence between compatible local rules.

It cannot:

- read a person’s intention or force an honest choice;
- create food, energy, water, or objects from nothing;
- grant a person skill, status, or power unrelated to an existing service or role;
- erase a memory, rewrite the Lattice’s own historical record, or resurrect the dead;
- bypass a physical distance, anchor, witness, or expiry;
- make a rule permanent without a renewing act by someone who accepts responsibility.

A permission can make a spell work only if the spell is already an established civic or technical function. A hospital evacuation ward can be authorized to repel a dangerous magical intrusion; it cannot be authorized to erase the intruder’s mind. A transit line can be permitted to cross a repaired bridge; it cannot make the bridge stronger by decree.

### Why permissions stay real

Every hinge is a physical node where the Lattice meets fiber, power, pipes, locks, transit equipment, or a civic building. A hinge is held in place by a combination of old charter stone, mundane wiring, and living witnesses. At least one witness must be present or consciously represented by a record that the community maintains. A rule with no living witness fades after its term ends. A rule with a dishonest witness becomes a hollow term: it may continue to function while producing downstream seams.

The Lattice is not omniscient. It understands the terms people give it, not the truth behind them. It can be misled by a technically valid promise, an obsolete word, a copied record, a coerced signature, or a new authority that has not been publicly recognized. It cannot reliably tell whether a person is happy, coerced, or speaking under duress; consent must be established through people, practice, and appeal.

## Physical signs of magic

Nacre’s magic is attached to objects and places, not to a floating game interface:

- brass or ceramic **access plates** beside doors and locks;
- **hinge lamps** that change color when a permission is failing;
- a line of silver dust around a public record case;
- seals painted on bridges, pump houses, clinics, and school gates;
- paper terms that darken when a claim is due;
- small shifts in ordinary infrastructure, such as a door opening for a person carrying a valid service need;
- a **Margin**, a thin visible seam that an authorized technician sees between a fault and the rule that caused it.

The System’s notices appear in the technician’s maintenance lens or on a municipal notice surface. They are plain, short, and incomplete. A notice may identify a missing term or a possible bearer, but it cannot prove motive or choose a morally correct answer.

## History

### Before the Lattice

Nacre was a difficult but ordinary port city. Flood control, water access, emergency shelter, and transit were managed through public works contracts, neighborhood associations, and a patchwork of legal permissions. During a major flood eighteen years ago, the city evacuated several low neighborhoods under a temporary emergency measure. The decision saved the Old Ward and much of the hospital system, but city officials later destroyed, sealed, and edited the human maps and indexes to make the emergency appear less selective. The Lattice retained the underlying witness claims.

### The Continuity Project

Thirty-two years ago, a civic engineer named **Iona Vey** led the Continuity Project. The project combined fiber, flood barriers, emergency power, and a strange charter stone uncovered during canal repairs. Its public promise was narrow: use the new network to coordinate shared infrastructure during disasters. Its private purpose was broader: make civic obligations enforceable without relying on courts, markets, or informal trust alone.

The first successful test opened a service corridor for a hospital during a blackout. People who had accepted the same emergency terms could cross together. A person denied the city’s ordinary routes was not trapped by a spell alone; doors, barriers, transit signals, and ration pumps followed the same permission structure.

### The Quiet Cut

Eighteen years ago, during an off-cycle emergency renewal rather than a scheduled Reclamation Season, the city entered a root-renewal crisis amid a flood and heat emergency. Iona Vey proposed a single continuity charter. Jonas’s father, **Evan Mercer**, a maintenance engineer who had worked with Iona, opposed the amendment and argued that the original Lattice required multiple witnesses. During the resulting crisis, Evan cut a lowland node to prevent the central charter from taking hold. The cut saved Lowglass’s residents from being classified as disposable, but it also removed the district from human maps and public indexes, not from the Lattice’s underlying history, and caused the event now called the **Quiet Cut**.

Iona disappeared from ordinary history. Evan disappeared with the cut. The city declared the district uninhabitable and told relatives that the workers had been lost to a storm. The Lattice continued to carry the missing terms.

### The present renewal

The Lattice operates on a twelve-year root-renewal cycle. The next cycle begins in three months. Old permissions are expiring unevenly. Bridges and clinics can be affected by contradictory clauses while ordinary people have no idea which part of the government is responsible. The current political answer is to privatize the system through the CivicCore Consortium. Jonas’s first repair occurs during the opening failures of this renewal.

## Factions and institutions

### Nacre Department of Civic Continuity (NDC)

The public department responsible for hinges, access plates, shared fiber, and the human technicians who repair the Lattice. It is underfunded, overworked, and split between service workers, managers, and elected officials who each understand only part of the network. Field technicians can make local repairs, but the department lacks the authority to amend a root clause.

### District Witness Councils

Local groups that maintain the people, records, and practical promises behind a permission. They are not elected magic officials. A council might consist of a school crossing guard, a clinic clerk, a tenant representative, a pump operator, and a resident who keeps the neighborhood archive. Councils are uneven, sometimes corruptible, and indispensable.

### CivicCore Consortium

A private infrastructure company that maintains physical nodes and sells priority access, emergency response, and “continuity assurance.” CivicCore is not a secret monster. It genuinely prevents outages and employs thousands of people. Its central mistake is treating a public obligation as a renewable product and assuming that a wealthy customer’s claim is more trustworthy than a poor district’s.

### The Civic Assembly

Nacre’s elected municipal government. It controls budgets, contracts, and public policy but cannot safely amend the root clauses without witnesses from the affected districts. Its Continuity Bloc favors centralized emergency authority. Its civic independents favor local stewardship. Most Assembly members do not know the full history of the Lattice.

### The Quiet Archive

A custodial network of archivists, engineers, historians, and former administrators who believe the Lattice’s history should be sealed because the public cannot be trusted with dangerous terms. The Archive is not a uniform evil. Some members protect people whose records were erased; others preserve the proxy because they believe only a single living authority can prevent another citywide disaster.

### Lowglass Witness Network

Residents, former workers, couriers, and caregivers who maintain the informal permissions Lowglass needs to survive. They are not a hidden army. They trade repairs, childcare, food, and witness time. Their central disagreement is whether to seek public recognition or build an independent civic system that can function without the city’s approval.

## Social and political consequences

Permissions touch everyone, but not equally:

- housing access can depend on a landlord’s service bond;
- a clinic may accept a patient only if a district has a valid care claim;
- school access can be suspended by a public-safety rule even when a child has no criminal record;
- a bus route can be valid for passengers but invalid for freight;
- a flood barrier can protect one district by diverting danger into another;
- a person who is absent from the civic register can still exert a real claim, which makes erasure politically useful;
- a successful repair can produce a legal debt even when the technical work was morally necessary.

Nacre’s ordinary argument is therefore also magical: a public promise is only real if someone can be held to it. The conflict is over who gets to define, renew, and revoke that responsibility.

## Story boundaries

The world remains contemporary and lived-in. There is no portal, dungeon, chosen bloodline, resurrection mechanic, or omncient world simulation. Magic is local, embodied, and dependent on civic life. The central mystery stays within Nacre’s history; later volumes may reveal that other cities have their own Lattices, but no new cosmic enemy is introduced to solve the ending.
