## Required Chapter Movement

### Chapter 31 — The First Offer

Open in the city with the department under strain and CivicCore's field teams visibly doing work the NDC cannot. Someone makes Jonas an offer that is not a threat: a permanent appointment, root-level notice access, a salary, and the continuity of every hinge Jonas has ever touched — in exchange for a blanket continuity waiver. He should want parts of it. Let him count what it would cost him in people before he knows he is counting. The Weir work must be in the conversation, not in a separate plot: at least one person must say what an offer of that shape would do to a street with no address. End with the offer open and Jonas not yet able to answer it.

### Chapter 32 — What Rook Can Show

Alden Rook arrives, and he is not a villain: he is effective, courteous, and can prove his point. He demonstrates private maintenance keeping more hinges running than the underfunded NDC, on real hinges, with real numbers, including hinges that failed for want of a body rather than for want of a competence. He should be right about enough of it that Jonas cannot dismiss him. Rook's argument must be a real one — that a price is more honest than an unaccountable queue — and Jonas must be shown what a service floor with a price on it looks like from the lowland side of it. Do not let Jonas win the argument by being right about the past.

### Chapter 33 — The Waiver, Refused

The blanket continuity waiver is put in front of Jonas to sign, and he refuses it, in person, in front of witnesses, minuted — as he refused the department rider and the hospital waiver, and for a reason that is new rather than repeated. The refusal must cost him something this time. The Weir's demand and Halvorsen's ninth-of-next-month board item should collide with the refusal in the same week, so that saying no to a machine that keeps hinges running is saying no while a tap runs brown on a street nobody can name. End with the refusal public and the city no safer.

### Chapter 34 — A Separate Closure

Oren Pell closes the **separate Lowland clinic-and-housing annex** under a renewed flood-safety order. This is a new site, not the old-canal shelter, and it must be a building with people in it and a claim. The order is valid and Oren is right about the structure, exactly as he was right about the old canal. The consequence is displacement, and this time the Weir is *not* behind it — make the residents of the annexed building people in their own right, with their own keeper, who have not agreed with the Weir about anything and do not want to. Someone must choose between a valid safety order and a service that is the only one in the district. Do not reuse the old-canal handoff scene.

### Chapter 35 — The Appeal and the Price

The new appeal is filed, and the cost of appealing appears: a hearing date, a representation requirement, a fee or a bond, a name that must go on the record, and time. The Weir's three positions and the annex's residents now have to decide separately whether to align, and at least one of them refuses to. Pia refuses to be a signatory again and says why. The board, the annex, and the old Weir clause should all be in the room in some form. End with a decision that is somebody else's.

### Chapter 36 — The Assembly Sits

The Assembly hears the continuity contract and the non-civic classification in the same sitting, and the classification is not resolved the way anybody wanted. The Assembly **delays** the contract; it does not defeat it. Jonas's own recommendation or its successor is before them, and the cheapest outcome for the city is the one that keeps him useful. The annex closure should be ratified, not reversed. End the section with the city more entangled, not less, and with the Weir's service question now a matter of public record that somebody in the Assembly has noticed and cannot act on.

### Chapter 37 — One Name Is Not Enough

The first attempt at a witness coalition, and it fails in an ordinary way: Jonas tries to recruit a witness and the witness asks what happens to them, and he has no answer that is not about him. A crossing guard, a clinic clerk, a market cook, a pump operator, a mechanic, a teacher, a Lowglass resident, a transit dispatcher, and Sanaa are the nine roles the volume needs; this chapter should recruit one or two of them properly and lose at least one. Alternate pressure types: do not make this another meeting.

### Chapter 38 — What Two Refusals Look Like

The two-refusal question returns **as work**, not as a speech. A shift is refused by two people on the same rota and the door is open, and Tomas's unanswered question is now a live operational problem with a body attached. Somebody has to be the least-bad option at four in the morning, and the answer must not be Jonas, and at least one person who refuses must not be argued out of it. Do not resolve Tomas's question; let it be worked at and remain open.

### Chapter 39 — The Instance and the Fragment

A civic instance or a physical fault gives Jonas a reason to be in a place where two incompatible service terms meet, and the work requires a witness he has not got. A fragment under a hinge seal may surface here, or it may not appear at all this batch. If it appears it must arrive as a physical object, it must not be an answer, and Jonas must not get to keep it. Keep the Weir's tin sealed.

### Chapter 40 — Nine Names, Not Yet

The batch ends with a list and a gap. Jonas has, at most, six or seven of the nine witnesses, one of whom has refused, one of whom has conditions, and one of whom is a person the city will not protect. He must decide what to do about the handover that is due at the 22:00 read and the term that expires on the 30th, and he must decide it without being able to make anybody consent. End on a person waiting, a deadline, a term somebody has to renew without him, or a demand from the Weir that is now a matter of public record. Do not end on an explanation.
